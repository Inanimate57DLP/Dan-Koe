## Chapter 24 — Process and the Content Ecosystem

Chapters 21 to 23 answered three questions in sequence: why writing is the base skill, what to write about, and how to structure a piece so that it captures and holds attention. This chapter answers the question that remains once those are settled, and it is the one that decides whether any of it survives contact with a working week: how does a single person actually produce, week after week, the writing that feeds an audience and a business, without the work swallowing the rest of life? Koe's answer is not a set of writing tips but a system, and the system has two halves. The first half is a process: how a piece of writing is assembled from captured ideas, an outline that serves as a frame of reference, and a draft that becomes a matter of synthesis rather than invention. The second half is an ecosystem: how that one piece is turned into many, how short and long formats divide the labor of attracting and keeping an audience, where the audience is finally housed, and how the whole apparatus doubles as a laboratory for testing business ideas.

The chapter proceeds in six steps. Section 24.1 reconstructs the weekly writing process: capture, the brain dump, the outline as "frame of reference," the inventories of questions and "elements" the author cycles through when he is stuck, and his reinterpretation of writer's block as incubation. Section 24.2 explains the claim that all of a creator's content is "one organism," in which short form attracts, medium form displays competence and long form builds authority, and it develops the metaphors (the iceberg, the catfish, "building a world") with which Koe argues that depth is what converts attention into trust. Section 24.3 presents the content ecosystem in its successive versions, from the "two hour content ecosystem" of 2022 to the single weekly project of 2026, together with the mechanics of repurposing and the case of the author's own product, 2 Hour Writer. Section 24.4 treats the newsletter as "the Hub": the owned audience that platforms cannot take away. Section 24.5 presents the idea that "a tweet is the new MVP": the short post as a free test of ideas that can be carried up a ladder to newsletters, products and businesses, and the related practice of selling before building. Section 24.6 closes with Koe's position, refined over several years, on writing with AI: it removes friction and accelerates learning, but it should not articulate one's opinions and beliefs.

The chapter depends on Chapter 23, whose structures (hooks, PAS, the problem-to-solution arc) are the units this process assembles, and on Chapter 22, whose hunting and capture of ideas supplies the raw material. It also draws on earlier parts of the book: the distinction between depth and novelty in learning (Part VI), the default mode network and the rhythm of focused work and rest (Chapters 12 and 13), and the thesis of Chapter 19 that "you are the niche," which here reappears as the reason a copied tweet gets half the engagement. It prepares Part IX, where the audience this system produces is analyzed as a source of distribution, leverage and brand.

### 24.1 The Weekly Process and the Outline

#### You do not write from a blank page

The starting point of Koe's process is a negative claim about where writing comes from. "Writing isn't — you don't do it from a blank page, you do it from ideas that you've already stored and want to piece together." Elements, documents and the connections among them are, in his phrase, "building blocks" for writing. The consequence is a diagnosis that reverses the usual complaint of the beginner: "If you don't know what to write it's because you haven't taken down enough notes, you haven't captured enough ideas, you haven't written enough before." Writer's paralysis, on this account, is not a problem located at the moment of writing; it is a symptom of what did or did not happen in the days before, when ideas were or were not captured.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

This connects directly to the hunting of ideas described in Chapter 22. There, capture was presented as a way of finding what to write about; here it becomes the precondition of the writing act itself. The two are the same practice seen from different ends.

In a 2026 video Koe compressed the whole matter into five components that, in his view, all content that "gains traction" and does not disappear "into a black hole of nobody caring" requires. The first is one topic or theme for the week, which in his case is the subject of that week's newsletter. The second is a place to jot ideas down quickly. The third is a way to capture inspiration from outside: social posts, YouTube videos, Substack articles, PDFs, images. The fourth is an understanding of how to package the hook or title so that people want to read the piece, which is the subject of Chapter 23. The fifth is the ability to pull effectively from one's ideas, inspiration and research while writing, which is, as he puts it, the reason for the first three: "that's why you jot down the ideas in the first place is to reference them while you're writing."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

Read as a sequence, the five components describe a pipeline. A theme narrows attention for a week; capture fills a reservoir; packaging decides whether anyone opens the result; retrieval during writing turns the reservoir into sentences. Most of what follows in this section is an elaboration of the last step, the one that converts stored material into a finished piece.

#### Four pages: topic, idea dump, outline, draft

In a 2024 video built around his note-taking product (spelled both Kortex and Cortex in the transcripts; they are the same software), Koe described the four things needed to write something persuasive, such as a newsletter. The first is a topic, preferably one that has already performed well. His suggestion is to search one's favorite accounts, find one of their ideas (any tweet one could see oneself writing about under one's own brand), and use the process to make it one's own; this is the "validated idea" logic of Chapter 22 applied at the start of the writing week. The second is an idea dump, a place to get all one's ideas out and to add new ones as they arrive, because "you won't have all of your ideas at once." The third is an outline page, where the ideas are organized "in a persuasive, impactful, attention-grabbing way." Of this page he says: "this is where most of your writing happens." The fourth is a draft page, where ideas and outline are strung together, and which "shouldn't take long as the work is already done." Any note-taking software works for this, he adds, although Kortex was built for it.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The striking claim is the third. If the outline is where "most of your writing happens," then writing in Koe's sense is mainly the act of deciding which ideas go where and why, and only secondarily the act of producing sentences. The draft becomes almost clerical. This is consistent with Chapter 21's thesis that writing is thinking: the thinking is concentrated in the outline, and the draft records its results.

A 2026 formulation of the same process names three stages and explains why they must be separate. "You don't just start writing the draft. Writers don't just open one page and then expect all the ideas to linearly fall into place." The first stage is the brain dump: writing down all the potential ideas under the topic, looking through one's notes, ideas and links and adding them. Koe does this on a board in Eden (the product his team later built), where he searches his library, asks the AI chat for ideas within it and adds them, so that the research is open while he writes. The second stage is the outline, in which the ideas are fitted into "a general storytelling framework, which usually falls within problem, insight, solution": what falls under the problem; "the main insight ... usually the exciting idea"; and "the solution or the step-by-step." The third stage is the draft: with an empty draft, the brain dump, the research and the outline in front of him, "all you need to do is synthesize them together and actually turn them into sentences."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

"Brain dump" is an ordinary expression that Koe uses with a specific meaning: the exhaustive listing of every possible idea on the week's topic, done before any ordering, as the input of the outline. "Problem, insight, solution" is his name for the default storytelling frame of the outline; it is close to the PAS structure and the problem-to-solution arc of Chapter 23, with the difference that its middle term is not "agitation" but the exciting idea that reframes the problem.

#### The newsletter as a weekly project and the outline as frame of reference

The most distinctive feature of Koe's process is its time scale. He does not write a newsletter in one sitting. In 2025 he described writing a newsletter as "a small project within a bigger project of my business," and explained: "I outline the newsletter at the start of the week I don't just write my newsletters all in one go this is a weekly process the outline creates my frame of reference." He then writes it day by day, slowly; he goes on walks, listens to things, researches, and "then novel ideas come to my mind that I can use to write." The alternative, sitting down and spitting out what is already in his head, "could be good but I'm not necessarily learning anything or doing anything unique." In his Cortex setup at the time, ideas captured on his phone could be linked to the outline document (by typing @ or +) without any AI responding, so that they were waiting for him when he sat down to fill the outline; he could also ask the software's assistant how to approach parts of it.

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md

"Frame of reference" here has a specific sense: the outline, created at the start of the week, is what makes the rest of the week's experience relevant to the piece. Without it, a walk is just a walk and a podcast is just a podcast; with it, both become potential sources for a section that is waiting to be filled. The mechanism is the one Chapter 6 described as the goal acting as a lens: an open question, once set, filters perception. The outline is a small, temporary goal that turns the week into research.

An earlier and more detailed account, from a 2024 "day in the life" video, shows the structure of one of these outlines. "I create an outline for the newsletter itself and therefore the YouTube video." It had five parts. At the top, a brain dump of ideas. Second, "connections": notes, ideas or previous writing he wanted to reference. Third, research: he asked ChatGPT questions, went through his previous content looking for good ideas to reference, and researched YouTube videos and Medium articles "to see what angles or ideas I want to include." Fourth, brainstormed hooks for the headline or title. Fifth, after these, a soft outline, written while reading the connections in a side panel for ideas. The timing is the notable detail: it was Thursday, he had already written that week's newsletter, and the outline on screen was for the following week, "giving myself over a week to add to this outline as I'm writing the actual content." When the time came to write, he created the actual document and used what he called "Cortex elements" (atomic sub-notes inside the software) to structure it, removing them once it became the final newsletter, and he wrote throughout the week with the outline and connections open beside it.

**Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md

By 2026 the same logic had moved into a different tool. In Eden, which his team builds, "a canvas is... where I do my weekly writing. Every week I create a canvas, I paste tweets on there, I paste YouTube videos that inspired me, I put a markdown document for my outline for my newsletter, I put my prompts on there for social posts and for title generation for YouTube and for B-roll ideas, and then I connect things to AI chat to stress test ideas or just talk to the specific content." If a video he had watched that week held an idea, "then I'd talk to it, then I'd go to my newsletter, open that and start writing again." He recommended working through his shared template on a desktop computer, "because that's where you're going to do work and you're not going to be distracted."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

In another 2026 video he described the same setup as a weekly "project board," noting that it could equally be a Notion page. Once the topic is chosen, he creates the board. Inside it go an outline note with whatever ideas have come to mind, "not even close to fleshed out," whose function is only to start creating structure; links to YouTube videos containing ideas he might use; AI chats open alongside, either to ask questions as he writes or to reference a video on the board and ask for questions that pull out the ideas he needs; and quick-captured ideas sent straight into the board, so they are with him while writing, which he calls "a dream come true for any writer." For titles, he references the finished newsletter in a chat; for data-backed titles, he has the AI search a specific creator's titles or consult swipe files saved on the board (a database of YouTube videos and titles he thinks work well). The point of the whole arrangement is summarized in a phrase: "you can write with your research alongside it."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

#### Where the process came from: golden nuggets, dissection and connection

The weekly project has a pre-history. In October 2022, describing what he called "the biggest game changer for me in my business," Koe laid out a three-step newsletter process. First, note "golden nuggets" from researching one's obsessions, as in his "Seven Days to Genius Ideas" challenge; these become the building blocks of newsletters. (A golden nugget, in his usage, is a piece of information picked up while researching an obsession that produces a "wow" or an "aha"; Chapter 22 treats the term in detail.) Second, take a topic one wants to write about and dissect it with his framework, "where you break it into goals benefits perspectives topics whatever." Third, piece together a newsletter and make connections. The connection step was the decisive one. He studies neuroscience and philosophy, notices that "these are saying the same things," and asks how to put them together in a newsletter, which forces him to articulate both. The dissection framework is only listed in the source, not explained, so it cannot be reconstructed beyond its four named headings, and the trailing "whatever" suggests that the list was meant as an illustration rather than a fixed method.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The same passage contains a short personal history. Taking notes, dissecting, fleshing out and writing in his own words, he said, had made his thoughts clear: his YouTube videos from six to eight months earlier were "nowhere near this level of articulation," and "it can happen that fast" if one dedicates oneself to valuable long-form content and opts out of "the short-form rat race," while still maintaining and systematizing short form so as to keep growing. He acknowledged a head start: he had been writing for a long time ("another advantage of mine"). And he drew the commercial conclusion: "most of the value and sales are going to come from nurturing your audience in your newsletter," which can then be turned into a YouTube video, a podcast or tweets. The 2022 video reappears almost word for word in a 2024 compilation, which confirms that he still endorsed the process as described.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The story should be read as what it is: one person's observation about his own improvement, with no control for the years of writing he admits preceded it. What it illustrates is the claim of Chapter 21 that writing is an apparatus for thinking; what it does not establish is a general timetable.

#### The evolution of the process

Across four years the tools and the time scale changed, while the logic stayed stable. The table summarizes the versions the corpus records.

| Period | Version of the process | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | Golden nuggets → dissection (goals, benefits, perspectives, topics) → connection across fields | Capture is driven by obsessions; tools of the time included Tweet Hunter, Twemex and Medium |
| 2024 | Outline in Cortex more than a week ahead: brain dump, connections, research, hooks, soft outline | The outline becomes a standing document filled over more than a week |
| 2025 | Outline at the start of the week as "frame of reference," daily writing, phone captures linked to the outline | The week itself becomes the incubation period |
| 2026 | Weekly canvas or project board in Eden with videos, tweets, outline, prompts and AI chats | AI chats sit beside the research to "stress test" ideas |

The underlying sequence (capture, outline, write with the research alongside) is the same in every version; what changes is the software, which shifts from Cortex/Kortex to Eden as his own products change, and the degree to which AI is integrated into the research stage. Koe gives no reason for the changes; they track the products he was building at each moment.

**Source:** EV-161 in `03b_evolucion.md`

#### Meta documents

Alongside the weekly outline, Koe keeps what he calls "meta documents": linked documents he opens to remind himself how to do the work rather than what to say. One is the pyramid principle, which sits inside his outline template so that he can open it at any time to remind himself what to do, and from there search for writing tips, hooks or persuasion notes. **Complementary context:** the pyramid principle, which Koe uses without naming its author, is the communication method associated with Barbara Minto: state the main point first, then the arguments that support it, then the evidence for each. Chapter 23 discusses how Koe positions it relative to problem-first structures.

The second meta document is harder to identify. The transcript renders its name as "cism" or "kism," which may be a name for his personal philosophy (an "-ism"), though the transcription is ambiguous. It holds all his categories and all the processes and concepts he has come up with, linked together. Setting it up takes time, but whenever he wants "to get back on track with My Philosophy or include one part of it in my newsletters," which he calls "a major part of building a personal brand," he refers to it and pulls ideas from it: the one-person business model, the value creator, "the most profitable niche is you."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The function of this second document illuminates a theme that recurs in Section 24.3: a body of writing produced over years tends toward a philosophy, and keeping that philosophy explicit lets each weekly piece reinforce it rather than drift away from it.

#### Filling the outline: questions, elements and predictable forms

An outline with a topic and a few ideas still leaves the writer with empty sections. Koe has described, on different occasions, three overlapping inventories for filling them. They are worth reading together, because each answers the same practical question (what goes here?) from a different angle.

The first is a set of questions. "Thinking in my opinion is not just a random process. There are good ways to think and bad ways to think." Successful writers, creators and filmmakers, he says, "have soft processes for how they think best," which usually consist in questioning their thoughts in a particular way. ("Soft processes" is his term for these habitual, non-rigid patterns of questioning.) When filling an outline he cycles through six questions. What is the big problem relating to the topic? What is "the consequential cascade of not solving the problem"? What is the ideal life he wants to inspire people to move toward? What novel concepts, perspectives or personal experiences shine an interesting light on the topic "without using someone else's advice"? What is an effective step-by-step process for overcoming the problem and moving toward the ideal life? What compelling quotes, anecdotes, studies or statistics add to the argument? Answering these, he says, produces "a pretty compelling brain dump of ideas." He does not always do it explicitly; mostly it happens in his head. He recommends the exercise in particular "if you are worried about having AI do all of the writing for you," which ties it to Section 24.6: the questions are a way of keeping the thinking one's own.

**Source:** How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md

The "consequential cascade" is a term of his own: the chain of consequences that follow from leaving the problem unsolved. In the structure of the questions it plays the role that "agitation" plays in PAS, but it is framed as a causal chain rather than an emotional intensification.

The six questions also map onto the larger structure of his best work. In the 2024 Kortex video he said that his outline usually contains a "concept element" and a "process element" to brainstorm each, and that his best videos ("the one-person business," "how to productize yourself") are "just the concept and process elements in my outlines." He chooses a topic and frames it according to the best ideas fleshed out in the outline. The concept element corresponds to the question about novel concepts and perspectives; the process element corresponds to the question about the step-by-step process. The observation suggests a simple diagnostic for a weak outline: it lacks either an idea that changes how the reader sees the problem or a procedure that tells them what to do about it.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The second inventory is a list of "elements." In a January 2024 video Koe said that if one is unsure what to say, "good writing is about piecing together what's available to you." The elements, as named in Cortex, are problems, benefits, examples, metaphors, quotes or tweets, personal or popular stories, comparisons, and concepts. Any of them can start, transition or end a section. "You don't just write things out; you have to understand the elements of psychology." He illustrated how they chain: start with a problem; if you don't know what comes next, use an example; then a metaphor; then a comparison; then a personal experience; then a quote you like "to bring more credibility to your argument," and continue as long as you want. (The word "elements" has three different senses in the corpus: these persuasive components; atomic sub-notes inside Kortex; and the days of a weekly template in the Cortex planner. Here only the first is meant.)

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The third inventory, from late 2025, is framed by a metaphor. Long pieces leave beginners staring at a blank screen not knowing how to fill sections. "Writing is like Legos with ideas. And ideas come in predictable forms. If you understand those forms, you can guide your mind to brainstorming what to write next." The forms are: a pain point (start a section with one and ideas flow); an example (which can go anywhere and grounds what you are saying); a personal story (a time in your life that relates); a statistic (a truthful one that adds authority); a metaphor ("explain a complex idea as if you're talking to a child. Alan Watts is incredible at this"); a quote ("easy because they're almost always great ideas"); a reframe (a different perspective on the point just discussed); or, finally, the questions what, how or why, "because when all else fails, writing and speaking are just thinking and thinking is questioning." "If I don't know what to write or what to say next, I tend to cycle through those in my head or latch on to the closest one." With practice, he says, this becomes second nature, "and your thinking process starts to evolve."

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The three inventories overlap heavily, and the overlap is informative.

| Function in a section | Questions (2025) | Elements (2024) | Predictable forms (2025) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Establish what is wrong | Big problem; consequential cascade | Problems | Pain point |
| Show what is possible | Ideal life | Benefits | — |
| Change how the reader sees it | Novel concepts, perspectives, experiences | Concepts; comparisons; metaphors | Metaphor; reframe |
| Tell them what to do | Step-by-step process | — | — |
| Support and ground | Quotes, anecdotes, studies, statistics | Examples; quotes or tweets; stories | Example; personal story; statistic; quote |
| When nothing else works | — | — | What, how, why |

The questions are the most complete version because they include the process; the elements and forms are more granular at the level of the paragraph. The metaphor of Legos, which Koe uses elsewhere for notes, work blocks and lifestyle choices, here carries the explanation: a section is not composed from nothing but assembled from a small number of standard pieces, and knowing the pieces turns a blank space into a choice among options. The final form ("thinking is questioning") links the whole apparatus back to Chapter 17's account of thinking as the asking of questions.

#### Writer's block as incubation

The last element of the process concerns what happens when none of this works. Koe's position, stated in a November 2022 video, is that writer's block "is the best thing that could ever happen to you." Every writer or creative struggles with writer's or creative blocks, and people assume this is bad, but it is the moment when "your mind is ready to munch on those problems." The claim has a precondition: one has to have started writing first, because only a mind already engaged with a problem has something to work on. When you hit a wall, "you go on a walk you lay by the pool you rest you let your brain turn on the default mode Network," so that it works on the problem in the subconscious; then, "out of nowhere boom," a revelation or idea is sent to the conscious mind, you write it down and continue. If one is stuck in instant gratification ("I can't write this right now"), the instruction is to put the piece down: "you're allowed to write a piece over a week or a month or however long it takes." In the same passage he attributes almost everything in his life to writing, from his mental health to his business success, notes that all his content (YouTube, Instagram, Twitter, LinkedIn, the podcast) begins with a script, and calls writing "the most profitable and non-replaceable creative skill," one that pairs with any other skill. He mentions his course The Two Hour Writer, which Section 24.3 discusses.

**Source:** The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md

**Complementary context:** the default mode network is a set of brain regions that is more active during rest, mind-wandering and self-referential thought than during focused external tasks; it has been associated in research with spontaneous thought and some forms of creative incubation. Koe uses it, as he does elsewhere in the corpus (Chapters 12 and 13), as an explanatory frame for why rest after focused work produces ideas. He does not cite specific studies for this application, and the claim should be read as his interpretation of a general finding rather than as a demonstrated mechanism of writer's block.

A June 2022 video adds a third-party version of the same pattern, with an attribution Koe himself marks as uncertain: "It's like gary halbert i think or david ogilvy or really most writers." Great writers, he says, brainstorm, outline everything, and then do nothing: they lie by the pool, listen to music, have a fun night out, and "the perfect idea pops into their brain," the breakthrough, the "aha moment," after which they come back and create something better. He then generalizes the pattern beyond writing: "this is a constant process of iteration refining your macro vision for the life you are trying to create." The attribution should not be firmed up: the transcript does not settle which copywriter, if either, described this practice.

**Source:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md

The two passages explain why the weekly time scale described earlier is not a matter of convenience. If the outline is created at the start of the week and filled day by day, the walks and pauses between sessions are built-in incubation periods. The 2025 description ("I go on walks, listen to things, research, then novel ideas come to my mind") is the 2022 theory of writer's block turned into a schedule. The block is not something to be fought at the desk; it is a signal to let the outline do its work as a frame of reference while attention is elsewhere.

### 24.2 One Organism: Short Form Attracts, Long Form Converts

#### Three layers of a single body

The process of Section 24.1 produces, in the first instance, one long piece a week. Why long, and what happens to the shorter formats that dominate social media? Koe's answer begins with a metaphor. In an October 2024 video he asked creators to think of all their content as a single unit: "your posts threads and newsletters are all one organism." The organism has three layers, each with a distinct function. Short form attracts a broad and somewhat shallow audience. Medium form (threads, shorter YouTube videos) goes deeper and lets the right people choose to join the newsletter, watch the videos, or read the books, guides or courses. Long form (newsletters, long videos, podcasts) is for dedicated fans who are aligned with the creator's goals and want to learn as much as they can.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The metaphor matters because it rules out a common way of thinking about formats, in which each platform is a separate project to be optimized on its own terms. If the formats are organs of one body, then judging a short post by whether it sells, or a newsletter by whether it goes viral, is a category mistake: each organ is judged by its contribution to the whole.

The same video specifies the function of each layer as a step. The first step is to write short form to attract. Koe insists that a traffic source is still needed to fuel a newsletter: "you can't write off social media." His analogy is the new author who publishes on Amazon expecting the platform to send traffic and make them a millionaire. Unless one is taking the traditional route, in which a publisher or a record label supplies centralized traffic, one has to market oneself and have one's own audience. "Short form is your base." The instructions for it are specific: write your opinions; give short, actionable advice but "don't fill in the blanks," leaving gaps so that people ask questions and engage; make polarizing statements that are true from your perspective, and let people who cannot see both sides filter themselves out. The simplest version of the practice is to pick any idea (even one from the video itself), write it from one's own perspective, not worry about the first draft, edit it to be attention-grabbing and impactful, "reference others content structures not ideas to enhance your own," and iterate based on the engagement it receives.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

"Don't fill in the blanks" is a coined phrase: give enough to be useful and leave enough unsaid to provoke a question. It is the short-form equivalent of the open loop discussed in Chapter 23, and it serves the organism by creating a reason for the reader to move one layer deeper.

The second step is medium form, whose role "is to display competence." The examples are threads, carousels, shorter YouTube videos, posts on Instagram Threads, and LinkedIn posts. These build authority and trust, give enough information for people to "instant follow," and lead many readers to the bottom of the thread, where the newsletter opt-in or a product is promoted. Koe concedes that one can build a high-quality, authoritative audience with threads alone. But follower count still does not guarantee reach, and it is more important for people to see one's content again and again than once, which is the argument for the third layer.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The third step is long form, and its function is formulated as a ladder he calls the "hierarchy of trust and value": "posts lead to readers threads lead to fans newsletters lead to super fans." All content should move people up this hierarchy. He connects it to the idea of "1,000 true fans," which he cites without naming its author: an engaged email list "is all you need to set yourself up for life."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

**Complementary context:** "1,000 True Fans" is the title of a 2008 essay by Kevin Kelly, which argued that a creator needs only about a thousand people willing to buy everything they make in order to earn a living. Koe adapts the idea by mapping the "true fan" onto the top rung of his format hierarchy: the super fan is the newsletter reader.

| Layer | Formats | Function | Who it reaches | What it produces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short form | Tweets, short posts, Reels, Shorts, TikToks | Attract; test ideas; stay top of mind | A broad, somewhat shallow audience | Readers |
| Medium form | Threads, carousels, shorter videos, long LinkedIn posts, "micro articles" | Display competence; earn the "instant follow"; route to the newsletter or product | People who choose to go deeper | Fans |
| Long form | Newsletters, long videos, podcasts, books | Build authority and trust; hold invested attention; sell | Dedicated readers aligned with the creator's goals | Super fans |

#### Short form builds the audience, long form keeps it

A more abstract version of the same division appears in a September 2023 video. There are two main types of content and communication. Long form includes podcasts, videos, articles, newsletters, conversations, movies, and sometimes long social posts such as threads. Short form includes tweets, Shorts, Reels, texts, emails, big ideas and one's favorite quotes. Each has advantages and disadvantages, but over the long term both are necessary to build an audience that trusts you, and "trust is the new sales hack that people are starting to catch on to." Short form is "for capturing attention, building a large audience and funneling that audience to deeper sources of value like long-form platforms, products and services." Long form is "for holding invested attention, segmenting your audience and building trust through expertise." And "the balance of both forces you to have value to deliver."

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

One detail of this classification is inconsistent with the rest of the corpus and should be noted: here emails are listed as short form, while the newsletter, which is delivered by email, is treated everywhere as long form. The likely reading is that "emails" here means short individual messages rather than the newsletter, but the source does not say so.

The phrase "segmenting your audience" names a function of long form that is easy to miss. A long piece is self-selecting: only people with a real interest in the topic will finish it, so the act of consuming it sorts the audience. This is the mechanism behind the hierarchy of trust: each step up is also a filter.

In 2024 Koe compressed the point into a symmetrical rule. For trust, authority and leverage, long form matters more; for traffic, virality and attention, short form matters more. Both matter, and relying on one limits long-term success. Shallow topics all day can make quick cash but do not build anything lasting "in digital and mental real estate." Without long form, a creator will be forgotten, and their business can be shut down by platforms "at the snap of a finger." "If you don't write short you don't build your audience. If you don't write long you don't keep your audience."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The same video frames the argument with a recent post by Naval Ravikant: shorts, reels and tweets are naturally shallow because there is no time to go deep. Koe's interpretation, explicitly his own extension, is that short-form platforms will not be as relevant to a creator's survival as people think, nor give all the results they want. Relying on YouTube ad revenue or X creator payouts is not a good way to control one's income; one needs to sell, consistently, products and services that help people and for which one can charge more. He gives his own figures: roughly $300 a week or a month from Instagram Reels, about $1,000 "here and there" from Twitter, about $10,000 "here and there" from YouTube, compared with $3–4 million a year selling his own products and services "with the same amount traffic me creating the same exact videos."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The figures are his self-report, given loosely ("here and there"), and the comparison is between platform payouts and product revenue generated by the same traffic. What they illustrate is the structural point of this section: attention captured in short form is monetized poorly where it is captured and well where it is taken next.

#### Attention held over time

Why should depth matter so much? Koe's most developed answer comes from a June 2023 video and begins with a realization he describes as having come late in his journey, during a podcast with Matt Gray. His tweets hold perhaps five seconds of attention each: scattered attention, not held for long. When he sees an idea on Twitter, he automatically relates it to a book he has read and attributes it to that book's author; anything on spirituality "goes straight to Eckhart Tolle," because he invested six to eight hours of attention in that author's book. From this he draws a general principle: "power in this modern world is determined by how much attention you hold over time." Short form (tweets, reels, Shorts, TikToks) will not build authority, will not make you memorable, and will not make "meaningful money," only "superficial shallow short-term money." Hence his emphasis on long form (the newsletter, YouTube, the podcast, writing a book), fed by top-of-funnel social platforms that are easier to grow through short form. He does not pretend this is easy: newsletters take a long time to get good at, YouTube even longer, and one has to raise one's skill to match the challenge of being an impactful creator. If this is one's life's work, which he believes it will be for 80% of the population over the next fifty years, one should spend one's time on it.

**Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md

"Attention held over time" is Koe's own formulation and it carries the argument of the whole section. It explains the asymmetry between formats not by quality but by duration: a book and a tweet can contain the same idea, but the book's reader has spent hours with its author, and that investment is what attaches the idea to the name. Note also what the passage nuances. In the same period Koe relied heavily on tweets as his primary content, and the realization about five seconds of attention is partly a correction of his own emphasis. The "80% of the population" figure is a prediction, not a finding, and is offered without support.

The same principle appears in an April 2023 video as a recommendation about business structure. "Long form content should be the pillar of your business because it forces you to develop depth behind your ideas" and actually educate people, rather than writing a 280-character post on Instagram, TikTok, Shorts or Twitter. With short form alone one is "not building much Authority" (he immediately qualifies: "you're building some right it's not black or white"), but not as much as one could, and one is not making as many sales or nurturing and educating the audience as much as one could.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The oldest version, from August 2022, is the simplest. Set up a blog or newsletter software mainly to build authority: "long form content builds authority more than like a short form tweet or reel or tick tock," and short form is difficult to make sales on. People use short form to drive people to their email list, and they sell once they have built authority "with the depth that they provide in their content and holding attention for longer." This passage reappears nearly verbatim in a 2024 compilation, where it serves as the preface to the "two hour content ecosystem" described in Section 24.3.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In January 2025 he added a further reason, tied to his interest in connecting disciplines. Cross-discipline connection "is much easier to do in something like a long-form video or a long-form newsletter." "If you're a creator who only makes short-form content like TikToks or Reels or even tweets… you're not building as much reputation as you think." "There's a reason you remember the author that changed your life with the book." One may remember a life-changing tweet, "but that's probably only because you've spent enough time with that person's content; you have all of the moving pieces that make that idea work." Without that context, "that singular tweet means nothing to most people." "That's what books do for you, that's what longer form content does for you." Social media, by contrast, "is good for attracting people who have that kind of beginner-level shallow awareness because they're not on social media to learn, they're getting on to be entertained. So you need to grab them and… take them to the deeper stuff and… raise their awareness."

**Source:** You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md

This adds a cognitive layer to the commercial one. A tweet that changes a life is not self-sufficient; it works because the reader already holds the "moving pieces" supplied elsewhere, usually by long form. Short form, on this view, is parasitic on context, and long form is where context is built.

#### The iceberg and the catfish

The October 2022 video in which Koe first developed these ideas at length is organized around a metaphor that runs through the whole talk. Content is an iceberg. The tip is shallow, catches attention, is "shiny and flashy." The question is what lies beneath it: "is there actually depth there or is it just the tip and then there's no depth." Without depth, "it's impossible to switch from that dopamine treadmill to okay I need to actually appreciate this and make it a part of my life." He applies the image to certain marketers who are "kind of world famous and hated by 50% of the people that see their stuff," but who, in interviews, turn out to be down to earth, grounded, "Geniuses": "they have the depth to back the front they are putting on." In the rest of the video the hook is the tip and the body of expertise is the depth.

**Source:** Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md

The metaphor connects this chapter to the analysis of dopamine in Chapter 11. The tip is the "up" chemistry of novelty and wanting; depth is what allows the attention it captures to settle into appreciation, which Koe associates with the "down" chemistry of satisfaction. A creator who offers only the tip keeps the audience on the treadmill and never converts their attention into anything durable.

He then gives the failure case a name. The attention game "can go awry" if one has only the tip and does not deliver on the value. The example is the catfish on a dating app: someone who displays and manipulates what catches attention, makes people desire them, but on the first date looks nothing like their photos, has a terrible personality, and "you guys don't mesh at all"; "that's not valuable." In business the equivalent is a promise like "I will make you five thousand dollars with this email marketing service" made by someone who cannot deliver because they have not taken "the three six twelve months to develop your skill set, the depth in order to deliver on that dopamine that you are catching attention with." Then "that's called a scam."

**Source:** Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md

The catfish image establishes an ethical constraint on everything in Chapter 23. Hooks, packaging and polarizing statements are legitimate only if what is under them can bear the weight of the promise. A hook without depth is not just ineffective in the long run; in Koe's framing it is a form of deception.

From the same video comes the example he returns to most often. James Clear, author of *Atomic Habits*, "could literally write the word habits, send the tweet and it would get a ton of engagement"; someone else posting the same word would not. The reason: "the people that follow James Clear understand that he is an expert and has Authority in the field because he has depth in the form of a book." This is what many content creators and freelancers miss. They start posting without developing depth "not only in themselves but in their brand and in their content and in their offers," and when they post the same thing as someone else and it fails, "it's because nobody knows who they are": they have not changed people's perspective "by developing depth with long-form content."

**Source:** Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md

And from the same source comes his dislike of a common piece of advice: "I don't like the advice of oh just just keep posting tick tocks just keep posting reels," because "that's not going to lead you anywhere good if you don't have a long-form blog, a long-form newsletter, maybe a podcast or a YouTube where you can actually show that you know your stuff." Long form is what builds "the true appreciation, depth and authority" that a brand, a creator or a human being is looking for.

**Source:** Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md

#### Building a world rather than a funnel

In January 2025 Koe restated the James Clear example in different terms. "Even if you were to get a million views on one YouTube video if you're starting from zero subscribers I think you'd be kind of disappointed" with the subscribers, retained viewers or fans it yields. It is "less about funnels" (which matter) and "more about like building a world." "The more content people are exposed to from you the more they can read your content from a better context." The consequence is freedom: once people have that context, one can be "a lot more flexible." "James Clear can say habits and 100 million people will like it when someone else could say it and it's going to get zero views."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

"Building a world" is a coined phrase for the cumulative context that a body of content gives each new piece. The contrast with a funnel is instructive. A funnel is a linear path designed to move a stranger to a purchase; a world is an environment in which repeated exposure changes how everything one says is read. Chapter 27 returns to this image when it treats the brand as an environment of transformation.

Two other passages draw the same conclusion from different angles. In a June 2024 video Koe told creators that their engagement "probably sucks because you've never taken your audience deeper than social media." Multiple free products substantially increase authority: one teaches more deeply, people like you more, engage more, start conversations in direct messages, and feel that you contribute more value. Without a newsletter, a lead magnet or a product, one lacks authority and becomes "this like high engagement account with little to show for it." If one does not know what to create, one can study what others offer (templates, checklists, swipe files); his own preference is a free product that helps the audience achieve one small, desirable goal through a mini course or an ebook. Mark Manson and James Clear, he adds, do not need to optimize their content, because so many people have bought and read their books and have spent hours with their ideas.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

In a July 2023 video he answered a question about whether constantly building products had made him "overly salesy." "No, it led to audiences consuming that information, changing their life, because it's ten times more deep than anything on social media can be just by the nature of it: it's a hierarchy." As a result, they read his content "from a lens of more experience" (experience he is helping them gain) and "from an understanding of who I am and My Philosophy." "Anyone can copy and paste my tweet but it's going to get half the engagement because I'm not the one posting it. You are the niche."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

This is the thesis of Chapter 19 seen from the side of distribution. The niche is the person, and the person's depth (products, long form, a philosophy) is what makes a given sentence land when they say it and not when someone else does. The "half the engagement" figure is a rhetorical estimate, not a measurement.

#### Why people avoid depth

If depth is so valuable, why do so few creators build it? In the same 2023 video Koe gives a psychological answer. "Most people are afraid": they don't publish anything substantial, they create content and think that is good enough, and "they hide behind a service." Used to one-on-one freelancing, they think "this isn't in public so I'm not going to be [under] scrutinous eyes or criticized, when that's what leads to growth." They do not realize that "long form content products and services are the backbone of your entire brand."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

A related heuristic from the same video explains a common frustration. To talk about certain interests, one needs foundational content (a lead magnet or a newsletter) in place to bring people up to speed. "If you aren't getting engagement when you start talking about a new interest it's because you haven't developed depth under it," and one does not realize that "your audience is not on the same page of understanding as you." The remedy is to start at the beginner level and introduce the interest "in a persuasive manner. Study persuasion." With time and effort, "within one or two years you can really start doing whatever you want."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

This heuristic applies the iceberg to a single topic rather than to a whole brand. Each new interest needs its own submerged mass before its tip can attract, which is also the practical form of Chapter 22's thesis that interests are persuaded rather than innate.

An October 2022 warning places the same problem in a wider frame. Many people escape the corporate rat race only to enter "the social media rat race," focused exclusively on growth and on what kind of content to write in order to grow. That is true to an extent, he concedes: one must grow, and one must "shut your ego off" to provide beginner value. But one must also write, dissect and "create depth behind the things that you're truly passionate about because that is how you become an expert."

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The concession is important, because the corpus contains a recurring tension here. Elsewhere Koe insists that most of the market are beginners and that a creator must serve them with simple content. The "social media rat race" warning does not retract that advice; it balances it. Beginner content is for growth, depth is for expertise, and a creator who has only the first is trapped in the race he left his job to escape.

#### Going the other way

Koe tells one story about this choice that belongs here because it shows the position being taken against the grain. In an October 2022 video he said that success is counterintuitive, and that counterintuitiveness and skepticism are values of his. When people told him to go short form, to make reels "because it's the future," he did the opposite and started writing long-form newsletters. He notes that he does make reels, but he did not abandon what was working. He presents the decision as an application of self-reliance.

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md

The anecdote should not be overread as a rule ("always do the opposite"). Its point is narrower: a creator who understands the function of each format can resist a platform trend that would otherwise pull all their effort into one layer of the organism.

#### The content funnel: grow on social, sell in the newsletter

The organism has a commercial anatomy as well. In a January 2023 video Koe described broad social content as top of funnel. Once people are in one's newsletter, lead magnet or some other funnel that "educates them in alignment with the service that you have to offer," that is where sales come from. In his words, "you aren't and shouldn't be promoting in all of your content."

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

A February 2023 video adds a distinction in specificity. At the beginning, Koe treated his podcast and YouTube as places for "this is what I want to talk about." But if a platform is heavily influenced by an algorithm, one may have to go broader in some cases. The newsletter is where one can "talk about whatever the [ __ ] you want," and there one should get more specific, because that is where an audience is nurtured toward a more advanced level: the upper three to five range of the customer's levels of awareness. The same applies to a lead magnet.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

**Complementary context:** the "levels of awareness" come from Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), which classifies prospects as unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware and most aware. Koe uses the scale without always naming its author. His "upper three to five range" is ambiguous: it most plausibly refers to the more advanced levels (solution-aware to most aware), where a reader is ready to hear about a specific product.

The two passages describe a gradient. On algorithm-driven platforms, content must be broad enough for the algorithm to distribute it to strangers; in owned channels, content can be as specific as the creator's real interests, because the people there have already chosen to be there. Breadth at the top, specificity at the bottom: the same principle Chapter 19 called "broad catch, specific sale."

How does a stranger enter the funnel in the first place? A February 2023 passage describes the best case. "All it takes is one piece of content." A good piece is retweeted; a big account sees it and retweets it; a huge account sees it; it lands on a meme page; perhaps it is mentioned in the news. Koe calls this "a way overly optimal case" that nevertheless "can happen to anyone," and "a matter of learning how to write a good piece of media" and having it spread. Then people know your name and what you do; you attract them to your audience; you nurture them with content so they get to know you more; and eventually you offer a product or service to bring in an income. Everything, he says, is "media," "content."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

The sequence (attract, nurture, offer) is the funnel stated in its most general form. The cascade at the start is presented as a possibility, not a plan; the plan is the rest of the sequence, which works whether or not lightning strikes.

#### Branching into long form behind validated topics

Koe's roadmap for building a one-person business, presented in December 2022 and repeated in a 2024 compilation, places long form at a specific point: the third step of the second stage. By then the creator knows which tweets, posts and threads perform well and which are their highest-performing topics. The task is to "create depth and authority behind them": turn them into a newsletter, flesh them out, add one's thoughts, then turn the newsletters into YouTube videos or podcasts, getting used to giving more value than a 280-character tweet. "This depth is what's going to lead to more authentic sales." One does not have to stick to direct-response copywriting, in which a thread leads to a click, a landing page and an immediate purchase. A creator is not in the position of a paid advertiser who must retarget repeatedly: people see the creator's content consistently, and in the newsletter the creator raises them "up the levels of awareness in marketing" to the point of wanting the product, which "doesn't have to happen the first time." The instruction is to stick it out, notice what is going right and wrong, and double down on what is right.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

"Authentic sales" here means sales that follow from understanding built over time, as opposed to sales extracted by pressure in a single exposure. The roadmap also fixes a sequence: in this version short form comes first, and long form is built "behind" topics that short form has already validated. Section 24.5 develops this validation logic; Section 24.4 records that in other periods Koe recommended starting the newsletter first.

#### A working rhythm: spray and pray, and traffic firepower

In September 2024 Koe translated the organism into a weekly rhythm. The first type of content is the short post (Section 24.5 describes it). The second type is the long post: X threads, Threads, Instagram carousels, long LinkedIn posts or carousels, or what he calls "micro articles," which he says had been doing well. He writes them in Cortex, copies them into Figma, downloads the image and posts it; if the piece fits a 4×5 image template, "you can post them anywhere": the YouTube Community page (from which he says he grows a lot), Instagram, LinkedIn. "It's all writing." The two types are treated differently. Short posts are "kind of like spray and prey" (the transcript's spelling of "spray and pray"): let them go, take the best and run with them. Long posts are strategic: they take more time to create and should be based on very good ideas that set one apart, such as valuable topics, breakdowns, actionable advice, one's story or a deep thought. Because one posts them only once or twice a week, they hold more attention, build more trust and lead to more followers, so one needs a network to get them in front of people rather than hoping that "the magical Twitter gods or YouTube gods" will spread them. The 1% of creators picked up by the algorithm "make it seem like anyone can"; Koe says he believes in "manual effort and investment to grow your brand." His recap: two to three short posts a day and one to two long posts a week, using all of one's "traffic firepower": friends sharing, potentially paying people to share, tagging people so they repost, and sending direct messages to get reposts and shares.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

"Micro articles," "spray and pray" and "traffic firepower" are his terms for, respectively, medium-form pieces in image format, the low-investment logic of short posts, and the network resources deployed behind each long piece. The networking side of "traffic firepower" is developed in Chapter 26.

A December 2024 heuristic adds the platform choice that corresponds to the organism. Koe favors pairing one short-form platform with one long-form platform, and says everyone needs an email list. One could swap the list for YouTube or a podcast, but then one needs audio or video editing and perhaps equipment. Short-form writing on social media plus a newsletter is "arguably the highest leverage route": newsletters can be used as YouTube video ideas and scripts, and the recordings posted to a podcast, so that writing the newsletter builds "a bank of potential future videos." He recommended beehiiv as newsletter software (free up to roughly 2,500 subscribers at the time, he said; he had recently switched to it, spoken with its CEO and liked its culture), mentioning a possible discount through his link while adding "don't hold me to that."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

#### How the position evolved

Koe's statements about short and long form across 2022–2026 do not form a clean chronological shift, and the corpus records them as a refinement with contradictory entry recommendations rather than as a change of mind.

| Period | Representative statements |
|---|---|
| 2022 | Long form builds authority and short form drives people to the list; he goes long when everyone says "go short"; "just keep posting reels" leads nowhere; "opt out of the short-form rat race" while still systematizing short form; in the roadmap, short form first and long form behind validated topics |
| 2023 | Tweets hold five seconds of attention; long form should be the pillar; short form "captures," long form "holds"; at times he recommends starting with the newsletter, at other times with short form |
| 2024 | Everyone should start a newsletter; the three-layer organism; "short posts are your base"; "short form is your base"; "both matter" |
| 2025–2026 | Get good at tweets first; long form as "a moat" because AI cannot replicate it; short form remains the mechanism for growth and testing ideas |

What changes is less the role of each format than the justification of long form. In 2022–2023 it is justified by authority and sales; by 2026 it is also justified cognitively (writing as thinking) and competitively, as the form that AI cannot imitate, which Section 24.6 discusses. The two formats can coexist because they answer different needs: short form for growth and testing, long form for depth, ownership of the audience and sales. The entry point (start short or start with the newsletter) is where the corpus genuinely disagrees with itself, and the most coherent reading, supported by the ecosystem entries in Section 24.3, is that the answer depends on whether one already has distribution.

**Source:** EV-148 in `03b_evolucion.md`

### 24.3 The Content Ecosystem and Repurposing

#### The two hour content ecosystem

If all content is one organism, the practical question is how one person keeps every organ fed. Koe's answer, first named in an August 2022 video, is what he called "the two hour content ecosystem." He "rarely use[s] fancy sales funnels or any of that [ __ ] anymore." Instead he writes a newsletter every week; condenses the actionable part of it into a Twitter thread; bases his tweets for the week on that thread; and plugs his "backlog newsletter" (an earlier issue, published on his blog) under his tweets, so that people can read it, subscribe, and buy the products and services mentioned inside it. "Hence you rarely see me promote on the timeline," unless he is launching a new product, as he was in that video with The Two Hour Writer. The passage reappears almost literally in a 2024 compilation, preceded by the argument for long form discussed in Section 24.2.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

Two terms are introduced here. The "content ecosystem" is his name for a self-sustaining content system that generates traffic toward products and replaces the sales funnel; "two hour" refers to the daily writing time it requires. The "backlog newsletter" is an old issue republished on the blog and linked under new posts. The backlog link is the quiet mechanism of the system: every new short post carries a path to a long piece that has already been written, and every long piece carries the offers. Promotion is therefore continuous but almost invisible, embedded in the routing rather than stated in the feed. (The corpus records that this "rarely promote" stance later gave way to an explicit promotion calendar; Section 24.4 returns to it.)

In a May 2023 video titled after his first-year revenue figure, Koe laid out the ecosystem as a numbered sequence. One: write one newsletter a week (about 1,000 words). Two: upload the newsletter as a blog post. Three: write one thread condensed from the newsletter (or a medium-form piece such as an Instagram carousel). Four: write three tweets a day from ideas in the newsletter. Five: these become YouTube video scripts, LinkedIn posts, Instagram carousels, reels, TikToks and a podcast. Six: cross-post the tweets to Instagram, LinkedIn and everywhere else. Seven: under those posts, link the blog post.

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

The same video explains how three tweets a day can be extracted from one weekly newsletter without running dry. From the weekly thousand words one has "a plethora of ideas": read one sentence and ask "how do I turn this into a tweet." His best advice is to create two or three lists. If one writes about bodybuilding, for example, a list might be "five reasons to become a bodybuilder"; list the five reasons, then turn each of them into its own tweet or list, "and then you just keep going." "That's how you ... start to create like infinite content ideas." This is done only once a week, because the topics rotate with each new newsletter.

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

A month later, in June 2023, he summarized the system as a funnel stated in a single breath. "My funnel is simple": write a big-idea-centric newsletter every week; write three punchy and memorable tweets every day; repurpose them to all platforms; newsletters become YouTube scripts. He teaches the newsletter method in The 2 Hour Writer, which he describes as centered on idea generation and covering the practical use of "productivity mode" and "creativity mode." He closed with two slogans: "you must capture, hold and nurture their attention with ideas," and "modern success is mental warfare; creativity, rest and short work hours are the catalyst."

**Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md

The second slogan connects the ecosystem to the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12. The ecosystem is designed so that writing takes a bounded block of the morning, leaving room for the rest and creative incubation that Section 24.1 described as part of the writing process itself.

#### The three-point micro business model and the flywheel

An April 2023 video gives the ecosystem a different name and, crucially, a loop. The "three-point micro business model" runs as follows: start a newsletter; write the newsletter; condense it into a thread, an Instagram carousel or any other repurposable format; turn those into tweets and let them go (also repurposed to other platforms); see which perform best; turn the best into a newsletter; and repeat the cycle "for years and years and years until you develop a philosophy that people love to read about." Koe's own philosophy, he says, is "the one person business model and a bunch of philosophical Concepts to back that." The structure "newsletter thread tweet" forms a traffic source, a "content ecosystem" that generates traffic to send to one's products or services. Most of Twitter's successful people, he says, run on this because they are writers; it is a flywheel of long, medium and short form content "holding the most attention and delivering the best ideas to people's minds."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The loop is what distinguishes this version. Content flows down from the newsletter to tweets, but performance data flows back up: the tweets that do best become next week's newsletter. Over years, the selection pressure of the audience shapes the writer's topics into a coherent body of thought, which is how a series of weekly pieces becomes a "philosophy." This also explains the meta document of Section 24.1: once the loop has run long enough to produce a philosophy, it is worth writing the philosophy down.

A February 2023 video describes the same loop as "the endless content flywheel": a post does well; turn it into a newsletter; break it down; new ideas do better; turn them into posts; break those down; and so on. "You just start to create this endless content flywheel."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

#### Top-down or bottom-up

The same February 2023 video states a preference about the direction of flow. Koe prefers to cover his topic tree (the map of intersecting interests described in Chapter 22) "from the top down": a long newsletter that can turn into a YouTube video; those break down into medium-form content (an Instagram caption, a long LinkedIn post, a Twitter thread); and those break down further, or the original newsletter is used for idea generation for tweets, Instagram posts, reels, TikToks, YouTube Shorts and YouTube Community posts. He covers this method in 2 Hour Writer.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

A January 2023 video, in which he recommended treating one's niche like a book, gives the top-down chain its most literal form, repeated "for the sake of repetition because it's important": write the sections of each chapter as an article or newsletter; repurpose that writing into a podcast or YouTube video; condense the main points into a thread, carousel or LinkedIn post; rewrite those main points as engaging tweets; turn those tweets into reel scripts and posts for other platforms.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

The two directions coexist in the corpus. Top-down starts with the long piece and descends; bottom-up starts with tested tweets and ascends to the newsletter. The flywheel combines both: the newsletter descends into tweets, and the best tweets ascend into the next newsletter.

#### Starting on X: the content stack

In March 2024 Koe described a version that starts at the bottom. He recommended "starting on X or Twitter and then building on top of that with a newsletter." The newsletter "eventually gets turned into YouTube scripts, podcast scripts," and "all of your tweets get turned into Instagram posts, LinkedIn posts, scripts for shorts, reels, TikToks etc." "It all starts with writing," and "if you test your ideas at the beginning and build an audience on something like X, that becomes the base social media platform for everything that you do." He noted that this is what is taught in Cortex University (his cohort program), whose first course, out of three depending on tier, is "all about writing, branding and building an audience."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

The corpus calls this the "content stack": a base social platform where ideas are tested, with a newsletter built on top and repurposing layered on both. A November 2023 heuristic gives the beginner's version. For writers, Koe always recommends X and a newsletter: one short-form and one long-form platform to start. When one has done this long enough and seen enough growth, one repurposes the newsletter to YouTube, a podcast and so on, and the tweets to LinkedIn, YouTube Community posts, Instagram, and scripts for Reels and Shorts. "This is why writing is the most important and valuable skill you can learn because it's the foundation of all of this." This, too, is taught in 2 Hour Writer.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

A January 2024 video adds a qualification about timing. At step three of his plan, write 500 to 1,000 words a week, because one needs "a balance of depth and growth": long form and short form. He is "big on a newsletter" as long form, "but that can wait." At the start, focus on one platform such as X, write threads as the long form once a week, and post two to three times a day. At step four, deconstruct and simplify ideas for daily posts, since long form gives one multiple ideas to pull from; but "don't try to copy-paste posts from your newsletters or threads; rewrite them as standalone short posts."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

"Depth and growth" is his name for the balance between the two formats. The instruction to rewrite rather than copy is important, and it contrasts with a later instruction to cross-post identical content, discussed below. The two are not the same operation: rewriting concerns turning a long piece into short pieces within a platform; cross-posting concerns publishing the same piece on several platforms.

#### The one-person media company

In July 2024 Koe restated the ecosystem as an identity. "Writing allows you to become a one person media company." The chain he described starts at the bottom: write one post on X and cross-post it to Instagram and LinkedIn; turn that post into a script for a reel, a Short or a TikTok; choose your best posts and expand them into threads or newsletters; these "then get repurposed into carousels video scripts and podcast episodes." "Even better you don't have to show your face as a writer or edit videos if you don't want to you just sit down in the morning and become one with the words on the page." And he added a reason that goes beyond distribution: "It's a very meaningful lifestyle and a means to Improvement you learn more by teaching organizing your thoughts and getting feedback on your ideas it is a catalyst for growth and value."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md

"One-person media company" is one of Koe's recurring phrases, with several senses across the corpus (the value creator's distribution under his own name, the online business as a media company of one). In this passage it means specifically the writer whose single daily piece is distributed across every platform. The final sentence links the ecosystem back to Chapter 21 and to the learning chapters of Part VI: teaching, organizing thoughts and receiving feedback are forms of learning, so the ecosystem is also a curriculum.

#### Everything in one weekly piece (2026)

By 2026 the ecosystem had returned to the newsletter as its hub and had been simplified further. In a March 2026 video Koe said one could "just start with a newsletter and a short-form platform, or only start on Substack, or only start on Twitter and post both tweets and articles." His own ecosystem: "I write the newsletter every week. That can splinter into posts, and then the best post can be used as a carousel or the script for a short or reel. The newsletter turns into a YouTube video, and then all of that just feeds back into each other."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

In a January 2026 video he explained a discovery about cross-posting. Testing, he realized he could post the same content to every platform. Most people won't try for fear that their audience will be annoyed by repetition, "but frankly, I haven't noticed anything"; perhaps one person asks why, "but they still do well because people are usually just consuming it on one platform." His reasoning: "Why would I waste my time to spread my ideas thin and try to create something new for each platform that would result in a decrease in quality, rather than focus on less content with higher quality ideas, and do those once a week? And my hypothesis was correct." The newsletter goes onto his blog, becomes a YouTube outline, and the YouTube video goes into the podcast: "I literally have all platforms on one piece of content a week that's broken down into the rest." "That's how you stand out in a world of products."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

"Cross-post" is an ordinary term to which Koe gives a specific justification: because each person consumes on one platform, the same piece is new to most of its audience wherever it appears, and creating platform-specific variants spends quality to buy a novelty that few people would notice.

In a July 2026 video he described the weekly project in full. He creates one project for the whole week: one newsletter, posted to Substack (and published as an article there) and copied to X Articles; it becomes a "soft" YouTube script; the YouTube video is uploaded to podcast channels; the original newsletter supplies tweet ideas through the week, alongside tweets written as ideas come; the tweets are cross-posted to Substack Notes and turned into images for Instagram and LinkedIn; and for tweets he especially likes, he reads them to camera for Reels, TikToks and Shorts. "It only took an hour or two every day."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

The same video closes with "the whole system in a nutshell," which joins the process of Section 24.1 to the ecosystem of this section. Capture ideas as they come; save inspiration (posts that did well, good title structures, good ideas); jot your own ideas; create one topic per week as a central project for all research (a note with links, or a board); write with the research alongside; then outline, write and repurpose. The structure of a newsletter, he adds, is "a topic for an entirely new video." Someone in the Eden community had written a guide to how his top Substack articles are structured; he turned it into a prompt that asks what the user wants to talk about and guides them to a first newsletter, article or script. His closing line: "Walking, reading, and about 60 minutes a day of writing forms the foundation for some of the most future-proof skills: learning, thinking, and earning independently."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

"One project for the week" or "central project" is his 2026 name for the single weekly topic that feeds every platform. It is the time-scale insight of Section 24.1 (the newsletter as a weekly project) generalized to the whole content operation.

#### How the ecosystem changed

The ecosystem went through several names and several orientations. The table organizes them.

| Version | Date | Hub | Direction | Cross-posting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Two hour content ecosystem" | 2022 | Weekly newsletter | Top-down, with backlog newsletter under tweets | Not discussed |
| Seven-step "content ecosystem"; "my funnel is simple" | 2023 | Weekly newsletter (≈1,000 words) | Top-down; three tweets a day | Tweets cross-posted everywhere |

The axis of the system alternates between the newsletter and X, and the direction of repurposing alternates between top-down and bottom-up, with top-down dominant in 2026. The only reason Koe gives for a change is that his hypothesis about quality over quantity "was correct." The versions are reconcilable by stage: starting on X serves the beginner without an audience, who needs a testing ground (Section 24.5); the newsletter as hub serves the creator who already has distribution. The instruction to "rewrite" and the instruction to "cross-post identical" are about different operations, as noted above. What remains constant is the core claim: one long piece of thinking a week, distributed as widely as possible, is more efficient than many separate pieces made for each platform.

#### The mechanics of repurposing

The April 2023 video that introduced the three-point model also describes, step by step, how the newsletter is distributed, and it is the most complete account of the mechanics in the corpus.

The first step is to use the newsletter as a YouTube script. "Most people wing it and the videos fall flat." Koe read his own newsletter as a "soft script" in that very video, from his phone, and remarked that nobody cares that it is a repeat: "people want to be reminded more than they need something new." He uploads each newsletter to a blog, so it can be reposted for life; if it contains his products or services, that is how they are sold. He embeds the YouTube video in the blog post, then plugs the post under a tweet, in an Instagram story or under any post; with products and services linked, his YouTube channel grows, his email list grows and people visit his offers. "If I do that on a daily basis that's how I make an income."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

A "soft script" is his term for a written piece used as a flexible guide for a video rather than read word for word or improvised from nothing. The phrase "people want to be reminded more than they need something new" is the same principle Chapter 22 used to defend repetition of ideas; here it defends repetition of a piece across formats.

The second step is to condense the main points into a Twitter thread. The reason given is to understand medium-form content: to stand out on short-form, top-of-funnel platforms (Twitter, Instagram, LinkedIn), longer-form content builds more authority and leads to more growth and sales. Threads can be repurposed into an Instagram carousel and a LinkedIn post, which then send traffic to the YouTube channel, newsletter or blog. The method: take the main ideas from the newsletter, outline a shorter and more concise post, and write it as a thread, "because you are forced to condense your ideas to be the most valuable," keeping it to ten to fifteen tweets at most so that it can be turned into an Instagram or LinkedIn carousel "with a few minutes of effort." "The longer you hold attention with newsletters and threads the more perceived value your brand has to any given individual."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The third step is to break the thread and the newsletter into short form. "Short form is your mechanism for growth and testing ideas": tweets, Instagram posts, TikToks, Reels, Shorts, LinkedIn posts, regular posts. Every part of a thread and many parts of the newsletter can become standalone posts; then the related newsletter or YouTube video is plugged under the post, with products and services inside it. "That's how you make sales": "an endless content flywheel of traffic circulation and nurturing your audience."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The three steps form a funnel in reverse: the long piece is written first and then condensed into progressively shorter forms, each of which links back up to the long piece. The direction of writing is downward; the direction of the reader is upward.

#### Repurpose the actionable part

Which part of the newsletter should be condensed into the thread? In August 2022 (repeated in the 2024 compilation) Koe's answer was the actionable, step-by-step section. Turn it into a Twitter thread, which can become an Instagram carousel or something similar. That is "usually how you grow on social media to start": actionable step-by-step content proves authority, "people just love lists," and writing a list a day gets one's "brain juices flowing." The simplest form is a list tweet: "how to do this: step, step, step, step," followed by a one-line summary. "Boom you're a tweet writer now."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The May 2023 video gives the reasoning a sharper edge. "Repurpose the actionable part of the newsletter because actionable and in-depth advice is the most potent and beginner friendly way to start growing on social media that's how you build Authority at the start don't try to be too clever when nobody knows who you are." His evidence: "your favorite YouTubers didn't start as lifestyle vloggers they gave actionable advice in an eternal Market health wealth relationships"; channels usually begin "by establishing Authority in one domain or a few domains like self-improvement," such as lifting weights or getting more dates.

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

This connects to the Eternal markets of Chapter 19, and it contains a tension the corpus records explicitly. In a conversation from March 2023 Koe said he had shifted to "pithy big picture stuff" because the tactical material was already documented; in January 2024 he argued that actionable advice and platitudes "don't sell as well as they did"; and in 2025–2026 he criticized abstract posts that "sound like a philosophical quote." The positions are best read by audience and stage: actionable content for a beginner without reputation, building authority in the feed; more abstract and philosophical content for a creator whose readers already know who they are. "Don't try to be too clever when nobody knows who you are" is the principle that reconciles them: cleverness is a privilege earned by depth already established. Chapter 22 treats the same tension under "perspective over prescription."

Repurposing also runs in the other direction. In February 2023 Koe showed how a structured tweet that did well can be expanded. Bullet-point list tweets become threads, Instagram captions or carousels. His example: the hook of the thread, newsletter or carousel is "Marketing is the greatest skill you can learn"; then the tweet's last line ("only fools expect to make an income without learning the skill that turns viewers into customers. Here are the three things you need to know") becomes the transition into the three points, which are that marketing pairs with any other skill, teaches human psychology, and helps monetize a niche interest.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

A case from an October 2024 video shows the upward direction across years. His most popular video at the time, on going monk mode and getting ahead of 99% of people (a title, he notes, that everyone then copied until it was "overblown"), was exactly a thread he had written about six months earlier: "I just read the thread to the camera." That thread, in turn, came from a post, "how to get ahead of 99% of people," that he had tweeted around 2020 or even 2019. Section 24.5 returns to the same tweet as a case of validation.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

#### Repurposing as a way of understanding

Repurposing is usually justified as efficiency. Koe adds a second justification. In January 2023 he said that the process "gives you a multi-dimensional understanding of the topic": "a tweet is a big idea it's not super actionable" (although it can be), while "an article you're fleshing out so much more" and connecting different ideas. Doing this, he said, "will change your life over six months."

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

"Multi-dimensional understanding" is his term for the comprehension that comes from expressing the same idea at several levels of compression. The claim parallels the learning principles of Part VI: explaining an idea as a headline, as a list and as an essay exercises different relations among its parts, much as teaching does. The "six months" is his estimate, not a measured effect.

#### From posts to a book

In an August 2024 video Koe extended the ecosystem to its longest form. Write short posts and threads to gain an audience; nurture it with longer newsletters; "before you know it you have thousands of posts and hundreds of newsletters that are what create a book that's what a book is." His example is James Clear: *Atomic Habits*, he says, started as a blog post, in fact a large series of blog posts, and Clear built an email list that let him sell so many copies up front that he did not need a publisher, even though he has one. Social media and personal brands are "just putting yourself online and open to everyone open for business": one does not need a publisher for a book or a record label for music, because "you are the audience." He concedes it is not the only path, but calls it "very accessible" and one that "everyone yes everyone can take."

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

The account of *Atomic Habits* is Koe's summary, offered as an example; he does not cite details beyond the blog series and the email list. What it shows within his system is the endpoint of the flywheel: years of weekly pieces, selected by audience response, accumulate into the material of a book.

#### 2 Hour Writer: the system as a product

Koe's own course, 2 Hour Writer (also spelled Two Hour Writer and The 2 Hour Writer; the corpus treats the spellings as the same product), is both the codified version of this ecosystem and a case study in how he builds products. Several passages, spanning 2023 to 2026, describe its origin and logic.

The product began with two problems of his own. In a January 2026 telling: "This all starts with identifying and solving problems in your life in a unique way." His problems were that he had no endless source of content ideas, and that he did not want to waste time creating content for every platform. His goal: "write all of my content in 2 hours a day. So, I had a constraint and that bred creativity." For ideas, he created swipe files, steps for generating ideas that worked for him, and templates for when he still could not think of anything. Then he looked at his week, which he calls "a very useful practice if you're going to create a product, is how does an individual's week look." One newsletter a week, three posts a day on Twitter and one thread a week seemed reasonable. Then came the questions that produced the system: must each of these be unique? Can I talk about one theme per week? Can I write the newsletter and pull the social posts from it? Testing on "a week-long timescale," one encounters problems, fixes them, "and then things start to flow."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

An October 2024 telling describes the same development as a method for building any system. Step one of creating a system is "having an extremely clear goal": a desired outcome, a time frame and a problem solved. His goal was "all of the content I need in under 2 hours a day," so that audience growth would be handled and he could focus on better products and on enjoying life. Then came iterative testing. For ideas, swipe files, steps and templates; if still stuck, a map of exactly what to write each week (three posts a day, one thread a week, one newsletter a week). He realized he could cross-post his writing to all social platforms (publicly visible daily), that threads could become carousels, and that newsletters could become YouTube videos. "If the system didn't flow I would try new things the next week." Then he copied the newsletter to his blog, embedded the YouTube video in the blog, promoted products in the blog, turned the blog into more content ideas, and linked the blog under his daily content. This led to more newsletter subscribers, YouTube subscribers and product sales. The final realization was the one that gave the system its center: "if everything I did was newsletter-centric, that's all I had to worry about" for growing the audience and promoting products.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

"Newsletter-centric" is his term for this arrangement, in which every other format derives from the newsletter. The story is a small-scale application of the cybernetic model of goals in Chapter 6: a clear target (all content in under two hours), weekly feedback, and correction until the system "flows." The constraint of two hours is not a limitation on the system but its design principle, a point Koe makes in other contexts with the phrase that creativity thrives within constraints.

The product was validated before it was a product. In December 2023 Koe recounted that about a year earlier he had written an X thread breaking down how he writes articles and newsletters so they can be repurposed across all platforms: "it did very well so I turned it into my two-hour writer course." This is the validation ladder of Section 24.5 operating on his own business.

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

In April 2023 he described "my writing product" (the transcript garbles the name, but another transcript from the same batch names the course 2-Hour Writer) as "my unique experience and the system I created while building a personal brand to millions of dollars ... and gaining millions of followers," the result of two to three years of experimenting with idea generation, newsletter writing and social media content.

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

In February 2025 he used the course to illustrate positioning. 2 Hour Writer presents writing, which is "often perceived as too boring or too academic, or that you need an English degree," in a way that is desirable to people like his past self. The system: write a newsletter, break it into a thread, break that into social posts, cross-post across all platforms; "a very simple system for posting content to all different platforms, with less than two hours of writing a day." Why a course? He could have turned the system into a freelance, coaching or consulting service, or into software (as he later did, in a sense, with Cortex), and it would have outperformed many services because it is based on a personal system. He did not, because he does not have time, does not like client work, and can help more people with a course. "It's the same frickin thing": the same system in a different vessel.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

Finally, in April 2024 he explained why this course in particular became central to his business. Writing is one of his main levers: he has to write a book, newsletters, content, emails, and responses to clients, students and business contacts. "Writing is literally the only thing I do for the first 2 to three hours of the day." If one is building an online business and is not writing and getting it in front of people, "you're not making progress." That is why he got rid of his other courses and now sells 2 Hour Writer and what he and his team help with in Kortex University: writing is "the main catalyst," and he feels it would be strange to sell anything before it.

**Source:** Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md

The figures for his own daily writing time vary across the corpus: thirty minutes each morning (2022), a ninety-minute block (2023), "2 hours a day" (2023), the first two to three hours of the day (2024), "two hours of writing in the morning" (2025), and "about 60 minutes a day" or "an hour or two every day" (2026). Koe does not present these as contradictions, and the only explanation he offers in the corpus is that he became more efficient as his skill grew; his recommendations for beginners are consistently lower than his own routine. The principle (a bounded block of morning writing as the base of the business) is stable; the numbers are not, and none of them should be treated as a precise prescription.

**Source:** EV-160 in `03b_evolucion.md`

### 24.4 The Newsletter as the Hub

#### Start precisely because nobody is watching

Every version of the ecosystem except the earliest-stage "content stack" places a newsletter at its center, and even that version builds one on top of X. Section 24.2 explained why long form matters; this section explains why the newsletter, specifically, is the place where long form should live, and why Koe calls it "the Hub."

The first obstacle he addresses is psychological. In April 2023 he observed that nobody wants to start a newsletter because they don't want to write to zero subscribers, "and that's exactly why you should start one, nobody is watching." The question he poses is a choice between two futures: would you rather write to nobody and get the bad writing out of your system before you write to 5,000 people, or never send the newsletter at all because you are afraid of what they will think of "your shitty letter," since you did not get better earlier?

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The argument inverts the usual relation between audience and practice. An empty list is not a reason to wait; it is a protected space for the period in which the writing is worst. The same logic appears in Chapter 26's treatment of "beginner hell."

#### Why a newsletter: authority, ownership and a place to sell

The same video lists three reasons for making the newsletter the first long-form platform. First, it develops the most authority: short form only holds so much attention, so the most sales come from long form. Second, one owns the audience one acquires: social platforms can go down at any time, so the newsletter "is like the Hub." Third, it is where one sells products or services "to people that care about you rather than spamming social media and looking scammy." For people who do not want to talk about their product or service in public at all, the solution is to create a newsletter and pitch only to the people who care.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

"The Hub" names the newsletter's structural position: the one node of the ecosystem that the creator controls, toward which all others route. The three reasons map onto three properties: depth (authority), ownership (security) and consent (a non-spammy context for selling).

The same video gives a recommended length: "just make it a thousand fifteen hundred words." Koe admits that he himself goes "pretty long," but advises a newsletter of roughly 1,000 words and "maybe 1500" at most.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

#### The newsletter as a vessel, even before it is written

A July 2023 video lowers the barrier further. "I always recommend starting a newsletter." One does not have to write it consistently at first: "you don't have to start writing one on a consistent basis you just have to have the newsletter," practicing promoting and marketing it to get subscribers. People procrastinate on this, he says, because limiting beliefs come up ("I'm not gonna write," "this is too long term," "I don't even have an audience yet"), and because it shows no immediate results, so it is not enticing to someone who does not understand the perspective behind it.

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

Koe uses "vessel" in many senses in the corpus (the business as a vessel for one's lifestyle, the brand as a vessel for one's life's work); here the newsletter is a vessel in the sense of a container for one's projects, a place that exists before its contents. The same video lists what the container is for. One practices developing depth behind one's ideas, which leads to original thought. The struggle of writing makes one better faster. The newsletter can serve as a place to write modules or sections of one's lead magnet or products ("it doesn't matter if you send it out first for free"). And it shows that one provides value off platform even as a beginner, which helps perceived authority, "because selling something helps your brand not selling something hurts your brand." Whenever one creates something in the subsequent steps of his plan, the newsletter should be used to write its first iteration, gaining authority by writing long form on it.

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

Another July 2023 video, on the "value creator," restates the case in a single principle. "Depth is what separates you on a superficial market." One does not need subscribers: the newsletter can be uploaded as a blog that grows over time, or used to build lead magnets or products in real time, and giving sections away for free does not matter. "My brand took off when I started writing long form." Then the complexity is condensed into tweets: the newsletter writes out everything on one's mind about a topic, which is the most valuable form, and then human psychology is used to create "short heavy hitting ideas" that attract people and lead them back to the long form, because people naturally read shorter form.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The passage connects the newsletter to the product. A newsletter written week by week is also a product being drafted in public: its sections can become the modules of a course, and the response to each issue is information about what deserves to be expanded. Section 24.5 makes this logic explicit as a validation ladder.

#### Why not funnels

The May 2023 video in which Koe laid out the seven-step ecosystem also contains his clearest statement about what the newsletter replaced. "I rarely use fancy sales funnels anymore and that's when my income [took] off." Direct-response marketing is important and good to learn, he says: it teaches the fundamentals of how "you facilitate the transfer of money and persuade people to see the value in your services." "But at the same time it gets a bit too crazy." When he "strip[ped] down everything made it super simple," his revenue rose to $800,000, with a projection of $2.5 million. Instead of funnels, he uses a content ecosystem.

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

The causal claim (simplifying coincided with the revenue increase) is his own reading of his trajectory and cannot be separated from other changes in his business at the time; the figures are self-reported. What the passage establishes is his preference: a newsletter-centered ecosystem is a substitute for the multi-step automated funnel, not an addition to it.

#### Deplatform your audience

The second reason for the newsletter, ownership, recurs throughout the corpus with growing emphasis. In October 2022 Koe stated it bluntly. "If you don't have an email list you're just gonna be screwed" if your social platforms are taken down: "they can't take emails away from you." He anticipated an objection, the limiting belief that "nobody checks their emails," and answered that people make seven figures from email lists alone; the way to find out is to test it oneself. The same passage, repeated in the 2024 compilation, places the newsletter immediately after the warning against the "social media rat race" discussed in Section 24.2: one escapes that race by prioritizing depth and clarity, and the newsletter, which he calls "the biggest game changer" in his business, is where that depth is built.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In June 2024 he framed ownership in terms of degrees of centralization. Radio had few channels; mass media and news were very centralized; social media, owned by large companies whose narrative is controlled to an extent, is more decentralized, since one usually sees content from the people one follows. But it is only relatively decentralized. For full control, provide value on a newsletter and get people onto it, because "you can download the email list at any time and take it anywhere with you."

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

"Deplatform your audience" is his term for this move: taking the audience out of a platform one does not own and into a channel one does. The expression inverts the usual sense of "deplatforming" (a platform removing a user) into an action the creator takes preemptively.

In October 2024 the argument acquired a new premise from changes in the platforms themselves. Because "For You" pages let anyone go viral, follower count no longer represents audience size. "An email list is the only true representative of your audience size. An email list is the new status symbol. Nobody can take away an email list from you."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

**Complementary context:** the "interest graph" refers to the shift in social platforms from feeds based on whom a user follows (the social graph) to feeds based on what an algorithm predicts the user will engage with. Under an interest graph, a post can reach millions of non-followers, and conversely a large following no longer guarantees that followers see one's posts. Koe uses the term to explain why followers lost their value as a measure.

The same video cites a tweet by Alex Lieberman (the transcript spells his name "liberman"). **Complementary context:** Lieberman is a co-founder of the business newsletter Morning Brew, which explains the weight Koe gives his view on newsletters. Lieberman said he was long-term bullish on newsletters; that "owned audience is more important than ever before as social algos switch to interest graph"; that podcasts are just as crowded as email and audience growth there is much harder; that the unit economics of newsletters are beautiful at scale, with margins above 80%; and that there are many new newsletters, but most care more about short-term monetization than long-term reader loyalty. Koe's gloss: social media becomes a first layer where followers are introduced to you, not where they are nurtured or educated, and newsletters are more important than ever for building and keeping an audience.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

Another October 2024 video, on "the death of the personal brand," states the principle in its most general form. "Long form is how you actually stand out, long form is how you actually make a change, long form is how you stop relying on social media algorithms for the security of your future," and start owning one's audience in something like an email list. Koe defers the details to other videos.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

The corpus describes this line of argument not as a change of position but as an expansion: in 2022 the list is insurance against losing a platform; by 2024 it is also the only real measure of audience size, because the platforms changed. Both arguments point to the same practice.

#### Every offer is an audience

A July 2023 remark extends the idea of an owned audience beyond the email list. "What many people miss when it comes to niching down is this: they don't realize that any newsletter, lead magnet, product or service you create is an audience. You're building an audience off platform."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

"Off platform" is his name for this: every asset that collects people's contact or loyalty outside a social network is part of the audience, even if it is not counted as followers. A buyer of a course is, among other things, a member of an owned audience. This is why, in Section 24.2, products and long form were called "the backbone" of the brand: they are where the audience actually lives.

#### The power of a list: two illustrations

Koe illustrates what an owned audience can do with two stories, one borrowed and one hypothetical.

The borrowed story comes from Ryan Deiss's *The Invisible Selling Machine*, which Koe says first introduced him to the power of email lists. (The transcript spells the name "dice.") He notes that it is one of the few business books he has read, since he gets most of his business thinking from psychology, metaphysics and philosophy, and that he no longer uses its tactics. The story, whose numbers he warns not to quote precisely: the author was in a very bad place and needed a lot of money fast; he typed and sent an email, and overnight he had over $100,000. Koe's first, naive interpretation was that this was magic. His later understanding was that the author "has a list full of people that want to buy from him," created a valuable offer, pitched it, had good rapport, and probably had not promoted too much before, so that when he did, "it's like a flood." People who have not experienced the power of a newsletter, he concludes, do not care to build one until they start and see it for themselves.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The reinterpretation is the lesson. The $100,000 email is not a trick of copywriting; it is the visible result of invisible conditions (a list of willing buyers, a good offer, trust, restraint in previous promotion), which are exactly what the ecosystem of this chapter is designed to produce.

The hypothetical comes from Koe's summary of his book *The Art of Focus* (January 2024). If one invests the time it would take to earn a degree in building an email list of 50,000 like-minded readers, "twice the capacity of what a professional football stadium can hold," and then crafts a persuasive message, writes a few emails and promotes a product created as a solution to one's own problems, "I would not be surprised if you made the average United States salary in a month minimum."

**Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md

This is a rhetorical projection, framed as what would "not surprise" him, not a forecast with stated assumptions about conversion or price. Its point is comparative: the same years invested in a degree and in a list lead to very different kinds of asset.

#### Social media is a bridge to the newsletter

By December 2024 Koe had condensed the relation between platforms and newsletter into a single image. When writing posts, he said, one needs somewhere else to lead people, so that one speaks directly with them, "not from a public podium or public group chat," but to people who actually want to buy. The email list is where one demonstrates competence or authority, gives education and insight based on one's skill, interest or expertise, and directly promotes products or services. If one is afraid of promoting on social media because of trolls (who, he observes, don't mind paid ads but get angry seeing a creator make money; "maybe that's more reason to just piss them off more"), the newsletter is probably where one will make the most money anyway, since promotion works best alongside long-form content that shows one knows what one is talking about. "Social media is where you capture attention newsletter is where you hold and deliver value on the attention social media is just a bridge to your newsletter."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The bridge image restates the one-organism model in terms of place. Short form is not a destination; it is a crossing. The value of a post is measured by how many people it carries across.

#### The nurture lever

What happens on the far side of the bridge? A November 2022 video, reflecting on lessons from a seven-figure one-person business, describes what Koe calls the "nurture lever." People think they must sell a "make money online" product to make money with a course, or that they cannot sell something they are interested in; he says that is false. Those people are usually in the survival mindset of needing money immediately, so they study direct-response marketing, which is a great skill, but it teaches how to get someone to click and buy in one go, and it is not general marketing. In a less niche or less profitable area, one must "dedicate yourself to educating your audience to the point of becoming customers." A creator attracts beginners, intermediates and advanced people, a global range, and not only the people at the highest levels of awareness. Filter them into the newsletter, which is not affected by the algorithm and where one can talk about whatever one wants; educate them on one's interests, why they matter and how they affect their lives; and then plug one's product or service. "That's where you make the most money."

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md

The nurture lever is the commercial counterpart of Chapter 22's argument that interests are persuaded rather than innate. Direct response sells to people who already want the thing; the nurture lever creates the want through education, which is slower but is the only route available to someone whose interests are not already a recognized market.

#### Fifteen minutes every morning

The last practical element is promotion of the newsletter itself. In October 2024 Koe described a habit taught "for the longest time" in 2 Hour Writer: put the newsletter in YouTube descriptions, plug it on Twitter once a day, plug it in cross-posted tweets, and spend fifteen minutes every morning promoting it (either the current issue or a backlog issue). The result is that everyone goes to "one central place for the people that actually want to hear from you." His summary of how to build an audience in 2025: with an email list, repurposed across all platforms.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

This practice should be read against the 2022 claim that "you rarely see me promote on the timeline." The corpus records a shift in Koe's stance on promotion: from almost invisible promotion (the backlog link under tweets, with explicit promotion only for launches) to explicit calendars and the 2024 exhortation "for the love of God promote yourself." The reason he gives is that followers do not know what one sells unless they are told, and that what deserves criticism is promoting a bad product, not promoting often. The two stances apply to different situations: the established creator whose ecosystem routes readers automatically, and the beginner with a product who must actively send people to it.

**Source:** EV-223 in `03b_evolucion.md`

Finally, a tension about entry order should be recorded here, since this section has emphasized starting a newsletter. In April 2023 Koe recommended the newsletter first; in November 2023 he said he almost always recommends starting with short form; in January 2024 he said the newsletter "can wait"; and in 2024 he again recommended that "everyone start a newsletter." The corpus marks this as a genuine inconsistency across programs rather than a settled change. The reading most consistent with the rest of his system is the one already suggested: the newsletter should exist early, even if only as a vessel, because it is where depth, ownership and sales accumulate; but a beginner's main effort at the start may have to go into the short-form platform that supplies traffic, since a hub with no roads into it serves no one.

### 24.5 A Tweet Is the New MVP

#### Twitter as a testing ground

The ecosystem of Section 24.3 has a property that Koe eventually made into a principle of its own. Because short posts are cheap, numerous and immediately measured by engagement, they are not only a way of attracting attention but a way of finding out which ideas deserve more investment. In April 2023 he described his own practice. He writes everything on Twitter, posts three times a day, then chooses the best posts and turns them into other content that "usually performs pretty well." "Twitter is a testing ground for my ideas." Almost all of his newsletter ideas, which then become YouTube videos and blog posts, were taken from top-performing tweets; all his videos are newsletters; and his shorter videos come from threads he writes from high-performing tweets. Because the ideas have already tested well on Twitter, he knows that the videos, newsletters and threads stemming from them will perform better and compound. He adds that "Twitter's the easiest platform to grow on if you understand what you're doing."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

"Testing ground" names the epistemic function of the short post: it is a cheap experiment whose result (engagement) decides whether the idea is promoted to a more expensive format. The practice resolves the apparent tension between the top-down ecosystem (newsletter first) and bottom-up validation (tweets first): the newsletter is written first in the week, but its topic was chosen because a tweet had already succeeded.

#### "A tweet is the new MVP"

In September 2024 Koe gave this practice a name and extended it beyond content. The flow: write short form (on X, where one can post many times and see what does best); turn the best into a longer post; if that does well, into a newsletter; if that does well, into a YouTube video; then a free download; then a product; then a new startup. "A tweet is the new MVP, so you can test all of your business ideas every single day, three times a day, and just bring it through that filter, and you'll be able to start a business just based on what does the best."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Complementary context:** "MVP," minimum viable product, is a term popularized by the lean startup movement (notably Eric Ries) for the smallest version of a product that can be released to test a hypothesis about customers with minimal investment. Koe adapts it by moving the test from the product to the post: before building anything, one can learn whether an idea attracts attention by stating it in 280 characters.

"A tweet is the new MVP" is his coined phrase for this filter. It is a hierarchy of formats in which each step is both more expensive to produce and more valuable if it succeeds, and in which an idea is promoted only after passing the test below it. Note that the hierarchy reaches beyond content into business: the top rungs are products and startups.

#### Why the tweet is the right unit

Why tweets rather than any other short format? Koe gives three related arguments across several years.

The first, from February 2023, concerns the discipline of the format. He practices writing in tweet form because a tweet is the perfect length for a persuasive, "heavy-hitting" message of 280 characters, which keeps it concise: "it really helps with your writing to try and articulate your ideas within 280 characters." The tweet is also the perfect shape for an Instagram post, can be copied to LinkedIn, and works as a short reel script; the good ones become newsletter topics.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The second, from January 2024, concerns transfer between lengths. Every part of a thread is a tweet; "if you know how to write good tweets you know how to write good threads"; and the same holds for newsletters, "because you're just writing tweets until you fill up that long page."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The claim is deliberately reductive and should be read alongside Section 24.1. A newsletter assembled from an outline of problems, insights and processes is more than a sequence of tweets; what the claim captures is that the sentence-level skill of the tweet (compression, punch, one idea per unit) is the skill that makes each line of a long piece hold attention.

The third, from March 2026, concerns transfer between platforms. "Tweets are like the lowest common denominator, where if you have a 280 character tweet that can really go to any platform or be used as the hook for a reel or whatever it is. I recommend getting really good at crafting tweet-style posts first, because that will impact all of your other short-form content." In the same passage he notes that his newsletter prompt "can also double as a podcast script or a YouTube script."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

"Lowest common denominator" is used here without any negative sense: the tweet is the format that every other short format contains, so mastering it improves all of them.

#### Short posts and the minimalist creator

In the September 2024 video that named the tweet as MVP, Koe also defined the short post. The first type of content is short posts: tweets, Reels, Shorts, TikToks, Instagram posts, "anything under 300 characters or 1 minute." His favorite method is to write a tweet and then paste it into an image template in Figma or Canva; his Instagram, he says, is writing in image form. "You don't need to post anything other than writing to build an audience on any short form platform": no video reels, no pictures of oneself. "You just need to write that's like the Baseline that's the minimalist Creator." Short posts are good for staying top of mind, attracting an audience and testing ideas: "short posts are your base." He recommends posting at least one a day on X, and two to three a day in order to test which should be posted to other platforms and turned into long posts.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The "minimalist creator" is his term for the creator who builds an audience with writing alone, presented as images on visual platforms. The term addresses a common obstacle (the belief that one must show one's face or produce video) and fits the ecosystem's logic: if the tweet is the lowest common denominator, a creator who only writes tweets can still be present on every platform.

On volume, the corpus records a shift that should be noted. Through 2022–2024 Koe recommended three tweets a day, one to three posts daily "to get the failures out of the way," and in one 60-day plan even three to five short posts a day. By 2026 he described "one piece of content a week" broken down into the rest and, against the high-volume approach of other creators, advised toning it down, raising quality and adding scarcity so that seeing a new post is itself a reward. The reasons he gives are that ideas beget ideas (so volume early produces material), and that if everyone is pushing volume, the advantage lies in doing something different. The two positions apply to different stages: volume for the beginner who needs data and validation, scarcity for the creator who already has an audience. The testing logic of this section belongs to the first stage.

**Source:** EV-155 in `03b_evolucion.md`

#### Validated ideas, accumulated for life

The test does not end with a single post. In September 2023 Koe described iteration on big ideas as a lifelong practice. "You're sticking this out for life." Everything is trial and error, so one iterates on one's big ideas: write and write, then after a month see which did better and lean into them. A year later one has fifty extremely good posts with far more engagement, and doubles down. Two years later one has five hundred posts, "all of those are validated ideas." Then one can take them to another platform: he took his validated ideas from Twitter to Instagram, "executed a growth strategy and blew up," knowing in advance that they would do well.

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

"Validated ideas" here means ideas whose capacity to attract has been demonstrated by engagement. The passage reveals a second payoff of the testing ground: validated ideas are portable. A new platform does not require new ideas, only the transfer of ideas already proven elsewhere, which is the bottom-up equivalent of cross-posting.

The case Koe uses most often to illustrate the whole filter is a single tweet. In February 2025 he recalled a tweet written almost five years earlier: "go quiet for 3 months; laser in on one big goal; self-educate like mad; apply everything you learn; fail as much as possible; every month take a break and have fun"; "bursts of intensity are how you blow past everyone else." As a beginner, the tweet got many likes and brought in the most followers he had ever gained from one post (about six hundred). Because it did well, he turned it into a thread, which did extremely well, and then into a YouTube video, which became his top video "with over 1.6 million views." The headline is now everywhere. He warns against copying it: copy-pasting the tweet might get some engagement, but "you're probably going to be disappointed because you're still taking shortcuts."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The same case appeared in Section 24.3 from the side of repurposing (the monk-mode video was a thread read to camera). Here it shows the validation logic: each promotion to a more expensive format was justified by the success of the cheaper one. The warning against copying echoes Section 24.2: the tweet worked partly because of who posted it and what came after it, not only because of its words.

#### Organic posting before paid ads

The testing ground has a commercial application that Koe developed over several years: organic posts as a substitute for, and later a preparation for, paid advertising.

The earliest statement comes not from Koe but from a guest. In a December 2021 interview, Justin Welsh said he had never run a paid ad; he said he lacked the competency and "just [doesn't] care." His belief: "I am more likely to make sales of my product by building this massive organic following, by having people know exactly what I stand for, exactly what I write about, exactly... what my opinions are, and exactly what my products help them do." He conceded that ads might yield "a three-to-one return" and admitted, "I just haven't focused on it. I wish I had a better answer." He named one condition under which he would use them: if he built a software product where "I can get enough people into good lifetime value," he would accept customer acquisition costs, "but someone else will run them for me." Koe agreed: he had once tried a Facebook ad agency, and given that organic growth was working, ads were not "worth splitting focus," though they remained "an option... when we decide to go after them."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

The position here is Welsh's; Koe's contribution is agreement and his own brief experience. The condition Welsh states (lifetime value justifying acquisition cost) is the standard economic test for paid acquisition, and it explains why ads were not attractive for low-priced information products at the time.

By December 2024 Koe had developed his own argument. Beginners do not have money for paid ads: one will spend $100 to $500 without the right angle and run out of money before the ads get good; one does not have the $1,000, $10,000 or $50,000 that large companies use to test, refine and make ads profitable. On social media, by contrast, "you just write posts every single day you're testing ideas and angles every single day." After one, two or three years, one knows exactly which ideas get engagement, capture attention, and bring followers and sales; then one can turn those into paid ads and start "on a much better foot."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The October 2024 video names the principle. "Social media posts are the new MVP minimum viable product." With an audience, one does not need much market research, because one does it every day by posting. Social media becomes a testing ground for ideas, angles and hooks, which can then be put into paid ads knowing they will do better than most, without having spent money to test them.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The corpus describes this as a refinement rather than a reversal. In 2021 ads were something "for later" and not worth splitting focus; by 2024 organic content is reframed as the laboratory that makes later ads profitable, and Koe elsewhere compares ads to "steroids" that come after "natural bodybuilding." The two positions fit different situations: a beginner without capital, for whom posting is free research, and a business with validated angles, for which buying distribution becomes rational.

**Source:** EV-173 in `03b_evolucion.md`

#### The validation ladder

The same October 2024 video extends the MVP logic into a full ladder from post to business. Post on social media; turn the best posts into threads and newsletters; turn the best threads and newsletters into free downloads; turn the free downloads into an information product or a service; and turn that into software, a physical product, a book or another scalable business. The ones that do well show what one's audience wants. Turning them into a free download and then an information product generates cash flow, because such products cost roughly nothing to make. Education, he argues, is arguably the most important product, for two reasons: for human behavior change and value (education determines how one sees the world and what one can do), and for its extremely high profit margins: "the economy favors the profitable." With cash flow and a validated idea, one can turn the basis of the product into something bigger, as he did with 2 Hour Writer and Kortex, without loans or venture capital.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

A February 2025 statement compresses the ladder. "Social posts are the new MVPs": post a lot; turn the best into newsletters; cross-post those everywhere; turn "the best of the best" into a digital product; refine it until sales are high; then turn that system into software or complement it with a physical product. His example is again 2 Hour Writer turning into Kortex, with a qualification: "not exactly." The software did not replicate the educational part of the course, but it let him do his workflow better in software; "it sprung the idea."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

| Rung | Format | What success at this rung shows |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Short post (tweet, short social post) | The idea captures attention |
| 2 | Thread, long post, newsletter | The idea sustains attention and builds trust |
| 3 | YouTube video (in the 2024 "tweet is the new MVP" version) | The idea holds attention at length and reaches a new audience |
| 4 | Free download or lead magnet | People will exchange contact details for the idea's development |
| 5 | Information product or service | People will pay for it |
| 6 | Software, physical product, book or startup | The idea can support a scalable business |

The table merges the two 2024 versions and the 2025 version; they differ slightly (one includes the YouTube video, one includes cross-posting), but share the principle that each rung is entered only by what succeeded on the rung below. The ladder also ties this chapter to Chapters 30 and 33, where the stages from service to product and the construction of the product are treated at length. The 2 Hour Writer case appears on both sides of it: as content (a thread that did well) and as product (a course that seeded a software company).

The corpus notes that Koe's use of "MVP" changed over time. In 2022 he used it about the person: "your minimum viable product was created at birth," meaning that one is oneself the first project to release and iterate. From 2024 the MVP is the piece of content, the first rung of the ladder. The two senses are compatible (one concerns the attitude of starting imperfectly, the other the mechanism of validation), but they should not be confused.

**Source:** EV-204 in `03b_evolucion.md`

#### Sell before you build

The last rungs of the ladder raise a question that content alone cannot answer: will people pay? Koe's answer, developed mainly in 2023–2025, is to test willingness to pay before building the product, by selling it first.

The premise is a warning. In June 2026 he put it simply: "You can have this great idea and you can build it out, but that doesn't mean it's good and it doesn't mean that other people want to use it and it definitely doesn't mean that they want to pay for it. So, you have to test all of those things."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

Three distinct hypotheses are packed into that sentence: that the product works, that people want it, and that they will pay for it. Engagement on a post tests only the second, partially. The methods that follow test the third.

In a February 2025 guide to making a first product, Koe stated the principle. "Most people waste 2 to 3 months building a product just for it to never be launched or worse, never make any sales. We're going to do the opposite. You're going to make money before you even start building the product." If one does not make money, one pivots fast and tries again. Once one "strikes gold," one gives it everything, builds it over two to three weeks, and launches. He adds that this is another reason education products are superior: they can be tested and iterated quickly, turned into software or another product, and they are genuinely helpful if good. "Most products should start as an education product and most founders could create a new revenue channel simply by adding some form of education, even if it's just an e-book."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

The same guide states the reasoning behind the order. Take the topic and make it irresistible. "You don't start with the product. You start with the marketing. Because if you get the marketing right, you can build the product around what works. It's a lot easier to build a product based on good marketing than it is to create marketing based on a bad product."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

"Strike gold" is his phrase for finding the positioning or offer that sells. The marketing-first principle is the validation ladder applied to the final rung: just as a tweet tests whether an idea attracts before a newsletter is written, a landing page tests whether an offer sells before the product is built.

A January 2025 conversation, recorded with the founders of the creator platform Stan, shows how Koe applies this himself. It "blows a lot of people's minds," he said: he has an outline of what he wants to sell, but knows "that may not be the right thing and I don't even know if people will pay for it." So "the first thing that comes for me is the landing page," because "then I know that I control the offer there, cuz that's what people are buying." He writes enticing copy "within the realm of what I can build," starts selling, and once he receives a payment, "I have a deadline for when that thing needs to be out... I don't have any other option." Most people lack that deadline urgency and "build something for 2 months and then they never end up even telling people about it." The principle: "start selling and then build it."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

"Control the offer" and "deadline urgency" are his terms for the two functions of the landing page in this method: it defines exactly what is being bought (which is the offer, not the product's internals), and the first sale converts an intention into an obligation.

An October 2023 video presents the same method as a "tactical stress" strategy for getting out of a rut. The problem is that creators and freelancers "take forever to build the product and then they never end up launching it and they never end up making money." Instead: "build the landing page build how you're going to sell it and accept pre-orders for it not even pre-orders but just set a launch date." His example: a launch date of January 1st; "I start promoting three weeks out every week up until then and once I make the first sale ... then the pressure's on I have to deliver I have to build the product before January 1st and that's an MVP that I can continue to iterate on." "That's what a minimum viable product means is something that you get out and you test and you iterate with customer feedback." He states a condition: the method requires marketing and sales skills.

**Source:** A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md

The February 2025 guide gives the operational details. With the marketing strategy done, create the landing page first. Koe recommends Stan (Stan Store), which hosts any digital product, coaching calls, lead magnets and courses with text-only pages that need no design, "arguably the highest converting landing pages on the market because it's just so quick to get through them." Then launch: "You're not going to build out any portion of the product aside from that outline before launching." Tell customers that it releases on a specific date; when they pay, they are added to a course platform that tells them when it releases and what to expect. "Once you make the first sale, the pressure is on. You can't quit now. You have to focus and build a good product or else you just stole people's money."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

The same guide sets out a launch plan. Once one has the MVP landing page, bare bones and without the product built: "Launch launch launch." Building the landing page should not take a beginner longer than one to two weeks, or one day if one has done it before. "Set the release date 3 to 4 weeks out from your first promotion." Promote in the newsletter every week; place the link everywhere, in one's bio and descriptions; write threads, carousels, reels or other content around the product's main topic; include small plugs or calls to action such as "if you struggle with [pain points], [product] goes live on [date]." Use the elements of the marketing strategy every time one promotes. When one is one to two weeks out, increase how often and how hard one promotes. An early-bird discount for preorders helps a lot.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

| Phase | Time | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| Landing page | 1–2 weeks for a beginner; one day with experience | Outline of the product; copy "within the realm of what I can build"; text-only page; no product built |
| Promotion | Release date set 3–4 weeks after the first promotion | Weekly newsletter promotion; link in bio and descriptions; content on the product's topic; small plugs with the release date |
| Final push | Last 1–2 weeks | More frequent and stronger promotion; early-bird discount on preorders |
| Delivery | After the first sale, before the release date | Build the MVP under the pressure of the deadline; iterate with customer feedback |

A related method lowers the stakes even further. In a June 2025 video Koe suggested ideas for a quick first product: record a 30-to-60-minute training on how to do one impactful thing in one's topic; find an old asset that got a desirable result and turn it into a template; or turn a well-received social post into a short guide or template. "We aren't trying to make the big bucks here ... we're trying to validate an idea that's worth paying for." Charge something like $10; if the conversion rate is "like 2.5% or higher," consider turning it into a more fleshed-out product, so that one does not waste time building something people do not want. "It really can be that simple."

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

"Validate" is a common word that Koe uses in a narrow sense: proving with a cheap product that an idea is worth a payment. The 2.5% threshold is his rule of thumb; he does not say how it was derived, and it depends on what the conversion rate is measured against (page visitors, list subscribers, followers), which the source does not specify.

The January 2025 conversation includes a third-party version of the same principle. Vitali, a co-founder of Stan, relayed something Conrad from Teachable had told him: "one of the most irrational things people do they finish recording a course before they start selling it." The alternative: make the first few lessons, sell the course, "drip it over time where the lesson comes out like every week," and survey customers after the first lecture ("did you like it, what do you think is coming next"), because "what they think is coming next is what you make content about"; then finish the product. Rather than spending two months on a "15,000 module thing," start with "a two page PDF," see who trusts you enough to leave an email or pay "20 bucks," and "see what people are willing to pay anything for and then you build up on that over time."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

The advice is Conrad's, relayed by Vitali; Koe's own method in the same conversation (landing page first, deadline imposed by the first sale) is consistent with it. "Drip" is the guests' term for releasing a course lesson by lesson after it has been sold.

Finally, the simplest operational instruction, from December 2024. One needs a place to send people from social media and the newsletter where they can pay. Koe's pick for simplicity is Stan (he says he met their team in Toronto with the Cortex engineering team, and praises their values and support). One logs in, adds a product (coaching calls, courses, communities, digital downloads), "you put it on there you slap a price tag on it and you promote it" to one's social media, audience or newsletter, "and you worry about the fine details later."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The recommendations of Stan in these passages come from someone who knew its team personally and recorded at least one long conversation with its founders; the reader should weigh them accordingly. The method itself does not depend on the platform.

One limit of the method appears later in the corpus and should be noted here. The deadline pressure that drives "sell before you build" is a tool Koe applied successfully to himself. In a November 2025 reflection on a costly mistake at his software company, he concluded that applying the same aggressive deadlines to a team made the whole operation fall apart, that principles that work for an individual must be refined to their underlying truth before being applied to a group, and that roughly two weeks of buffer should be added to any public date. The method of this section is designed for one person who controls every variable; Chapter 37 treats the transition to a team in detail.

**Source:** EV-301 in `03b_evolucion.md`

### 24.6 Writing With AI

#### The question and the shape of the answer

Every element of this chapter (capture, the outline, the drafting of a weekly newsletter, the extraction of tweets, the writing of landing pages) is a task that generative AI can now perform or assist with. Koe built note-taking and AI software during the same years he taught this system, so the question of what AI should and should not do in writing runs through the later part of the corpus. His answer was not fixed. It moved from skepticism (2023–early 2025) to an integration with limits (late 2025–2026), and the limits themselves were restated several times. The corpus summarizes the movement as a shift from "AI is no use for generating ideas" to "AI generates variants, structures and data; the opinions are yours." This section reconstructs the position in roughly chronological order, because the reasons he gives at each stage are what make the final position intelligible.

#### "It's human nature to want human"

The earliest statement in this chapter's material, from April 2023, is an argument about demand rather than about the technology. Given AI's exponential growth, "it's human nature to want human": however human a robot is, it is seen through a human lens. Koe could make his account entirely AI-generated if the technology were perfect, he said, but he doesn't want to. He cited a tweet (from someone the transcript calls Jim Claire, whose identity the corpus does not confirm) answering the "AI bros" who say "you can automate all of your writing": "what if I want to write?" The same applies to videos: "I want to create, it's fun, it feels good." From this he drew a prediction: "AI is naturally going to shift to take over the things that humans don't want to do," the need for the human will remain, and creators and personal brands will be the next media layer, generating the most value.

**Source:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md

The argument has two premises: people prefer human-made media, and people enjoy making it. Neither is demonstrated; both are offered as observations about human nature. What follows from them, if they are true, is that AI will be pushed by both consumers and creators toward the tasks nobody wants, which is the role Koe eventually assigns it in writing: the removal of friction.

In January 2024 he restated the point while presenting writing as a high-income skill. The "secret" is writing ("shocker"), and he admits labeling it a secret to capture attention and change behavior. "The future of media is decentralized: people are and will continue to turn to social media for news, courses for education, creators for knowledge." He did not "want to hear about AI disrupting writing just yet": it is hard to believe people will get their media fix entirely from AI-generated content, because "that's just not how humans work." AI is "a tool that helps writers and humans become more prolific in their work," that lets them "do more but better," and has other use cases.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

A conversation from December 2024 with the science-fiction novelist Devon Eriksen adds a complementary illustration from another medium, and it should be clearly marked as the guest's view. Eriksen described how audiobooks began as "books on tape," simply someone reading the book onto tape. "What we're starting to discover really resonates with people is good narration and moving over into it being an audio production with real acting something akin to a sort of a 1930s radio play." For his own novel (*Theft of Fire*), he went from "oh I suppose I should do an audio book" to "oh we're going to make something special," found very talented voice actors, and "directed every moment of it personally." Koe responded that audiobooks are "my thing." (Eriksen also mentioned that release timelines had been delayed by his wife's cancer, and that the sequel would be the second of a planned four-book series.)

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The relevance to this section is indirect but real. In a period when audio could be generated cheaply, Eriksen's response was to move up in effort and craft, toward the human-directed production. It is the same bet as Koe's on long-form writing: as the cheap version of a medium becomes abundant, value shifts to the version that visibly carries a human vision.

#### The essence behind the tool

Also in December 2024, Koe distinguished the tool from what it is used for. "I'm not talking about the tool by which someone creates; I mean the essence behind what that thing is." The essence of writing, as he lists it, is "the human desire to create, sifting through 100 ideas to find one, constructing a creative narrative toward a goal, capturing the attention of a group of people, deeply relating with them through conscious experience, documenting high signal information for future generations." He is not "against AI in the slightest" (Cortex was adding AI features at the time), "but if you aren't a writer or don't learn from a writer your AI outputs are probably going to disappoint you. Good luck building anything if, one, you don't know what you want to build, and two, you're still too lazy to learn."

**Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md

The list is worth reading as a specification of what cannot be delegated. Sifting a hundred ideas for one is judgment; constructing a narrative toward a goal requires a goal; relating through conscious experience requires having had the experience. The "essence behind the tool" is the part of writing that is not a matter of producing sentences, which, as Section 24.1 showed, is also the part where Koe locates most of the work (the outline, not the draft).

In the same video he dismissed a popular claim about AI and knowledge. "People are saying you can access any knowledge at the tip of your fingertips, you have a PhD in your pocket; you've already had that, you're just not going after the information. If you can't type it into Google you're not going to type it into chat GPT." If one had wanted to find the information in the past, one would have; now that it is one click away, "it's not that different and it's only attracting more lazy people." The marketing of AI "is just the get-rich-quick scheme of literally everything: oh you can do it all with AI now, but we all know where those things lead."

**Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md

The heuristic locates the bottleneck in agency and curiosity, not in access. It anticipates a theme of Chapter 35: tools amplify what a person already does, and a person who did not seek information before will not be transformed by a better search box.

In January 2025, again in the conversation with the Stan founders, Koe described his own use at that point. AI "is very helpful for inspiration and creativity at least in writing content, creating content." But having it take over his content, even as a background agent, was something else: "I don't really care how good it gets that takes a lot of the joy of it away from me." And "right now it could absolutely not do what I do cuz I've tried, my prompts are insane for me trying to replicate what I've done." He did not know where the technology would go.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Two separate reasons are given here, and they have different durability. The second (AI cannot yet replicate his work) is an empirical claim about a moment in the technology's development and could become false. The first (it would take away the joy) does not depend on the technology's quality at all: "I don't really care how good it gets." This is the "what if I want to write?" argument of 2023, now stated in the first person.

#### Intelligence is not creativity

In April 2025 Koe gave his most categorical statement. "AI can't read a book for you. Sure, it can read the book and spit out a summary, but it can't read the book for you. But it can help deepen your understanding as you read." Using AI heavily (building Cortex and trying to bake it into his own writing), he had realized "it just can't write for me. I don't think it ever will be able to. Not because it can't write exceptionally well. It really can. But because when I read over what it wrote, it's just not me. It's not my ideas. I didn't come up with it. And I don't like putting things out that I don't come up with." In conversation with Matt, Cortex's co-founder, he had concluded that AI is very useful for learning (getting information, going through large swaths of it). "You have access to intelligence. That doesn't mean creativity ... Intelligence can aid in those things ... but intelligence itself is not those things."

**Source:** How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md

The argument is careful in one respect: it concedes that AI writes well. The objection is not about quality but about authorship. A text not produced by one's own thinking is not one's own, regardless of how good it is, and Koe does not want to publish what he did not come up with. The distinction between intelligence and creativity is his frame for this: AI supplies processed information (intelligence); the generation of one's own ideas from experience (creativity) is a different capacity, which intelligence can aid but not replace. This frame should be read as his interpretation, not as a settled claim about what AI systems can do.

The same video describes what he does use it for. "For writing specifically, I found that AI is very good at giving me information that can spark the ideas that I want to come up with. It can create outlines. It can create potential posts for social media. It can create YouTube headlines. It can give me summaries from information that I can include while I'm writing like I would search with Google." "Ultimately I feel with writing that I have to come up with the idea that goes into the writing. But if I come to that idea from the information connected by AI, then that's fine and can lead to a lot better results. I see the information from AI as a way to trigger more thoughts and ideas in my head." This, he adds, is also how AI is used for learning.

**Source:** How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md

There is a tension inside this period that the corpus records. "It just can't write for me" and "it can create potential posts" are both from April 2025. The reconciliation he offers is the distinction between information and idea: AI output, even when it takes the form of a draft post, is treated as information that may trigger an idea, and the idea that goes into the writing must be his.

A month later, in May 2025, he described a further effect. The more he uses AI, the more he sees that he is not outsourcing his thinking but enhancing it. When he tries to get AI to perform a task accurately (rather than "yelling at it to write me an entire book in one sentence prompt"), he is exposed to clear, intelligent thinking; he reads and understands more; and he discovers his own blind spots, because his instructions are often not clear the first time. For newsletters, videos or books, he uses AI to learn: for the video in question he had asked for clarification and examples on "permissionless leverage," which he already grasped, in order to connect the dots further and weave the story. "AI to me is like reading a book that's tailored to you in real time." And AI is "closing the gap between learning and doing": since it accomplishes tasks according to one's instructions (landing-page copy, a viral marketing plan), one completes the task and learns to complete it better next time.

**Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md

The mechanism here is specific and worth separating from the slogan. Instructing an AI precisely is an act of articulation, and the failure of a first instruction reveals what one had not yet thought through. This is the same mechanism Chapter 21 attributed to writing itself: the attempt to put a thought into words exposes the gaps in it.

#### Use AI to learn, not to write

By 2025–2026 the position had settled into a working method whose principle Koe stated in March 2026: "How you use AI with content isn't to just write it and post it for you. It's to learn and understand faster. It's to see a post on social media, take that post, plug it into AI, and say: 'Hey, why does this work? And if I have this related idea, how can I take the principles from this social post that did extremely well and apply it to my own?'" "After doing that over time, it's like you're not taking a course to learn the principles; you're just learning them by doing, because AI is so integrated with our workflows nowadays that that's just how you do things now."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

In January 2026 he gave the prompt he uses for this. "AI is very good at helping you learn how to do this. It's not very good at writing for you." The prompt: "Do a comprehensive analysis on this social post, the overall idea, how the sentences are structured, and choice of words. Analyze why people engage with it, why it works so well, what psychological tactics are being used, and how I can replicate this style step by step with my own ideas." Paste it into a model such as Claude with one post, a newsletter or several posts, and "it's going to create like a little mini course on why those worked and how to replicate it." Then take another idea and practice. "Those are all of my secrets for content." (He refers the question of growing without relying on the algorithm to an earlier video on building an audience from zero.)

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The method is the AI version of a practice that runs through Chapters 22 and 23: studying validated content and reproducing its structure, not its ideas. What AI adds is speed and explicitness: the analysis that a writer would otherwise have to perform intuitively over hundreds of examples is produced on demand as "a little mini course."

The March 2026 video describes how his own content prompts were built in the same way. "I first took tweets or posts that I really liked and then I plugged them into AI and I said: 'Hey, break down exactly why these work. What are the principles here? What are the psychological tactics? Just teach me how to write these style of tweets.' And then I took that and I turned it into a prompt and I refined it a bit so that the output was decent." Using it, one types a topic or pastes reference content (newsletters one has written or liked, YouTube videos), and it "will generate potential tweet drafts that you can refine and make it sound like you." His newsletter prompt "is based off of my own structure and how I write"; to make the output sound more like oneself, paste in previous writing, or emulate someone else's voice "so you can start refining your own," since the output is "just a starting point, a prototype."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

"Prototype" here is an ordinary word used in a specific sense: AI output as the starting point of a piece, to be refined until it sounds like oneself. Note that this passage does describe AI generating draft tweets, which seems to stand in tension with "not very good at writing for you." The reconciliation, consistent with the April 2025 distinction, is that the draft is a prototype to be passed through one's own judgment and voice; the later passages of this section make that filtering explicit.

A December 2025 tweet, which he says got a lot of engagement, generalizes the method to any task. "Choose a task. Find a YouTube expert that teaches it. Have AI summarize their video. Add examples/context. Have AI turn that into a meta prompt. Test, refine, and reuse that prompt." "This has led to the best results in almost everything I have AI do." He presented it as part of an argument that AI is not a threat to high-agency people.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

A "meta prompt," in this usage, is a reusable prompt built from an expert's method, tested and refined; elsewhere in the corpus he also uses the term for "a prompt that helps you create a prompt." The workflow has the same structure as the reverse-engineering of posts: expert knowledge is extracted, made explicit, and turned into an instruction that can be applied repeatedly.

A May 2025 video applies the method to books. For landing pages or emails, upload *Breakthrough Advertising*, *Great Leads* or another copywriting book and ask for a summary and "the exact very detailed way of writing copywriting"; then turn those instructions into a prompt. He did this with Gemini 2.5 Pro: "Research the book Breakthrough Advertising and give me an extremely detailed guide on how to write persuasive copywriting," and got a guide for every step. Why it matters: good copy takes a long time to learn, and if one would rather focus on one's craft but needs marketing or sales, prompts can get one "90% there." A landing page written without this knowledge, he says, will not convert nearly as well.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md

**Complementary context:** *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) is Eugene Schwartz's classic of direct-response copywriting, source of the levels of awareness used in Section 24.2; *Great Leads* is a book on the opening of sales letters by Michael Masterson and John Forde. Koe names the books but not their authors in this passage.

This application is notable because it concerns persuasive copy (landing pages, emails) rather than the writer's own ideas. It fits the line he draws later: AI is acceptable for the technical and factual layer of writing, the layer that "a course would give you anyways."

He summarized the idea in a March 2026 image: "an entire course can fit into a prompt, and if a prompt helps you actually do the thing, then that's like a course on steroids, because you're actually getting results potentially." The example he had in mind was his own set of prompts for building a one-person business, shared on a canvas.

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

"Course on steroids" is his term for a prompt that contains a course's method and also executes it with the learner. It explains why, in Section 24.3, the 2026 version of his writing system ends with a prompt rather than a template: the prompt both teaches the structure and helps produce the first draft.

#### Iterations and two filters

In May 2026 Koe described using AI earlier in the process: to choose what to write about. He uses AI "for iterations on those ideas." His reasoning: ideas can come to mind on their own, "but when I'm actually sitting down to write a post, like you kind of need a topic to write about. You don't necessarily need to brainstorm and... have this non-practical way of coming up with an idea or reject AI. You're just trying to choose a topic to write about, so that then you can think... in a certain direction." The process: take really good posts one likes; ask the AI "just give me iterations of these"; read through them and "wait for one to hit"; then write about it, usually not taking exactly what it says. There are thus two filters (the AI's iteration, then one's own mind), so that "by the end of that process, I usually have something that's pretty original." Since he has "read and pattern recognized and pattern matched so many good posts," his own writing structure comes through over time, and he uses "my own taste to refine it to make it even further mine." "That's content 101 in a nutshell."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The "two filters" are his name for this sequence. The passage extends the January 2025 statement that AI is "helpful for inspiration" without contradicting it: the AI proposes, but the topic is accepted only when it "hits," and the writing that follows is his. What it adds is a clear statement of AI's function at the start of the process: to give thinking a direction. The passage also assumes something a beginner may lack: a trained taste, formed by having "pattern matched so many good posts." The second filter is only as good as the person applying it, which is the point he made in 2024 ("if you aren't a writer or don't learn from a writer your AI outputs are probably going to disappoint you").

#### Remove friction, and decide what gets published

The most complete statement of the position comes from an August 2026 video. "Use AI to remove friction, not write for you ... like you would use a course or a mentor or an editor while you are learning the skill because AI is good at scanning through large swaths of information." He acknowledges that it can hallucinate, and turns the point around: "do you think everyone just accepts that as law or do they question it?" The same is true of Instagram's "human experts" whose opinions people absorb, "and that's exactly what happened to the anti-AI people's brains"; but "the all 100% pro-AI people are also dumb as rocks." The applications are specific. If one does not know how to structure a piece, "ask for a list of structures that your ideas could fit into, something that a course would give you anyways." If one does not know what to write next, "ask for a few directions ... and then choose your own or choose none." If one does not understand something, research it, as writers always have, only more efficiently (the internet also gives false information). And the governing rule: "You are ultimately the one who decides what gets published ... If it doesn't represent the values, ideas, and beliefs you want it to, then refine it until it does, or don't post it."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

"Remove friction" is his name for this role. The analogy with a course, a mentor and an editor defines its scope precisely: each of those offers structures, options and corrections, but none writes the student's work. The instruction to "choose your own or choose none" preserves the writer's judgment even over the options offered.

The same video clarifies, explicitly, what his earlier slogan meant. "When I say, 'Don't use AI to write for you,' what I'm actually saying is 'Don't use AI to articulate your opinions and beliefs.'" Someone had asked whether he used AI in "my viral Twitter article, the most viewed article on Twitter." He did: for a section of bullet points with short descriptions of the nine stages of ego development, "from Susan Cook-Greuter. It's not my ideas." Any writer would look them up; "I don't want to mess it up. I want it to be as close to Susan Cook-Greuter's version ... as possible." Using AI for that section "doesn't take away from the value of the article." Rewriting them by hand would be "what you do in school for the sake of memorization," which ties back to another principle of his: "if you need to remember it, it's not important." He reserves the effort of articulation for "the ideas that I love ... because I understand the value of articulating them myself and how it improves my mind." "There is value in struggling to articulate your own thoughts and opinions because it creates a gap that you can then go learn and fill."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

This is the clearest line the corpus draws. Factual material belonging to others (here, Susanne Cook-Greuter's stages, which Chapter 38 discusses) may be summarized with AI, because accuracy is the goal and the writer's own articulation adds nothing. One's own opinions and beliefs must be articulated by oneself, because the struggle to articulate them is how they are formed and improved; the "gap" that struggle reveals is the next thing to learn. The rule thus has the same justification as writing itself in Chapter 21: writing is valuable because it is thinking, so delegating the articulation of one's thinking defeats the purpose. It also clarifies the April 2025 statement that AI "can't write for me": what it cannot do is write his ideas, because then they would not be his.

#### Control of the ideas, and the language of the machine

A February 2026 video places the question in historical perspective and is the most permissive statement in the corpus. Skills are "abstracted up a layer" as they always have been: human labor gave way to animal labor, then machine labor, then AI labor. One no longer needs to set up a typewriter, and perhaps one no longer even needs to know how to write. Koe says he still writes manually and researches with AI, but he sees no problem with others writing with AI "as long as they're in control of the ideas" and the text accurately represents what they mean "down to the word." What annoys him is that AI's patterns are everywhere, such as "oh, it's not this, it's this," especially on Reels. He leaves a question open: are those patterns simply more noticeable now, and "are we adopting the language of AI or is AI adopting the language of us?"

**Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md

"Abstracted up a layer" is his phrase for what happens to a skill when technology automates its lower levels: the human work moves to a higher level of abstraction. Applied to writing, it suggests that the skill that remains is control of the ideas and judgment of whether the words represent them, which is consistent with the August 2026 rule. The corpus notes that this passage qualifies an earlier warning not to give AI control over one's craft: here, AI writing is acceptable on the condition that the person controls the ideas.

The open question about language had already received a darker treatment in an October 2025 video. "In 2024 the Max Planck Institute found the first evidence that ChatGPT altered human speech patterns": the use of certain words that AI favors had increased. "You're starting to talk like the machine that learned to talk like you." Since language shaped how critically one could think as a child, he argues, with language "becoming dumber with AI and immature Gen Z lingo taking over social media, our minds are slowly narrowing to further guarantee mediocrity." In the future, AI will be "a foundational layer that precedes social programming from parents and schools": teachers use AI to teach, students use AI to learn, and those students become teachers and parents. He stresses that AI is great, that he uses it daily, but that it must be used in a certain way "so that you don't become as dumb as a rock."

**Source:** You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md

The study is a third-party finding that Koe reports; the corpus does not give its details beyond the increase in AI-associated words. The rest of the passage (narrowing minds, AI as a layer of programming preceding parents and schools) is his interpretation and prediction, built on the model of conditioning developed in Chapter 1. Within that model, the warning is consistent: if language is part of the program a mind runs, then a population that absorbs the machine's language absorbs part of its program.

#### Context creators, moats and the magic of discovery

Three late statements explain why, in Koe's view, writing by hand becomes more valuable rather than less as AI spreads.

The first is the idea of "context creators," from December 2025. AI can make a beautiful image on command, but there is a huge difference between someone with a vision using AI to execute it and someone wanting a quick image; many artists, he notes, use AI for first drafts and then refine them in Photoshop, because image generators cannot get very detailed. "AI has exposed what really matters in the creative process." When one asks AI to make all the decisions, to guess what works from hundreds of thousands of internet opinions, "there is no through line, no theme, no personality, no vision, no context. That's what creators are, context creators. They're not content creators. The content is meaningless without context." Apart from brain rot and memes (which he admits enjoying, and which keep people on platforms until they see ads), AI is "practically useless unless the person using AI is already good at creating content." "99% of AI generated content goes straight to the bottom of the barrel"; if AI content works, it is likely orchestrated by a human "passing off their personal context to it."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

"Context creators" connects this section to Section 24.2. There, context was what long form and a body of work give to each new post ("building a world"); here it is what a person gives to AI output. In both cases context is the scarce input, and content without it is worthless. The "99%" is a rhetorical figure, not a measurement.

The second is the claim that long-form writing is a "moat." In March 2026, promoting a fourteen-day challenge to "build a 2-hour content system" with fourteen AI prompts, one per day, Koe described what the prompts were for. "These aren't AI prompts that write posts for you or write newsletters for you. We're not that desperate yet. They help refine your thinking. They help stress test ideas. They help you learn. They help you gain clarity on what you actually want to do… They help you learn how to structure your ideas in a way that captures attention… so that you don't just put out fortune cookie tweets and act like you're Marcus Aurelius when nobody's going to care about you because you're not Marcus Aurelius." "In order to be creative, you need something to create." "Long-form writing is a moat in today's world because AI can't really replicate it. It's never been a better time to start writing, especially authentically." He added that X articles were "doing really well right now."

**Source:** If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md

"Fortune cookie tweets" is his term for aphoristic posts without the context or authority to support them: the tip of the iceberg with nothing underneath. "Moat," a common term of business strategy, is used here for a durable advantage that competitors (in this case, AI-generated content) cannot cross. The claim that AI "can't really replicate" long form is, like the January 2025 claim about his own prompts, a statement about the technology at a given moment; its stronger version is the one he gives in the next passage, which does not depend on capability.

The third is the most philosophical. In an April 2026 video urging viewers to write essays, Koe said he had recently realized that AI destroys "the magic of surprise and discovery," that is, of creativity. AI can be creative in some ways, but "you can tell AI to share something novel, but then you are anticipating it. It is no longer novel." Asking for a surprise eliminates the possibility of stumbling upon a discovery. It may give good material, but not from one's own creative ability and thinking. The more he uses AI the more useful he finds it, but "on the other edge of the sword" it exhausts creativity extremely fast, because creativity is sparse: it requires going out, experiencing something novel, and then having a little to write about. With AI one hands it everything and becomes "empty creatively," without having sat with the material, understood it or thought it through. As someone who remains "still very pro-AI," he believes the most relevant content going forward will be essay-form, slow content: not only long form but anything opposite to an article.

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

This argument differs from the others because it is not about quality, authorship or demand but about the structure of discovery. A surprise cannot be requested without ceasing to be a surprise. If creativity consists partly in encountering what one did not expect, then a tool used to fulfill expectations, however well, cannot supply it. The argument connects to Section 24.1: the outline as frame of reference works precisely by leaving room for walks, reading and incubation, during which ideas arrive that were not requested. "Slow content" is his term for writing that takes the time to discover something, as opposed to "fast content" optimized for engagement; Chapter 21 treats the essay in these terms.

#### How the position fits together

The table orders Koe's statements on AI in writing and makes visible both the continuity and the tensions.

| Date | Statement | Role assigned to AI |
|---|---|---|
| 2023-04 | "It's human nature to want human"; "what if I want to write?" | Will take over what humans don't want to do |
| 2024-01 | "Don't want to hear about AI disrupting writing just yet" | Tool that makes writers more prolific |
| 2024-12 | Essence behind the tool; "if you aren't a writer... your AI outputs are probably going to disappoint you"; Google vs ChatGPT | Amplifier of an existing writer |
| 2025-01 | Helpful for inspiration; would remove the joy; cannot replicate his work | Inspiration |
| 2025-04 | "It just can't write for me"; intelligence is not creativity; information that triggers ideas | Information and sparks |
| 2025-05 | "A book that's tailored to you in real time" | Tutor; closes the gap between learning and doing |
| 2025-05 to 2025-12 | Books into copywriting prompts; meta prompts; outlining questions "if you are worried about having AI do all of the writing"; "99% ... bottom of the barrel"; context creators | Extractor of methods; tool for experts |
| 2026-01 to 2026-03 | Reverse-engineer posts; content prompts as prototypes; "a course on steroids"; fine "as long as they're in control of the ideas"; prompts "refine your thinking"; long form as moat | Learning accelerator; drafting aid under the writer's control |
| 2026-04 | Destroys "the magic of surprise and discovery"; essays and slow content | Useful but creatively exhausting |
| 2026-05 | Iterations and two filters | Topic generator |
| 2026-08 | "Remove friction"; "don't use AI to articulate your opinions and beliefs" | Course, mentor or editor |

The movement is from skeptic to integrator with limits. What stays constant is the claim that the writer's ideas, opinions and beliefs must be the writer's own, and the reason given for it changes in emphasis: in 2023 it is the pleasure of creating; in 2025 it is authorship ("it's just not me"); in 2026 it is the cognitive value of struggling to articulate one's thoughts, and the structure of discovery. What changes is the range of tasks AI is allowed: from inspiration only, to research and summaries, to structures and directions, to iterations of posts and drafts treated as prototypes.

Two tensions remain unresolved in the corpus. The first is between accepting AI as a drafting aid "as long as they're in control of the ideas" (February 2026) and defining the essay by the fact that AI cannot write one (April 2026); the most coherent reconciliation is that the essay is the form whose purpose is discovery, while the post and the landing page are forms whose purpose is communication, and AI can help with the second but defeats the first. The second tension concerns whether AI makes people less intelligent. In May 2025 Koe said that AI does not make people stupider or take away their creativity; by August and October 2025 he warned that handing it all control and decisions is "a surefire way to become stupid" and that language is "becoming dumber with AI"; and in April 2026 he described AI as exhausting creativity. He does not explain the change. The corpus reads it as a conditional acceptance: the tool does not degrade thinking in itself, but its passive use does. That reading is consistent with every passage in this section, all of which distinguish between using AI to think better (instructing it precisely, interrogating posts, testing ideas) and using it in place of thinking.

**Source:** EV-149 and EV-127 in `03b_evolucion.md`

The position therefore closes the chapter where it began. The process of Section 24.1 locates the real work of writing in the outline: in choosing, connecting and questioning ideas, with incubation in between. The ecosystem of Sections 24.2–24.5 multiplies the results of that work across formats and turns them into an audience, a hub and a laboratory for products. AI, in Koe's final position, may assist every part of the system that consists of retrieving, structuring, condensing and testing, but not the part that consists of having something to say. That part, which he calls context, is what the rest of the system distributes, and what Part IX analyzes as the source of an audience's trust.

### Exercises

1. **Reconstruct your own week.** Using the five components of content that "gains traction" (one weekly theme, a place to jot ideas, a way to capture inspiration, packaging, retrieval while writing), audit how you currently produce any piece of writing, whether for work, study or social media. Which component is missing or weak? Then design one week on Koe's model: what would you put in the outline on day one, so that it works as a "frame of reference" for the rest of the week? At the end of the week, compare the ideas that arrived during walks, reading or rest with those you had at the start. What does the comparison suggest about his claim that writer's block is incubation?

2. **Map one organism.** Choose a creator you follow closely. Classify their output into short, medium and long form, and trace the routes between them: where do short posts send you, and where does the long form send you? Identify their hub, if they have one. Then apply the iceberg: is there depth under the tip, and how would you know? Finally, find one post of theirs that works mainly because of context built elsewhere, in the sense of Koe's "that singular tweet means nothing to most people," and explain what that context is.

3. **Choose an entry point and defend it.** The corpus contains conflicting advice on where to start: on X with the newsletter "can wait," or with a newsletter from day one, even unwritten, as a "vessel." For a specific person (yourself, or a described case such as a nurse with no audience who wants to write about burnout), decide which entry point you would recommend, and justify the decision with the reasons Koe gives for each. State what evidence after three months would make you change the recommendation.

4. **Repurpose with judgment.** Take a piece of your own long writing (an essay, a report, a long message) of roughly 1,000 words. Following the mechanics of Section 24.3, extract a thread of no more than fifteen units, three standalone short posts, and one list post. Then evaluate the result against two of Koe's instructions that pull in different directions: "rewrite them as standalone short posts" and "repurpose the actionable part" versus "don't try to be too clever when nobody knows who you are" and his later criticism of "fortune cookie tweets." Which of your short pieces would only work if the reader already knew you?

5. **Design a validation ladder for an idea.** Pick a product or business idea you have considered. Write the post that would be its first rung, and then specify, for each subsequent rung of the ladder (thread or newsletter, free download, paid product, larger business), what result would justify moving up and what result would justify stopping or pivoting. Where does Koe's $10 test with a 2.5% conversion threshold fit in your ladder, and what would you need to know to decide whether that threshold is meaningful in your case?

6. **Examine "sell before you build."** Reconstruct the reasoning behind Koe's launch method (landing page first, a release date, the first sale imposing the deadline). Identify the conditions under which it works, including the one he states (marketing and sales skill) and others you can infer. Then construct a case in which following it would be a mistake or would harm buyers, using his own warning that otherwise "you just stole people's money" and his later lesson about deadlines applied to a team. What safeguards would you add?

7. **Draw your line on AI.** Write down, as precisely as you can, which parts of your own writing you would let AI do and which you would not, using Koe's final distinction between factual or structural help ("remove friction") and the articulation of "your opinions and beliefs." Then test the line against two of his arguments: "there is value in struggling to articulate your own thoughts and opinions because it creates a gap that you can then go learn and fill," and "you can tell AI to share something novel, but then you are anticipating it." Does either argument move your line? Finally, identify one weakness or unsupported premise in his position (for example, in "it's human nature to want human" or "long-form writing is a moat... because AI can't really replicate it") and say what would count as evidence against it.
