## Chapter 23 — Structure and Attention

Chapter 22 answered the question of what to write: value is ideas written through the lens of a goal or problem, the raw material is the writer's own lived problems and beliefs, interests are organized into a topic tree, ideas are hunted rather than waited for, and repetition with perspective beats the search for novelty. That answer leaves the second half of the craft untouched. An idea that is never read changes nothing, and Koe's corpus is unusually insistent on this point. "Most people don't have an idea problem," he says in 2025, "they have a structure problem." This chapter collects what he teaches about the second half: how an idea is shaped so that another mind opens it, stays with it and comes out the other side changed.

The chapter moves in six steps. Section 23.1 presents Koe's method for learning structure, which is to borrow it: templates and frameworks used as "training wheels," the distinction between structure and content that he calls "a metaphysical principle," the practice of imitating and then innovating, the pyramid principle as a "meta framework," and his answer to the recurring objection that frameworks make writing inauthentic. Section 23.2 deals with the outermost layer of any piece, the hook or title, through his metaphor of content as a gift with contents and packaging, the ghostwriter experiment in which one changed line multiplied a thread's likes two hundredfold, his PPP framework, the six building blocks of a hook, Justin Welsh's three-line hook, and the principle that "looks come before depth." Section 23.3 gathers the tools for capturing and holding attention that Koe eventually called the Ten Commandments of Engagement, together with their psychological foundation ("people remember how you make them feel") and his rules of readability. Section 23.4 steps back from tactics to the three feelings that produce engagement (education, entertainment and inspiration), the "philosophical to the tangible divide" discussed with the founders of Stan, and the conditions under which a writer earns the right to be abstract. Section 23.5 explains why story is the deepest structure of all: the mind as a sense-making machine that understands the world in stories, the curiosity loop, story as structure rather than a chain of events, and the thesis that sales is storytelling. Section 23.6 closes with the long-form structures: hook–body–conclusion, the problem → context → steps skeleton, PAS as "the micro story," BPAS, Koe's own APAG framework, cross-domain synthesis and the classic copywriting formulas.

The chapter leans on several earlier ones. The claim that a framework "orders the reader's mind" is an application of the model of psychic entropy from Chapter 5 and of attention as a scarce resource from Chapter 6. The idea of putting the reader "in flow" through a hierarchy of goals comes from the game and flow model of Chapter 10. The negativity bias that Koe exploits in his third commandment rests on the law of conceptual survival from Chapter 3. The "dopamine dealer" presupposes the distinction between cheap and earned dopamine from Chapter 11. The pain and process framework that Chapter 17 presented as the origin of "almost everything" Koe writes reappears here as one member of a family of problem-first structures, and the concept of time under attention, defined in Chapter 21, returns as the condition that allows an established writer to be pithy. The chapter prepares Chapter 24, which places these structures inside a weekly process and a content ecosystem, and Chapter 32, which develops persuasion, levels of awareness and the ethics of marketing that this chapter only touches.

### 23.1 Templates and Frameworks as Training Wheels

#### The structure problem

The section's thesis is stated most bluntly in a February 2025 video on escaping what Koe calls "beginner hell": "most people don't have an idea problem, they have a structure problem." The claim is stronger than it sounds. "Any idea can go viral if and only if you structure it the right way." Koe's reasoning is an analogy with any other achievement: just as there is a series of steps that leads to any result, "there is a series of sentences or a structure that makes any idea go viral." Generating ideas, in this view, is the easy part. He remarks to his viewers that they have "just passed by at least 50" ideas in the course of the video. The hard and interesting part is to take an idea one suspects will not do well and rewrite it until it does. Practicing that, and failing at it many times, is in his words "how you become a master creator."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The same passage answers an objection that beginners raise when they are told to work from other people's ideas: "I didn't come up with this myself." Koe's reply is that "you don't have to talk as if you came up with it, you just share the idea." The ownership of an idea, for him, matters less than the craft of presenting it, and even the worst idea can be turned into something people want, for instance by telling it as a story (the subject of Section 23.5). Chapter 22 discussed the broader question of borrowing validated ideas and the tension it creates with the demand for an original perspective; here the point is narrower. Structure is the variable a beginner controls, and it is the one most beginners neglect.

Two years earlier, in a November 2023 video on productizing one's mind, Koe had listed "not understanding structure versus content" as the second of several traps that keep people from building a knowledge business. He added a remark that he did not develop: "this goes far beyond writing content for business, it is a metaphysical principle." The transcript leaves the remark unexplained, and it would be a mistake to reconstruct a metaphysics from one sentence. What can be said is that the distinction recurs throughout the corpus in other domains (the difference between a life's structure and its events, between a game's rules and its moves, between a story's arc and its incidents, which Section 23.5 develops), so the phrase probably signals that Koe sees the separation of form from content as a general way of understanding things, not a writing trick. The practical instruction attached to it is concrete: until one's writing ability develops, take one's ideas and use "high-performing structures and frameworks as training wheels," looking at quotes, newsletter structures and post structures and plugging one's ideas into them.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

#### "The structure is not the actual words but what the words are"

Koe describes his own apprenticeship in writing as an apprenticeship in structure. In a December 2024 video for "normal people" building a one-person business, he says he did not learn to write by studying writing. He learned by studying what worked on social media, scrolling the timeline "not from the lens of consumer but a Creator." The internal monologue he reports is a procedure in miniature: "he used this as the hook, he had a bullet list of these points, then one last line that tied it all together; let me try one with my own ideas." He repeated it "over and over." He did the same with newsletters, studying other people's, and with YouTube, where he started and then noticed what he needed to improve: speaking better, adding text screens, B-roll, quotes and graphics, lessons that also shaped his newsletter.

The sentence that gives the method its precision is this one: "the structure is not the actual words but what the words are." The first line of a post is not a particular sentence; it is a hook, and the question to ask is why it is important. Then come the bullets, and then the last line. To study a post is to name the function of each part, not to memorize its wording. Koe's practical version is to copy a post into a note (in his case, into Kortex, his writing software), open it on the side, and emulate it with one's own idea. He admits it is hard at first. And he insists that this is copying the structure, not the content: "you change the idea and words completely."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The earliest version of this practice appears in his December 2022 "One Person Business Roadmap," where it is the second step of the first stage: "use content templates as training wheels." Almost every social media course, he notes, has templates for structuring posts so they get more engagement. He copies certain tweets into a note and uses their structure to write other tweets. He shows on screen an early tweet of his own that did well (its text is not read aloud, so the transcript does not preserve it) and describes its anatomy: a hook; three bullet points that hold attention while delivering actionable value; and a wrap-up conclusion naming a pain point the reader may face. He noted what worked about it and "moved in a better direction" as he learned. The passage reappears almost verbatim in his February 2024 compilation "The One-Person Business Model (Full Guide)."

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The phrase "lens of a creator, not a consumer" is the hinge of the whole method, and it connects this chapter to Chapter 16, where Koe describes the shift from consumer to researcher. In an April 2023 video on the "micro education" business model, he frames structure as the step that follows having an idea: practice articulating it "in a persuasive and structured manner that holds people's attention," which means writing it so that it illustrates or implies the reader's pains, problems and goals, and the solution between them. The steps he gives are to study top-performing tweets, deconstruct what they look like and why they work, and practice writing them with one's own idea; to study small copywriting frameworks such as PAS and AIDA "to tap into human psychology"; and, for long form, to study PASTOR or the systems of his course The Two-Hour Writer. Above all: "consume social media and marketing content from the lens of a Creator not a consumer," and practice. The justification is blunt. Ideas are impactful only if they are consumed; if one cannot structure an idea and capture attention, nobody sees it, no following forms and nobody pays.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

#### Imitate, then innovate

The principle that governs the use of borrowed structures appears in an August 2022 video and again, nearly word for word, in the 2024 compilation: "as with learning anything you have to imitate and then innovate." The best way to learn short-form writing is to read short-form content, imitate someone's tweet and use it as a **soft structure** for one's own. Koe's term is precise: a soft structure is a shape one leans on, not a mold one fills. He is explicit about the limit: "do not copy or plagiarize." One takes the structure as inspiration and makes one's own piece. Then, once one has practiced and seen what works and what does not, one starts "playing around" with the structure to find what works best for oneself. Imitation is the first phase of a sequence whose second phase is experimentation.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

A January 2024 video presents the same idea under the name **emulation**, which Koe calls "the key" to short-form writing. He learned to write good tweets through observation, and the procedure he lists is a set of habits more than a technique. Take ideas from one's own long-form writing. Study quotes from favorite authors. Use research tools such as Tweet Hunter to find high-performing posts. "Be a researcher, not a consumer": read social content to study how posts are built. For every post one sees, try to recreate it with one's own ideas, to train the mind. "Drown yourself in good short form writing." Use other writers' sentence structures "as training wheels to write your own." And contemplate why a post does well, which includes how it makes one feel and why. He adds that any part of his APAG framework (Section 23.6) can structure a tweet, an Instagram or LinkedIn post, or a reel or video script.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

**Complementary context:** Tweet Hunter is a commercial tool for X (formerly Twitter) that, among other functions, lets users search large libraries of high-engagement posts by topic. Koe mentions it as one research aid among several; nothing in the method depends on it.

#### Reverse-engineering a post

The most detailed demonstration of the method comes from the February 2025 video, where Koe dissects one of his own posts on "how to get ahead of 99%" of people and then rebuilds it around a different idea. The breakdown has four parts. First, a broad hook that names a desired outcome and contains a specific number. Second, five exaggerated, polarizing steps in a bullet list. Koe explains why bullets help: they aid readability, and they let a reader engage with any single point without accepting the rest ("don't agree, don't care, oh I love that one, like, retweet"). Third, additional context answering the objection he knew readers would raise, "I can't disappear completely, I have friends and family," which he could anticipate only because he had written enough content to know it. Fourth, a last line that ties everything together.

Then he plugs in a new idea taken from a book, "meditation is running for the mind," and rewrites the post: "How to clear your mind in 60 seconds: lock yourself in a room, sit in the corner, breathe deep into your belly, feel the breath with all senses, repeat for 1 minute; any time you get distracted refocus on the breath. Meditation sprints are how you achieve calm fast." The new post keeps the skeleton (outcome-and-number hook, steps, closing line) and changes everything else. Koe offers an alternative hook built from one of the engagement tools of Section 23.3, a negative spin: "How to destroy negative thoughts in 60 seconds." His summary of what he is doing is the clearest statement of the method in the corpus: "I'm not just copying people's tweets, I'm studying the structure, studying what works and plugging a five-word idea into it."

The method applies, he says, to anything: newsletters, landing pages, YouTube videos, reels, TikToks. The training-wheels metaphor also carries its own exit. One keeps a structure open while learning; once one can write on one's own, one takes the wheels off, "start[s] to wiggle a bit, and that's where you learn the most." The wobble after the wheels come off is not a failure of the method but its most productive phase.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

A smaller example from February 2023 shows the same move at the scale of a single sentence. One of Koe's top tweets read: "the greatest skill is decreasing the gap between idea and execution." To demonstrate plugging a new topic into a proven structure, he swaps in marketing: "the greatest skill of the 21st century is learning to capture attention," adding that "it can be better than that." The point is not the quality of the improvised line but the economy of the procedure: the structure is already laid out and validated, so testing a new topic inside it is a low-risk bet.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

A September 2024 beginner guide turns the method into an exercise for people who "still don't know how to write content." Make a list of pain points one can help solve or has had. Make a list of solutions or desired outcomes, the goals people want. Go to X, Instagram or another platform and scroll until one finds a post that did relatively well; look at how it is written; use it as a template and plug one of one's ideas into it. Koe suggests taking one of his own top posts, keeping its structure and inserting one's pain point, rewriting until the post stands alone and comes from one's own idea.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

#### The museum of structures and the 80/20 of content

Koe's later formulations turn the habit into a small system. In a January 2026 video addressed to people with multiple interests, he connects it to the **idea museum**, his name in that video for the swipe file where one notes ideas as soon as they appear. The instruction is to go through the idea museum, break down the structures of the ideas stored there, and "create a second idea museum of structures that you can use." The practice he proposes is combinatorial: take the first three ideas one notes, break down how each is structured, and then interchange them "like a 3 by 3 matrix": "take idea one, plug it into idea three structure and you practice writing like that." Three ideas and three structures yield nine exercises, and the separation of content from structure becomes a literal grid.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

A March 2026 video on building a one-person business with AI compresses everything into what Koe calls the 80/20 of content, three steps followed by practice. "First is just write down ideas like crazy: read more books, listen to podcasts, reflect on your life and try to catch ideas that are unique and beneficial." Second, "immediately think of a pain point or benefit: train your mind to think of why this idea is important to more than just yourself." Third, "save ideas that you like the structure of," keep them in a folder (in his Eden software, in that video), and as a beginner "imitate the structure or framework of these with your own ideas as the topic." Then "you simply practice writing until it becomes second nature." The second step deserves attention because it links structure to value: an idea becomes writable for others only when it is attached to a pain or a benefit that matters to someone other than the writer. This is the same condition that Chapter 22 called writing through the lens of a goal or problem, now applied at the moment of capture.

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

#### Why frameworks work: structure orders the reader's mind

Why should a borrowed structure make an idea more effective? Koe's most interesting answer connects writing to his model of the mind. In the January 2024 guide, he addresses people who say "I don't know what to write." His first question is whether they are using a framework that tells them what to write next; if not, that is "problem number one." A writing framework, he argues, leverages psychology, because frameworks "help people focus their mind." He then draws an explicit parallel with psychic entropy, the state of disorder in consciousness that Chapter 5 described as the mind's default. Just as he himself becomes overwhelmed without a project to order his mind, "if your writing doesn't have a structure that orders the reader's mind then it's not going to be good writing; they're not going to finish it; you're not putting them into the flow state; your writing isn't a game that they're trying to read."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The passage compresses several ideas from earlier in this book into one argument about the reader. Chapter 5 argued that consciousness left to itself drifts toward chaos and that goals are what impose order on it. Chapter 10 argued that a game is a structured hierarchy of goals and that flow arises when challenge matches skill. Koe's claim here is that a well-structured piece of writing performs the same function for the reader, temporarily: it gives the reader's attention a sequence of small goals (finish this line, find out the third step, reach the resolution) and so lends their mind an order it would not otherwise have. On this reading, the framework is not a constraint imposed on the writer for the market's sake; it is the mechanism by which writing does what Chapter 21 said it does, which is to order other people's consciousness. The APAG framework of Section 23.6 makes the link explicit by naming its last step "Gamify."

The claim is Koe's interpretation, built from his own frameworks, and he presents no evidence on reading behavior beyond his experience. It is nonetheless consistent: the same mechanism that explains why a person without a project feels lost explains, in his model, why a reader without a structure stops reading.

#### The standard frameworks and how to keep them open

Koe describes his own early dependence on frameworks without embarrassment. "When I first started writing, frameworks were a lifesaver," he says in February 2025; most people, by contrast, do not write according to any framework. He read the book *Cashvertising* and learned basics such as AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action). Instead of writing "willy-nilly," he recommends using AIDA, PAS and PASTOR, asking ChatGPT or Kortex's AI for "10 copywriting frameworks for short form content," and then practicing by writing a single idea in all of them. He also offers his own "pain and process" framework (developed in Chapter 17) as a free template.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Complementary context:** *Cashvertising* (2008) is a popular introduction to advertising psychology by the copywriter Drew Eric Whitman; the transcripts do not name its author. AIDA is one of the oldest models in advertising, usually traced to the American advertiser E. St. Elmo Lewis around 1900. PAS (problem, agitate or amplify, solution) and PASTOR (whose letters Koe spells out as problem, amplify, story, testimony, offer, response) are standard copywriting formulas whose authorship is diffuse; Section 23.6 discusses how Koe uses them.

The same video adds a working routine, presented as the third tip on training wheels: have the frameworks open while writing. Capture ideas throughout the week. Sit down two or three days a week for one or two hours. Open Kortex or another writing or notes application, paste the content frameworks into it, open them in a side pane, and write out the ideas while referring to them. The routine is modest by design. It is meant for beginners, and its figures (two to three days a week, one to two hours) are lower than those Koe reports for himself elsewhere, a discrepancy Chapter 24 discusses.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### The pyramid principle: a meta framework

One framework occupies a special place in Koe's teaching because he treats it as a framework for frameworks. In a September 2024 video on "follow-worthy content," he introduces the **pyramid principle** as something that "sets the scene for almost any persuasion": "think of this as a meta framework for any other framework," used as training wheels in tweets, newsletters, speaking, "everything." It has three steps. First, start with the answer, conclusion or big idea. Second, prove the conclusion with three or more key arguments. Third, support the arguments with facts, data, anecdotes, quotes and similar evidence, "from a big picture lens." His summary is a single line: "state your point, explain why and back it up."

He gives three reasons it works. The reader knows immediately whether the piece is relevant and whether to continue. The reader can follow the writer's logic to the conclusion. And the structure is simple and easy to replicate. All his videos, he says, are built this way. The video in which he explains it starts with the thesis "persuasion is the greatest skill of the 21st century" and backs it up with key points, using quotes from books, his own tweets and other people's tweets; other creators, he notes, use scientific literature. He then turns the authenticity question on himself: does using the framework make him inauthentic, or does it give him "a vessel to accurately distribute" and articulate his thoughts? He keeps the pyramid principle in his outline template as what he calls a "meta document," a reminder he sees every time he writes. And he places it in a sequence: "five levels of awareness come before the pyramid principle," meaning that before deciding what conclusion to lead with, a writer must know how aware the reader already is of the problem (a concept from direct-response marketing that Chapter 32 develops).

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

In December 2025, in a video on articulating one's thoughts, Koe returns to the pyramid principle with a more formal definition: "a communication framework that structures ideas in a hierarchical logical way to make information more palatable and persuasive." Its parts are the main idea (the key conclusion or recommendation), the key arguments (usually three to five) and the detailed evidence (data, examples, analysis). What has changed is its position. In 2025 it is no longer the meta framework underneath everything but the "intermediate" framework in a ladder of three, after PAS for beginners and before cross-domain synthesis for advanced writers (both treated in Section 23.6). And Koe now defines it by contrast with problem-first structures: "Unlike most content today that waits to give you the answer until the end… this takes an answer-first approach." He illustrates with a line from Alex Hormozi about being in a great mood, which he treats as the top of a pyramid: "you're not starting with a problem… you're starting with the idea, the conclusion, the answer." The key arguments are generated by a simple procedure: "All you have to do is ask why three to five times and come up with a compelling answer." Evidence follows: examples from one's life, data and statistics, anecdotes from clients. Once one understands these frameworks, he adds, one sees them everywhere, for instance in YouTube titles, some of which state a problem while others state a big idea.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The shift is a refinement, not a reversal, but it is worth stating precisely because Chapter 17 and Chapter 21 touched on it from other angles. In 2024 Koe presented the pyramid principle and problem-first structures such as pain and process as complementary, with the pyramid as the general scaffold. In December 2025 he ranks them: problem-first for beginners, answer-first for intermediates. In 2026, as Chapter 21 showed, he assigns the answer-first approach to the article and contrasts it with the essay, which "figures it out" rather than starting from the conclusion. The common thread is that the pyramid is a tool for persuading readers of a conclusion one already holds. It is not a tool for discovering what one thinks.

**Complementary context:** The pyramid principle was formulated by Barbara Minto, a former McKinsey consultant, in *The Minto Pyramid Principle*. Koe does not name her.

#### The authenticity objection

The most common resistance Koe meets is not technical but moral. In the September 2024 video he reports the objection as he hears it from course students, members of the Kortex community, calls and podcasts: "what if I don't want to use a framework, what if I want to be authentic and write whatever I want?" His answer is a compromise with three supporting reasons: "write what you want but organize your writing into a framework until you understand what makes writing impactful."

The first reason is that there is another person on the other side of the writing. If that person does not see the writing as valuable, and if the mind makes sense of words in a specific structure, the writer will fall short. The second is that frameworks do not hurt creativity; they enhance it. The third is the sharpest: one is already using a framework, namely one's own mind and the way one was programmed to think. It is simply unconscious, and in Koe's words "clearly unimpactful or else you would have the things you want in life." The conclusion follows with some irony: "write what you want... like you've been doing and keep getting the results you've been getting."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The third reason applies to writing the diagnosis of Chapter 1. There, Koe argued that a person who does not choose goals has goals assigned to them by conditioning. Here, a writer who refuses a conscious framework does not write without one; they write inside the unexamined one they inherited. The choice is not between structure and freedom but between a chosen structure and an inherited one. The argument also qualifies Koe's other advice, in Chapter 22, that one can write about whatever one wants: the freedom concerns the topic, while the discipline concerns the form.

Two further passages give the principle its general name. In October 2024, discussing what he called the death of the personal brand, Koe says that to set ourselves apart from the influencer and the generic content creator "we need to learn to be ourselves but in a structured way so that it actually makes sense to people," because one can be oneself with someone and they may still not like it. "Creativity is in the constraints; creativity isn't freedom and chaos, it's the limitations that you impose to create that art." In February 2025 he answers the objection that templates feel "soulless" by agreeing in part and then drawing the line: "templates and frameworks don't make you inauthentic: creativity thrives with constraints, and a framework allows you to express your authentic ideas in a way that other people care about." Being purely authentic without regard for others is great, he says, "but good luck being able to sustain that as a lifestyle that pays you." He adds that money is not the end-all but it is important, "and it's only as evil as the person who wields it," a principle Chapter 34 develops.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md; Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The coined phrase **creativity is in the constraints** (also "creativity thrives with constraints") connects the writing advice to a theme from Chapter 8, where constraints and anti-goals were tools for focusing a plan. In both cases the limit is what makes a creative act possible, because it narrows the space of options to one the mind can work within.

#### Paint between the lines: frameworks as lenses, not identities

Having defended frameworks, Koe immediately warns against holding them too tightly. In the same September 2024 video he says that one cannot become "very dogmatic" about these frameworks: "you kind of have to paint between the lines." His example is concrete. His pain and process framework consists, strictly, of a pain and a process; yet for a tweet, adding a last-line conclusion is good. To a student who thinks "I can only have the pain point and the process, I don't know if I can add another sentence," he answers: "dude come on yes you can just try one post where you're testing things out isn't going to ruin your life."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The phrase "paint between the lines" inverts the familiar "color inside the lines." The lines are there, but the painting happens in the space they leave, and a writer is allowed to blur them. The same video then generalizes the warning into a principle about all advice. Frameworks, systems and most education are training wheels, "things to experiment with." They are not meant to become an attached part of one's identity; "they should be used as lenses from which you view specific situations." Koe describes the failure pattern with an example from health. People see a novel solution, such as a keto or ancestral diet, believe it will solve their problem for good, make an identity out of it, post about it (which solidifies the identity), and eventually get hurt because they are stuck defending it. They do not realize that progress in business, health or relationships requires shedding that ideology and pursuing what is relevant to their situation now. The purpose of advice is to give one something to experiment with, to find one's own way, and to pass it down "in this continuous flow of information that helps people grow and evolve." As an educator, Koe thinks it wise to preface his own advice with an invitation to think critically, avoid dogmatism and stay open-minded; the one-person business, for example, may suit a certain kind of person, but there are other paths.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

This is a direct application of the law of conceptual survival from Chapter 3. Once a framework becomes part of the self, the mind defends it as it defends any other part of identity, and the tool that was supposed to serve a goal becomes a position to protect. Treating frameworks as lenses keeps them on the instrumental side of the line.

#### From frameworks to principles

What comes after the training wheels? The September 2024 video gives a five-step answer for learning persuasion in general: research writing and speaking frameworks; write them down next to each other; practice them in the real world; notice the patterns between them; and break the rules. In practice, Koe suggests researching and writing down five to ten frameworks to try in posts, conversations and newsletters, "then drop the frameworks and use the principles that made them work in the first place" to become a persuasive person at any moment. One can find frameworks by searching online or by asking ChatGPT for "10 copywriting/persuasion frameworks." He mentions that he created his own, APAG, the main framework of his 2 Hour Writer course, without explaining it in that video (Section 23.6 reconstructs it from another).

The fourth step carries the weight. Placing several frameworks side by side makes their shared logic visible, and what is shared is the principle. Section 23.6 shows that Koe himself performed this analysis on AIDA, PAS and PASTOR and extracted what they have in common.

The same video ends with a recap of how to start practicing persuasion, in seven steps. First, accept that one needs power and status "to break out of the hierarchy someone more powerful than you put you in." Second, use the pyramid principle as a starting point: state, argue, back up the point. Third, use a copywriting, content or persuasion framework to structure one's writing, speaking and scripts. Fourth, create an outline first, based on that framework. Fifth, have a place to capture ideas, "because the best ideas won't come when you try to brainstorm them all." Sixth, start the draft and string the ideas together. Seventh, post it, so as to get data and improve.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The first step may surprise a reader who expected a writing lesson, but it is consistent with Chapter 2, where Koe described social life as a set of dominance and attention hierarchies. Persuasion, in his framing, is the means by which a person who was placed in someone else's hierarchy acquires the standing to leave it. The remaining steps form the bridge to Chapter 24, which develops capture, outlining and drafting as a weekly process.

#### Training wheels and "resist the template"

The training-wheels position is stable from 2022 to 2025. In April 2026, however, Koe published a video urging his audience to start writing essays in which he says, among other things, "write to discover, not to perform," "resist the template... for now just write," and "build a body of work, not a content calendar." Taken alone, this looks like a reversal of everything above. Chapter 21 discussed that video in detail. Three months later, in July 2026, he is again saying that "there's journaling, and then there's writing," and that writing means capturing attention with structures that work (Section 23.2).

The two positions are compatible once their purposes are distinguished, and the corpus supports the distinction without stating it as a rule. The essay, in Koe's 2026 framing, is slow content written to change what one believes; it puts thinking first and structure later. The post is distribution, written to capture attention; it puts packaging first. Frameworks are training wheels for the second activity. The 2026 essay advice does not say that structure is unimportant; it says that, for the activity of discovering what one thinks, the template comes too early. A related tension runs through the whole corpus regarding prescribed paths: Koe both rejects "absolute advice" that relieves people of thinking for themselves and teaches step-by-step systems. His own partial resolution, stated in 2023, is that "as a beginner prescriptions are very good," and that later one must experiment and find one's own form. The training-wheels metaphor is that resolution applied to writing: the wheels are meant to come off.
