## Chapter 23 — Structure and Attention

Chapter 22 answered the question of what to write: value is ideas written through the lens of a goal or problem, the raw material is the writer's own lived problems and beliefs, interests are organized into a topic tree, ideas are hunted rather than waited for, and repetition with perspective beats the search for novelty. That answer leaves the second half of the craft untouched. An idea that is never read changes nothing, and Koe's corpus is unusually insistent on this point. "Most people don't have an idea problem," he says in 2025, "they have a structure problem." This chapter collects what he teaches about the second half: how an idea is shaped so that another mind opens it, stays with it and comes out the other side changed.

The chapter moves in six steps. Section 23.1 presents Koe's method for learning structure, which is to borrow it: templates and frameworks used as "training wheels," the distinction between structure and content that he calls "a metaphysical principle," the practice of imitating and then innovating, the pyramid principle as a "meta framework," and his answer to the recurring objection that frameworks make writing inauthentic. Section 23.2 deals with the outermost layer of any piece, the hook or title, through his metaphor of content as a gift with contents and packaging, the ghostwriter experiment in which one changed line multiplied a thread's likes two hundredfold, his PPP framework, the six building blocks of a hook, Justin Welsh's three-line hook, and the principle that "looks come before depth." Section 23.3 gathers the tools for capturing and holding attention that Koe eventually called the Ten Commandments of Engagement, together with their psychological foundation ("people remember how you make them feel") and his rules of readability. Section 23.4 steps back from tactics to the three feelings that produce engagement (education, entertainment and inspiration), the "philosophical to the tangible divide" discussed with the founders of Stan, and the conditions under which a writer earns the right to be abstract. Section 23.5 explains why story is the deepest structure of all: the mind as a sense-making machine that understands the world in stories, the curiosity loop, story as structure rather than a chain of events, and the thesis that sales is storytelling. Section 23.6 closes with the long-form structures: hook–body–conclusion, the problem → context → steps skeleton, PAS as "the micro story," BPAS, Koe's own APAG framework, cross-domain synthesis and the classic copywriting formulas.

The chapter leans on several earlier ones. The claim that a framework "orders the reader's mind" is an application of the model of psychic entropy from Chapter 5 and of attention as a scarce resource from Chapter 6. The idea of putting the reader "in flow" through a hierarchy of goals comes from the game and flow model of Chapter 10. The negativity bias that Koe exploits in his third commandment rests on the law of conceptual survival from Chapter 3. The "dopamine dealer" presupposes the distinction between cheap and earned dopamine from Chapter 11. The pain and process framework that Chapter 17 presented as the origin of "almost everything" Koe writes reappears here as one member of a family of problem-first structures, and the concept of time under attention, defined in Chapter 21, returns as the condition that allows an established writer to be pithy. The chapter prepares Chapter 24, which places these structures inside a weekly process and a content ecosystem, and Chapter 32, which develops persuasion, levels of awareness and the ethics of marketing that this chapter only touches.

### 23.1 Templates and Frameworks as Training Wheels

#### The structure problem

The section's thesis is stated most bluntly in a February 2025 video on escaping what Koe calls "beginner hell": "most people don't have an idea problem, they have a structure problem." The claim is stronger than it sounds. "Any idea can go viral if and only if you structure it the right way." Koe's reasoning is an analogy with any other achievement: just as there is a series of steps that leads to any result, "there is a series of sentences or a structure that makes any idea go viral." Generating ideas, in this view, is the easy part. He remarks to his viewers that they have "just passed by at least 50" ideas in the course of the video. The hard and interesting part is to take an idea one suspects will not do well and rewrite it until it does. Practicing that, and failing at it many times, is in his words "how you become a master creator."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The same passage answers an objection that beginners raise when they are told to work from other people's ideas: "I didn't come up with this myself." Koe's reply is that "you don't have to talk as if you came up with it, you just share the idea." The ownership of an idea, for him, matters less than the craft of presenting it, and even the worst idea can be turned into something people want, for instance by telling it as a story (the subject of Section 23.5). Chapter 22 discussed the broader question of borrowing validated ideas and the tension it creates with the demand for an original perspective; here the point is narrower. Structure is the variable a beginner controls, and it is the one most beginners neglect.

Some fifteen months earlier, in a November 2023 video on productizing one's mind, Koe had listed "not understanding structure versus content" as the second of several traps that keep people from building a knowledge business. He added a remark that he did not develop: "this goes far beyond writing content for business, it is a metaphysical principle." The transcript leaves the remark unexplained, and it would be a mistake to reconstruct a metaphysics from one sentence. What can be said is that the distinction recurs throughout the corpus in other domains (the difference between a life's structure and its events, between a game's rules and its moves, between a story's arc and its incidents, which Section 23.5 develops), so the phrase probably signals that Koe sees the separation of form from content as a general way of understanding things, not a writing trick. The practical instruction attached to it is concrete: until one's writing ability develops, take one's ideas and use "high-performing structures and frameworks as training wheels," looking at quotes, newsletter structures and post structures and plugging one's ideas into them.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

#### "The structure is not the actual words but what the words are"

Koe describes his own apprenticeship in writing as an apprenticeship in structure. In a December 2024 video for "normal people" building a one-person business, he says he did not learn to write by studying writing. He learned by studying what worked on social media, scrolling the timeline "not from the lens of consumer but a Creator." The internal monologue he reports is a procedure in miniature: "he used this as the hook, he had a bullet list of these points, then one last line that tied it all together; let me try one with my own ideas." He repeated it "over and over." He did the same with newsletters, studying other people's, and with YouTube, where he started and then noticed what he needed to improve: speaking better, adding text screens, B-roll, quotes and graphics, lessons that also shaped his newsletter.

The sentence that gives the method its precision is this one: "the structure is not the actual words but what the words are." The first line of a post is not a particular sentence; it is a hook, and the question to ask is why it is important. Then come the bullets, and then the last line. To study a post is to name the function of each part, not to memorize its wording. Koe's practical version is to copy a post into a note (in his case, into Kortex, his writing software), open it on the side, and emulate it with one's own idea. He admits it is hard at first. And he insists that this is copying the structure, not the content: "you change the idea and words completely."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The earliest version of this practice appears in his December 2022 "One Person Business Roadmap," where it is the second step of the first stage: "use content templates as training wheels." Almost every social media course, he notes, has templates for structuring posts so they get more engagement. He copies certain tweets into a note and uses their structure to write other tweets. He shows on screen an early tweet of his own that did well (its text is not read aloud, so the transcript does not preserve it) and describes its anatomy: a hook; three bullet points that hold attention while delivering actionable value; and a wrap-up conclusion naming a pain point the reader may face. He noted what worked about it and "moved in a better direction" as he learned. The passage reappears almost verbatim in his February 2024 compilation "The One-Person Business Model (Full Guide)."

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The phrase "lens of a creator, not a consumer" is the hinge of the whole method, and it connects this chapter to Chapter 16, where Koe describes the shift from consumer to researcher. In an April 2023 video on the "micro education" business model, he frames structure as the step that follows having an idea: practice articulating it "in a persuasive and structured manner that holds people's attention," which means writing it so that it illustrates or implies the reader's pains, problems and goals, and the solution between them. The steps he gives are to study top-performing tweets, deconstruct what they look like and why they work, and practice writing them with one's own idea; to study small copywriting frameworks such as PAS and AIDA "to tap into human psychology"; and, for long form, to study PASTOR or the systems of his course The Two-Hour Writer. Above all: "consume social media and marketing content from the lens of a Creator not a consumer," and practice. The justification is blunt. Ideas are impactful only if they are consumed; if one cannot structure an idea and capture attention, nobody sees it, no following forms and nobody pays.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

#### Imitate, then innovate

The principle that governs the use of borrowed structures appears in an August 2022 video and again, nearly word for word, in the 2024 compilation: "as with learning anything you have to imitate and then innovate." The best way to learn short-form writing is to read short-form content, imitate someone's tweet and use it as a **soft structure** for one's own. Koe's term is precise: a soft structure is a shape one leans on, not a mold one fills. He is explicit about the limit: "do not copy or plagiarize." One takes the structure as inspiration and makes one's own piece. Then, once one has practiced and seen what works and what does not, one starts "playing around" with the structure to find what works best for oneself. Imitation is the first phase of a sequence whose second phase is experimentation.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

A January 2024 video presents the same idea under the name **emulation**, which Koe calls "the key" to short-form writing. He learned to write good tweets through observation, and the procedure he lists is a set of habits more than a technique. Take ideas from one's own long-form writing. Study quotes from favorite authors. Use research tools such as Tweet Hunter to find high-performing posts. "Be a researcher, not a consumer": read social content to study how posts are built. For every post one sees, try to recreate it with one's own ideas, to train the mind. "Drown yourself in good short form writing." Use other writers' sentence structures "as training wheels to write your own." And contemplate why a post does well, which includes how it makes one feel and why. He adds that any part of his APAG framework (Section 23.6) can structure a tweet, an Instagram or LinkedIn post, or a reel or video script.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

**Complementary context:** Tweet Hunter is a commercial tool for X (formerly Twitter) that, among other functions, lets users search large libraries of high-engagement posts by topic. Koe mentions it as one research aid among several; nothing in the method depends on it.

#### Reverse-engineering a post

The most detailed demonstration of the method comes from the February 2025 video, where Koe dissects one of his own posts on "how to get ahead of 99%" of people and then rebuilds it around a different idea. The breakdown has four parts. First, a broad hook that names a desired outcome and contains a specific number. Second, five exaggerated, polarizing steps in a bullet list. Koe explains why bullets help: they aid readability, and they let a reader engage with any single point without accepting the rest ("don't agree, don't care, oh I love that one, like, retweet"). Third, additional context answering the objection he knew readers would raise, "I can't disappear completely, I have friends and family," which he could anticipate only because he had written enough content to know it. Fourth, a last line that ties everything together.

Then he plugs in a new idea taken from a book, "meditation is running for the mind," and rewrites the post: "How to clear your mind in 60 seconds: lock yourself in a room, sit in the corner, breathe deep into your belly, feel the breath with all senses, repeat for 1 minute; any time you get distracted refocus on the breath. Meditation sprints are how you achieve calm fast." The new post keeps the skeleton (outcome-and-number hook, steps, closing line) and changes everything else. Koe offers an alternative hook built from one of the engagement tools of Section 23.3, a negative spin: "How to destroy negative thoughts in 60 seconds." His summary of what he is doing is the clearest statement of the method in the corpus: "I'm not just copying people's tweets, I'm studying the structure, studying what works and plugging a five-word idea into it."

The method applies, he says, to anything: newsletters, landing pages, YouTube videos, reels, TikToks. The training-wheels metaphor also carries its own exit. One keeps a structure open while learning; once one can write on one's own, one takes the wheels off, "start[s] to wiggle a bit, and that's where you learn the most." The wobble after the wheels come off is not a failure of the method but its most productive phase.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

A smaller example from February 2023 shows the same move at the scale of a single sentence. One of Koe's top tweets read: "the greatest skill is decreasing the gap between idea and execution." To demonstrate plugging a new topic into a proven structure, he swaps in marketing: "the greatest skill of the 21st century is learning to capture attention," adding that "it can be better than that." The point is not the quality of the improvised line but the economy of the procedure: the structure is already laid out and validated, so testing a new topic inside it is a low-risk bet.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

A September 2024 beginner guide turns the method into an exercise for people who "still don't know how to write content." Make a list of pain points one can help solve or has had. Make a list of solutions or desired outcomes, the goals people want. Go to X, Instagram or another platform and scroll until one finds a post that did relatively well; look at how it is written; use it as a template and plug one of one's ideas into it. Koe suggests taking one of his own top posts, keeping its structure and inserting one's pain point, rewriting until the post stands alone and comes from one's own idea.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

#### The museum of structures and the 80/20 of content

Koe's later formulations turn the habit into a small system. In a January 2026 video addressed to people with multiple interests, he connects it to the **idea museum**, his name in that video for the swipe file where one notes ideas as soon as they appear. The instruction is to go through the idea museum, break down the structures of the ideas stored there, and "create a second idea museum of structures that you can use." The practice he proposes is combinatorial: take the first three ideas one notes, break down how each is structured, and then interchange them "like a 3 by 3 matrix": "take idea one, plug it into idea three structure and you practice writing like that." Three ideas and three structures yield nine exercises, and the separation of content from structure becomes a literal grid.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

A March 2026 video on building a one-person business with AI compresses everything into what Koe calls the 80/20 of content, three steps followed by practice. "First is just write down ideas like crazy: read more books, listen to podcasts, reflect on your life and try to catch ideas that are unique and beneficial." Second, "immediately think of a pain point or benefit: train your mind to think of why this idea is important to more than just yourself." Third, "save ideas that you like the structure of," keep them in a folder (in his Eden software, in that video), and as a beginner "imitate the structure or framework of these with your own ideas as the topic." Then "you simply practice writing until it becomes second nature." The second step deserves attention because it links structure to value: an idea becomes writable for others only when it is attached to a pain or a benefit that matters to someone other than the writer. This is the same condition that Chapter 22 called writing through the lens of a goal or problem, now applied at the moment of capture.

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

#### Why frameworks work: structure orders the reader's mind

Why should a borrowed structure make an idea more effective? Koe's most interesting answer connects writing to his model of the mind. In the January 2024 guide, he addresses people who say "I don't know what to write." His first question is whether they are using a framework that tells them what to write next; if not, that is "problem number one." A writing framework, he argues, leverages psychology, because frameworks "help people focus their mind." He then draws an explicit parallel with psychic entropy, the state of disorder in consciousness that Chapter 5 described as the mind's default. Just as he himself becomes overwhelmed without a project to order his mind, "if your writing doesn't have a structure that orders the reader's mind then it's not going to be good writing; they're not going to finish it; you're not putting them into the flow state; your writing isn't a game that they're trying to read."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The passage compresses several ideas from earlier in this book into one argument about the reader. Chapter 5 argued that consciousness left to itself drifts toward chaos and that goals are what impose order on it. Chapter 10 argued that a game is a structured hierarchy of goals and that flow arises when challenge matches skill. Koe's claim here is that a well-structured piece of writing performs the same function for the reader, temporarily: it gives the reader's attention a sequence of small goals (finish this line, find out the third step, reach the resolution) and so lends their mind an order it would not otherwise have. On this reading, the framework is not a constraint imposed on the writer for the market's sake; it is the mechanism by which writing does what Chapter 21 said it does, which is to order other people's consciousness. The APAG framework of Section 23.6 makes the link explicit by naming its last step "Gamify."

The claim is Koe's interpretation, built from his own frameworks, and he presents no evidence on reading behavior beyond his experience. It is nonetheless consistent: the same mechanism that explains why a person without a project feels lost explains, in his model, why a reader without a structure stops reading.

#### The standard frameworks and how to keep them open

Koe describes his own early dependence on frameworks without embarrassment. "When I first started writing, frameworks were a lifesaver," he says in February 2025; most people, by contrast, do not write according to any framework. He read the book *Cashvertising* and learned basics such as AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action). Instead of writing "willy-nilly," he recommends using AIDA, PAS and PASTOR, asking ChatGPT or Kortex's AI for "10 copywriting frameworks for short form content," and then practicing by writing a single idea in all of them. He also offers his own "pain and process" framework (developed in Chapter 17) as a free template.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Complementary context:** *Cashvertising* (2008) is a popular introduction to advertising psychology by the copywriter Drew Eric Whitman; the transcripts do not name its author. AIDA is one of the oldest models in advertising, usually traced to the American advertiser E. St. Elmo Lewis around 1900. PAS (problem, agitate or amplify, solution) and PASTOR (whose letters Koe spells out as problem, amplify, story, testimony, offer, response) are standard copywriting formulas whose authorship is diffuse; Section 23.6 discusses how Koe uses them.

The same video adds a working routine, presented as the third tip on training wheels: have the frameworks open while writing. Capture ideas throughout the week. Sit down two or three days a week for one or two hours. Open Kortex or another writing or notes application, paste the content frameworks into it, open them in a side pane, and write out the ideas while referring to them. The routine is modest by design. It is meant for beginners, and its figures (two to three days a week, one to two hours) are lower than those Koe reports for himself elsewhere, a discrepancy Chapter 24 discusses.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### The pyramid principle: a meta framework

One framework occupies a special place in Koe's teaching because he treats it as a framework for frameworks. In a September 2024 video on "follow-worthy content," he introduces the **pyramid principle** as something that "sets the scene for almost any persuasion": "think of this as a meta framework for any other framework," used as training wheels in tweets, newsletters, speaking, "everything." It has three steps. First, start with the answer, conclusion or big idea. Second, prove the conclusion with three or more key arguments. Third, support the arguments with facts, data, anecdotes, quotes and similar evidence, "from a big picture lens." His summary is a single line: "state your point, explain why and back it up."

He gives three reasons it works. The reader knows immediately whether the piece is relevant and whether to continue. The reader can follow the writer's logic to the conclusion. And the structure is simple and easy to replicate. All his videos, he says, are built this way. The video in which he explains it starts with the thesis "persuasion is the greatest skill of the 21st century" and backs it up with key points, using quotes from books, his own tweets and other people's tweets; other creators, he notes, use scientific literature. He then turns the authenticity question on himself: does using the framework make him inauthentic, or does it give him "a vessel to accurately distribute" and articulate his thoughts? He keeps the pyramid principle in his outline template as what he calls a "meta document," a reminder he sees every time he writes. And he places it in a sequence: "five levels of awareness come before the pyramid principle," meaning that before deciding what conclusion to lead with, a writer must know how aware the reader already is of the problem (a concept from direct-response marketing that Chapter 32 develops).

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

In December 2025, in a video on articulating one's thoughts, Koe returns to the pyramid principle with a more formal definition: "a communication framework that structures ideas in a hierarchical logical way to make information more palatable and persuasive." Its parts are the main idea (the key conclusion or recommendation), the key arguments (usually three to five) and the detailed evidence (data, examples, analysis). What has changed is its position. In 2025 it is no longer the meta framework underneath everything but the "intermediate" framework in a ladder of three, after PAS for beginners and before cross-domain synthesis for advanced writers (both treated in Section 23.6). And Koe now defines it by contrast with problem-first structures: "Unlike most content today that waits to give you the answer until the end… this takes an answer-first approach." He illustrates with a line from Alex Hormozi about being in a great mood, which he treats as the top of a pyramid: "you're not starting with a problem… you're starting with the idea, the conclusion, the answer." The key arguments are generated by a simple procedure: "All you have to do is ask why three to five times and come up with a compelling answer." Evidence follows: examples from one's life, data and statistics, anecdotes from clients. Once one understands these frameworks, he adds, one sees them everywhere, for instance in YouTube titles, some of which state a problem while others state a big idea.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The shift is a refinement, not a reversal, but it is worth stating precisely because Chapter 17 and Chapter 21 touched on it from other angles. In 2024 Koe presented the pyramid principle and problem-first structures such as pain and process as complementary, with the pyramid as the general scaffold. In December 2025 he ranks them: problem-first for beginners, answer-first for intermediates. In 2026, as Chapter 21 showed, he assigns the answer-first approach to the article and contrasts it with the essay, which "figures it out" rather than starting from the conclusion. The common thread is that the pyramid is a tool for persuading readers of a conclusion one already holds. It is not a tool for discovering what one thinks.

**Complementary context:** The pyramid principle was formulated by Barbara Minto, a former McKinsey consultant, in *The Minto Pyramid Principle*. Koe does not name her.

#### The authenticity objection

The most common resistance Koe meets is not technical but moral. In the September 2024 video he reports the objection as he hears it from course students, members of the Kortex community, calls and podcasts: "what if I don't want to use a framework, what if I want to be authentic and write whatever I want?" His answer is a compromise with three supporting reasons: "write what you want but organize your writing into a framework until you understand what makes writing impactful."

The first reason is that there is another person on the other side of the writing. If that person does not see the writing as valuable, and if the mind makes sense of words in a specific structure, the writer will fall short. The second is that frameworks do not hurt creativity; they enhance it. The third is the sharpest: one is already using a framework, namely one's own mind and the way one was programmed to think. It is simply unconscious, and in Koe's words "clearly unimpactful or else you would have the things you want in life." The conclusion follows with some irony: "write what you want... like you've been doing and keep getting the results you've been getting."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The third reason applies to writing the diagnosis of Chapter 1. There, Koe argued that a person who does not choose goals has goals assigned to them by conditioning. Here, a writer who refuses a conscious framework does not write without one; they write inside the unexamined one they inherited. The choice is not between structure and freedom but between a chosen structure and an inherited one. The argument also qualifies Koe's other advice, in Chapter 22, that one can write about whatever one wants: the freedom concerns the topic, while the discipline concerns the form.

Two further passages give the principle its general name. In October 2024, discussing what he called the death of the personal brand, Koe says that to set ourselves apart from the influencer and the generic content creator "we need to learn to be ourselves but in a structured way so that it actually makes sense to people," because one can be oneself with someone and they may still not like it. "Creativity is in the constraints; creativity isn't freedom and chaos, it's the limitations that you impose to create that art." In February 2025 he answers the objection that templates feel "soulless" by agreeing in part and then drawing the line: "templates and frameworks don't make you inauthentic: creativity thrives with constraints, and a framework allows you to express your authentic ideas in a way that other people care about." Being purely authentic without regard for others is great, he says, "but good luck being able to sustain that as a lifestyle that pays you." He adds that money is not the end-all but it is important, "and it's only as evil as the person who wields it," a principle Chapter 34 develops.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md; Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The coined phrase **creativity is in the constraints** (also "creativity thrives with constraints") connects the writing advice to a theme from Chapter 8, where constraints and anti-goals were tools for focusing a plan. In both cases the limit is what makes a creative act possible, because it narrows the space of options to one the mind can work within.

#### Paint between the lines: frameworks as lenses, not identities

Having defended frameworks, Koe immediately warns against holding them too tightly. In the same September 2024 video he says that one cannot become "very dogmatic" about these frameworks: "you kind of have to paint between the lines." His example is concrete. His pain and process framework consists, strictly, of a pain and a process; yet for a tweet, adding a last-line conclusion is good. To a student who thinks "I can only have the pain point and the process, I don't know if I can add another sentence," he answers: "dude come on yes you can just try one post where you're testing things out isn't going to ruin your life."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The phrase "paint between the lines" inverts the familiar "color inside the lines." The lines are there, but the painting happens in the space they leave, and a writer is allowed to blur them. The same video then generalizes the warning into a principle about all advice. Frameworks, systems and most education are training wheels, "things to experiment with." They are not meant to become an attached part of one's identity; "they should be used as lenses from which you view specific situations." Koe describes the failure pattern with an example from health. People see a novel solution, such as a keto or ancestral diet, believe it will solve their problem for good, make an identity out of it, post about it (which solidifies the identity), and eventually get hurt because they are stuck defending it. They do not realize that progress in business, health or relationships requires shedding that ideology and pursuing what is relevant to their situation now. The purpose of advice is to give one something to experiment with, to find one's own way, and to pass it down "in this continuous flow of information that helps people grow and evolve." As an educator, Koe thinks it wise to preface his own advice with an invitation to think critically, avoid dogmatism and stay open-minded; the one-person business, for example, may suit a certain kind of person, but there are other paths.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

This is a direct application of the law of conceptual survival from Chapter 3. Once a framework becomes part of the self, the mind defends it as it defends any other part of identity, and the tool that was supposed to serve a goal becomes a position to protect. Treating frameworks as lenses keeps them on the instrumental side of the line.

#### From frameworks to principles

What comes after the training wheels? The September 2024 video gives a five-step answer for learning persuasion in general: research writing and speaking frameworks; write them down next to each other; practice them in the real world; notice the patterns between them; and break the rules. In practice, Koe suggests researching and writing down five to ten frameworks to try in posts, conversations and newsletters, "then drop the frameworks and use the principles that made them work in the first place" to become a persuasive person at any moment. One can find frameworks by searching online or by asking ChatGPT for "10 copywriting/persuasion frameworks." He mentions that he created his own, APAG, the main framework of his 2 Hour Writer course, without explaining it in that video (Section 23.6 reconstructs it from another).

The fourth step carries the weight. Placing several frameworks side by side makes their shared logic visible, and what is shared is the principle. Section 23.6 shows that Koe himself performed this analysis on AIDA, PAS and PASTOR and extracted what they have in common.

The same video ends with a recap of how to start practicing persuasion, in seven steps. First, accept that one needs power and status "to break out of the hierarchy someone more powerful than you put you in." Second, use the pyramid principle as a starting point: state, argue, back up the point. Third, use a copywriting, content or persuasion framework to structure one's writing, speaking and scripts. Fourth, create an outline first, based on that framework. Fifth, have a place to capture ideas, "because the best ideas won't come when you try to brainstorm them all." Sixth, start the draft and string the ideas together. Seventh, post it, so as to get data and improve.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The first step may surprise a reader who expected a writing lesson, but it is consistent with Chapter 2, where Koe described social life as a set of dominance and attention hierarchies. Persuasion, in his framing, is the means by which a person who was placed in someone else's hierarchy acquires the standing to leave it. The remaining steps form the bridge to Chapter 24, which develops capture, outlining and drafting as a weekly process.

#### Training wheels and "resist the template"

The training-wheels position is stable from 2022 to 2025. In April 2026, however, Koe published a video urging his audience to start writing essays in which he says, among other things, "write to discover, not to perform," "resist the template... for now just write," and "build a body of work, not a content calendar." Taken alone, this looks like a reversal of everything above. Chapter 21 discussed that video in detail. Three months later, in July 2026, he is again saying that "there's journaling, and then there's writing," and that writing means capturing attention with structures that work (Section 23.2).

The two positions are compatible once their purposes are distinguished, and the corpus supports the distinction without stating it as a rule. The essay, in Koe's 2026 framing, is slow content written to change what one believes; it puts thinking first and structure later. The post is distribution, written to capture attention; it puts packaging first. Frameworks are training wheels for the second activity. The 2026 essay advice does not say that structure is unimportant; it says that, for the activity of discovering what one thinks, the template comes too early. A related tension runs through the whole corpus regarding prescribed paths: Koe both rejects "absolute advice" that relieves people of thinking for themselves and teaches step-by-step systems. His own partial resolution, stated in 2023, is that "as a beginner prescriptions are very good," and that later one must experiment and find one's own form. The training-wheels metaphor is that resolution applied to writing: the wheels are meant to come off.

### 23.2 Hooks and Packaging

#### Content is a gift: contents and packaging

Every structure in the previous section begins with the same element, the hook. Koe's most compact explanation of why it matters is a metaphor from a July 2026 video on the writing system that, in his words, "saved my brain": "writing or content is like a gift." A gift has two parts. There are the actual contents, what one wants to say, and there is the packaging, "what makes others want to open it and see what's inside," which in writing is the hook or the title. The metaphor does two jobs at once. It separates two tasks that beginners tend to merge, deciding what to say and making someone want to hear it. And it ranks them in time without ranking them in value: the packaging is seen first, but nobody keeps a gift for its wrapping.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

In the same passage Koe gives two principles for picking a topic, and the second of them is the packaging principle. The first asks: is this something I find important or worth sharing? By filtering an idea through his own perspective and "not overthinking myself to death," it becomes unique. He has a specific identity and, he says, will probably never speak to "soccer moms in their 40s" no matter how hard he tries; talking about what he finds worth sharing attracts the people he can impact most. This is the thesis of Chapter 19 (you are the niche) applied at the level of a single post. The second principle asks: is it packaged or framed to grab attention? "As a writer, that's what you do. That's your job. There's journaling, and then there's writing." One grabs attention or one's writing does not get read, and one learns to do it by studying what works on social media and applying its structure and principles to one's hook, title and structure.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

The coined contrast between **journaling and writing** is useful because it does not disparage journaling. Journaling is writing for oneself, and Koe recommends it in many other places as a tool for thinking. What distinguishes writing in his sense is that it is addressed to someone who has not yet agreed to read. The hook is the act of addressing them.

#### The ghostwriter experiment

The case Koe uses most memorably to prove the importance of the hook comes from an October 2022 video on cheap dopamine. He is friends with several Twitter ghostwriters, people who write tweets for others, "like a social media manager but for Twitter which is a writing platform." One of them had multiple clients in similar domains who talked about the same things. He copied and pasted the same body of a thread (a short-form article on Twitter) from one client to another and changed only the first tweet: the hook, "the first thing that people see, the tip of the iceberg." One version got perhaps 100 likes, because its hook was merely okay. The other got 20,000, because its hook was excellent and led people into the thread. Koe's conclusion: "the value inside does not matter until you catch attention. If you aren't playing the dopamine game then you're never going to let people see your value."

**Source:** Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md

The story is second-hand, reported from a friend, and it is a single anecdote rather than a controlled comparison: the two clients had different audiences, and the transcript does not say how large each account was. Its value is illustrative, not statistical. But it isolates the variable cleanly in the way the gift metaphor does. If the contents are held constant, the packaging alone can change the outcome by two orders of magnitude.

The phrase "the dopamine game" in that passage is worth flagging. The video in which it appears is a critique of cheap dopamine, the effortless gratification that Chapter 11 analyzes. Koe's point is not that a writer should supply cheap dopamine, but that the writer competes for attention in an environment shaped by it and must win the first second of attention to deliver anything else. Section 23.3 develops this through his figure of the "dopamine dealer."

**An unresolved tension: attention or ideas?** The ghostwriter story is one pole of a tension that runs through the corpus without a chronological resolution. In the video that tells it (October 9, 2022) Koe states the attention side bluntly: the value inside "does not matter until you catch attention." Three weeks later, in "The Rise of the Value Creator," Koe called capturing attention "the name of the game" (below; the passage reappears almost verbatim in the February 2024 compilation, which is why it can look like a later statement). Two months after that, in December 2022, he said in his one-person business roadmap that "it's not about catching attention... it's about valuable ideas." In April 2023 he said that "the ideas matter the most," not the animations or tweet structures, while crediting animations in the same passage with his Instagram jump from 250,000 to 1.4 million followers. In 2025 he warned that writers who drop their hooks lose their readers (below). Both poles, then, are already present within the last three months of 2022; there is no swing back toward attention in 2024. Koe does not explain the alternation, and he does not present any of these statements as a retraction. The pattern suggests that he stresses whichever side his audience is neglecting at the moment: ideas for those obsessed with hooks, hooks for those who think good ideas sell themselves. The gift metaphor reconciles the two, since a gift needs both a wrapping and something inside it, but the reconciliation is the reader's, not a position Koe states in those terms.

#### The PPP framework: pull, perspective, punchline

Koe's own framework for short, punchy writing dates from an August 2022 video and reappears in the 2024 compilation. It sits as the seventh step of a system for building a writing business: turn the impactful ideas of one's long-form newsletters into tweets. His favorite way to do so is what he calls "the PPP framework that I came up with," taught in what was then his new course: **pull, perspective, punchline**.

The **pull** draws the reader in with something that catches attention. There are many options, and Koe recommends studying headlines and the ways people lead into the body of a post. Among them are a statistic, a shocking fact, the word "you," or a bit of exaggeration. His example of exaggeration is "the greatest skill of the 21st century is writing," which keeps people reading because they want to know what the skill is. The underlying requirement is to understand what catches attention and what leads people into the valuable parts of a post. The **perspective** is the unique point of view, or a different way of framing the idea. The **punchline** is "something that ties it all together."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

Koe illustrates the framework with two of his own tweets, and the analyses show that the three parts are functions, not slots. The first tweet is a single sentence: "People will spend 10 hours worrying about a task that takes 10 minutes to complete." It starts with "people," which readers relate to (his gloss is "people on people — I'm going to read this"); the numbers "10 hours" and "10 minutes" catch attention by themselves; the sentence offers a unique perspective on worrying about trivial things; and the sentence is "the punchline in itself as well." One line can contain the whole framework.

The second tweet spreads the same three functions across a list: "Most people will spend eight hours on someone else's dreams, four hours hiding from their own, two hours going through the motions, 16 hours awake, 24 hours of sleep. It doesn't have to take 40 years in a retirement plan to realize that a participation trophy isn't fulfilling." (The arithmetic of the list as transcribed does not add up; the transcript may have garbled it.) "Most people will spend" is the pull; the itemized day is the perspective, a view of what people do with their time, with numbers holding attention line by line; the closing sentence about forty years and a participation trophy is the punchline. The list form shows that PPP can structure longer posts as well as one-liners.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The framework is an early, compact statement of ideas that the rest of the chapter elaborates. The pull anticipates the engagement tools of Section 23.3 (numbers, the word "you," exaggeration as conviction). The perspective anticipates the APAG framework's second step, which paints a contrasting point of view. The punchline anticipates the "last line that ties it all together" that appears in nearly every post structure Koe dissects.

#### The six building blocks of a hook

In January 2024 Koe gave the hook its most analytical treatment. Hooks, he says, are built from pieces of one's outline (the problems, benefits and experiences one has already listed), and these pieces are "the building blocks of a perfect hook and even a product," because product descriptions and landing pages must be persuasive too. The framework therefore applies to how one structures products as well as posts. The six blocks are:

| Building block | What it does |
|---|---|
| Big problem | Summarizes all the listed problems into one |
| Big benefit | Summarizes all the benefits into one |
| Big idea | States the most impactful part of the post in one sentence |
| Transformation process | Hints at the process with a number or a unique name, posing an information gap |
| Time frame | Quantifies how long it takes to read or to get the result, using numbers |
| Negative personal experience | Implies a low point and the emotion attached to it |

Not every block needs to appear in the headline. Koe recommends "sprinkling" them through the hook and the section that follows it. And he repeats the research instruction of Section 23.1 in a new form: read titles, headlines and tweets "not as a consumer, read as a researcher."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

He then analyzes one of his tweets to show how much a short hook can imply: "You won't make it without obsession. But nobody starts out obsessed. They start out curious. They experiment. They build things. They break things. They fail. And eventually they can't pull themselves away. Curiosity turns into obsession with time and persistence." The first sentence, "you won't make it without obsession," implies an end result (making it), a problem (not making it), and therefore a transformation between them. A hook can be as simple as "seven steps to avoid a life you hate." The general rule is the one Koe repeats across years: "It's always pains and benefits and a potentially unique process for bridging the gap." He also widens the definition of a hook beyond one sentence. A hook can be a whole tweet, the first slide of a carousel, or the text of a LinkedIn post that appears before the "read more" link.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The six blocks reduce, on inspection, to the triad that structures nearly all of Koe's teaching on persuasion: a problem, a benefit, and a process that connects them. The "time frame" adds the dimension of effort (how fast and how easily), and the "negative personal experience" adds the dimension of story (Section 23.5). The same triad reappears in the "Attention" step of APAG (Section 23.6), where relevance, awareness and effort are the criteria a headline must meet.

#### Meat first, then the hook: Justin Welsh's framework

A guest supplies the most practical procedure for writing hooks. In a December 2021 interview, Justin Welsh, a former SaaS executive who built a one-person business on LinkedIn and later on Twitter, describes a post-writing process whose order of composition differs from its order of reading. He writes, first, **the meat**: "what do you want to teach someone? What is the thing someone should learn from reading this post?" Usually this takes the form of numbered steps, as in a Twitter thread ("step one, step two, step three"). Second, he writes **the hook**: "how do I actually want to get people to stop and read this?" Third comes a **recap**, like a thread's TL;DR, "here's all the things you just read in a short summary," so that people who want to participate "don't have to go back up and reread everything." Fourth comes a **call to conversation**: "What would you add? Do you agree, disagree?", which invites readers into the comments. The reader encounters the parts in a different order: hook, meat, recap, call to conversation.

Welsh attaches a modest promise to the procedure: following it, "even as a first-time writer... you're going to grow slowly if you continue to be consistent." He says it translates to Twitter, where he had gained "really good traction" after posting for just five weeks.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

Within the hook, Welsh gives each of the first three lines a job. The first line is the **scroll stopper**: "how do you get people who are scrolling on their phone to stop when they read that very first line?" The second is the **validator**: the reader is "validating reading the second line that it was a good idea for me to stop after reading the first line." The third is the **hook line**, whose job is "getting them to click the see more button, which exists on LinkedIn, but does not on Twitter." After "see more," the reader is in the meat. The last step is tied to LinkedIn's truncation of long posts, but Welsh says the logic "translates well to Twitter."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

The framework is entirely Welsh's; Koe presents it as the guest's system and does not claim or modify it. It complements Koe's own approach in a specific way. Koe's six building blocks say what a hook is made of; Welsh's three lines say how a hook unfolds in time, as a sequence of small decisions the reader makes (stop, confirm, expand). And Welsh's order of composition, meat before hook, has a logic that Koe's building blocks imply but do not state: one cannot summarize a post's big problem, big benefit or big idea until the post exists.

#### "The name of the game": capturing attention without becoming a salesman

Why should a writer accept that attention-grabbing is part of the job? Koe's argument, first made in the October 2022 video on "the rise of the value creator" and repeated almost word for word in the February 2024 compilation, places it in a chain. Capturing attention is "the name of the game" in the information age, or "idea age." If one cannot stop people to read a post, they will not like it, repost it, comment, or click on the profile; and once they are on the profile, one must guide their attention further, to a lead magnet, to emails, to a product or service. Every later step depends on the first.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md (2022-10-31); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

He then answers the fear that this requires dishonesty: "you don't have to become a sleazy used car salesman to catch attention," the "but wait, there's more" style of selling. The alternative is to study psychology. He gives three examples, which Section 23.3 shows to be the original core of his Ten Commandments: problems (a relatable problem opens a curiosity gap, because people want the solution), numbers ("I spent 4,500 hours studying this so you don't have to, here's what I learned"), and statistics ("99% of people say they regretted something before they died, how do you avoid this"). It all takes practice, and he recommends studying sales and persuasive copywriting.

#### Looks come before depth

The deepest version of the argument for packaging is not about writing at all. In a March 2024 video on "the value equation" and high status, Koe formulates a principle he calls **looks come before depth**. A nice car can lead a billion-dollar founder to approach a person and ask what they do, opening a world of opportunity; a fit body gets someone in the door with a partner who values health, strength and discipline. In relationships, nobody cares about one's personality before talking to one: looks attract, then the other person meets the depth, and then the depth offers opportunities. Many people close their minds to material or superficial pursuits without realizing that these are only the starting point.

Koe singles out a recurring kind of post by successful people: "I used to have the nice watch, the nice car," followed by a renunciation of that part of their journey. They do not realize, he argues, that they can give that lesson only because they pursued those things. He cites a tweet by the marketer Neil Patel that ended "do what you want, buy them, but learn from your mistakes": many superficial pursuits will be mistakes, but mistakes allow corrections, and corrections get one where one wants to go. The image that sums up the principle is geological: "You don't start digging from the core... you start digging from the surface; the material is a portal into the immaterial." Becoming valuable, in this framing, means increasing the impact of both looks and depth, not choosing between them.

**Source:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md

The application to content follows directly. People think YouTube titles should not be clickbaity or lean into human nature. But if ethical creators refuse to do so, "the unethical businesses are going to rise to the top without competition." Koe says he could and should be clickbaity, though less so than others, because the game is to introduce people to the depth, to content he believes is more impactful, and nobody watches it unless he first grabs attention with looks. His observation about how titles are perceived is telling: "You only care about the looks until you are exposed to the depth." Those who see only the title comment "clickbait" without watching; those who watch do not remember the title, only that the video taught them a lot. The lesson he draws is a slogan: "don't hate the game, don't hate the player, master them both." He closes with a definition that links the passage to Chapter 10: "Status, as far as I'm concerned, is your experience level in the game of life."

**Source:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md

#### Judging clickbait by the overarching narrative

In October 2024 Koe states the same defense in a more analytical form. People assume that clickbait hooks are all bad, which, he says, is not the case: "one stock can be down while the index fund is up." The analogy from investing makes a point about levels of evaluation. A single component can be poor while the whole performs well, and it is the whole one should judge. If a hook is shallow and clickbaity but the content as a whole expands someone's mind and takes them beyond the hook, then "the overarching narrative of the thing you're trying to label good or bad is good."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The criterion of the **overarching narrative** is the ethical boundary of the packaging principle. It licenses a superficial hook only on condition that the depth it leads to is real. A clickbait title on an empty video fails the test, because the index fund is down as well as the stock. This condition becomes important in the light of a later shift in Koe's thinking, discussed at the end of Section 23.4: in 2026 he criticizes engagement-optimized "fast content" in strong terms, without retracting his defense of the hook as a door into a deeper piece.

#### Bake your voice into the principles

The last passage of this section addresses writers who learned the fundamentals and then abandoned them. In a March 2025 video on strategic thinking, Koe describes a failure pattern he sees often. Writers start by following the fundamentals and principles of good writing and then try to do it "in their own voice and their own way." He has no objection to that: "There's nothing wrong with that — you just have to bake your own voice into the principles." The failure lies in the sequence that follows when the principles are dropped. One stopped writing compelling hooks; people stopped paying attention; people stopped caring; and then one got angry and concluded, "okay well people only like it when I'm this attention-grabbing person." His answer is short: "You have to grab attention."

**Source:** How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md

The phrase **bake your own voice into the principles** completes the argument of Section 23.1 about authenticity. There, the framework was presented as a vessel for authentic ideas; here, the voice is presented as an ingredient of the principles rather than a replacement for them. Voice and hook are not alternatives. The writer who drops hooks in the name of voice does not become more authentic; they become unread, and then they misread their own failure as the audience's shallowness.

### 23.3 The Ten Commandments of Engagement

#### "People remember how you make them feel"

Before listing tools, Koe states the principle they serve. In a November 2023 video on what he called the most important high-income skill of the next ten years, he opens with a saying he considers "worth repeating" and returns to later in the same video as the root of engagement: "People don't remember what you say, they remember how you make them feel." His explanation is anthropological. Humans are emotional, not logical, and "99% of the population have not developed themselves to the point of being free from or in control of their ego." That, he says, is "the foundation of capturing and holding attention": **poking your reader's ego**. One can feed the ego, making the reader feel good, or starve it, making them feel bad. And if one can take people on an emotional journey (love, hate, excitement, deceit, calmness, any emotion) that eventually ends in a benefit for their life, "you have mastered the art of content creation."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

**Complementary context:** The saying is widely attributed to the American poet Maya Angelou, though its exact origin is disputed. Koe uses it without attribution.

The principle rests on the model of identity from Chapters 3 and 4. If the ego is a network of ideas that the mind defends as if it were a body, then content that touches the ego, by confirming or threatening it, registers with the force of something that concerns survival. Koe's figure of "99%" is rhetorical rather than measured, and the claim that most people are not free from their ego is a premise of his developmental model (Chapter 38), not an empirical finding he reports. But the operative instruction does not depend on the number: content is remembered for the feeling it produces, and the feeling arises where the content meets the reader's sense of self. The condition attached to the emotional journey is also important. It must "eventually end in a benefit." The principle does not license provocation for its own sake.

#### The creator as a dopamine dealer

Koe gives the writer who works with this principle a provocative name. In the November 2023 video on productizing one's mind, the third trap he lists is that people do not research or use high-performing angles. "Any idea can go viral but only when it is positioned in an attention-grabbing way that entertains, educates or inspires. You are a **dopamine dealer**." He anticipates the objection that dopamine is bad. If it is used for mindless pleasure and entertainment without action, he concedes, perhaps; "but that's not what educators do; learning and dopamine associated with that is good dopamine." One raises readers' dopamine by teaching them something new, giving them a new point of view, entertaining them, or showing them that they are capable of more, which sparks behavior change. The practical instruction is to study the most popular posts, Instagram posts and YouTube videos of one's favorite accounts and to "start programming your mind to think in high performing headlines."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

In February 2025 the figure returns as a diagnosis of beginner hell. The second step out of it, Koe says, is to admit that "your content just isn't that good": if one is not growing, one is doing something wrong, and once one is honest about that one can change it. One cannot simply pick a topic and write; one must account for the reader's psychology. "You need to become a dopamine dealer: you need to capture attention, hold attention and deliver value." Most people think they do this but do not; they post without asking whether the post is good. And each of the three tasks, he says, "require[s] a lifetime of study within themselves."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The three tasks of the dopamine dealer organize the rest of this chapter. Capturing attention is the work of hooks and of the commandments below. Holding attention is the work of readability, story and the curiosity loop (this section and Section 23.5). Delivering value is the work of the long-form structures and of the three feelings of Section 23.4. The term itself is a deliberate provocation that depends on Chapter 11's distinction between cheap dopamine, which rewards consumption without effort, and earned or "good" dopamine, which in Koe's vocabulary accompanies learning, solving the right problems and making progress toward a meaningful goal. The dopamine dealer of this chapter is supposed to sell the second kind while competing in a market built for the first.

#### Separating the message from the messenger: Alan Watts

The November 2023 video adds a reflection on the writer's place in the exchange, through the example of the British-born philosopher Alan Watts, whose recorded lectures Koe listens to. Watts, Koe says, "may have been an alcoholic in his later years and died because of it," though someone in his Art of Focus community suggested that this might be a "scop" (the word as transcribed; its meaning is unclear, possibly a garbling of "psyop" or "scam"). "Who knows." Regardless, "his work has changed my life for good," and Koe will not deny himself Watts's message because the man may have held values unlike his own, "when the message can truly change the way I see the world." He draws a general lesson about consumption: refusing to watch people one dislikes, or creators who use clickbait titles and thumbnails, may mean missing "the one idea" that would radically change one's life.

He then turns the observation back on the principle that people remember feelings. He remembers some of Watts's quotes because of the clarity they gave him, "the feeling of having zero worries in the world." When he wants that feeling again, he puts on a Watts lecture. The lesson for the writer is a statement of modesty that coexists, in the same video, with the aggressive language of dopamine dealing: "you are but a humble messenger of a life-changing message."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

**Complementary context:** Alan Watts (1915–1973) was a writer and lecturer who popularized Zen Buddhism and other Asian philosophies for Western audiences. His heavy drinking in his later years is documented in biographies; Koe's hedging reflects his uncertainty, not a dispute in the historical record.

The passage matters for the structure of the chapter because it explains what the reader actually takes away. The reader does not keep the writer's words, nor necessarily the writer's name; the reader keeps a state of mind that the words produced, and returns to the source to reproduce it. Engagement, on this account, is not a transaction around information but around feeling, and the creator's role is to carry the message well, not to be its object.

#### From three hooks to ten commandments

Koe's checklists for capturing attention grew over three years, and the growth is itself instructive. The earliest version, from an October 2022 video on "the rise of the value creator," names three psychological hooks.

The first is **problems**. Marketing, copywriting, branding, human behavior and stories all revolve around problems. Starting with a relatable problem ("if you struggle to grow your business") opens a curiosity gap: the reader wants the solution. Koe calls this "the duality... of problems and benefits": what is the problem, how does one overcome it, and what are the benefits. The second is **numbers**, as in "I spent 4,500 hours studying this so you don't have to, here's what I learned"; on text-heavy or image-heavy platforms, numbers act as a pattern interrupt. The third is **statistics**, as in "99% of people say they regretted something before they died, how do you avoid this," which leads into something else. All three take practice.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

In July 2023 the same triad reappears with a reading recommendation and a framing sentence: "Attention is the currency of the 21st century and ideas are how you capture attention." Koe recommends Robert Cialdini's *Influence*, or Cialdini's courses. His examples change slightly. Every good story starts with a problem the reader faces, opening a curiosity loop, and "problems are at the root of human behavior." Specific numbers are a pattern interrupt while scrolling: "I read 347 self-help books so you don't have to." Statistics set the scene: "97% of the population has seen a decline in focus, here's how to fix yours." He admits that the examples "can use a bit of work but you see the power in it."

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

**Complementary context:** Robert Cialdini is an American social psychologist whose *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (1984) describes principles such as reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity. Koe's triad is not one of Cialdini's lists; he recommends the book as background for understanding why such hooks work.

In October 2023, in a video on building an audience from zero followers, Koe used a list of six elements of persuasive content (hook, big problem, clear solution, big benefit, confidence or polarization, novel perspective), of which a post should hit three or four (**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md, 2023-10-15). Then, in the November 2023 video, he introduced the **10 Commandments of Engagement**, and in February 2025 he presented a revised version under the name **Ten Commandments of Engagement**, also calling them "the 10 commandments of impactful writing." He gives no reason for the expansion or the renaming. The original triad survives inside both lists: numbers and statistics become the first commandment, problems the fifth.

The two versions of the ten differ in only one item, and the differences in emphasis are clearer side by side.

| # | November 2023 | February 2025 | Mechanism Koe attributes to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Specific numbers | Specific numbers | Numbers stop the scroll; the more specific, the stronger |
| 2 | Pattern interrupts | Pattern interrupts | Breaks the reader's conditioned scrolling pattern |
| 3 | Negativity bias | Negativity bias | The brain attends to threats; identity threat registers as survival threat |
| 4 | Group call-out | Target call-out | Readers pick a side and compare themselves |
| 5 | Problem call-out | Problem call-out | Brings subconscious pains into conscious awareness |
| 6 | Potential benefit | Potential benefit | Opens a curiosity gap between now and after |
| 7 | Social proof | Social proof | Implies an information gap; works best as a "humble flex" |
| 8 | Confidence and conviction | Confidence and conviction | Energy; "metaphorically impactful, not factually correct" |
| 9 | Active voice | Punch, rhythm and readability | Anticipation (2023); guidance down the page (2025) |
| 10 | Warnings and cautionary advice | Warnings or cautionary advice | Positions the writer as someone who knows the traps |

In both versions Koe insists on the same mode of use. The commandments are "tools like in your tool box," pieced together into something persuasive and attention-grabbing; most of them, he notes in 2023, are themselves pattern interrupts. In 2023 he names the eighth, confidence and conviction, as the most potent; in 2025 he calls it his favorite. The 2023 video was accompanied by a free PDF containing the commandments and thirty-two post structures to emulate. In 2025 he explains their purpose with an image of the feed: on social media one competes with memes and attractive people, and "there is something I can write... that will stop their scroll and deliver something valuable — that's the creative challenge." The commandments are "little things to sprinkle" in content to capture or hold attention. One does not use all ten at once. One keeps the list open while writing and then refers back to it with a single question: "how can I make this better?" They come from his 2 Hour Writer course.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md; Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### Commandments 1 and 2: specific numbers and pattern interrupts

The first commandment is to use **specific numbers** in hooks, headlines and tweets, because numbers make people stop to see what they relate to. The 2023 version distinguishes four forms: statistics ("there are 7 billion people on this Earth"), dollar amounts ("Apple's new $1,175 iPhone has this new feature"), metrics ("I sent 322 cold emails," "after 293 days...") and lists ("seven bad habits that are preventing you from..."). "The more specific a number is, the more it catches people's attention." Koe immediately relieves the pressure: if one cannot make a number very specific, one should not stress, because it is one tactic among many. His example is the hook of a thread that led many people to join his course Digital Economics: "How I turn one piece of content per week into a $45,2[xx]/month creative income and 340,000 followers in 2 hours a day (in five simple steps)." The exact dollar figure is garbled in the transcript. The hook combines several commandments at once: specific metrics, a time frame, a list.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The second commandment is the **pattern interrupt**, which Koe defines as "something that breaks people out of their normal conditioned patterns." In 2023 his example is a feed full of political commentary, in which a well-formatted list tweet will make the scrolling reader stop. In 2025 he adapts the idea to each platform: on X people see blocks of tweets, on Instagram they see people talking to the camera, and one needs something different that breaks "their conditioned process." That can be a visual interrupt in video ("something on the screen that makes them be like, what the heck just went on"), and the specific number itself is a pattern interrupt because most people do not use one.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md; Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The term "pattern interrupt" is a common phrase that Koe uses in a specific sense, and the sense depends on Chapter 1. Scrolling is a conditioned behavior, a loop that runs on autopilot. The pattern interrupt is the writer's way of momentarily breaking the autopilot, which is why Koe can say that most of the commandments are pattern interrupts: each one introduces something the scrolling mind did not predict.

#### Commandment 3: negativity bias

"The human brain is wired to notice and pay attention to negativity." Koe's 2023 examples are everyday: it is why people get trapped in comment sections or in drama on Reddit, and why they watch scary movies on Halloween (the video was recorded on Halloween). The likely reason, he says, is survival: we attend to threats. Then he adds an explanation that ties the commandment to his own theory of identity: "since humans survive on a conceptual level," surviving the identity they attach to and consider who they are ("which we're not, an idea, but that's what we do"), when that idea of who they are is threatened, they feel threatened. He notes that he discusses this in his book. The tactic is to spin a positive message as a negative. Instead of "you're going to achieve great things," write "you will never hit rock bottom again." The message is the same, but the second version is more potent. And he sets a limit: this does not mean making a whole video negative. It is a way to catch attention so that people see the value of something "overwhelmingly positive."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The 2025 version keeps the same example and sharpens the contrast: the first framing gets "okay cool, thank you," while the second is "a lot more potent," though only the framing has switched. It also adds "remember" to the definition: the brain is wired "to pay attention to and remember negativity." Again, Koe stresses that this does not mean being negative; it means trying to spin some writing in a negative light. The "destroy negative thoughts in 60 seconds" hook from Section 23.1 is an application.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Complementary context:** "Negativity bias" is an established term in psychology for the tendency of negative events and information to weigh more heavily on attention, memory and judgment than comparable positive ones. Koe's explanation through conceptual survival is his own extension of it, drawing on the law he formulated (Chapter 3), not a finding of that literature.

#### Commandment 4: group call-out or target call-out

The fourth commandment is to call out the specific person one is talking to: "if you're in your 20s," "calling all creators, coaches and freelancers," "fathers are a gift to humankind." In 2023 Koe makes a subtle point about who responds. Even readers who are not in the named group can pick a side and compare themselves. His example tweet: "A bodybuilder is happier than the average person when eating bland chicken and rice every day because he is progressing towards a meaningful goal. Things that are often boring and monotonous become enjoyable when you have a reason to pursue fulfillment over pleasure." Non-bodybuilders still relate. A post about fathers still reaches Koe, who is not a father but might be one day, while attracting more fathers. His conclusion introduces a coined phrase: "when you niche down smart you're not necessarily alienating people... you're just making what you say more potent." The tactic is especially powerful when promoting: call out who one's product is for.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

In 2025 he renames it **target call-out** and adds that it need not be hyper-specific. A call-out like "calling all coaches that sell a $10,000 offer with a lead generation system making $50,000 a month" is possible, but so is "if you're in your 20s," "calling all creators, creatives, musicians," or simply talking about a group, as in "fathers are a gift to mankind," which can become "mothers are..." The observation he adds is about the idea itself: one idea told to a different group "takes a completely new shape."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The commandment is the writing-level version of the principle "broad catch, specific sale" from Chapter 19. A group call-out narrows the address of a single post without narrowing the brand. In 2026 Koe goes further and recommends explicitly naming whom a product is *not* for, because exclusion creates belonging; the 2023 language of "not alienating" people and the 2026 language of excluding them are different emphases, probably suited to different moments (attracting a group versus deepening an identity already reflected).

#### Commandment 5: problem call-out

The fifth commandment, **problem call-out**, is the one Koe ties most directly to his theory of persuasion. He is, by his own description, "so big on pains and benefits" that his students remind themselves of the phrase. Talking about people's pains makes them aware. People do not walk around with their problems top of mind; they are distracted, scrolling. If they are not getting anywhere, the problem sits lower, in the subconscious, and the writer's job is to bring it into the conscious mind.

Koe then connects the commandment to what marketers call the levels of awareness. To sell or to catch attention, one hits on a problem, makes people aware of it, then of a solution, then of how the solution benefits their lives, whether over a landing page, a year of content or a single video, until they are ready to buy. "You create customers by educating them on their problems, how to solve it, and you providing a better solution, or a more streamlined, efficient and aggregated solution." His example is a tweet he curated for its high engagement: "You feel terrible because your subconscious knows that you could be doing better." Turned into a thread, it got about 20,000 likes.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The 2025 version is a single instruction, "use more pain points," but its brevity reflects how thoroughly the principle has spread through the rest of the system: by then, Koe says elsewhere, eighty percent of a beginner's content should start with a pain point.

**Complementary context:** The "levels of awareness" (usually given as unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware and most aware) come from Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966). Koe does not name the source here; Chapter 32 discusses how he adapts the model.

#### Commandment 6: potential benefit

The sixth commandment is the mirror of the fifth. In 2023 Koe notes that problem call-out is more potent, because of the negativity bias, but that both are needed. When one sells a transformation, one describes where people are now and where they will be: "the curiosity gap between those two makes them want to learn more from you on how to achieve that transformation." His example is a YouTube thumbnail with before-and-after fitness pictures, which the viewer reads as "how did he achieve that, I relate, I want that potential benefit, I'm going to watch."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

In 2025 the commandment acquires a general principle: "all good content implies a transformation, a story." What is the benefit of overcoming the pain point? Content must answer "what's in it for me" quickly, or people will not pay attention, and Koe adds a nuance about degree: "it's not zero or one, it's in between." Content can answer the question more or less fully and more or less fast.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The sentence "all good content implies a transformation" is one of the bridges of this chapter. It makes the potential benefit a property of the whole piece, not just of its hook, and it anticipates Section 23.5, where Koe argues that a transformation is a story and that sales is storytelling.

#### Commandment 7: social proof and the humble flex

Social proof, in Koe's account, is authoritative and implies an information or curiosity gap. When one flexes results or credentials, people assume one knows more than they do, take one more seriously, and read on to find the missing information. But "this works 100 times better when it's not viewed as a flex." Cocky titles ("I made a billion dollars in a day, here's how") trigger a negative feeling. Koe calls Justin Welsh "in my eyes the current king" of the **humble flex**, and in 2023 cites one of his posts: "Sometimes you need to ask people to buy your product. If you don't, you're missing out on 12x days. Simple as that," accompanied by a screenshot of his revenue. The proof illustrates a point instead of being the point.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The 2025 version returns to the same post, now describing the screenshot as showing about $468,000 in a month and the framing as "my little one-person business hit this revenue goal." People see Welsh as an authority, yet he comes across as humble. Koe then gives the commandment a new rationale tied to the age of AI: talk about your results "because AI can't compete with results." AI might tell someone how to lose twenty pounds, but it cannot lose them for him; the person who did it can say so. And results need not be large. One can share small goals, such as finishing a first chapter or reading five pages, because one is "not trying to prove anything, just trying to show that you're doing something because most people don't."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The 2025 addition shifts the commandment's meaning. In 2023 social proof is a status signal that must be softened; in 2025 it is also evidence of action, available to beginners, and a form of differentiation from machine-generated content. Chapter 36 develops the larger argument about what AI cannot replace.

#### Commandment 8: confidence and conviction

The eighth commandment is the one Koe calls the most potent (2023) and his favorite (2025). The 2025 passage explains it at length. Many people refuse to write with conviction because "nothing's black or white, nothing's absolute"; Koe's mocking gloss is "you just read a book on metaphysics." His answer introduces a coined phrase: "You're not doing this to be factually correct, you're doing this to be **metaphorically impactful**." Writing is not about the literal words but about "the energy," the point people reach in their own minds. The method has four parts: eliminate words implying uncertainty; speak in absolutes when possible; exaggerate one's point to add energy; and "do not abuse these for the sake of engagement." His example is a rewrite. "It may be wise if some people develop their skill set" becomes "it is crucial that everyone on planet Earth develops their skill set," which, he says, is "pretty true most of the time."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The commandment is easy to misread as license for overstatement, and Koe's own limit ("do not abuse these") shows he is aware of the risk. The distinction he draws is between the literal claim and the conclusion the reader reaches. A hedged sentence gives the reader permission to dismiss the point; a confident one transfers the energy needed to act on it. Whether the reader's resulting belief is accurate depends on whether the exaggeration points in a true direction, which is what Koe's "pretty true most of the time" concedes. Chapter 22 treated conviction as a stance from which one writes (picking a side, authentic polarization); here it appears as a tool applied sentence by sentence. In the 2023 six-element list it appeared as "confidence or polarization," and the move to "confidence and conviction" and "metaphorically impactful" is a renaming that shifts the emphasis from provoking disagreement to transmitting energy.

#### Commandment 9: active voice, or punch, rhythm and readability

The ninth commandment is the one that changed between versions. In 2023 it is **active voice**. "Active voice implies that there is a story; it eases people into what you were saying." Passive voice is often boring and "gives away the mini story too early": it removes the anticipation that makes people want to know what happens next. Koe's practical advice is to look up an article on active voice and use it to edit one's posts until one gets the hang of it. Active voice also helps one be perceived as confident, which links the ninth commandment to the eighth.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

**Complementary context:** In the active voice the subject performs the action ("I sent 322 cold emails"); in the passive voice the subject receives it ("322 cold emails were sent"). Koe's claim that active voice "implies a story" rests on the fact that it puts an agent at the front of the sentence, and a story needs an agent.

In 2025 the ninth commandment becomes **punch, rhythm and readability**. Start short and get into the point, then continue the idea. Use line breaks. People love bullet points. Then Koe points to his own on-screen text: "do you notice how I'm guiding you down the page?" Start short, keep the reader engaged, deliver a hint of value. And he adds a precise micro-rule: order bullet lists from the shortest point to the longest.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The replacement is less a change of mind than a change of level. Active voice is a property of sentences; punch, rhythm and readability are properties of a page. The 2023 video had already covered readability separately (below), so the 2025 list absorbs into a commandment what had earlier been a separate lesson.

#### Commandment 10: warnings and cautionary advice

The last commandment asks what people should look out for when trying to achieve something, "preferably within your brand or worldview." In 2023 Koe grounds it in the idea that a brand has goals: a product or service leads people toward a goal, and "you wouldn't attract followers if you weren't a leader." Can one warn followers about something they will encounter as they try to reach where one is? His example is a tweet that hit a high-engagement topic (dopamine) while warning about another hot topic (goals): "Be careful telling people about your goals. It releases dopamine similar to achieving them. Skip the instant gratification. Go quiet and build." It sparked controversy.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

In 2025 he uses the same post and notes that the idea was taken from a book he does not name. The structure of the post is the lesson: it starts by warning about a trap, then explains why. And he gives the commandment a general justification: "every topic has an entire list of traps that people can fall into," so giving warnings positions the writer as someone who knows what they are talking about.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### Readability: "we aren't in high school anymore"

The 2023 video devotes a separate passage to readability, which the 2025 list later folded into the ninth commandment. "We aren't in high school anymore," Koe says: long, professional paragraphs reduce readability, especially on X, where "people log on to that platform to escape the professional world." He gives four techniques. First, bullets: numbers, lists and other devices that break apart what one says, noting that lists "create a story in themselves." Second, line breaks whenever possible, but not for their own sake; they should be used creatively to emphasize specific sentences and help the flow. Third, start short and concise: shorter sentences and paragraphs at the start, capture attention with short, punchy statements, and then "let loose." Fourth, break sentences apart with parentheses and em dashes instead of commas. His illustration is self-referential: "this sentence is too long (until I make people want to know what's in these parentheses)." He uses parentheses constantly, he says, even in YouTube titles. His example hook: "Most people suck at DMs. 'Hi how are you, please follow back' will probably get you blocked more than it gets responses. Let me make DMs easy for you in seven steps." He remarks that "(in seven steps)" could itself be put in parentheses.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

A December 2023 video on the Build Teach Earn method lists writing structure as the fourth element of content and explains why the medium changes the rules. One can have the greatest ideas, but one must pay attention to where one posts them: in a book, people accept blocks of text; on social media, they need to be guided or they will scroll past. Hence line breaks between sentences, and the study of high-performing structures: popular X and Instagram posts, YouTube titles, article headlines. Koe recommends writing more lists, and his reasons explain why lists perform on social media. A bullet list opens a curiosity loop. People can like, share, follow or comment on the basis of any single bullet. Lists force each bullet to be condensed and impactful. And people add their own thoughts or nuance in the comments, which boosts reach. "Anything can be turned into a list": take an idea and turn it into a list of lessons, benefits, pains or steps. The general structure of a post is a hook, a body (the list of reasons, benefits, lessons or pains) and a conclusion, which is the skeleton Section 23.6 develops for long form.

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

Readability belongs to the second task of the dopamine dealer, holding attention. Its rules are not aesthetic preferences but applications of the same psychology as the hook. Each short line is a small, achievable goal for the eye; each bullet is a self-contained reward; each parenthesis opens a micro-loop that the next words close. A page built this way is, in miniature, the game that Section 23.1 said good writing should be.

### 23.4 Educate, Entertain, Inspire

#### Three feelings that create engagement

The commandments are tools for the first second of attention. The question of what a reader actually gets in exchange for that attention belongs to a different level, and Koe answers it with a triad that he presents in several forms between 2023 and 2024: education, entertainment and inspiration. The fullest version appears in the November 2023 video, right after the principle that people remember how one makes them feel. What creates engagement, he says, are three feelings.

The first is **education**: learning something new or gaining clarity, which raises dopamine. One creates clarity through novel perspectives, metaphors, stories and personal experiences; he notes that these are the elements his Cortex software (spelled Kortex elsewhere in the corpus, and not yet released at the time) was designed to work with, software he hoped would "revolutionize writing and creating for the entire creator economy." His definition of the educator's job is disarmingly simple: "Your job is to make sense to a specific group of people. You do this by sharing how certain topics make sense to you. That's it."

The second is **inspiration**, which Koe carefully distinguishes from motivation. Inspiration helps people connect the dots on their own, giving them the clarity to make their own decisions and change their behavior. The mechanism he describes is psychological and slightly paradoxical: "people feel good when they think they came up with the answer themselves, but will still attribute that feeling to you." Motivation, by implication, pushes from outside; inspiration arranges the conditions for the reader to reach the conclusion, and the reader then credits the writer with the experience of reaching it.

The third is **entertainment**: a joke, a meme, an interesting point of view, or the emotional texture of a film, with its humor, drama, emotional moments and battles that spark excitement. "Entertainment, education and inspiration are the main goals of your content," and a content creator is "a mixture of an entertainer, an educator and an inspiring person."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The distinction between inspiration and motivation deserves emphasis because it governs how Koe writes. Much of his content does not tell readers what to do; it reframes a situation and leaves the decision to them. The 2023 passage gives the reason: a conclusion reached by the reader is more durable and more pleasurable than one handed over, and the reader's gratitude flows to the source anyway. The same logic appears in Chapter 17, where Koe advises against taking any advice as law, and in the "make tangibility tangible" heuristic below, where the reader is invited to experiment rather than obey.

A January 2023 passage adds a qualification that prevents the triad from collapsing into usefulness. "Value does not always mean actionable; value can be as simple as entertainment." Netflix has value to many people because it offers an escape from reality (about which, Koe adds, there are different interpretations). And entertaining does not necessarily mean humorous or mind-numbing: including a quote or an interesting fact in a video keeps attention because it is entertaining and introduces something new; "it gives you a novel idea."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

This passage widens the concept of value that Chapter 22 defined as ideas through the lens of a goal. Novelty itself is a form of value, because it gives the mind something to process. It also explains why a quote or a fact from another domain can hold attention in an otherwise practical piece, which is the principle behind the cross-domain synthesis of Section 23.6.

#### The holistic creator: blending is what makes you the niche

The earliest formulation of the triad, from April 2023, presents it as three "pillars" a creator banks on: inspiration, education and entertainment. Some creators rely on one alone. MrBeast, in Koe's example, is solely entertainment; a "millionaire motivation" theme page is solely inspiration. Koe prefers what he calls "the holistic approach": encompassing all three in the message one puts out, in order to attract the audience one wants.

He says he did this "unconsciously" and had to reflect to see how. He entertains with his philosophical musings and his takes on evolutionary psychology. He inspires with his personal experience and the story of building businesses. He educates with what he learned along the way, through the courses he bought, the things he did, and what he enjoys studying. His conclusion connects the triad to the thesis of Chapter 19: "the usage of all three of these or the blending of these is what makes you the niche," or, in another phrase from the same passage, "you are the most profitable niche."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The example he offers of a creator who blends all three is the podcaster Joe Rogan, who "just is himself": "he educates people on what he knows, he inspires people with his story, and he entertains people just with his humor." The point of the example is that blending does not require a strategy for each pillar. A person who shares what they know, what happened to them and how they see things already covers all three.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The argument explains why the triad belongs in a chapter on structure. A piece that only educates competes with every other source of the same information; a piece that only entertains or only inspires has no durable claim on the reader once the feeling fades. The blend is specific to a person, because nobody else has the same mix of knowledge, story and sensibility, and so it is the blend, not any one pillar, that differentiates.

#### Three functions of the value creator, and why nobody follows company accounts

In May 2024 Koe restated the triad as three functions, each with a specific role, performed by the figure he calls the **value creator** (developed in Chapters 18 and 35). **Education**: "you teach your interest in a way that helps people achieve a goal toward your vision." **Entertainment**: "throw in a dash of personality to attract people like you, the people you can help the most, because you are the niche." **Inspiration**: "show people what you've built so they can see what's possible."

**Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md

Compared with the 2023 versions, these definitions are tied more tightly to the creator's own project. Education serves a goal "toward your vision," so it is not neutral information but a step on the creator's path. Entertainment is a filter: personality attracts people similar to the creator, who are the people the creator can help most. Inspiration is evidence: what one has built shows what is possible. The triad has become a description of how a personal brand works.

An August 2024 video uses the triad to explain why corporate social media fails. Some brands post only images of their product or updates, which is common with software. Some people follow for updates, Koe concedes, "but you're not really attracting any new customers that way," and it is a boring way to run social media: "if that's your life's work, why is it not better?" Other brands make a valuable post and then "can't help but just throw a promotion in there." Koe recalls, without naming the brand, a good post followed by an unrelated line celebrating "the best American business there is on the planet" and a supplement plug: "you would have probably made more sales if you just didn't include that." He admits, in the same breath, that he does not actually know. His diagnosis: "Most of these brands just don't show that they're a human. They never educate, entertain, or inspire... They only sell. Nobody follows company accounts. People follow educators, entertainers, and inspirers."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

He follows the diagnosis with a case. "Red Bull has a massive following because they don't have a single picture of their product on their Instagram. It's all inspirational content about the lifestyle people are living because of Red Bull." The lesson he draws is general: "If all you do is talk about your product, that is a great way to never grow your brand, have engaged readers, or see exponential growth in revenue." And: "a personal brand is the most potent traffic source for your product, service, or brand in general."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

Koe's description of Red Bull's Instagram is a generalization from his observation; the transcript does not document the account. Its use in the argument is as an illustration of a company behaving like an inspirer rather than a seller.

A November 2023 passage makes the same diagnosis of individual marketers. Marketers try to build an audience on social media, but their page is just promotions: "they're just writing ads the entire time. That doesn't work." The remedy is education within a broad personal brand: "You have to focus on education. You have to create customers by having a broad personal brand where you are the niche, build trust and educate them over time, and then it compounds."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The phrase "create customers by educating them" recurs in the problem call-out of Section 23.3 and in Chapter 22's discussion of making interests interesting. It is the link between content and sales that Section 23.5 develops under the thesis that "90% of your selling is done through content."

#### The philosophical to the tangible divide

A January 2025 interview with John Hugh and Vitali, the co-founders of the creator platform Stan, adds a guest perspective that pushes against one of Koe's own tendencies. Koe raises the problem himself. Many people in his audience are drawn to philosophy and abstract, high-level topics, and when they start posting about business, "it sounds like a philosopher speaking, it sounds like a philosophical quote." He does not think such posts are bad, "but I also don't think they lead to sales or something that will allow you to sustain that thing." He asks his guests what mental checklist a beginner should use to know whether a piece will perform, since beginners "don't know what well is."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

John Hugh's answer is that performance "comes down to how much your content can make someone feel something," a claim that converges with Koe's own principle from Section 23.3. Hugh admits he historically approached content "from a very cerebral lens," and he builds his answer as a chain. Consider the base case: human beings scrolling a feed. The lowest common denominator is that "we're literally animals, we're like meat boxes, and we feel emotions." Put yourself in the audience's shoes after a long day of work. In the algorithm, "you're competing with naked hot people and funny dumb memes," or awesome food, or a good-looking person. So the question is "what are you going to do to emotionally or personally hook me in?" Purely philosophical content "takes more work" to hook people. Hugh adds a condition: one should never be inauthentic to one's style, but "it's up to the artist and the entrepreneur to decide how much they want to play the market game."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

His recommendation for beginners is to "try to be as tangible and concrete as possible," or, in a better framing, to ask "how can I make this as relatable as possible." He learned it from obsessively studying Red Bull and talking with its brand team, "the best in the business at making content." Someone on that team told him: "John, you know what your problem is? You don't have a physical product." Red Bull can show a can. Stan, by contrast, is abstract. Saying that Stan "is literally the best value product in the market... the simplest and easiest way to start an online business" means little to people. One has to break it down: one can host a course, a community, a digital product and calendar booking, tools that would otherwise cost hundreds of dollars a month, for $29 a month. Breaking it down takes time and attention. Hugh calls the problem **the philosophical to the tangible divide**: "Red Bull, this is a can, it gives you wings, and so maybe that's a great example of the philosophical to the tangible divide." The task is to find one's own balance and style within it.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Koe extends the idea with a heuristic of his own, which he phrases as **make tangibility tangible**. One should ask, "is there a different way I can think about something, a different way I can go about my life," and tie what one is talking about "to just simple behavior changes that people can experiment with." It need not be a specific set of steps. The payoff is attribution: "if those experimentations get results, then [people] are going to remember you for that." His example is James Clear and *Atomic Habits*: "anytime you hear the word habits you think James Clear."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Koe's extension preserves something of the philosophical that Hugh's advice might seem to sacrifice. A tangible piece need not be a list of instructions. It can be a new way of thinking about something, as long as it can be tested in behavior. This is the inspiration mechanism of the 2023 triad applied to the abstract writer: the reader runs the experiment, gets the result, and attributes the result to the source. It is also the practical form of Koe's thesis that "value is behavior change" (Section 23.5).

Hugh closes this part of the conversation with what he calls "the mantra we've built Stan off of" and his personal philosophy on content: **give, give, give**. He reads "pain point" as "how can I give as much value as possible," and proposes it as the lens every content maker can use: "what is the problem someone has, how can I offer to solve that." "If you give, give, give, like, you will receive back in some sort of way." Koe agrees that "problem solving in general is such a good mindset" at any stage of a business, "especially at the beginning."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

The ideas in this subsection belong to Koe's guests, with the exception of "make tangibility tangible" and Koe's framing of the problem. They are included because Koe endorses them and because they mark a change in his own emphasis, which the next subsection examines.

#### Earning the right to be abstract

The philosophical-to-tangible divide raises a question that Koe answers in several places: when is a writer entitled to be abstract? His answer has two parts, reputation and time.

The first part appears in a March 2026 warning to beginners. People "write this frilly tweet and they think they're Marcus Aurelius, but you don't have the reputation authority that Marcus Aurelius has. You're not Alan Watts. You're not these people who have this reputation built up so that anything they say is valuable. You have to practice persuasion. You have to capture attention. You have to illustrate, 'hey, why is this idea important for my life?' And that usually comes in the form of a pain point, so helping people understand why they should implement the idea."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

The term **reputation authority** names what the beginner lacks. A sentence by Marcus Aurelius is read as wise partly because of who wrote it; the same sentence from an unknown account is read as a platitude. The beginner's substitute for reputation is the pain point, which supplies the reason to care that a famous name would otherwise supply.

The second part is time. In a June 2025 video on personal branding, Koe lists **time under attention** as the first of three elements of authenticity. "The more attention people give you, especially in long-form content like articles, books, and videos, the more they perceive your posts through the rest of your ideas." He uses himself as the example. With two books, years of YouTube videos, many newsletter subscribers and more than 100,000 views per newsletter article, he can post simpler content, and people read it through the lens of "Dan posted this, I know his entire philosophy," which boosts engagement. "Your engagement isn't only determined by the quality of the idea. It's determined by both the quality of the idea and your time spent in the game." This, he says, is why beginners quit: they never give themselves enough time for people to spend enough time with them.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

Chapter 21 presented the fuller 2023 definition of time under attention as a product of several factors (attention per piece, quantity, quality, recurrence in the reader's mind and effortless spread); the 2025 passage reduces it to the two variables that matter here. The coinage plays on "time under tension," the weightlifting term for the duration a muscle is loaded during a set.

The same logic explains why Koe wrote his book. In a March 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, he says that reading comments on his posts (not the hostile ones) he kept thinking, "they're not understanding what I'm saying... this is getting good engagement but... they have no idea what I'm trying to get across." People's questions often were not about business problems and required a different lens. "I just kept getting evidence that... I need to give people the lens to view the content from so that it is more impactful." The book also spreads ideas and builds the brand, but it was not meant to be actionable or to lead into courses. His model is James Clear: "when you think of habits you think of James Clear... if you've read *Atomic Habits*, you read his tweets... from a much better lens, and because of that he gets so much more engagement." The book was "my way of creating a modality for myself to be able to write whatever I want, no matter how philosophical it is... and have it be understood," whereas a standalone tweet without that depth would only do "okay."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md

The book, in this account, is an investment in time under attention: a long-form artifact that gives readers the lens through which short pieces become intelligible. Earning the right to be abstract is therefore not a matter of permission but of infrastructure. The abstract tweet works only when the reader already holds the framework it compresses.

In the same conversation Koe describes his own trajectory in these terms. A common observation on Twitter is that at 1,000 followers people talk about whatever and at 100,000 they post platitudes. Koe says he has shifted "into much more pithy big picture stuff because all the other stuff is documented," and that the high-level reminders hold more meaning for him and are impactful "for just... nudging people to stay on the path." He still gives actionable material, but personally prefers high-level reminders "to use as... part of the lens for me to go about life." Dickie Bush adds a model of the progression. Early sharing is the writer making sense of low-level things; "once you have made sense of those low-level things, you only can ascend to the high-level things," or else "you pick a different angle" and go back down to low-level, actionable content (his example is an in-person "mastery facility" he was considering). Creating online, in Bush's description, is learning new things, documenting them and continuing to grow, and "picking that thing to go and learn is the fun part"; even "I want to completely understand my own philosophical mind" can become the next public experiment.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md

**An unresolved tension: actionable or philosophical?** Koe's statements on this question point in two directions over time, and they should be read together rather than reconciled silently. In 2022 he called the how-to the heart of a perspective. In March 2023 he described his own move to "pithy big picture stuff," and in another video that same month he went further, saying that he likes "fluff," because it supplies the why, the nuance and the big ideas, and "most people don't do [the steps] because they don't have the fluff that resonated with them." In May 2023 he told beginners that "actionable advice is how you build an audience at the start" (Section 23.6) and warned them not to try to be too clever when nobody knows who they are; a month later, in June 2023, he described his own videos as non-prescriptive seeds that grow through pattern recognition, results arriving "once you are able to create your own prescription." In January 2024 he said that actionable advice and platitudes "don't sell as well as they did," because the market was saturated with generic tips, and in August 2024 he defended the length of his own newsletters and videos against the 90% who tell him to "cut the fluff": the little ideas give the mind something to attach to. Section 22.5 ("Perspective over prescription: in defense of 'fluff'") develops these philosophical-side passages, which balance the series; read without them, the chronology leans misleadingly toward the actionable. In 2025, with the founders of Stan, he observed that philosophical posts do not lead to sales and proposed "make tangibility tangible." In 2026 he criticized beginners' "fortune cookie tweets" and their imitation of Marcus Aurelius. Koe does not present these as a single development, and the reasons he gives differ by moment: tactics were already documented (2023), generic advice was saturated (2024), abstraction did not sell (2025). The concept of time under attention offers a reading that fits most of the statements: the tangible and actionable suits the beginner without reputation and the short-form feed, while the pithy and philosophical suits the creator whose long-form body of work supplies the lens. But that reading is an interpretation the corpus supports, not one Koe states as a resolution.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12, fluff); Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md (2023-06-11, seeds); This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md (2024-08-04, "cut the fluff")

#### Two post styles that build authority

A February 2023 video gives two concrete post styles that a beginner can use to build authority in one interest. (The video presents three; the second, "importance of a topic," belongs with Chapter 22's discussion of making interests interesting.)

The first style is **actionable principles**: a list of principles or actionable steps a beginner can take to understand or use the topic. Koe's example, written on the spot about marketing, has a hook ("Your content sucks because you don't:"), a body ("hook in the reader / agitate their problem / relate to their problem / give them a valuable solution") and a conclusion ("human psychology will always beat what you think will work. Clear not clever."). The structure is a hook at the top, a body of actionable principles and a conclusion that gives the big idea. He warns that the examples will not be perfect because he wrote them live: "none of my content is, it just slowly gets better with time." The exercise he assigns is to pick a topic from one's topic tree (Chapter 22), write a post in this style, and publish it whether one likes it or not.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The third style is **calling out common mistakes or problems**, which establishes authority because readers perceive the writer as "helping people solve problems in the thing I'm interested in." His example: "Stop writing hyper-specific content. As a beginner you should be focused on growth: make your writing relatable, focus on a common problem, talk about the topic from a high level. Don't expect to grow if only a few people would share your content." He improvises a fitness version: "Most people lift weight too fast. It's killing your gains. Do this, this, this instead. It will make all the difference."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The two styles map onto the triad. Actionable principles are education in its plainest form. Calling out mistakes combines the problem call-out and the warning (commandments 5 and 10) with an implicit display of competence. Both are ways for a writer without reputation to earn the authority that, in the previous subsection, permits abstraction later.

#### Meeting people where they are

The last strand of this section concerns depth. How advanced should content be? In a September 2023 video Koe asks his viewers whether they are beginner, intermediate or advanced, and answers for the market: "99% of the market are beginners." He asks them to think about how they consume. One is not on social media to be educated; when one searches for something to learn, one has intent. Most people who post beginner-level advice succeed "because people are on social media to be entertained or reminded of the principles that work." Even a fitness video that pops up on YouTube repeats principles one already knows, but it helps "because it shows you that you're on the right path." So one should not get too high-level on social media, if one wants to resonate and grow, and should "save the high level information for when people have intent to either buy from you or search."

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

The distinction between browsing and intent is the practical core of this advice. The feed is a place for reminders and entertainment; search and purchase are places for depth. Matching the depth of content to the reader's state of intent is a structural decision, prior to any choice of words. It also links to Chapter 22's thesis that people need to be reminded more than they need something new.

In October 2024 Koe adds two passages, one a warning and one an argument. The warning is that many people do not want to be shallow or "play the short-form game": they feel "too good for beginners," who actually care about the shallow topics, topics that help those beginners at their level of development. The argument explains why staying shallow is not a loss. To remain relevant on social media one cannot go as deep as before; this is not bad, because one is forced to meet people where they are, at the beginner level, and change their lives to the point where they can understand one's depth. Koe refers to stages of psychological or ego development (the models Chapter 38 presents in detail): people operate at different stages, most are at lower ones, and those are the people on social media, unfocused on a goal and very distracted. If one is trying to raise consciousness or help people develop, one must start shallow. Many people in spirituality get this wrong, he says, by targeting the unitive or transcendent stage, "like 1% of the population." One can still talk about one's interests, "but you have to frame them for the people lower and raise them up the ladder to where you are."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

A November 2024 video describes how Koe applies this to his own work. Many creators at the "achiever" stage of development (in the ego-development models he uses) "could be intelligently putting on an act to attract people in this stage," and he says this is often what he does: "sometimes my YouTube titles are very superficial and shallow and focused on making a lot of money or getting a specific desired end result, but inside the video itself I try to raise people to the next stages of development." He gives the example of the creator Hamza, who talks about *The Way of the Superior Man* (by David Deida, whom the transcript does not name), "a very spiritual book" that is often interpreted in a "red pill masculinity frame," and suggests that Jordan Peterson may do something similar with Christianity.

**Source:** How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md

These passages complete the argument of "looks come before depth" (Section 23.2) by adding a developmental dimension. The superficial title is not merely a surface that leads to depth in general; it is a message pitched at the stage where most readers are, and the content inside is a ladder toward the next stage.

**A change of emphasis: engagement-optimized content and the "epistemic commons."** These defenses of shallow hooks and achiever-stage titles date from 2023 and 2024. In 2025 Koe began describing short, fast content as "entropic," with exceptions for what he called "syntropic" short form by writers such as James Clear, Naval Ravikant and Andrew Huberman. In April 2026 he went further: creators, he said, optimize for engagement instead of transformation, and "fast content," which includes hot takes and "engagement-optimized threads" that give "the feeling of understanding in 30 seconds," poisons what he called the epistemic commons. He contrasted such content ("informed but empty") with slow content such as essays. Koe does not retract his defense of the hook or of meeting people where they are, and he gives no explicit reason for the change of tone. The two positions can apply to different objects: a superficial hook or title as the door into a piece that genuinely deepens the reader, which the 2024 criterion of the "overarching narrative" permits, versus a piece that is shallow throughout and only simulates understanding, which the 2026 critique condemns. The criterion that separates them is the one Koe already stated in 2024: judge the index fund, not the single stock.

### 23.5 Story as Structure

#### The mind makes sense of the world in stories

Every structure discussed so far has an implicit shape: a problem, a movement, a resolution. This section explains why Koe believes that shape is not one technique among others but the native format of the mind. The thesis appears in its plainest form in a March 2025 video on strategic thinking, as part one of "how your mind interprets reality": "The mind makes sense of the world in stories. A story or game provides the mind with a frame to maintain order and clarity. It gives you fulfilling steps to take toward the end goal." The consequence is stated as a diagnosis: if one does not understand the story one is living out or the game one is playing, one cannot make sense of one's place in the world; "you lose purpose, entropy ensues and you get lost in chaos."

**Source:** How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md

A May 2025 video on gamifying one's life applies the thesis to the experience of feeling lost. "Your mind makes sense of the world in stories. That's why you feel lost, because you don't know what story you are living out, or you're living out a story someone else assigned to you, and you can feel that misalignment in your soul." The hardest part of taking back control, Koe says, is "collecting the right puzzle pieces until just the right amount of vision forms" for one to act with confidence. At first the puzzle is a jumbled mess that the brain cannot make sense of; one becomes stressed and worried, which leads to "a narrow and negative mind" in which it is painstakingly hard to notice new opportunities: "that's why you feel stuck." In the same video he mentions an AI prompt he wrote that turns one's life into a video game, identifying passions and producing a main quest, side quests and character traits to improve.

**Source:** How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md

These passages restate in narrative terms the central claims of Parts I, III and IV of this book. The "story someone else assigned to you" is the assigned goal of Chapter 1. The entropy that ensues without a story is the psychic entropy of Chapter 5. The narrowing of perception under stress is the negative side of the goal-as-lens model of Chapter 6. And the story or game that orders the mind is the game of Chapter 10. What this chapter adds is the reversal of direction: if a story orders the writer's own mind, a story can order the reader's. The writer who understands this is not decorating information with anecdotes but giving the reader's mind the frame it needs to hold the information at all.

The earliest version of the thesis, from May 2022, explains why meaning requires a frame. The hierarchy of goals, Koe says, brings up "sense making" or clarity: the mind craves clarity, and we find it in hierarchies of goals, in games (which are structured hierarchies of goals), in stories, "or other things that make sense to us." We have to label something to understand it. And meaning depends on surrounding context: "a hand wouldn't be a hand if a forearm wasn't there to support it." Similarly, a word he is saying would not make sense without the words before it, and the next word would not make sense without this one. This happens, he says, "in all directions across all planes of reality that we latch on to."

**Source:** Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md

The hand and the forearm make the point that a story is a kind of context. A fact without a story is a hand without an arm: it may be perceived, but it does not mean anything. Writing that places a fact in a sequence (before, during, after) gives it the context in which it becomes meaningful, which is why a framework, as Section 23.1 argued, can make the same idea land where it otherwise would not.

In September 2024 Koe turns the thesis into a principle of persuasion. Humans make sense of the world in stories; they plug any idea or information they come across "into their mind, a sense-making machine," which tries to see how that information is relevant to their goals and past experiences. So one can write whatever one wants, but what is the purpose? To persuade, and to get what one wants? "If there isn't someone on the other end of your writing, it can't really be persuasive, can it." He adds that these methods apply far beyond social posts: to newsletters, landing pages, networking, sales calls, direct messages, relationships, decision-making, marketing and promotion.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The phrase **sense-making machine** gives the reader a precise model to write for. The reader does not receive an idea neutrally; the reader runs it against their goals and their past. Writing that anticipates this, by naming a goal and evoking a familiar experience, does part of the reader's sense-making for them. This is why the problem call-out and the potential benefit of Section 23.3 work: they supply the goal-relevance that the reader's mind is already searching for.

#### Your own little world

A related idea, which Chapters 8 and 11 have already discussed in the context of vision and focus, returns here because it shows the story principle applied to a life rather than a text. In a January 2025 video, Koe advises creating "our own little world: a frame, a perspective, a little mental boundary to operate within." He notices that the most successful people he knows "live in their own little world." Walking down the street, they pay no attention to others; they do not live in other people's heads or wonder what people think; they are focused. "They've programmed their own video game": something addictive and enjoyable, a logical sequence of steps that yields experience, which tells them what is worth attention and what is worth learning. It is "a frame for their mind that repels distractions and constrains entropy." One builds it by setting the foundations of a new identity, a "full circle" frame of reference that serves as a lens to notice opportunities, make better decisions and sense when one is off track in order to correct course. Koe sets an expectation for the process: it took decades to become who one is, so one should expect at least twelve months to expand into the new mold.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md

In September 2025 he describes the same idea as the purpose of a video built around "12 rules to create your own rules." To increase enjoyment and creativity and reduce distraction, do what every successful person has done: "discover and create a set of principles that you operate by. You need to create your own little world and become immersed in it." He then distinguishes three levels of help. Most people give you the destination (what one finds on social media, even "12 months to change your life"); some give the steps of the journey; "very few people give you the navigation system to create your own destination and create the steps." The video therefore offers not a proven system but "a framework, a meta view" for discovering one's own rules.

**Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md

The **navigation system** is the meta-level that the earlier sections of this chapter approached from the writing side. A template is a destination; a framework is a set of steps; the principles that remain after the frameworks are dropped (Section 23.1) are a navigation system. The same distinction governs what Koe tries to give readers: not prescriptions, which end when the destination is reached, but a frame within which they can generate their own steps. For the purposes of this chapter, the lesson is that a writer who wants to order a reader's mind is offering, at small scale, the same thing a person needs at large scale: a story in which the next step makes sense.

#### Executive function as storytelling: Devon Eriksen

A guest gives the story principle its most radical formulation. In a December 2024 conversation, the novelist Devon Eriksen, author of *Theft of Fire*, locates the uniquely human capacity in "that executive function, it's that ability to tell ourselves stories." We look at the universe and say, "all these things I see fit together into a story," and then "tell myself another story, which is my plan, and I'm going to make my plan happen." He adds a qualification that is also a theory of planning: "Of course a plan is a list of things that don't happen, but that just means you have to make another plan midway through, and eventually you hit the moving target if you have your feedback loop running." Koe mentions an article in which Eriksen defined intelligence as the ability to tell stories, and he does not simply adopt the definition. Reading that article, he says, led him to conclude that "maybe intelligence wasn't the right word for me to use but perspective": the ability to collect, integrate and widen one's perspective, which increases "your ability to maybe not tell a story but to create a story because you can see further." Eriksen, for his part, reframes the whole debate as one about effectiveness, "what makes an effective human being." Section 3.3 develops this exchange and the change of terminology it records.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

Later in the conversation Eriksen compresses the thesis further: "stories are important because they appeal to the part of our brain that does the actual thinking. Intelligence is stories, consciousness is stories. So stories influence us in important ways." The transcript gives no further elaboration of the claim.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

These are Eriksen's ideas, not Koe's, and they are stated as a view, not argued with evidence in the conversation; Koe's own word for the faculty in question is "perspective," the capacity to create a story by seeing further, rather than "intelligence." Their relevance here is that they connect two uses of story that the corpus otherwise keeps apart. A plan is a story told forward, and the feedback loop that revises it midway is the cybernetic model of Chapter 6 (intelligence as steering by correction). A piece of writing is a story told to someone else. If the same faculty does both, then the skill of structuring a post and the skill of planning a life are not merely analogous; they are exercises of one capacity. This inference is the book's, and it holds under Koe's correction as well as under Eriksen's formulation: whether the faculty is called intelligence or perspective, it is what turns what one sees into a story that can be told forward.

#### The reverse side: the stories the ego fabricates

The story principle has a darker application, which Koe describes in a November 2022 video on the power of being alone. The ego, he says, makes sense of the world through stories, and one can watch it do so. While scrolling Twitter, observe your mind. You read a 280-character tweet and, if you do not understand it, you fill in the blanks, applying it to some aspect of your life where it does not make sense, which makes you angry and want to leave an angry comment. The alternative is to see things for what they are: read the tweet for what it is, see someone for what they are, take what they say as what it is, without filling in a past or future of who you think they were or will be, and interact in the present moment. Koe recommends doing this alone, and when scrolling or reading a book, to "observe how it starts to fill in pieces and manipulate the picture that you are seeing to fit your world view."

**Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md

For a writer, the passage is both a warning and a piece of reader psychology. It is a warning because the writer's own reading of other people's content is distorted by the same machinery. And it describes what happens to the writer's words in the reader's mind: a short text that leaves gaps will have those gaps filled from the reader's worldview, often against the writer's intent. This is one reason Koe, in Section 23.4, wanted to give readers "the lens" through a book: a reader who holds the writer's framework fills the gaps from it rather than from their own projections.

#### Life is story: highs and lows

Why should stories in particular have this power over the mind? Koe's answer, in the November 2023 video, is cosmological. "Life is story, song is story; the universe, or 'uni-verse, one song.'" Architecture, music, plant life and everything else follow universal principles of highs and lows, creation and destruction, growth and death. Humans make sense of the world in stories, and "your job is to use the power of word to make people feel something; stories mimic the natural highs and lows of emotion." He relates this to his book *The Art of Focus*, which has a chapter on the universe ("big picture understanding of what truth is"), and says that every other chapter of the book, on the new economy, on acquiring skills, on understanding and reinventing oneself, "mimics universal patterns"; "once you understand that, you master yourself, and then you can master the world."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

**Complementary context:** The Latin *universum* derives from *unus* ("one") and *versus* ("turned"), roughly "turned into one"; "one song" is a popular folk etymology.

Koe uses "uni-verse, one song" as a mnemonic for a worldview rather than as philology. The substantive claim is that rhythmic alternation (highs and lows, tension and release) is a general pattern, and that stories move people because they reproduce the pattern in emotion. Chapter 39 discusses the same principle under the name of polarity, as a description of life's ups and downs. Here its function is structural: a piece of writing that has only highs, or only information, lacks the alternation the mind expects.

#### The curiosity loop: how attention is held

The first task of the dopamine dealer was to capture attention; the second is to hold it. In the same November 2023 video, Koe gives the mechanism: "Once the curiosity loop is open, the mind feels committed to figuring out the rest of the story; that is what holds attention." Hence the importance of crafting a compelling story, and his definition of what a story is made of: "stories are layers of metaphors, concepts, perspectives and experiences that take people on a journey." People want to understand the whole story: what came before, during and after any given argument.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The terms **curiosity loop** and **curiosity gap** are used interchangeably in the corpus, and they recur throughout this chapter: in the problem hook (Section 23.3), in the potential benefit, in bullet lists that open loops, and in the information gap of social proof. The definition here explains what they share. A loop is an unresolved element, a question the mind registers and wants to close. Once it is open, the reader's attention is no longer held by the writer's effort but by the reader's own need for closure.

A March 2024 passage shows how a story opens the loop. Koe says that the sequence problem–goal–process "constructs the perspective or overarching narrative," while the smaller elements of value (which he calls micronutrients, discussed in Chapter 31) guide people within the story. He invites his viewers to deconstruct why we love stories and games, why stories make things make sense, and why they make us want to help the teller. A story frames perspective by "opening a curiosity loop with a problem." His example is the first line of a novel he does not name: "there was a cold spot on the side of the bed." Why? What will the characters do? The goal or resolution is implied, and the process is everything that happens in the book until the end. A game works the same way. He encourages his audience to understand their own lives through narrative, story and game.

**Source:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md

The cold spot on the bed is a near-perfect example of an open loop because it states an effect without its cause. Someone is missing, or left, or died; the sentence does not say which. Section 23.6 shows that Koe found this same mechanism, under the name "the law of cause and effect" ("hint at the effect and then dive into the cause"), at the core of the classic copywriting formulas.

#### Story is structure, not a string of events

What does Koe mean by "story" when he says that all good content is a story? Not, he insists, a literal narrative. In the November 2023 video he explains that "a story is also metaphorical." It is not "I woke up, made coffee..." He means structure versus content, the distinction of Section 23.1. Story structure is highs and lows: one walks people through a setting, a problem, rising action, a climax, falling action and a resolution. "That's what a landing page is: a landing page is a story." Even educational content, such as a piece on how to start a copywriting business, guides people through a story with problems, benefits and solutions. Alternatively, one can think in terms of "purpose, path, priority," another chapter of his book and "a universal principle," or in terms of the goal (what people want and the associated benefits), the problem (where they are now and how it ruins their lives), and the path, system or plan in between that takes them to the next step. "That's a story."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

**Complementary context:** The sequence of setting (exposition), rising action, climax, falling action and resolution is the classic five-part dramatic arc often associated with the nineteenth-century German writer Gustav Freytag. Koe does not name it.

The coined principle **story is structure** dissolves the apparent difference between storytelling and the problem–solution frameworks of Section 23.6. A how-to article that begins with a reader's problem, raises the stakes, explains a concept and ends with steps toward a goal has the arc of a story even if it contains no characters. The "purpose, path, priority" triad links this to Chapter 8, where the principle of goal, path and problem was presented as the foundation of planning. A plan and a story have the same shape, which is consistent with Eriksen's claim above.

#### The shortest possible story: tension

How short can a story be? The Stan interview of January 2025 offers two answers from the two co-founders. John Hugh, contrasting his approach with Vitali's ("we have different frameworks and approaches to life"), says he looks for "the formula I can distill down for everyone." Looking at "every successful story that's propagated through the ages," he concludes that "all you need is tension and conflict and ideally some sort of resolution." And he derives the hook from it: "What is the hook? It's just tension in some sort of way." A story arc can be "a sentence, half, like three sentences"; "that's all you need, and that to me is a story."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Koe, in a July 2026 video, gives three examples of what he calls **survival tension**, lines he has posted that grab attention "because of this exact tension": "You wake up at 30 and realize you've been living on autopilot and the gap between who you are and who you want to be is so large that change feels impossible." "You spend a decade building someone else's dreams just to find that they don't care about you at all." "There are people dumber than you making 10 times more than you because they don't think about the risk."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md

Each line is a complete story in Hugh's minimal sense: a situation, a conflict and an implied (unwelcome) resolution. And each touches a fear that Koe's diagnosis in Part I places at the center of the default path: autopilot, building someone else's dream, being out-earned by those who took risks one avoided. The term "survival tension" indicates why such lines work in his model: they activate the same threat detection that the negativity bias exploits (Section 23.3), here at the level of identity and life trajectory.

#### A story at any length; clarity and interest

Vitali, the other Stan co-founder, answers a related question from Koe. Koe admits that before he understood storytelling deeply, he thought "tell a story" meant "write a book," and asks how one tells a story in something as short as a LinkedIn post or a TikTok. Vitali's answer: "you can write a story of any length in like any format." A book can be reduced to a one-pager, and a one-pager to a five-line LinkedIn post. He points to Koe's own "original style" of one-sentence lines as distinctive and intentional; even others who write this way "still stick with maybe couple line paragraphs." Great concise content "can be five lines long, but you need to really spend time figuring out how to say and what to say." He quotes the saying "if I had more time I would have written you a shorter letter." And his answer to the request for a method is explicitly a non-framework: "it starts with intention, and then it goes with consistent practice, and then you'll find it."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

**Complementary context:** The line about the shorter letter is usually attributed to Blaise Pascal, who wrote a version of it in his *Provincial Letters* (1657). Vitali does not name the source.

Vitali's non-framework is a useful counterweight to the abundance of frameworks in this chapter. Concision, in his account, is not the output of a template but the product of time spent deciding what matters, and it is learned through intention and repetition. This is compatible with Koe's training-wheels position, which also ends in practice beyond the frameworks.

The same interview gives two content structures that Vitali uses. One way to make content memorable is to start with the hook, explain something from first principles, and then make a case, an approach he calls "kind of philosophy-esque." The alternative still starts with a hook and "something memorable that is like a quotable piece of it," but then follows up "with a story that is very personal." Those personal stories "make people feel in a very particular way," do "remarkably well," and are "organic and unique"; he calls them "probably the biggest differentiator." For him, connection with an audience "to great degree comes to the storytelling."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Devon Eriksen gives the simplest criterion of all. "The goal of writing is to communicate something in a clear and interesting way. If it's not clear, they won't understand it; if it's not interesting, they won't read it." Writing *Theft of Fire*, he says, he felt no responsibility to convince the world of anything ("I was just saying, this is my story"). His responsibilities were to be clear, to be interesting, to entertain people and to make the story resonate with who they are: to "make sure that it was a story people would like."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

Eriksen's two conditions map onto the two halves of this chapter. Interest is the work of packaging and attention (Sections 23.2 and 23.3); clarity is the work of structure (Sections 23.1 and 23.6). Story serves both, which is why it sits between them.

#### "Your message must mimic the universe"

Koe's own synthesis of these ideas is a sequence he gave in his January 2024 summary of *The Art of Focus*. The components, in the order he states them, are: tell stories; lead people through highs and lows; spark emotions as one's favorite movies do; open a curiosity loop to capture attention; agitate a problem that then shapes their perspective; relate with them through personal experience; imply a goal with a desirable end result; and provide a path for them to follow to achieve that result. The heading he gives it is "**your message must mimic the universe**," and the conclusion he draws is a three-word definition: "**value is behavior change**."

**Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md

The sequence is not a writing template in the sense of PAS or APAG; it is a checklist of the elements that make a message move people. Read against the rest of the chapter, each element has a home. The highs and lows come from "life is story." The curiosity loop is the mechanism of attention. Agitating the problem is the "amplify" of PAS. Relating through experience is the personal story that Vitali calls the biggest differentiator. Implying a goal and providing a path is the problem–goal–process structure. And the conclusion, value is behavior change, sets the standard by which all of it is judged. A message that is read, enjoyed and forgotten has captured and held attention but has not delivered value in this sense. This is also the reason for "make tangibility tangible" (Section 23.4): a behavior change is the form in which value becomes visible to the reader and attributable to the writer.

#### Your story is your brand

If a transformation is a story, then the writer's own transformation is the most available story they have. Koe states this in a January 2023 video on the niche of one, as the fourth step of a process: write your story. "Your story is what makes you unique and relatable." He points out that he opened that very video with a personal experience (an advertising-agency course that promised six figures) around the topic he wanted to discuss, "in order to frame the situation." The relatable aspects of a story "give people hope and make them aware of the possibility that it is possible to overcome the low point in life that they're in, or to take advantage and double down on the high point."

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

The fullest statement of the principle comes from the December 2022 one-person business roadmap, repeated almost verbatim and joined with an adjacent passage in the 2024 compilation. A story has highs and lows and problems overcome over and over: "an endless story." Stories sell; they are how humans make sense of the world. Sales imply a transformation, and a transformation is a story. Copywriting starts with a lead that illustrates and agitates a problem, then shows how to overcome it, then gives testimonials as proof. Marketing and sales are tactics built on a foundation of human psychology, used to show someone that they can transform and, preferably, to help them transform: "that's the right way." Exploitation and manipulation toward a non-mutual benefit is what gets labeled a scam: promising something one cannot deliver. "If you can't deliver and don't refund, that's a scam."

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

Koe then gives an example of why individual stories retain value even when goals are shared. Suppose he pursues financial freedom, a big, broad goal. His path differs from another person's: he learns web design in order to freelance, while someone else takes a high-paying tech job, invests and retires. These are completely different stories with different problems, reaching the same goal. Each story is valuable to "the generation under you"; it is what people want to follow, as they watch a movie or read a book for an interesting story. The 2024 compilation adds a conclusion: put one's story online while distributing actionable advice and valuable information, and one has a personal monopoly, or what Koe calls a **mental monopoly**. The equivalence needs a warning. Here, in the 2024 compilation, "mental monopoly" is used in a second sense, as one's own story plus actionable advice put online, and as a synonym of personal monopoly. The term's first sense, introduced in Section 18.4, is "having great ideas that people tie to you": big ideas that stick in people's heads. In the roadmap video, and again in the compilation that repeats it, Koe first uses the two terms interchangeably and a few minutes later presents the mental monopoly as "the next thing" beyond the personal monopoly; Section 27.5 follows that distinction.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The argument explains why the saturation of a topic does not saturate the stories within it, a point Chapter 27 develops as "saturation doesn't exist." Two people with the same goal have different paths, and the path, not the goal, is what a follower wants to see. It also contains an ethical boundary that the rest of the chapter assumes. Persuasion is legitimate when it shows a real transformation and helps produce it; it becomes a scam when the promised transformation is not delivered. Koe's use of the word "manipulation" shifts over the years: in 2022 he called "exploitation and manipulation toward a non-mutual benefit" a scam, while in 2023 he described humans as constantly manipulating their environment and said he manipulates viewers "hopefully in a positive way, mutual benefit"; from 2024 he treats persuasion and manipulation as "two separate words," with unconscious persuasion counting as manipulation. The criterion of mutual benefit remains constant through the change of vocabulary, which Chapter 32 discusses.

#### Sales is storytelling

In a September 2024 beginner guide Koe states the commercial version of the thesis as a definition, in a phrase of his own coinage: "**sales is storytelling**. You are showing someone where they can be, making them aware of the pain points of their current situation and offering them a path to get from point A to point B: pain point, desired outcome, path to get there, preferably your unique way of doing it. That's what sales is, that's what content is, that's what a landing page is, that's what all good and persuasive anything is." The definition applies to persuasion in general, to writing and to articulate speaking, and Koe recommends practicing sales in every situation: writing, conversation, earning. For a creator, he adds, sales calls are not the main concern: "90% of your selling is done through content," which makes people aware of problems, gives them solutions to test and illustrates their future life.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

A November 2023 passage applies the principle to promotions. "Promotions are why you achieve the goal. People don't care about features, they care about benefits. Assume that everyone is selfish because they are, even if that selfishness comes from feeling good about themselves by being selfless. Marketing is about creating the most powerful why." A why "implies a transformation," and transformations are what people want: physique transformations get large numbers of views on YouTube, and life transformations make one emotionally invested in a person. "A transformation is a story, and that's what our minds are wired to be attracted to, trust and connect to."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The claim that everyone is selfish is deliberately provocative, and Koe qualifies it in the same sentence: the selfishness may consist in feeling good about being selfless. Its function in the argument is to direct the writer's attention to the reader's benefit rather than to the product's features, which is the same move as the potential benefit (Section 23.3) and the "what's in it for me" of APAG (Section 23.6).

The same passage ends with a heuristic for writing a promotion: "You need to do one of two things: one is tell people why the product will change their life; two, tell people why it changed your life, because they will read it as if they were changing theirs." The second option is the inspiration mechanism of Section 23.4 applied to selling. The reader who follows the writer's story of transformation experiences it, vicariously, as their own, and the conclusion they reach (this could change my life) is one they feel they reached themselves.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

#### Storytelling and salesmanship: Sahil Bloom

A January 2025 conversation with the writer Sahil Bloom gives the principle its broadest scope. Koe asks which meta-skills drive income when one's "enough" number is still far away. Bloom answers: "storytelling and salesmanship are the two most important skills for life... fundamentally, life is about sales. Life is about being able to sell yourself, your ideas, your vision... to whatever stakeholder." One sells oneself to prospective partners in dating and marriage, to friends, to customers in business, to bosses and colleagues: "you're always selling." Those who sell well "can story tell very effectively... take a whole bunch of data in and have a story come out on the other side." From time spent with "some of the most successful CEOs in the world," Bloom reports that "they're not the smartest people in their organizations... They are the best storytellers... the best at taking a seemingly disparate... pool of data and turning it into something that makes sense." And the skill can be honed.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

Asked how, Bloom describes a method he was applying during the conversation itself. "When you tell a story, when you are getting your reps... take note of the things that make the other person's eyes light up. Take note of what makes the person lean in." He tells Koe that throughout their talk he had been making mental notes of the moments when Koe's eyes widened or he smiled: "Those are things that the next time I talk about these topics, I probably want to lean in on... articulate those points a little bit better." The principle he draws is about the quality of practice: "It's the rep and then it's iterating on the rep. It's not about just reps. It's not about pounding your head into the wall. It's about getting smarter with each time that you do."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

Bloom's ideas are his own, and Koe presents them as such. They connect to this chapter in two ways. Bloom's account of the CEO as storyteller ("data in, story out") is the sense-making machine described from the speaker's side: the storyteller does for the listener the work of turning disparate facts into a sequence that means something. And his method of **iterating on the rep** is the spoken equivalent of the data loop Koe recommends for posts: publish, observe which parts produce a response, and lean into them next time. Chapter 26 develops the written version under the name of validated content.

### 23.6 Long-Form Structures and PAS

#### Hook, body, conclusion

The structures of this section all elaborate a skeleton that Koe states as a non-negotiable principle in February 2023: whatever the post style, "you can't take away the principles of content writing: you need a hook, you need a body and you need a conclusion." In a 280-character tweet the three can overlap, sometimes inside a single sentence (as the PPP example in Section 23.2 showed). In a newsletter they need to be fleshed out. Every framework below is a way of specifying what goes into each of the three parts as the piece grows longer.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

#### The micro story: PAS

The framework Koe recommends to beginners, and the one he says he still uses when he is lost, is the oldest of the copywriting formulas he teaches. In a December 2025 video on articulating one's thoughts, he presents it as the first rung of a ladder of three frameworks (beginner, intermediate, advanced), and he introduces it with a claim about the mind that reformulates the thesis of Section 23.5: "The human mind is a **story engine**; humans can't help but pay attention to a story, especially if it's short and impactful. Once you learn how to do it well, you can effectively short-circuit someone's brain into being interested."

The bridge from story to framework is a definition: "The foundation of a story is transformation. This does not have to be a transformation about a specific person. A transformation can be as simple as introducing a problem and giving a solution." That minimal transformation is what Koe calls **the micro story**, and its structure has three parts. **Problem**: "state a relatable problem or pain point that you've observed or experienced." **Amplify**: "illustrate how that problem leads to a negative outcome if it is not solved." **Solution**: in a short post, one sentence or a short bullet list; in a long newsletter or script, all the key points with their explanations. "The problem and amplification would account for the hook." He adds: "If you've studied copywriting at all... it's called the PAS framework. And after six years... that's still my go-to when... I'm just lost." He points out that the section of the video in which he says this is itself the solution part of a PAS structure.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

He then writes a PAS piece live. "What's a problem? You don't know how to articulate yourself. Okay, how do you amplify that? What does that lead to down the road? You won't get the girl. You won't land the job. You won't be able to start your own business. You're going to live in your mom's basement for the rest of your life. Okay, what's the solution? Use the PAS framework. That's a tweet. It would actually probably do pretty good." The same piece, he says, could be expanded into a newsletter, a YouTube video or a podcast answer. If someone asks "What's the greatest skill to learn?", one says "Articulation." Why? "Because most people don't know how to articulate themselves." Then one amplifies and solves.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The live example shows what the amplification step does. The problem ("you don't know how to articulate yourself") is abstract and easy to shrug off. The amplification converts it into a chain of concrete consequences that touch status, love, work and independence, and ends in a deliberately exaggerated image (the mother's basement) that applies the eighth commandment (Section 23.3). Only then does the solution have weight. The example is also recursive, since the solution it proposes is the framework being demonstrated.

PAS has a history in Koe's teaching. In 2023 it appears as one copywriting formula among several to study; by 2025 it has become "the micro story" and his default. The change is not in the framework but in the status he gives it, from a borrowed tool to the base of his own system, consistent with the evolution of the pain and process framework described in Chapter 17, of which PAS is a close relative.

#### PASO: problem, amplify, solution, offer

A September 2024 video presents a variant with an optional fourth step. **PASO** stands for problem, amplify, solution, offer, and "the O is optional," used only when promoting or selling. The problem takes one or two sentences. The amplification illustrates how the pain point impacts someone's life, and Koe gives an extended example about tiredness: being tired can make one's spouse think something is wrong, so little problems spring up; one cannot perform when building one's side hustle ("will it ever work?"); when one gets home, all one wants is to lie down and eat junk food. "The more descriptive and relatable you can get, the better." He adds that amplification is much easier with personal experience, and refers to his videos on becoming the niche and on the most profitable niche being oneself: dig into past experiences and use those pain points as topics or to amplify, so that "you aren't just making things up." The solution is the step-by-step process or simply a solution, "one sentence if it only takes one sentence." The offer links a free download, product, service, website or blog and frames it as a further solution.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

The instruction that amplification draws on lived experience connects this framework to Chapter 22's excavation of raw material. The pain points one has lived through are not only topics; they are the source of the descriptive, relatable detail that makes amplification work. A writer who has never been tired in the way described can still write the paragraph, but it will read as invented.

#### Problem, context, steps: the 1,000-word piece and its evolution

Koe's earliest structure for long-form writing is older than PAS in his teaching, and its evolution over four years shows how he refines a framework by changing its middle section. In an August 2022 video, repeated in the 2024 compilation, the third step of building a writing business is to write 1,000 words or more. The principle that governs the piece is stated first: "Every single piece of online content, or at least good content, is based around a personal or professional problem." The structure, usable for a newsletter, a blog article or even a long Instagram caption, has three sections. First, after the headline, comes the **lead**: one explains the problem and paints a picture of how it affects people's lives. ("Lead" is a copywriting term for the opening section after the headline.) Second, one can either go straight into step-by-step advice or first give context: the benefits of overcoming the problem and why it is important to do so, "to persuade people further to actually take action." Third comes step-by-step advice on how to do it. Koe notes that the video he is recording follows the same structure: context, then nine steps.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In May 2023, in a video whose title claims $800,000 in one year from writing two hours a day, the middle section changes. "Every piece of content is based around a personal or professional problem," and the 1,000 words are structured as: paint a picture of the problem; talk about one's personal experience; give step-by-step advice on how to solve the problem. The rationale is about the beginner's position: "actionable advice is how you build an audience at the start to prove your worth and value." The 1,000 words are written "just to practice, just to get it out of the way," and they become the weekly newsletter (the hub of the content ecosystem that Chapter 24 describes).

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

In January 2024 the structure expands into a general framework for persuasive writing with five parts: a hook; a lead, which introduces and agitates the problem; a body of key points, numbered or not, separated by headlines to help readers learn and understand the topic; a conclusion, which can be a summary or step-by-step advice for overcoming the problem; and a call to action, which tells the reader what to do next (not necessary on short posts). Koe then makes a claim that links the framework to Section 23.5: "This is storytelling." Storytelling is not always a literal story; it can be metaphorical. Stories consist of anecdotes, research, examples, metaphors, quotes, tweets, videos, other content "and anything else you can do to help someone else understand." This is why he recommends a second brain (in his case, Cortex) filled with collected ideas: as it grows, writing becomes easy, because "you just piece ideas together in infinite ways; you present a problem and guide someone to overcome it."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The progression can be summarized as a sequence of versions of the same problem-to-solution skeleton:

| Date | Opening | Middle | End |
|---|---|---|---|
| August 2022 | Headline, then lead (paint the problem) | Context: benefits and why (optional) | Step-by-step advice |
| May 2023 | Paint the problem | Personal experience | Step-by-step solution |
| January 2024 | Hook, then lead (introduce and agitate the problem) | Body: key points under headlines | Conclusion (summary or steps), call to action |
| September 2024 – 2025 | Pain, with personal story inside it | — | Process: each step with what, why and how ("pain and process," Chapter 17) |

Koe gives no reason for the changes. They are best read as successive refinements and renamings of a single structure, whose constant elements are the problem at the start and the steps at the end. What varies is how the writer earns the right to give steps: by persuading with benefits (2022), by establishing credibility with experience (2023), or by teaching the concepts the steps depend on (2024).

#### From point A to point B

A June 2026 video restates the long-form skeleton in the vocabulary of the "map" that Chapter 22 described, in which content moves an audience from point A (where they are) to point B (where the writer can take them). In a newsletter or a YouTube video, "the introduction is illustrating the problem that point A is at. The middle is explaining the concept or idea that you want to talk about, and then the end section or the actionable section is like, 'Okay, here's the steps to get to point B.' And that's how you create a newsletter. That's how you create a YouTube script. That's how you create anything long-form." For short form, one ideates short posts and studies how others package and structure them (in that video, using his Eden software). Koe explains why this matters: "if you can't get people to click and stay... who's going to see your product?" Without that skill, he says, one stays in survival mode, "selling a product for your boss rather than yourself."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

The 2026 version reintroduces into the middle section what the January 2024 version called the body: the concept or idea. This is the element that distinguishes a piece that teaches from one that only instructs. The structure is problem → concept → steps, and the next two frameworks make the concept section more elaborate.

#### BPAS: turning an anomaly into a newsletter

In October 2024 Koe described a framework for one specific task: turning a post that performed unusually well into a newsletter. When a post stands out as an **anomaly** in engagement (Chapter 26 discusses how Koe defines and studies such outliers), one should turn it into more content. For a newsletter, he recommends **BPAS**, which he presents as an evolution of PAS from his "4 frameworks" video: big idea, problem, amplify, solution.

The **big idea** states the idea of the original post, "so you can get to the point faster." The **problem** illustrates a relatable problem based on the big idea. **Amplify** gives examples of how that problem affects people's lives. The **solution** gives the steps, lessons or insights that solve it. The layout is specific: one sentence for the big idea, a few sentences for the problem and a few for the amplification, which together form the introduction; then a headline stating what the piece is about; then the step-by-step solution, with subheadlines (step 1, step 2, step 3...) and paragraphs that give context and teach how. "That's how you write a newsletter."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The added "B" is a small change with a clear rationale. An anomaly post has already proved that its idea captures attention, so the newsletter can open with that idea rather than build up to it, borrowing the answer-first logic of the pyramid principle (Section 23.1) while keeping the problem-first logic of PAS for the body.

#### APAG: attention, perspective, advantage, gamify

Koe's own general-purpose framework, and the main one taught in his 2 Hour Writer course, is **APAG**: attention, perspective, advantage, gamify. (The transcript spells it variously as "apag" and "apack.") He presents it in the January 2024 guide, in a part of the video he says is pulled from the course, and he gives it the widest scope of any framework in the corpus. It can structure newsletters, podcast or YouTube scripts, threads or medium-length posts, sales pages, landing pages, opt-in pages, chapters or sections of a book or ebook, course modules, lead magnets and other educational material, and ideas for tweets and short posts: "when you go to write anything, think of this framework." Any part can be a single line or an entire section. As long as one hits each part, one is fine, and if one cannot think of more to say in a part, one moves on to the next.

His worked example maps a newsletter about a daily routine onto the four parts. Attention: the title "the daily routine that changed my life." Perspective: a picture of the average everyday routine. Advantage: why his routine is better. Gamify: the routine itself, one habit each for mind, body, business and relationships.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

**Attention** is "the art of hooks and headlines," which Koe calls the most important part of a piece: if the hook fails, readers scroll past, "leaving your hard work gone unnoticed." Three things make them keep reading. **Relevance**: how relevant is the piece to their everyday life? Use resolved pains or potential benefits; ask "what's in it for the reader." **Awareness**: is it simple or complex enough for the level of awareness one is targeting, and will readers understand what one shows them? **Effort**: how fast will they receive the result (education, entertainment or inspiration), and how easy is it to get? Not all three need to be in the headline, but all should be considered; the most potent go into the headline of a newsletter or article, which has less room than the hook of a thread or LinkedIn post. The six building blocks of a hook presented in Section 23.2 belong to this step.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

**Perspective** means "painting a picture of the enemy, or why a certain perspective is wrong." The writer relates to and amplifies the reader's current problems, and "this is also where you create the enemy of your story." The best way, Koe says, is to paint a common perspective on the topic and place heavy emphasis on its problems and pains, stated directly or implied. This sets up the writer's **advantageous perspective**. If everyone at one's high school says to go to college and get a job, one's own perspective might be to buy courses and learn online. "You're presenting points of view online in your writing... that's all people are reading, is points of view from humans."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

His example of the perspective step is a tweet he later used to open *The Art of Focus*: "Wake up, hit snooze four times, stare at your phone, roll out of bed, make coffee, sit in traffic, 8 hours of unfulfilling work, sit in traffic again, argue with your significant other, walk the pet, watch TV, pass out, repeat. This should scare the [hell] out of you." The tweet paints a mediocre lifestyle. In the book's introduction it serves to hook readers and make them aware of a problem, so that they want to overcome it and keep reading.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The mediocre-day tweet is also a compact demonstration of several earlier principles: it is a story built from a string of events that, unlike "I woke up, made coffee," has the structure of a loop ("repeat"); it uses the negativity bias and survival tension; and it is a description of the default path of Chapter 2, written so that the reader recognizes their own day.

**Advantage** means "paint a picture of the hero or the new vision, or why your perspective is right." Persuasive writing, Koe says, presents a credible argument in order to sell something, and "you are always selling, or at least you should be": products, ideas and better ways of doing things. The aim is to "sell people out of a low-consciousness existence." Having painted readers' faulty perspective, the writer provides a better one, asking: how can I educate them to understand my perspective? What do they need to know to understand where I am now? What is missing that prevents them from understanding? One presents novel ideas, concepts, social proof and experiences, and quotes, tweets or references add credibility. His example is a tweet: "Writing is a great skill to learn because it pairs with any other skill or interest. If you can write you can do whatever you want and distribute the value you discover from those pursuits. Writing allows you to make a living from living. The problem is that most people aren't [writing]." He adds a note on long form: the opening of each section of a long piece is treated like a tweet, with a hook and a wrap-up that get readers interested.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

**Gamify** means "create a hierarchy of goals or a game for people to follow." A game, Koe repeats, is a set hierarchy of goals, quests and missions that gives clarity on what to do. Done correctly, "when you give people a challenge that matches their skill, you put your readers into a flow state of discovering novel ideas that aid in their future." In short, the gamify step gives step-by-step advice for making the transformation from the old, problem-ridden perspective to the advantageous one: clear, concise, actionable, making the next steps obvious. What steps help overcome the problem described in the perspective section? What can readers implement now? One can list steps, key points or books. His example is a tweet that later became the daily-routine newsletter (and could equally have become a thread or a carousel): "You need four habits: one that builds your mind, one that builds your body, one that builds your business, one that builds your relationships. The good life is the process of becoming everything you could be."

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

| Step | Question it answers | Main devices | Link to earlier chapters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attention | Why should I stop and read? | Relevance, awareness level, effort; hook building blocks | Packaging and commandments (23.2–23.3) |
| Perspective | What is wrong with how I see this now? | The common view and its pains; the "enemy" | Anti-vision and the default path (Chapters 2 and 7) |
| Advantage | What is the better way to see it? | Novel ideas, concepts, proof, experience, references | Vision; selling people out of low consciousness |
| Gamify | What do I do now? | Steps, key points, lists; challenge matched to skill | Hierarchy of goals, game and flow (Chapters 8 and 10) |

The table makes visible what the framework carries over from the rest of Koe's system. Perspective and Advantage are, at the scale of a single piece, the anti-vision and vision of Chapter 7: first the life one does not want, then the life one does. Gamify is the hierarchy of goals of Chapter 8 and the flow condition of Chapter 10, offered to the reader. The framework is therefore not a neutral writing template but a compression of Koe's model of personal change into the form of a text: the reader is taken through, in a few minutes, the same sequence of awareness, contrast, vision and action that the book describes over a life. The flow claim, like the similar claim in Section 23.1, is Koe's adaptation of flow psychology to reading, not an empirical result.

#### Cross-domain synthesis: the advanced framework

The third rung of the December 2025 ladder is **cross-domain synthesis**, which Koe calls "my favorite because I have multiple interests… I want to weave things in." He studies psychology, philosophy, health, business, design and technology, and this is how he structures most of his newsletters, except purely tactical ones. The structure has three parts. First, **problem and amplify**: the introduction states a relatable problem and what happens if it is not solved. Second, **cross-domain synthesis** proper: "patterns or concepts from your other interests that help support your argument." His example is deep work explained through entropy from physics, to illustrate how distractions work; "this teaches my audience something new, and I can sleep well knowing that all other deep work content out there does not do this." Third, a **unique process or solution**: a list of ideas or steps that solve the problem, "solidifying the transformation. These should come from your own contemplation rather than someone else's prescription," not "I watched this YouTube video… I'm going to take that and put it in mine."

In practice, whether the piece is a newsletter, an article, a thread, a carousel or a script for ten to twenty minutes of video, it has a title; an introduction with the problem; a section that teaches a concept from another discipline; and then several sections, each describing one key point of the unique solution. Koe adds that the framework helps with writer's block and speaker's block, presumably because it tells the writer where to look for material at each step.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

Cross-domain synthesis is where the structural teaching of this chapter meets the content teaching of Chapter 22 and the generalist argument of Chapter 20. The middle section requires interests outside the topic, which only a writer with several interests can supply. The final section requires a process derived from one's own contemplation, which is the "perspective over prescription" of Chapter 22 written into the structure. The framework is advanced not because its form is complex (it is PAS with a concept inserted) but because its two distinctive sections cannot be borrowed. Its demand that the solution come from one's own contemplation also sits at the far end of the training-wheels arc of Section 23.1: what began as imitation of other people's structures ends with a structure whose content is, by requirement, one's own.

#### Copywriting: what it is and what its frameworks share

Koe defines the discipline that supplies most of these frameworks in a February 2025 guide to a first profitable product. Landing-page copywriting is easy once one has the marketing strategy; one simply needs to learn structure. Copywriting is not copyright, the area of law concerned with intellectual property: "it means persuasive writing. The entire point of copywriting is to illustrate the offer in a persuasive and clear way." If the marketing strategy is clear, "we just need to put that into writing."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

The definition gives a common word a specific sense in Koe's vocabulary. **Copywriting** is not a genre of advertising but a function, the persuasive and clear illustration of an offer, and the frameworks of this section are its tools.

Koe's treatment of the classic formulas evolved from naming to analysis, as the dated passages show. In an October 2022 video, repeated in the 2024 compilation, he recommends studying sales or persuasive copywriting for a deeper understanding of attention (and mentions his 2 Hour Writer course for more depth). Free to research, he says, are the AIDA copywriting framework, "very basic" but useful, especially for tweets, and PASTOR, which he spells out letter by letter as a longer-form framework that helps write Instagram captions, emails, sales pages and landing pages and that gives a structure for the sales process. He also recommends researching "the sales process." In neither version does he expand the acronyms.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In April 2023, in a video on value creation, he spells them out and gives a way to practice them. PASTOR is problem, amplify, story, testimony, offer, response; AIDA is attention, interest, desire, action; PAS is problem, amplify, solution. The best practice is to pair them with what he calls the eight steps of value creation (presented in Chapter 31, where Koe calls them "marketing Legos"): level of awareness, angle (positioning through the eight human desires), big problem, unique mechanism, benefits, proof, big idea, and risk reversal with the end result. One structures those eight elements within the frameworks and improves over time. On a landing or sales page, the headline, the subheadline and everything "above the fold" is where one tries to fit and summarize all eight steps.

**Source:** Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md

**Complementary context:** "Above the fold" is a term from newspaper layout, where it designated the top half of the front page, visible when the paper was folded on a newsstand. In web copywriting it refers to the part of a page visible without scrolling.

In January 2024 Koe analyzes what the frameworks share, which is the fourth step of his method for learning persuasion (Section 23.1) carried out in public. He recommends studying PASTOR, AIDA and PAS or PASO ("just Google those"), and identifies three shared mechanics. First, a problem is introduced or implied, making people curious about its cause; he calls this **the law of cause and effect**: "hint at the effect and then dive into the cause." Second, the frameworks expose people to a potential sequence of events, making them want to understand the sequence that led to a resolution or "happy ending." Third, they create an information gap, implying that the piece contains educational, entertaining or inspiring information the reader desires. Once curiosity is sparked, the writer must deliver three things: personal, client or other experiences that state or imply a transformation, with a before (the problem) and an after (the resolution and its benefits); step-by-step advice for reaching the resolution more quickly, ideally a unique solution; and a crystal-clear call to action that leads readers deeper into one's content or sparks behavior change, so that they associate the good behavior with the writer.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The analysis ties the copywriting tradition to the story principle of Section 23.5. "Hint at the effect, then dive into the cause" is the cold spot on the bed: an effect without its cause opens a loop. The "sequence of events toward a happy ending" is the story arc. The information gap is the curiosity loop under another name. And the call to action that leads to behavior change is the standard set by "value is behavior change." The classic formulas, on this reading, are specialized story structures.

In the September 2024 video Koe gives PASTOR its fullest treatment, while noting that he does not cover it in depth. It is best suited to a promotional or launch newsletter or a landing page, that is, to "sales copywriting." The problem and the amplification work as in PAS. The **story** can be personal, a client's, a success story, or an anecdote found online that backs one's point. The **testimony** is proof that the reader can achieve the same result: testimonials, or further data and facts. The **offer** breaks down what is being sold; on Koe's landing pages, the offer section shows what is inside the program, each module and how it benefits the buyer, which he summarizes as "what are the features and what are the benefits of those features." His example: in 2 Hour Writer one gets "the 2 hour content ecosystem," and the benefit is that one can batch-write twenty-three posts in two hours a week. The final letter, **response**, is named but not explained in the transcript, and its meaning in Koe's usage cannot be reconstructed from the corpus.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md

**Complementary context:** In the copywriting literature from which Koe draws the acronym, the "R" of PASTOR is usually glossed as "response" in the sense of the reader's requested action, that is, a call to action. This is the common gloss, not something Koe states.

The frameworks discussed in this section can be compared by what they add to the minimal problem–solution core:

| Framework | Letters | Origin in Koe's usage | Typical use he assigns | What it adds to problem → solution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AIDA | Attention, interest, desire, action | Copywriting tradition | Short posts, tweets | Stages of the reader's engagement; ends in action |
| PAS ("micro story") | Problem, amplify, solution | Copywriting tradition; still his "go-to" after six years of writing (2025) | Any post; beginner framework | Amplification, which forms the hook |
| PASO | Problem, amplify, solution, offer | His variant | Posts that promote | An optional offer framed as further solution |
| PASTOR | Problem, amplify, story, testimony, offer, response | Copywriting tradition | Sales copy, launch newsletters, landing pages | Story and proof before the offer |
| BPAS | Big idea, problem, amplify, solution | His evolution of PAS | Turning an anomaly post into a newsletter | Leads with the validated idea |
| Pyramid principle | Main idea, key arguments, evidence | Communication framework (Minto, unnamed by Koe) | Intermediate framework; a "meta framework" in 2024 | Answer first; reasons and evidence |
| APAG | Attention, perspective, advantage, gamify | His own (2 Hour Writer) | "Anything you write" | Enemy and hero; a game for the reader |
| Cross-domain synthesis | Problem + amplify, concept from another field, unique process | His own | Advanced; most of his newsletters | A borrowed concept and an original solution |


#### A ladder, not a menu

The December 2025 video arranges three of these frameworks explicitly as a progression: PAS for beginners, the pyramid principle for intermediates, cross-domain synthesis for the advanced. The ladder clarifies what each rung asks of the writer. PAS asks only for a problem one has observed or lived and a solution; anyone with experience can supply them. The pyramid asks for a conclusion one is prepared to defend and three to five reasons generated by asking "why" repeatedly; it requires having a position. Cross-domain synthesis asks for knowledge from outside the topic and a process derived from one's own contemplation; it requires a body of study and a perspective that cannot be borrowed.

Read this way, the long-form structures recapitulate the arc of the chapter. One begins by borrowing structure (training wheels), learns to package and to hold attention, discovers that the deepest structure is the story of a transformation, and ends with a framework whose distinctive parts must come from the writer's own mind. The frameworks are scaffolding for a capacity that, in Koe's account, is ultimately not a writing skill at all but the ability to make sense of something and help another person make sense of it too. Chapter 24 places this capacity inside a weekly practice: how ideas are captured, outlined and drafted, how short and long forms feed each other, and how a single newsletter becomes an ecosystem of content.

### Exercises

1. **Separate structure from content.** Collect five posts or short pieces that performed well in a field you care about. For each, write its structure as a sequence of functions (for example: "outcome-and-number hook, five polarizing steps, objection answered, closing line"), without reusing any of its words. Then build Koe's 3×3 matrix with three of your own ideas and three of the structures you extracted, and write at least three of the nine combinations. Which combination surprised you, and what does that suggest about the relationship between an idea and its form?

2. **Test the authenticity objection.** Koe argues that a writer who refuses frameworks is still using one, "your mind and how you were programmed to think." Take a piece you wrote recently without any conscious framework and reconstruct the implicit structure it actually follows. Then rewrite it with PAS and with the pyramid principle. Which version expresses your idea most faithfully, and which would a stranger most likely finish? Under what conditions, if any, would you defend the unstructured version as better?

3. **Diagnose a clickbait title.** Choose a video or article whose title you consider clickbait. Apply Koe's criterion of the "overarching narrative" ("one stock can be down while the index fund is up"): does the whole piece deliver depth that justifies the surface? Then apply his 2026 critique of engagement-optimized "fast content." Does the piece pass one test and fail the other? Formulate, in your own words, the condition under which a superficial hook is legitimate.

4. **Rewrite with the commandments.** Write a plain, hedged version of an idea you believe in (two or three sentences). Rewrite it four times, each time applying a different commandment: specific numbers, negativity bias, a target call-out, and confidence and conviction. Then evaluate the four versions against Koe's own limit, "do not abuse these for the sake of engagement." Which rewrite moved closest to "metaphorically impactful," and which crossed into saying something you do not actually believe?

5. **Locate yourself on the philosophical-to-tangible divide.** If you write or plan to write, classify your last ten pieces (or ten ideas) as philosophical or tangible. For each philosophical one, apply "make tangibility tangible": what simple behavior change could a reader test? Then assess your time under attention honestly. Given the reputation and long-form body of work you currently have, which kind of piece have you earned the right to publish, and what would you need to build before the other kind could work?

6. **Write your transformation as a micro story.** Choose a real transformation in your own life, however small. Write it three ways: as a one-sentence line of "survival tension," as a PAS post, and as an APAG outline in which the "perspective" paints your former view as the enemy and "gamify" gives the reader steps. Then ask which version would let a reader "read it as if they were changing theirs," and why.

7. **Analyze a framework critically.** APAG maps Koe's model of personal change (anti-vision, vision, hierarchy of goals, flow) onto a piece of writing, and he claims that a well-structured piece puts readers into a flow state. Identify which parts of this claim are supported by evidence he presents, which are interpretations drawn from his own frameworks, and which are analogies. Design a simple way you could test, with your own writing and readers, whether gamified structure changes how many readers finish a piece or act on it.

8. **Compare two rules for openings.** The pyramid principle starts with the answer; PAS and the problem → context → steps structure start with the problem; the 2026 essay advice starts with neither, aiming to discover the conclusion. Take one topic and write the first paragraph three times, once in each mode. For what reader, platform and purpose would each opening be the right decision? Defend your choice for a piece you actually intend to publish.
