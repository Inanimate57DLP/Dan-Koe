## Chapter 16 — Reading, Note-Taking and Learning with AI

Chapters 14 and 15 dealt with learning as an activity: the meta-skill of learning how to learn, curiosity as a guide, the warning against information that outruns execution, the prescription to build in order to learn, and the mechanics by which skills accumulate through techniques, progressive overload and pattern recognition. Both chapters kept returning to the same diagnosis. Most people consume a great deal and change very little, because what they take in is not filtered by a goal, not applied to a project and not tested against reality. This chapter turns to the inputs themselves. What should a person who wants to create do with the books, posts, podcasts and videos that fill their days? How should they read? Where should the ideas that strike them go, and how should those ideas come back when they are needed? And what changes now that an AI model can summarize a book, answer a question at the moment of struggle or interview a person until it understands their project?

Koe's answers form a sequence that mirrors the structure of the chapter. Section 16.1 describes the change of stance that comes before any technique: the move from **consumer** to **researcher**, the intermediate step between consuming and creating. Section 16.2 gathers everything Koe says about reading: why to read, what not to read for, what to read, and how to read, including his 2025 method of reading in two layers with AI. Section 16.3 follows his long and changing relationship with note-taking systems, from the 2022 "dissect and distill" template and the database of intellectual property to the 2026 verdict that the second brain is "a false god" and his preferred alternative, the **second subconscious**. Section 16.4 presents AI as a tutor, from the return of apprenticeship to the detailed method he calls using AI "better than 99% of people." The chapter prepares Chapter 17 (how to think) and Chapter 18 (creativity and originality), which take the ideas collected here and ask how they are combined into something new, and Part VIII, where those ideas become writing.

Three earlier threads should be kept in view. From Chapter 6 comes the goal as a lens: the mind notices what serves the goal it is pursuing. From Chapter 11 comes cheap dopamine: consumption that feels like progress but produces disorder. From Chapter 14 comes the principle that information without application is false progress. Much of this chapter is an application of those three ideas to the specific acts of reading, saving and prompting.

### 16.1 From Consumer to Researcher

#### The consumer: used by the internet rather than using it

The figure against which this section is written is the **consumer**. Koe gives the word a specific sense that goes beyond "someone who buys things." In a 2023 video titled "If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind," he asks why people keep doing things they hate. He does not claim to be immune: he describes himself as someone who has escaped and sees the situation from higher up the mountain, "until he inevitably falls back down after crossing the peak." The consumer's life, as he paints it, is praying for the weekend, dreading Monday, hiding who you are to talk to people you don't like working with, and using the internet as an escape: scrolling, clicking, shutting the mind off "because when it's on you don't like what it's doing." "This is the life of the consumer, the person who is used by the internet rather than the one who uses it" (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22).

The definition places the difference not in how much a person uses the internet but in the direction of the relationship. The same feed, the same platforms and the same hours can either use you or be used by you. That is why Koe adds that there is no escaping the new world: you can fight it or flow with it, and the internet is "a big problem and also the solution." The consumer is the person for whom it is only the problem.

In the same video Koe traces the consumer's condition backward through a causal chain, which is worth reconstructing step by step because each link names a deficit that later sections of this book address:

1. Your life feels bad because you don't contribute to humanity.
2. You don't contribute because "you don't have something so valuable that you can't help but share it."
3. You don't have it because you ignore the problems in your life that beg for a solution.
4. You ignore them because you lack clarity on how to achieve the goal that would solve them.
5. You lack that clarity because you have nothing to build: nothing to frame and guide your learning.

(**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22.)

The last link is the one that matters for this chapter. Learning without something to build has no frame, and without a frame, information has nothing to attach to. The consumer is not someone who consumes too much; it is someone whose consumption is not directed by a project. Read backward, the chain also explains why the cure Koe proposes is not "consume less" but "consume differently."

A few months earlier, closing a video about getting angry at where you are in life, Koe had already summarized the way out as a six-step sequence: (1) feel into the situation you are in; (2) transmute that energy toward a goal; (3) acquire the skills necessary to reach that goal; (4) "pursue your curiosity to avoid the mechanical nature of prescriptions"; (5) exchange your knowledge, projects and solutions for attention and income; (6) use your **self-reflective consciousness** to identify gaps in your ability and fix them. You have the resources, he says; it will take years; you may not start now, because "awareness takes time to blossom." Push forward in reality at your experience level, seek big-picture understanding, and don't get trapped in technical details (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11).

Two steps in that sequence belong directly to this chapter. Step four warns that learning by prescription, following someone else's steps, becomes mechanical; curiosity keeps it alive. Step five names the economic end of the process: what a person learns is eventually exchanged for attention and income. Between the consumer who uses the internet to escape and the person who exchanges knowledge for income there is a missing role, and Koe names it.

#### The intermediate step: researcher before creator

The usual self-improvement advice says: stop consuming, start creating. Koe thinks the jump is too large. "It's not about transitioning from consumer to creator, it's about transitioning from consumer to researcher first." The reason is that you must research and gather your own knowledge before you can create with it: you dissect other things through reading in order to reconstruct them into your own creation, whether that is writing, a video or a newsletter. A **researcher**, in this sense, "implies that you are intentionally consuming information": you have a goal for how you will use and apply what you take in (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31; the passage reappears almost word for word in the 2024 compilation `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

The researcher is therefore defined by two features. The first is intention: consumption is directed by a goal. The second is the operation performed on what is consumed: dissection followed by reconstruction. Neither feature requires consuming less. A researcher may read more than a consumer; the difference lies in what happens to what is read.

Koe adds an example that shows where a researcher's originality comes from. Suppose you are working toward a big goal, such as financial freedom, while being very interested in spirituality. Ask how spirituality is helping you get there. The connection between the two is "a very unique perspective... a very unique connection." No one else holds that particular combination of a goal and an interest, so the researcher who consumes through the lens of their own goal produces connections that a generic consumer cannot. This is the seed of the argument, developed in Part VII, that the person is the niche: the uniqueness of a perspective comes from the intersection of what someone is pursuing and what they are curious about, and research is the activity that makes those intersections visible.

#### Research instead of consume: the hermetic law of use

Koe states the principle in its shortest form in a 2023 video: "Rather than consuming information, research information" (**Source:** `The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md`, 2023-11-05). The fullest explanation of why comes earlier, in a 2022 video titled "Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead," where "stop consuming and start researching" is step two of a creative process.

"Consuming," he explains, implies that you aren't doing anything with information. You are not enacting what he calls "**the hermetic law of use**," so you will lose what you take in. Koe borrows the phrase from Hermetic philosophy without specifying a text; in his usage it amounts to "what is not used is lost." When you adopt the perspective of a researcher, by contrast, "everything starts to gain utility," because you are actively trying to connect and apply it; there is intention and purpose behind the consumption. You are no longer on social media to be entertained and "mentally masturbate," but to research: to take something, dissect it, expand on it, summarize it, connect the dots to other things you have learned, and form a new perspective that you can create and make make sense to others. His example is modest: while re-reading the first chapter of your favorite book, something lights up your brain, so you take a note (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). That note is the first step of the system described in section 16.3, which in 2022 he packaged as a free guide called **7 Days to Genius Ideas**.

The list of operations deserves attention, because it is the researcher's toolkit: **dissect, expand, summarize, connect, form a perspective**. The consumer receives; the researcher performs these five operations on what is received. Each of them is a small act of creation, which is why research is the bridge to creating: it is creation practiced on someone else's material.

The negative image recurs in 2025 in stronger language. "Most people don't know how to learn because they fry their brain with podcasts and YouTube videos and books and textbooks and social media, and six months later, after learning so much, they have nothing to show for it." "Why are you even learning in the first place if not to do something with it?" "Learning for most people has become a form of **mental masturbation**. It's the same cheap dopamine you get from scrolling on your phone, but even worse, because it makes you feel as if you're learning something, but the reality is you'll forget about it by tomorrow" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). The phrase "mental masturbation" is Koe's recurrent name for learning without doing: an activity that produces the pleasure of progress without its result. The connection to Chapter 11 is direct. Learning-as-consumption is a form of cheap dopamine that disguises itself as its opposite, which makes it more dangerous than ordinary scrolling, because the person does not feel the need to stop.

The 2023 formulation adds a practice that makes research concrete in the place where most people consume most: the feed. While scrolling, ask yourself what caught your attention, why you are still reading, and how the writer is holding your attention. Look for the principles of attention (the "10 commandments" of the video in question) on the timeline. "Pattern recognition helps with learning": when you spot one of them in the wild, you feel a dopamine increase ("oh, that's what Dan was talking about") and you remember it so you can use it in your own content, writing or speaking (**Source:** `The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md`, 2023-11-05). Here the dopamine that the consumer spends on novelty is redirected to recognition. The same scrolling session that drains one person trains another, depending on the question each brings to it. This links to the pattern-recognition mechanics of Chapter 15: recognizing a pattern you have learned is both the evidence that learning happened and a reward that reinforces it.

#### Reading like a researcher: how Koe started on Twitter

Koe tells the origin of his own writing career as a story of researching rather than consuming. He never cared for Twitter. One day he saw a self-improvement post, followed one person, liked content, followed more, and his feed filled with that kind of content. Then he saw a man selling web design services and products through Twitter at a full-time income, and asked himself "why can't I do the same thing?" He "started emulating their content structure and posting the ideas in my head." His background in marketing, sales and copywriting let him notice patterns: "I wasn't reading it like a consumer, I was reading it like a researcher." He describes what that looked like from the inside: "that's a hook... this is leading people down the page... this is where it delivers value... this is the conclusion or call to action that actually leads to engagement. I'm going to take these pieces and put my own thoughts related to my own interests in them." He adds a caveat: content alone is not everything; it also needs eyes on it, which is the subject of a separate video on growth (**Source:** `How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md`, 2023-10-08).

The story illustrates the reconstruction half of the researcher's work. Reading the post as a researcher meant separating its structure (hook, body that leads down the page, value delivery, conclusion or call to action) from its content, and then filling the structure with his own ideas. Koe calls this **emulation**, and it returns in Part VIII as the use of proven structures as "training wheels" for articulation. It is an experience, not a law: Koe's marketing background gave him the vocabulary to see structure where others saw only posts. Part of what the researcher stance asks is that the reader acquire that vocabulary.

#### Consumer mindset versus creator mindset

The same contrast appears in 2022 under a slightly different pair of names. Don't just scroll and consume the content, Koe says: "that's the **consumer mindset**." The creator takes a post, looks at it and deconstructs it: why did this do so well? Why is this type of post performing well? How do I take "the essence of this post" and make it my own so I can grow and "play the game"? (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31; repeated in `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06.)

The phrase "the essence of this post" carries the method. What is taken is not the post's wording, which would be copying, but its essence: the underlying idea or the mechanism that made it work. "Play the game" connects the practice to the game metaphor of Chapter 10: social media has rules that can be learned, and dissecting successful posts is how a player learns them.

Two examples from Koe's videos show what dissecting the essence looks like in practice. In 2026 he walks through a post that reads: "Major cheat code for life: increase your recovery speed. You will get rejected. You will lose money. You will embarrass yourself. The goal isn't to avoid the fall, it's to shorten the time between the fall and the reset. Fast recovery compounds." The essence, "increase your recovery speed," is in his words "kind of Sahil Bloom coded," an idea associated with another writer. But the opening, "major cheat code for life," "you could definitely use that as a hook. Why not?" And reading into the body ("you will get rejected, you will lose money"), "you start to think, hmm, do other ideas spin off of that? And most of the time, they do" (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md`, 2026-05-14). A single post, dissected, yields at least two kinds of material: a reusable hook and a set of spin-off ideas suggested by its body. Neither is a copy of the post.

The second example shows how fast the process can be when it has become habitual. While preparing a 2023 video, Koe had to create a post within an hour, so he found an idea quickly in a Medium headline: "10 habits that changed my life." The post he wrote for X was: "How to clear your mind: walk more, read more, write more, listen more, lift more, strategize more, build more; scroll less, indulge less, criticize less, ruminate less, sit less, procrastinate less. Clarity is reserved for those who don't resist reality but engage with it." He explains a formatting choice: he could have used bullets, but X cut off long posts at the end at the time, so he did not space it out; with a shorter list he would have used bullet points (**Source:** `How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md`, 2023-12-03). The headline supplied a format (a list of habits); Koe supplied the content and the closing line. The example is minor, and Koe presents it as such, but it shows the researcher's operation at its smallest scale: a source becomes a structure, and the structure is filled with one's own view.

#### The edge of understanding and anomaly research

In a 2025 video on personal branding, Koe gives the researcher stance its most systematic form. He starts with a diagnosis of why people struggle to find ideas worth writing about. There are two reasons, which he presents as mirroring the reasons for poor performance and low excitement: "their mind isn't trained to be articulate, and they don't consume information at the **edge of their understanding**." The single most beneficial fix for writing, he says, is to "use high-performing post structures as training wheels for your articulation," so that an idea can become something others like, share and follow you for (**Source:** `How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md`, 2025-06-08).

The second reason is the one that bears on reading. Consuming at the edge of one's understanding means choosing inputs that are slightly beyond what one already grasps: neither so familiar that they add nothing nor so foreign that nothing attaches. It is the reading equivalent of the challenge–skill balance of Chapter 10, and it explains why the consumer, who consumes what is comfortable, runs out of ideas.

The fix comes as an exercise that Koe calls finding **anomalies**, which can be reconstructed step by step:

1. In a notebook or notes app, list five to ten writers, thinkers or creators you admire for their articulation.
2. Spend one hour a day for the next week scrolling their accounts.
3. Find their anomalies: "posts that have at least 2x the engagement as their other posts." Screenshot them or write them down. The key shift, he says, is to "stop reading ideas on the internet as a consumer... start doing so as a researcher": you are reading for your own writing, not for entertainment.
4. Once you have saved at least 20 to 30 posts that represent the articulation you want, break down why they work. Under each, write three to five bullets: why it did well, what psychological patterns it uses, how it captured attention, what the value is, why people care.
5. Optionally, paste a prompt he provides into an AI along with a post to get a breakdown of why it worked, its structure, and a step-by-step way to replicate it with your own ideas. (The prompt was shown on screen; its contents are not in the transcript.)

The point, he concludes, is that "you are dissecting why already validated ideas have done well." Writing that stops the scroll and "nearly demands readers to absorb the idea" makes "growth a byproduct" (**Source:** `How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md`, 2025-06-08).

Three design choices in the exercise are worth making explicit. The sample is restricted to people admired for their articulation, so the researcher studies what they want to become, not what is merely popular. The anomaly criterion (twice the writer's own average) controls for the writer's audience size: a post that outperforms its author's usual results reveals something about the post, not about the author's reach. And the written breakdown forces articulation: the researcher must say why the post works, which is the skill the exercise is meant to train. The exercise therefore addresses both of the reasons Koe gives for lacking ideas at once.

In 2026 he extends the logic to topics. "**Foundational content topics** are just those that are evergreen. They're the ones that you know work, the ones that you see everywhere, and you should probably implement under your own brand in your own voice because they just work." "**High-performing topics** are the outliers that tend to do very well": when you see a YouTube video "doing so much better than everyone else's videos or even that person's own videos, that's an outlier. That's a signal that you should probably take note of that and put it in your **swipe file**." Then "you take your own ideas and you start to reformulate them so that they work better" (**Source:** `How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md`, 2026-03-15). The swipe file, a marketer's term for a collection of proven material to study, becomes in section 16.3 one of the forms of the idea museum.

The same 2026 period contains Koe's assessment of how most people actually do this research. Some bookmark posts "and hope that they come back to it and remember it when it's time to actually create," then stare at a blank page, write something that isn't good and "wonder why it didn't do well since they're actually doing the steps." Others search YouTube for similar titles before writing ("a solid way of doing it") or go to a creator's account, filter by most popular and study those titles. Others jot ideas as they come "and hope that those ideas are good enough... and sometimes they are." "All of those are fine, but they're not really strategized. They're not really a system... you can rely on. And frankly, they're just time consuming and they're slow" (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md`, 2026-05-14). The verdict is measured: the habits are not wrong, only unreliable. What they lack is what the anomaly exercise supplies: a criterion for selection and a step of analysis. The complaint about bookmarks that are never revisited anticipates the critique of second brains in section 16.3.

#### Researcher and a vessel

In 2026 Koe raises the researcher stance from a technique to a vocation. Addressed to people with multiple interests, the claim is unusually absolute: "Your sole job is to become a researcher and a vessel. And I'm serious about this. You must consider it your full-time job to research": reading books, watching YouTube, listening to podcasts, using the discovery features of his software, "because that's what writers, creatives, and visionaries do. That's what Jordan Peterson does. That's what Andrew Huberman does. That's what Marcus Aurelius and all your beloved figureheads of the past did. They probably don't care about being a 'personal brand', but they do care about their interests." Creating content, "which was once just considered writing before it took the specific form of media on the internet, is their chosen vessel for earning an income" (**Source:** `If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md`, 2026-06-13).

The term **vessel** recurs across Koe's work for the container through which accumulated knowledge reaches others and returns as income (the "creative firepower, vessel, currency" sequence developed in later chapters). Here the order matters: research first, vessel second. The figures he names are offered as examples of people whose public output follows from private research into their interests, not as evidence that research alone produces an audience. The claim also completes the arc of this section. The consumer uses the internet to escape; the researcher uses it to learn with intent; the researcher who treats research as a full-time job and content as the vessel for it has turned consumption into the raw material of a career.

The rest of this chapter describes the researcher's instruments: how to read (16.2), where to keep what is found (16.3) and how to use AI to learn faster without outsourcing the learning (16.4).

### 16.2 Reading

Reading is the researcher's main instrument, and Koe has said more about it than about any other input. His statements span 2022 to 2026 and do not always agree: he recommends summaries and later condemns summary culture; he presents a tactic for reading only the first chapters next to a tactic for finishing books; he titles a video "How To Read Books Fast" and advises within it that "the slower the better." This section reconstructs his position in four movements: why to read, what reading is not for, what to read, and how to read. It ends with his most developed method, reading in two layers with AI. Where his positions conflict, both versions are presented.

#### What reading does

Koe's most compact case for reading comes from a 2024 video called "This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life." If you want a more fulfilling career, better relationships, more intelligence, to rediscover the depth of life and to slow down in a busy, distracted world, read. "Reading is how you acquire new knowledge. Reading is how you expand your worldview. Reading is how you spot new opportunities. Reading is how you train your focus. Reading is how you absorb 10 years of experience in 8 hours." And then a rule that requires no justification: "When you're bored, pick up a book, not your phone. You don't need a reason to read" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

The list names five functions, and they are of different kinds. Acquiring knowledge and expanding a worldview concern content. Spotting opportunities concerns perception, in line with the goal-as-lens idea of Chapter 6. Training focus concerns the act of reading itself: sustained attention on long-form text is practice for the attention that deep work requires (Chapter 12). Absorbing years of experience in hours is a compression claim. The figure is rhetorical rather than measured, and Koe does not hold it constant: a few weeks earlier, in a 2024 video on life as a video game, he said that "books give you 10 years of effort from 6 hours of reading" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The two numbers are not reconciled anywhere in the corpus; what stays constant is the idea that a book compresses someone else's years into a reader's hours.

The same video then reframes the question. Once the case for reading is made, the remaining questions are what to read so that you want the habit, how to read to get the most from it, and which tactics make the change enjoyable. "It's not if you read, it's what you read and how you read it" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). The rest of this section follows that division.

In 2025 Koe reduces the habit to its minimal form as part of a set of daily commitments (one project, one book, and so on). The **one book** habit is to read a single book 30 minutes a day; at that rate you are bound to finish it within six to twelve months, and you can read more, but "one book" matched the structure he was proposing. The reasoning is that to change your life you need knowledge, school probably won't give it to you, so you must self-educate by immersing your mind "in the thoughts of those who have lived the life you want to live." Learning out of curiosity, he says, is "a prime representation of the true," and exploration brings the novel ideas that lead to progress (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29). The time figure (30 minutes) appears elsewhere in his work as part of a 30–60 minute daily self-education habit; the numbers vary across videos, but the principle of a small daily dose sustained for months does not.

#### Why most people read badly

If reading does so much, why does it seem to do so little for most readers? Koe's 2025 video "How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read)" opens with a provocation. He quotes a tweet: "Reading books is now a waste of time. AI reasoning models can distill key insights and tell you exactly how to implement them based on everything they know about you." He was upset at first, he says, and now "kind of just feel[s] bad for this guy." But he concedes a point: "There's some truth to what this guy is saying, but that's because most people in general don't know how to read and they don't know how to remember what they read." Not because they are illiterate, but because they learned to read in a "Prussian style education system designed to create obedient soldiers, compliant citizens, civil servants, and well-behaved workers" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

**Complementary context:** The "Prussian model" refers to the system of compulsory, state-run, age-graded schooling developed in Prussia in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and promoted in the United States by reformers such as Horace Mann. Koe uses it, here and in Part I, as shorthand for an education designed to produce compliant workers; in his reading it explains why people read to pass tests rather than to change.

The diagnosis continues with a description of how most people read. "Reading to most people is a **performative act**. They read to find specific information for a test, be it a school test or a life test. They read to show others that they've accomplished something. Like how people flex that they've somehow both read and retained information from 52 books in a year. They avoid reading difficult books because if they don't understand it, what's the point?" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). Reading as performance has three forms: reading for a test, reading for display and avoiding difficulty. All three are oriented toward an outcome outside the reader's mind: a grade, an impression, a feeling of competence.

A year earlier Koe had put the motive of display in sharper terms. "Why are you reading?" For some it is status: to be able to say you have read a lot and seem like you are doing something with your life, to impress others. "That's fine, but if you haven't created anything that benefits humanity, the pursuit of status will leave you hollow" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). The phrase "that's fine" matters: Koe does not condemn the motive, he predicts its outcome. Reading for status without creation is the consumer's condition from section 16.1 in a more respectable form.

In the 2025 video Koe then lays out the consequences as a chain of four problems:

1. Very few people read.
2. When they read, they don't remember any of it.
3. People don't like discomfort, so they don't read difficult books.
4. Since they don't increase the complexity of knowledge they can make sense of, they can't take on more challenging situations, "the ones that lead to more than superficial and short-lived success."

"They turn 50 years old with the intellectual maturity of a 15-year-old" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The chain runs from avoidance of difficulty to the absence of growth in the complexity one can handle, and from there to the inability to face real challenges and, finally, to shallow success. The logic connects reading to the developmental themes of the book. The capacity to make sense of complex material is not an academic luxury; in Koe's account it limits the complexity of the situations a person can navigate, and therefore the kind of success available to them. The final line is hyperbole, but it states the mechanism: intellectual maturity is a function of the difficulty one has been willing to face.

#### You don't read to find answers

The most counterintuitive part of Koe's position is his claim about what reading is not for. "You don't read to find a specific answer. That's just stupid. Why would you waste 8 hours reading a book just to find a few steps to accomplish something when you can type a question into Google, Reddit, or now AI?" "If you need to figure out how to be more productive or start a business, a book is probably the last place you should look," if you are actually trying to learn "rather than to feel as if you're making progress." He qualifies this immediately: for the big picture of productivity or business, a book is a good place; "but I'd still argue that intelligent and calculated research is still a better option for that specific use case" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The positive claim follows. "You don't read to find information that can already be found. You read to explore the unknown and pursue your curiosity. You read to discover things you didn't know before and didn't even think about searching with Google or ChatGPT. You read to expand your mind to places you didn't know were there. You read to slowly **reprogram** who you are by exposing yourself to new ideas that can't be intentionally searched by an identity that didn't even think to search for them" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

This is the core of Koe's answer to the tweet. The claim that AI makes reading irrelevant, he argues, "assumes that everyone reads because they want to find information that is already known," information everyone else can also find, "and that means you're replaceable if you're going after that information." A search, whether in Google or in an AI chat, can only return what the searcher thinks to ask for, and what a person thinks to ask for is determined by their current identity. A book written by someone else, with a different identity, contains answers to questions the reader would never have asked. Reading is therefore one of the few ways to get outside the closed loop of one's own questions, which is why it serves the identity change described in Part II. The argument also distinguishes two uses of text: reading for how-to (where search and AI are better) and reading to change one's view of the world (where they cannot substitute for the book). This distinction resolves several apparent contradictions in the corpus, as the following subsections show.

#### Reading is about changing your mind

The next step of the 2025 argument goes further. "It's not about what information you have. It's not even about what you do with that information. It's about changing your mind. Because your mind is how you interact with reality. It's how you make decisions that lead to a good or bad life. Your mind doesn't change with a few key points from a book summary or an AI generated summary. It changes when new ideas, actionable or not, fill in missing pieces, challenge old beliefs, or give you a new angle to view a situation from." And because each reader brings different beliefs, goals, problems, story and interests, "no single person reads a book and gets the exact same lessons out of it" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The passage names three mechanisms by which a mind changes: new ideas fill in missing pieces, challenge old beliefs, or provide a new angle. None of them requires the idea to be actionable ("actionable or not"). This is a notable nuance in Koe's work. Elsewhere he insists that "the purpose of knowledge is action," and Chapter 14 is built on that insistence. Here he places mind change first and lets action follow from it. The two positions are compatible if action is understood as the downstream effect of a changed mind rather than a requirement each piece of knowledge must meet on its own; but the shift in emphasis is real and he does not comment on it.

The same video gives the change a precise image. "Most people read to memorize as much as possible because that's how they were trained to read in school." But "you don't read to remember every single sentence in a book. You read to change how you view the world by adding a few nodes to the **web of ideas** that compose your mind, helping you see situations in a new, more complex and meaningful light." He recalls, while admitting he is unsure of the exact quote, Richard Feynman saying that physics deepened his appreciation of nature, because he understood a deeper layer of how it works. Likewise, understanding the inner workings of a plant deepens your appreciation of it, and this applies to "literally any topic or domain of knowledge" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The web of ideas is the model that holds this whole section together. A mind is a network of connected ideas; a worldview is that network seen as a whole. Reading adds nodes. A single new node can reconfigure the network because it connects to many existing ones, which is why a book can change a reader without the reader remembering most of it. The Feynman example adds that the change is not only cognitive: a more complex network makes the world appear richer, so that the same plant or the same night sky is experienced differently. Reading, on this model, changes perception, not just knowledge.

This position marks a change from Koe's earlier work. A 2022 video was titled "Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn)" and aimed at retention, through intention, curiosity and closing the gap between learning and execution. By 2025–2026 remembering everything "is not the point of reading at all," and the purpose is mind change and identity change. The two can be reconciled if the 2022 retention is read as a consequence of practice rather than a goal in itself, but the emphasis has moved, and Koe does not explain the move.

#### Against summaries, and against the obsession with remembering

The model of the web of ideas explains Koe's hostility to summaries. In the 2025 reading video he observes that "book summaries have been around for forever." Many of the most helpful books, "the books that will make you millions of dollars," are available as summaries. "But that doesn't mean that people go and read them," nor that those who read them do anything, nor that those who do anything get results, "because the book summaries are missing what makes it work" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20; the transcript literally says "always get results," but the context indicates "don't always get results"). What a summary removes is precisely what changes a mind: the reasoning, examples and connections through which an idea fills a gap or challenges a belief. The conclusions remain; the mechanism that would make them believable and usable is gone.

In a 2025 video on original thinking he generalizes the point into a critique of a culture. "No, bullet point summaries of something that's popular isn't going to give you anything original. I hate the **bullet point summary culture**." People who comment asking for "a five bullet point summary" are "absolutely missing the point." "It's just cheap dopamine; even in learning, even when you're trying to do something good, you can't escape the cheap dopamine." He links this to his earlier video on "the cheap dopamine epidemic," where short-form content is called "entropic" and long-form content, as long-term gratification, "syntropic": "one leads to more order and clarity in your life, the other leads to more disorder." Hence his conclusion: "Having bullet point summaries for 20 books is going to cause a lot more chaos in your mind than just reading one book, and you're going to get a lot more out of that one book" (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19). (The transcript renders the second term as "Copic"; "syntropic," the opposite of entropic, is the likely word.)

The argument applies the entropy vocabulary of Part III to inputs. Twenty summaries add twenty disconnected sets of conclusions to the mind, none of them integrated, each competing for attention: disorder. One book read fully adds a few well-connected nodes: order. The claim is not that summaries are false but that they are entropic: they increase the number of items in the mind without increasing its coherence.

This position also marks a change. In 2023 Koe described his own process for choosing books: he read short summaries in the Shortform app (an affiliate link at the time) to get a book's big ideas; if he liked them, he listened to the audiobook; if he liked that, he ordered the physical copy, joking that it was "so I can put it on my bookshelf and never touch it again" (**Source:** `The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md`, 2023-07-19; his example was a summary of *The Sovereign Individual*). By 2025 summaries had become "cheap dopamine." The two positions can coexist if the summary is used to choose a book rather than to replace it: in the 2023 process the summary is a filter that leads to deeper engagement, while the 2025 target is summaries consumed instead of books. But Koe never draws that distinction himself, and the change in tone is clear.

The last element of this argument is the line with which Koe opens a 2026 video: "If you need to remember it, it's not important. If it's important, you will remember it." Remembering everything you read has become an obsession, he observes, with videos on the subject reaching millions of views (some by people he is friends with). People feel that if they don't retain what they read, they failed or wasted their time, "when that's not the point of reading at all" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). He returns to the line later in the same video to justify letting AI handle non-original factual material, and in a different 2026 video he uses it to defend messy note-taking (section 16.3). The principle is a heuristic, not a finding: it assumes that importance and memorability coincide, which holds for ideas that connect to one's goals and experience (the nodes of the web) but less obviously for facts one needs only occasionally. Koe's own use of AI and notes for such facts suggests he applies it in that limited sense.

#### The challenging book and the fluff

If the reader is not reading for answers, for display or for retention, what should the experience of reading feel like? Koe's answer is that it should often feel difficult, and that the difficulty is part of the value.

In the 2024 reading video, the first type of book he recommends is a challenging one. "A challenging book is the perfect metaphor for diving into the unknown": you don't know what to expect, you struggle to make sense of it, you take it one page at a time. Then things start to make sense, it feels good, you make connections, there are "dopamine spurts," progress. Soon you can't put the book down; the ideas are life-changing; your mind floods with potential: "I should start that business, I should learn that skill, I shouldn't be distracted right now, I'll go on a walk instead." Such a book teaches more than its ideas; it teaches the shape of progress: "progress is not linear, nothing happens, then everything happens." It won't always make sense, it will overwhelm you, and it's silly to think you shouldn't feel overwhelmed when learning: "confusion and overwhelm mean your mind is searching for answers and trying to learn. Don't stop it" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

The **challenging book** is a metaphor in the strong sense that runs through Koe's work: the experience of reading one is a small-scale model of every venture into the unknown, from learning a skill to building a business. The phrase "nothing happens, then everything happens" is the non-linear growth curve of Chapter 15 experienced on the page, and the reframing of overwhelm as the mind's search for answers repeats the treatment of overwhelm as a signal of learning in section 15.7. The difficult book trains a tolerance that transfers.

Two years earlier, in "The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind)," Koe had given reading the same role as the first of three or four daily practices for diving into the unknown. Many people don't read, he said, because they want "the value without the **fluff**," and he called this "the stupidest argument": "the fluff is where the magic happens," where you make new discoveries. He gave two rules. First, don't stop at the first hint of struggle or lack of comprehension: "that's where you **prime your mind** to actually learn the lesson," whether in one year or ten. Second, when you "catch a whiff" of something interesting or exciting, don't neglect it: stop reading and research it further, because it can lead down a rabbit hole into an obsession and a lifestyle (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20).

He illustrated each rule with his own experience. When he first read Eckhart Tolle's *The Power of Now*, he did not understand some of its teachings; when he reread it five years later, he understood all of it, because the first reading had primed his mind to recognize those experiences when they came. And a fitness book mentioning muscular tension sent him to research the concept, which led to an obsession with fitness and eventually to a lifestyle built around it.

Taken together, the two rules describe two opposite reactions to two different signals. Difficulty is a signal to continue, because the struggle prepares understanding even when understanding does not arrive immediately. Excitement is a signal to stop, because the interesting idea deserves research before the next page buries it. The "fluff," for Koe a common word with his own meaning, is not padding but the reasoning, stories and nuance around an idea, which is exactly what summaries remove and where, in his experience, discoveries happen.

#### Reread the books that change your life

A reader who stops at excitement and continues through difficulty will not finish many books quickly. Koe's response is that the number of books is the wrong metric. "Reading the same five books that truly impact you is worth 50 books of knowledge." He orders his own reading list so that the books "lead to the most pattern recognition," and advises that to become more intelligent, smart or open-minded you should "cycle through the five to seven books that really change your life... so that they can slowly become your worldview, because a worldview is just a web of ideas" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

The phrase "a worldview is just a web of ideas" is the same model as in the previous subsections, here used to justify depth over breadth. Rereading strengthens the nodes and connections a book has already contributed until they become part of how the reader sees. Fifty books read once contribute fifty sets of weakly held ideas; five books read repeatedly contribute a structure.

In 2025 he adds a rule for spacing. He recommends "spacing out how often you read a book by like a year, two years, because once you've gained more experience in life and you come back to the book that initially changed your life or didn't mean anything to you... when you come back to it from a lens of new experience, you tend to get a lot more out of it." "A good piece of advice is to read the books that changed your life over and over again rather than jumping to a bunch of new and different books that don't do anything for you" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). The *Power of Now* story is the clearest case: the same text yielded different lessons because the reader had changed. Spacing ensures that each rereading meets a different reader.

As early as 2022 Koe had defended repetition from the other side, as a writer. He admits he repeats himself a lot and justifies it with an analogy: "Why do you like music? It's the repetition that creates the feeling for you." There are "only so many foundational principles that exist in my work, in the work of many others; it's worth repeating the important things and internalizing those" (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). The analogy works in both directions. A writer repeats foundational principles because they are few; a reader rereads foundational books for the same reason. In both cases repetition is what moves a principle from something known to something internalized, which is the difference between knowing and understanding examined in Chapter 17.

#### What to read

Koe has published several reading lists, and they differ. The 2024 list, from the reading-habit video, consists of seven books he reads and rereads:

| Book | Author | What Koe says it offers |
|---|---|---|
| *The Art of Impossible* | Steven Kotler | A practical and scientific book on achieving the impossible |
| *Flow* | Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi | Understanding how to sustain enjoyment in life |
| *A Brief History of Everything* | Ken Wilber | Making sense of the world holistically |
| *Awareness* | Anthony de Mello | A refreshing, humorous lecture series to help you not take life so seriously |
| *Becoming Supernatural* | Joe Dispenza | Changing your life by breaking the cycle of repetition in the familiar and the known; "potentially pseudoscience," but impactful |
| *The Kybalion* | The Three Initiates | An introduction to Hermetic philosophy and the patterns of reality; "some say this is not a good book for hermeticism," but a good starting point for him |
| *The Way of the Superior Man* | David Deida (a student of Ken Wilber) | Masculine and feminine dynamics and spiritual growth |

"My life would not be the same without these books," he says, and his own book *The Art of Focus* "is kind of a synthesis between all of these"; readers who found his book dense could use the seven as "a primer for it" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). Koe's own qualifications in the table should be kept: he flags Dispenza as possibly pseudoscientific and acknowledges that *The Kybalion* is contested as an introduction to Hermeticism. The list is a record of what shaped him, not a claim about the scientific standing of each book. Several of these authors supply the frameworks of earlier chapters (Csikszentmihalyi's flow and order in consciousness, Kotler's intrinsic drivers, Wilber's developmental maps), which is consistent with his description of *The Art of Focus* as their synthesis.

In a 2024 video on intelligence, asked what to read for "the **generalized principles of reality**," Koe narrows the recommendation further. "If I could give you one thing to read to understand the generalized principles of reality, it would be the Kybalion." Most scripture also helps, "just interpreted in a different way": it is useful for stories and history "or perceived history," but it "speak[s] a different language that you may not understand." You may reach a higher understanding by researching the same ideas in more modern texts, "but that does strip a bit of the magic and soul from it if it's approached from that scientific lens, so read both." He also recommends *The Systems View of Life*: "kind of dense, kind of scientific, there isn't much of a story... but if you're just looking for information, then it's worth reading" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03).

The advice to "read both" ancient and modern texts is a small instance of a larger pattern in Koe's thought: ancient symbolic texts and modern scientific ones are treated as two languages for overlapping patterns, each with something the other lacks (meaning and soul on one side, precision on the other).

In 2026 he adds two symbolic frameworks to study. The first is the Hermetic principles of *The Kybalion*: "Mentalism, correspondence, vibration, polarity, rhythm, cause and effect, and gender. A compact set of lenses that have influenced a lot of new thought traditions. This is where 'as above, so below' comes from." The second is Jungian alchemy: "the psychological and spiritual process of transformation... a framework for how deep change actually works in a person or system" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19). He presents both as lenses, compact frameworks for seeing patterns, not as doctrines to adopt.

**Complementary context:** *The Kybalion* was published in 1908 under the pseudonym "Three Initiates" and presents seven "Hermetic principles"; its connection to ancient Hermetic writings is disputed by historians, which is the controversy Koe alludes to. Carl Jung studied medieval alchemy as a symbolic map of psychological transformation (individuation), notably in *Psychology and Alchemy*. *The Systems View of Life* (2014) is by Fritjof Capra and Pier Luigi Luisi.

The earlier list, from a 2022 video called "7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022," overlaps only partially with the 2024 one. Koe opens that video with a claim about why books with no apparent connection to business belong on a business creator's list. His recommended books "don't really have anything to do with business, but they have helped my business in more ways than one," for two reasons: pattern recognition, and the fact that in business "you're working with other people." Understanding others, improving yourself and gaining self-awareness "is also a business hack, because you are gaining awareness of how other people live"; "you're diving a bit into psychology whether you know it or not" (**Source:** `7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md`, 2022-04-23).

He comments on three of the books in that video, and each comment reveals a different reason to read:

- *The Way of the Superior Man* (David Deida) is "kind of like a spiritual guide to masculinity," about what masculinity "is supposed to be rather than... the egoically driven toxic masculinity that you see nowadays." It helps understand the masculine–feminine balance and being in a healthy relationship as a masculine man. Koe reports Deida's own preface: the book won't really apply to people with more of a feminine essence; it is for those with a masculine essence who don't know how to harness it for fulfilling relationships. Here the reason to read is self-understanding in relationships.
- *The Kybalion* breaks down "seven principles of how life works from a very meta foundational perspective," which helps you navigate the world and zoom out; "I think it is how reality functions," he says, though "you have to read it to understand it" (the principles are not enumerated in that transcript). When he posted the list on TikTok, the comments calling it "satanic" from people who had not read the book revealed, in his view, "what developmental stages people are in." Here the reason to read is a big-picture frame, and the anecdote links reading choices to the developmental stages of Part XIV.
- *The Wisdom of Insecurity* (Alan Watts) is valued less for its contents than for its writing. Some argue all of Watts's work is discredited because "he died an alcoholic or something"; Koe replies that this is in line with his teachings: "he's a bodhisattva, right? He's still human." What he loves is that Watts serves as inspiration "for my writing and just the way I want to be able to think." "He's very poetic. What he says is art... it has taken a lot of practice." The real value is "being awestruck by how he wrote that specific thing. And that alone holds value within itself." Here the reason to read is a model of articulation.

(**Source:** `7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md`, 2022-04-23.)

The three comments, read together, give a taxonomy of reasons to read that complements the 2025 argument: to understand oneself in relation to others, to acquire a frame for reality, and to absorb a way of writing and thinking. None of them is reading for an answer.

#### Read what you love, slowly

On how to read, Koe's first principle is borrowed. He follows a line from Naval Ravikant ("because I love Naval"): "**Read what you love until you love to read**." The reasoning is that the principles of habit formation apply beyond books: a habit sticks when the activity is rewarding. Applied, this means choosing books that truly interest you and, if a book doesn't, being quick to experiment and swap it out, the way you would swap a gym exercise you dislike rather than skipping leg day altogether (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). The analogy guards against a misreading: swapping a book is not quitting reading. The habit is fixed; the book is variable.

In 2025 he turns this into a set of permissions. "If it doesn't interest you, you probably won't learn anything, so you can drop it. You're not in a race to finish the book. In fact, the slower the better. So take a year or two to finish if you need to." There should be no rigid structure, "because that's just not how digesting or consuming information in your mind works." And: "If you hit an idea that is very important to you, you may not want to continue reading." If you keep going, the following information "may take over and you may forget about it completely... And then you don't take anything from the book." "If you take one... life-changing idea from the book, that is worth all the time it took to actually get that idea" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The advice contains a tension that Koe does not address: it comes from a video titled "How To Read Books Fast With AI," and it says "the slower the better." In 2023 he had described reading "extremely slowly," a page at a time. The likely resolution is that "fast" in the title refers to extracting ideas with AI and to non-linear reading, while "slow" refers to the pace of digestion; but the corpus leaves this implicit. What is consistent is the measure of value: not the finished book but the life-changing idea, which justifies the time spent and which should be protected from being buried by the next page. This is the same rule as the 2022 instruction to stop reading when something excites you.

#### Hunt for an idea, or finish the book

The 2024 reading video contains two tactics that point in opposite directions, presented one after the other.

The first is Cal Newport's. Koe describes how the author of *Deep Work* reads five books a month with five tactics: (1) choose more interesting books; (2) schedule reading sessions as you schedule exercise; (3) form a ritual and make it more enjoyable; (4) do **closing pushes** to finish books in one sitting; (5) take everything interesting off your phone. In short, Newport is "an expert at removing distractions and making room for things that are more meaningful." The closing push is the distinctive tactic: when 50 or 100 pages remain, sit down and finish the book at once; if your normal sessions are 15 minutes, stick it out for an hour that day, "because no one ever finishes books" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

The second is Koe's own: "Hunt for an idea or two, then put it down." Most books' gist lives in the first two to three chapters, because books are structured from the big picture (what the whole book covers, holistically) to the technical details (the process or actionable steps). If you want the process or the steps, read further; if you want exactly what the book promises, read the whole book; if you just want ideas or to solve one problem in your mind, the first two or three chapters of "really any book" suffice (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

Koe offers the two as alternatives depending on what the reader seeks, and the 2025 advice not to keep reading "because you feel like you have to finish it when you don't" (examined below) leans toward his own side. The criterion that decides between them is the reader's purpose: finishing serves the reader who wants the whole of what the book offers, including its process; hunting serves the reader who reads for ideas, which, by the argument of this section, is most reading worth doing. The structure Koe attributes to books (big picture first, technical details later) also connects to the principles-over-tactics argument of Chapter 15: the first chapters usually carry the principles, and the later chapters the tactics.

#### Non-linear reading: three books, books everywhere, walks

Koe's remaining tactics share a feature: they abandon linear reading of one book from start to finish.

He often reads three books at a time, one physical, one on Kindle and one as an audiobook, and occasionally rotates topics and genres for pattern recognition. He reads the physical copy first to get the gist in the first few chapters. If he wants to highlight and save it, he downloads the Kindle edition and reads back through it highlighting; the highlights go to Readwise (the app that saves highlights) and are then imported into Cortex, his note-taking software, so he can reference them in his writing (the quotes shown in his videos come from there). The audiobook is either a fiction book or the book he is reading digitally, so that he can repeat and better understand its ideas and catch what he missed. The result, he says, is that his mind holds "an array of ideas, big ideas, not just like a million social posts worth of ideas... three big ideas so I can connect the dots between them" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

The process assigns each format a function: the physical book for the gist, the digital edition for capture, the audiobook for repetition or for pleasure. It also feeds directly into the note-taking systems of section 16.3: reading produces highlights, highlights flow into a library, and the library supplies the writing. The phrase "three big ideas so I can connect the dots between them" states the purpose of reading several books at once: connections form between what is held in mind simultaneously.

The 2025 video confirms the practice and its effect. "You can read multiple books at a time. I often have three books and an audiobook that I bounce between, and you'd be surprised how many connections you make between them, especially if they're on similar topics." He recalls reading on systems thinking, flow and evolutionary psychology while listening to something on Spiral Dynamics (a model of successive stages in the development of values and worldviews, which Chapter 38 examines). Books are spread throughout his house: "Whenever I'm bored, I just pick it up, read for 10 minutes. I get something out of it. I go on a walk. I munch on it. I put on another book." "I don't like reading linearly." It may make you enjoy reading more, because "you don't feel like you have to commit to an entire book when 80% of it isn't something you really care about" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The 2024 video adds two environmental tactics. The first is to have books lying everywhere, "embracing the **chaotic creative lifestyle**": on the desk, in the bathroom ("not in the toilet"), on the kitchen counter, on the patio, on a bookshelf in sight, on the nightstand, so that wherever you go there is "that little temptation to figure out what the words on the page say." The second is to create a reading space you love: he still reads on his patio with a cup of coffee during little breaks, or with lunch. Whenever he would normally watch YouTube or scroll, he tries to replace it with a book, and his goal is "to just find one good idea, that's it" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). Both tactics apply the environment design of Chapter 11 in reverse: instead of removing the cues for cheap dopamine, they multiply the cues for reading.

The last tactic is to walk while listening. Koe treats walks as **creativity blocks**, time set aside for ideas as opposed to productivity. People at home, he says, "don't realize that everything is a distraction": the resistance to sitting down to read comes from little distractions popping into your head unnoticed. So go outside, headphones in, audiobook on. "I'm sure there's some science behind this": moving your body and walking "probably makes your brain more receptive to ideas" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). The claim about the brain is a hedged guess, explicitly marked as such; the practical point, that leaving the house removes the low-level distractions that make sitting down to read feel like work, rests on his experience.

#### Immersion: three books and the economics of effort

When the goal is to learn a domain quickly rather than to read for ideas, Koe's advice changes register. In the 2024 video on life as a video game, describing how to immerse yourself in a new field, he recommends three kinds of input. Read books: "books give you 10 years of effort from 6 hours of reading." Consume lectures, long articles and videos: "3 days of effort in 20 minutes." And follow new people, which is less about consuming short-form content and more about training your mind to belong to that tribe. Then a concrete prescription: "Buy three books: one bestseller, one technical and one historical; burn through them and don't let your obsession die." It may seem a lot, "but we aren't trying to learn something on a surface level, we are trying to become masters as fast as possible" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

The triad of books is a design for coverage: the bestseller supplies the accessible big picture, the technical book the mechanics, the historical book the context of how the field came to be. The effort ratios are, again, rhetorical compressions rather than measurements, and the figure for books differs from the one in the reading-habit video. The broader tension should also be noted, because Koe's statements on whether reading books is learning oscillate over the years. In May 2023 he tells viewers to "purchase one to three books on the topic and devour them" as part of immersing the mind in a new identity (**Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). In December 2023, in the Mastery Method, he says the opposite in its starkest form: "reading books is not learning, watching tutorials is not learning; you learn by building a project and facing reality" (**Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The sharpest contrast falls inside the very video quoted above. In July 2024, a few minutes before "buy three books... burn through them," Koe says that without application learning is leisure, "useless soon to be forgotten brain fog," and that "reading a business book without having a business is kind of dumb," while "reading a philosophy book works in all situations because it's your life that you're applying it to" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). In September 2025 the position hardens again: books and YouTube are not bad, "but that's not learning. That's entertainment" for another purpose (calm, stress relief), and reading in order to learn means "you're filling your mind with endless options. You're just creating more chaos" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The reason he gives in the critical passages is constant (unapplied information is wasted; only struggle with a real problem teaches), and the 2024 video already contains the condition that would reconcile the two sides: a project for technical reading, life itself as the project for philosophy. The tension is also eased by the distinction between reading to see the world (always useful) and reading to learn to do (useful within immersion and alongside a project, as Chapter 14 argues). But Koe does not resolve it explicitly; both registers coexist, and the chronology does not show a settled move from one to the other.

#### Reading in two layers: consumption and digestion

The 2025 video "How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read)" contains Koe's most structured method of reading, and it integrates the principles above with AI. Its premise is a framework: "Smart people read in two layers. The first layer is **consumption**. They soak in the information to the point of overwhelm and struggling to understand. Layer two is **digestion**. They study deeper... to systematically reflect on what they learn and attempt to connect the dots together through action." The layers are not two sequential passes through a book ("it's not like you read a book all the way through once... then read a second time"), though you can do that. They alternate as you read. AI plays a different role in each: in consumption it is a reading partner; in digestion it is a tool for reflection and exploration (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

| | Consumption | Digestion |
|---|---|---|
| What the reader does | Soaks in the information to the point of overwhelm and struggle | Studies deeper, reflects systematically, connects the dots through action |
| What triggers it | Opening the book; continuing while understanding holds | An idea that "really makes you want to stop and think" |
| Role of AI | A "reading companion" holding the text, consulted whenever understanding fails | A tool for reflection: clarifying questions and connections to one's own life |
| Pace | As fast as comprehension allows | Much slower than reading; some ideas take years |

**Preparing to read digitally.** The method requires the AI to be able to reference the text, so the first step is to have PDFs of books. Kindle downloads are increasingly restricted by Amazon (and were hard to use digitally anyway), and with physical books the AI cannot reference the information. Koe's advice at this point is to use a site like Z-Library or its Telegram bot, and he immediately qualifies it: "it's a catch-22. This probably isn't a smart thing to do," and "I would recommend just supporting the author when you can if you download their PDF," by buying the paperback (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). Koe flags his own recommendation as questionable, and it should be read with that flag. The functional requirement is a digital copy the AI can access; how it is obtained is the reader's responsibility.

**The consumption layer: a reading companion.** In Cortex, or any AI that can reference PDFs, Koe uses a workflow he calls "be my reading companion": "help me understand this as I read it," with the PDF dragged in. He uses Gemini Flash for its large context window and speed. The AI first produces clarifications and an overview of the concepts in the book. Then, "what you do inside of this chat is you just come back to it as you're reading." Whenever you reach a point where you lack understanding or want to dive deeper, you type the question or paste the passage from the PDF (his example, while reading *Purpose and Profit*, his own free book, discussed in Chapter 8: "I'm having trouble understanding the concept of deep generalism"), take notes there, and send them each time. Over the course of the book you build "this long context window of you conversing back and forth with the book itself," which deepens understanding as you read and lets you keep going "while maintaining clarity." "You don't want to continue going through the book if you don't understand what the book said" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The reading companion addresses the third of the four reading problems, the avoidance of difficult books. A reader who can ask a question at the moment of confusion has less reason to abandon a hard book, and the conversation becomes a record of what was hard. It also fits the rule of the challenging book with a refinement: struggle remains (the reader still meets the difficulty and must formulate the question), but it is no longer a dead end.

**The transition.** The signal to switch layers is an idea that stops you. "For the consumption layer of reading, you're trying to focus on just getting to an idea that really makes you want to stop and think, and then you transition to digestion." Turn off the audiobook, put your bookmark in, "start talking to AI with it. Start thinking deeper. Start questioning it. Start taking notes. Really sit and think about the idea. That will aid you so much more than continuing reading... because you feel like you have to finish it when you don't" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). This is the 2022 rule (stop when something excites you) and the 2025 permission (you don't have to finish) given a mechanism.

**The digestion layer: making sense of an idea.** One option for digestion is a Cortex workflow called "make sense of a topic or idea," which gives "a bunch of different connections and how it relates to your life... how you can integrate it into your life through actionable steps." Koe demonstrates it, switching from Gemini to Claude ("I like Claude better") with the request: "I want to explore the concept of deep generalism from @Purpose and Profit and how it relates to my life." The AI first asks five clarifying questions: which aspects of the concept resonated, his current approach to learning and skills, his primary life goals, the tension he feels between specialization and generalism, and his specific challenges. Its output has three parts. A topic overview. A "personal connection" section that grounds the abstract idea in his situation: freedom in education, the potential of digital creation, an "AI proof career path" (pure specialists in coding, writing or graphic design are seeing their work automated, and "the human advantage will be in connecting diverse knowledge and providing coherent vision"), an entrepreneurial advantage, and a reframing of his ambivalence: "the tension of choice... isn't indecisiveness. It's recognition that artificial constraints don't serve your goals." And a practical integration section: T-shaped knowledge (breadth across many domains, depth in two or three), synthesis projects, project-based learning, a learning system, generalist mentors and daily habits of cross-disciplinary connection. You can also save a response from the reading-companion chat as a document ("notes") and reference it in this workflow ("I want to explore @notes"). The value of the step, Koe says, is that "it helps you ground what could be this abstract idea into what you can potentially do with your life," which "adds a completely new layer to learning that is rarely taught" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

Two attributions frame the demonstration. The book is Koe's own, so the example shows the author reading and digesting his own work, which makes it a demonstration of the workflow rather than an encounter with an unfamiliar text. And the concept is not his coinage: the "deep generalist" comes from a quotation Koe attributes to Daniel Schmachtenberger ("study the generalized principles of nature and be a deep generalist"), which he adopts as an identity suited to an age of automation; Chapter 17 cites the quotation and Chapter 20 examines the term and its adaptation. The example is worth reading for what it does, not only for its content. The clarifying questions collect the reader's context, so that the connections the AI proposes are connections to this reader's goals and problems. That is the researcher's operation from section 16.1 (connecting what is read to one's own goal) carried out with help. The same two-phase structure, first gather context and then produce, reappears in section 16.4 as the core of Koe's prompt design.

**Why digestion takes time.** The method ends with a principle that ties the whole section together. "Fully digesting a book's ideas takes much longer than reading the book over a weekend. Some ideas even take years to make sense of. There have been many times in my life where the insight didn't strike until I had enough experience for it to click." "Most people remember very little of what they read because they don't see how it applies to their life. They can't integrate the lessons in a way where they don't need to remember every sentence of the book, because it reflects in their daily actions." "You don't need to memorize lines if it's a deep part of who you are." And finally: "The purpose for reading is **behavior change through identity change**. When you spot the connections that lead to the book's lessons clicking in your head, that's when positive behavior change happens" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

Here the threads of the section converge. Reading is not for retention, because what matters is integration; integration happens when an idea connects to the reader's life; connection may require experience the reader does not yet have, which is why rereading after a year or two pays; and the result is not a remembered sentence but a changed identity that shows up in behavior. The formula "behavior change through identity change" links reading to the model of Part II, where behavior follows identity: a book changes what a person does only by changing who they are, and it does so one node at a time.

### 16.3 Second Brain, Commonplace Book and Idea Museum

Reading produces ideas, and ideas arrive at inconvenient times: on a walk, in the middle of a podcast, while scrolling, far from the project they might serve. Every creator therefore faces a practical question: where do these ideas go, and how do they come back when they are needed? Koe's answers to this question have changed more than his answers to almost any other. He has built a template (2022), a personal system adapted from established note-taking methods (2023–2024), a software company around note-taking (2023–2026), and then turned against the most popular version of the idea, calling the second brain "a false god" (2026). This section follows that history in order, because the changes are instructive: what he discarded shows what he concluded a capture system is for, and what he kept across every version shows the principle underneath.

A caveat applies throughout. Many of the passages below come from videos in which Koe promotes his own software (Cortex, later spelled Kortex, later evolved into Eden). The promotional context does not invalidate the method, but it should be kept in mind when he describes tools; where possible, this section separates the function he describes from the product that performs it.

#### Why a place for ideas at all

The simplest statement of the need comes from a 2022 video on learning skills, where creating a note-taking system is step three of a process. As you pursue the intention of improving your life as a project, along with creative or work projects, ideas pop into your head when you are away from those projects and in a creative state. "You need a place to write them down and possibly develop those ideas further" so they fuel your work on your projects, whether the project is your life or your business. He adds that he could write an entire book on note-taking, that he is building software for creators to take notes for their creations ("like *Steal Like an Artist* but in software form"), and that his current system is available in a free seven-day creative challenge (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

The logic follows from the goal-as-lens of Chapter 6. A person pursuing a project notices ideas relevant to it everywhere, but the noticing happens at random times and the project is worked on at scheduled times. A capture system bridges the gap between when ideas are noticed and when they are used.

A 2023 description of his own practice shows the most basic version. For all his newsletters he kept a **database of big ideas**: when a tweet stuck out while he was scrolling, he copy-pasted it into Notion; when he read a book, he highlighted it, and Readwise, connected to Notion, uploaded the highlights. He could refer to them at any time. "You need a place to capture ideas," he says, so you can sprinkle them through newsletters and landing pages (**Source:** `Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md`, 2023-04-09).

In 2024, promoting Cortex as a place to capture ideas, highlights and bookmarks, to create "atomic notes" and to reference ideas in "an interconnected database," Koe states the economic premise that makes the effort worth it. "I talk about writing in all of my videos because it's a first principle to anything in business, anything in making money. Ideas in this day and age are your resource for building wealth. **Ideas are the new oil**." The problem the software addresses is saving ideas "in random apps" and "losing all of your best ideas in just long running notes" (**Source:** `You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md`, 2024-09-29). If ideas are the raw material of value creation, as Part XI argues, then losing them is losing capital, and a capture system is the equivalent of storage for a resource.

#### The commonplace book: an older tradition

Before following Koe's own systems, it helps to start with the tradition he places them in, even though he invokes it only in 2026. Before popularized second brains, he says, people "had a **commonplace book**." Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* "is essentially a private commonplace book with notes to himself and digested philosophy, and it was never meant to be published." Leonardo da Vinci "filled thousands of notebook pages with sketches, observations, questions, and borrowed ideas." Mark Twain, H. P. Lovecraft, Thomas Jefferson, Ronald Reagan, Rick Rubin and others kept variations. The major difference from "your second brain productivity bro" is this: "Their collection of ideas was the fuel for their creations." He would go so far as to say you wouldn't know who these people are without some variation of a commonplace book, while clarifying that a digital commonplace book "isn't like an absolute requirement to success, but it definitely helps" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

**Complementary context:** The commonplace book was a widespread practice in early modern Europe: readers copied passages, quotations, observations and arguments into a personal notebook, often organized under headings ("commonplaces"), as a resource for their own writing and speaking.

The sentence "their collection of ideas was the fuel for their creations" is the criterion against which every system in this section can be measured. A collection is justified by what it produces. The historical examples are illustrations, not proof that the notebooks caused their keepers' achievements, and Koe's own caveat ("not an absolute requirement") keeps the claim modest.

#### 2022: "7 Days to Genius Ideas" and dissect and distill

Koe's first systematic method appears in the 2022 video "Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead," as part of a creative process whose step two was "stop consuming and start researching" (section 16.1). Steps three and four, and the result they produce, form a complete system.

**Step three: get in touch with yourself and write it down.** You can't view what you take in from a very narrow perspective that leaves no room to explore options, connect dots and expand your mind, and this is not possible if you aren't conscious or are closed-minded. If focus is narrowed, creativity "is very hard to exist in that head space"; if focus is "spatial and expanded," creativity comes into play because you can pull from many sources. His example: when you are angry, your mind is so fixated that you can't remember what you ate for lunch; when you are stress-free and open, you feel more creative: "you are much smarter when you're not narrowly focused on something." Getting into that open state allows dopamine "to be more accurate in separating signal from noise," and the signal is what you write down: the "**golden nuggets**" that cause excitement while reading (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28).

The step has two parts: a mental state and an act. The state (expanded rather than narrow focus) is the precondition for noticing; the act (writing down what excites you) is the capture. The physiological language ("dopamine... separating signal from noise") is Koe's explanatory frame for the experience of excitement as a marker of relevance; it should be read as an interpretation, not as a neuroscientific claim he supports with evidence. The practical rule is clear regardless: excitement while reading is the selection criterion for what to keep.

**Step four: dissect and distill.** "You aren't writing their entire book, you're taking a piece out of it": take a piece from one book, another book, social media, and expand on it with your personal experiences, your own summary, how it makes sense to you, and perhaps a step-by-step process for the problems it presents. This forms the foundation of articles, posts and products. The structured way is to take the sentence that caught your attention and write down seven things:

1. The topic it relates to.
2. A brief summary of how you understand it.
3. The common problems associated with that topic.
4. The common benefits of overcoming those problems.
5. The objections or limiting beliefs people have about solving those problems.
6. Your personal experiences and stories with that topic.
7. A step-by-step process for overcoming the problem and reaping the benefits.

(**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28.)

The template is not a note-taking format in the archival sense; it is a writing format in disguise. Its fields are the components of a persuasive piece of content: a topic, a point of view, a problem, a benefit, objections, a story and a solution. Filling it out converts a borrowed sentence into the skeleton of something the note-taker could publish, and the personal-experience field guarantees that the result is not a copy.

**The result: a database of your intellectual property.** Repeated over time, the process builds "this database of just your intellectual property that you can pull from at any given time." Koe describes his own use: to create a video, he comes up with a topic and goes to the database: this note helps with the introduction, this one with the body, this one with the conclusion, this makes more sense here. To people who ask how he is so creative, he answers: "it's structured. It's not like I just pull this out of my ass and start talking." People who can create "off the cusp" are what everyone aims for, and he can do it more now, "only because I developed and understood the ideas in a way that made sense to me, so I can make them make sense to you." His free guide, 7 Days to Genius Ideas, included a template for building the database (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28).

The passage demystifies creativity in a way that connects to Chapter 18: apparent spontaneity rests on prior structured work, and the ability to improvise grows from having already developed ideas until they made sense to oneself.

A year later, recommending the free challenge to beginners, Koe described what it had done for him. It was a content ideation challenge, he said, but it brought more: articulation, mental clarity, thinking at a bigger-picture level, and "it's allowed me to create original ideas from the ideas that I heavily resonate with, and allows me to spin them in my own words" (**Source:** `How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md`, 2023-02-05). The claim is autobiographical, an opinion about his own development, but it names the intended outcome of dissect and distill: originality that starts from resonance with someone else's idea and ends in one's own words.

#### 2023–2024: synthesis software, value notes and the public notebook

In a 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, Koe described, without naming it (so that people couldn't "hop on that"), the software he was building: a system for "how to take an idea, make it your own." You take an idea you like, make it your own, have "some form of a second brain, like an intellectual database," and are able to connect and reference those ideas when you start a project: sales pages, newsletters, articles, tweets, book chapters. It is "a note-taking system at the highest level, but directed at a lens of creating from those rather than just gathering materials." Dickie Bush summarized it: "it's like synthesis" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). The distinction between creating and gathering is the same one that, in 2026, separates the commonplace book from the "second brain productivity bro." It was present in Koe's thinking from the start; what changed later was his judgment about which tools serve it.

In a 2024 compilation Koe named the lineage of his method. His free products (the Power Planner and 7 Days to Genius Ideas) illustrate how a personal system becomes a product: 7 Days to Genius Ideas is how he has creative ideas through note-taking, and he created his own system. "You can use other people's systems as inspiration": he uses Smart Notes and Zettelkasten "in order to create what I call **Value notes**," and was building an entire software around that system to sell (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). The transcript does not explain how value notes work, so what can be said is only that they are Koe's adaptation of those two methods.

**Complementary context:** Zettelkasten ("slip box") is the note-taking method associated with the sociologist Niklas Luhmann, built on small, atomic notes linked to one another; *How to Take Smart Notes* (Sönke Ahrens, 2017) popularized it and distinguishes fleeting, literature and permanent notes. Koe's later "elements" in Kortex are explicitly compared to permanent and literature notes.

The same compilation contains a simpler idea that requires no software at all. As a step in building an information product, Koe tells viewers to document what they learn and to think of social media as "a public note-taking software, a public journal and a public school." When he writes a newsletter, he has everything on Twitter already: he uses Twitter's advanced search to look up, for example, the word "valuable" across his own tweets, and he has material to work from. Social media is a note-taking system, a journal for relatable thoughts, worries and ideas, and good writing practice (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). The **public note-taking** idea dissolves the line between capture and publication: the note is published as it is written, and the archive is searchable by anyone, including its author. It connects to learning in public (Chapter 14) and anticipates the later idea, in 2026, that ideas should be solidified "through the act of thinking, writing, and sharing in public."

#### 2024: Kortex, Core Notes and the database of intellectual property

By 2024 Koe's software, Kortex (spelled Cortex in many sources), was approaching launch, and his videos from that year describe both a refined method and the mechanics of the tool.

**Core Notes.** Alongside Kortex, which he describes as "the second brain software, note taking, writing software that we built," his team created a system called **Core Notes** (transcribed once as "cor notes"). It had been taught in the free 7 Days to Genius Ideas course, which was to be replaced by a free second brain course inside the Kortex community; "over 70,000 people have learned this system." It is meant for writers, creators and marketers, not for people who just want to save information, to turn ideas into "their own **creative firepower** that can be used as **Legos** for whatever content they create." And it does not require Kortex: "you need to learn to think like this" (**Source:** `The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md`, 2024-10-06).

The process has three steps. First, have an idea: from a conversation or a video (he uses ideas from the same video as examples: "the death of the personal brand," "brand as an environment," "content is novel perspectives," "how to think with impact"). Second, pick three or four of six elements and flesh out the idea with them:

| Element | What to write |
|---|---|
| Problem | A problem associated with the idea and how it impacts people's lives |
| Goal | The desirable goals people want to achieve to overcome the problem |
| Example | A personal experience or tangible example that grounds the idea |
| Benefit | The benefits of overcoming the problem and achieving the goal |
| Process | A step-by-step process for overcoming the problem and achieving the goal |
| Concept | "A word or name that helps make sense of everything above" |

Third, write it down now. One sentence per element is enough to start, but it helps to keep expanding. Notes are never done: you keep capturing more ideas into them, fleshing them out and making connections. Then you string notes together into a piece of writing, or turn individual elements into social posts. Doing this with multiple ideas and connecting them, he says, makes you realize "I know so much more than I thought I did," and lets you write articulately (**Source:** `The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md`, 2024-10-06).

Core Notes is a refinement of the 2022 template, and comparing the two shows what Koe learned in between. The seven fixed fields of dissect and distill (topic, summary, problems, benefits, objections, experiences, process) become six optional elements, of which three or four are chosen. Topic and objections disappear as fixed fields; goal appears as the positive counterpart of the problem; and **concept** is added: the act of naming the idea. Koe does not explain the change. Two effects are visible: the lower requirement (three or four elements, one sentence each) makes the method easier to start, and the concept element makes the note-taker coin a term, which Chapter 18 treats as one of the ways original thinking becomes visible. The metaphor of **Legos** describes the use: notes are not finished pieces but interchangeable blocks that can be assembled into a newsletter, broken into posts or connected to other notes.

**The database of intellectual property.** Koe's 2024 descriptions of Cortex return to the phrase he had used in 2022. Promoting the app in his reading-habit video, he says "the entire point of it is to create your fridge, or to have a database of intellectual property that is your own": to store ideas, cultivate your own ideas and discover new ones through connections. "We are building a brain for you to have" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). The "fridge" metaphor suggests a store of ingredients from which meals are cooked as needed.

A December 2024 video on building a one-person business from zero gives the idea a business dimension. Writing the newsletter in one tool (beehiiv), the products in another (Stan) and the posts on social media leaves them "lost, disconnected and disjointed," while you also have ideas to save. Koe recommends doing it all in Cortex, where he writes posts, threads, newsletters and products and plans his days: "a more minimalist version of Notion" built for writers and creators, with capture (texting yourself ideas), connecting and referencing information, saving Kindle highlights and, at the time, upcoming AI synthesis and chat. The reasoning goes beyond convenience. Products and services usually include worksheets or templates (weekly coaching worksheets, course templates, the "life reset planner" he gave to email subscribers). If you create these in one place, "you're technically just building a database of intellectual property that you can eventually package up and sell," for example as a template micro-product (**Source:** `Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md`, 2024-12-01). In this version, the database is not only a source for writing but an asset: the accumulated byproducts of one's work become a product.

A third use is persuasion. To make content more persuasive, Koe says in a 2024 video on writing frameworks, "you need some kind of **swipe file** or just database" to save Kindle highlights, tweets, links to scientific studies, or your own ideas that back up your arguments. He has his own template for videos and newsletters inside Kortex, and that is how he fleshes out how his ideas will be persuasive (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). Here the database holds evidence: the material that turns an opinion into an argument.

**The mechanics: tags, connections, elements and the weekly outline.** The same 2024 video walks through how Koe used Kortex, and together these passages describe a complete workflow.

*The weekly outline.* Every week he duplicates an outline template, changes the title and adds tags. The template is divided into sections he calls **elements** (like sub-notes or atomic notes, each with a title, customizable and linkable). It contains an organization element; a brain dump; topics he wants to write about; potential hooks, researched from YouTube, his past videos and his tweets to decide naming and hook; connections, used to search through stored ideas; a research element with embedded tweets he will reference; and questions. Then comes what he calls "the meat" of persuasion: for each topic, he fills in problems related to the topic, goals ("the opposite of a problem"), personal experiences, examples, and a process. "You can think of this as problem and amplify, and then solution." From all of these he creates a "key points" element, which is what he writes from. The outline feeds the newsletter, the YouTube titles and the tweets of the week (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). The persuasion fields are the Core Notes elements applied at the level of a week's topic, and "problem, amplify, solution" names the copywriting structure (problem–agitate–solve) they serve.

*Tags, connections and panes.* Tags are categories or keywords: clicking one shows all documents with that tag, which helps find more ideas for writing (his example is a "writing tips" tag that gathers all his writing tips). Connections are backlinks between documents; he follows them to find ideas to include and to dive deeper. Panes let him open several documents side by side. "The more you keep up with connections and tags, the more connected your second brain becomes" (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). This sentence matters for what follows: it states that the value of the system depends on maintenance, and it is precisely this claim that Koe abandons in 2025–2026.

*Capture.* Most writers, he observes, write a document at their computer and, through the week (or while writing a book), capture scattered ideas on their phone. Kortex's "entire thing is turn scattered ideas into articulate writing." **Capture** is "pretty much texting ideas to yourself" into an inbox; captures can be tagged and connected. A "tweet idea" tag filters all his tweet ideas; captures connected to the current newsletter (at the time, "the greatest skill of the 21st century") sit beside his outline. Most people keep a running Apple Note and can't find their ideas "even though they think they do." And the best ideas won't come when you try to brainstorm them all at once, which is why capture throughout the week matters (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). The last point justifies the whole apparatus: ideas cannot be produced on demand, so they must be collected as they come and retrieved when they are needed.

*For people who don't take notes.* If you are not a note-taker, go through your newsletter and highlight the parts you would want to turn into elements (searchable, connectable, taggable sub-notes). "My note-taking is done with my writing." Most writers don't like taking fleshed-out notes, so they can turn their writing into broken-apart notes instead. He compares the types of elements to the permanent and literature notes of Zettelkasten (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). This reverses the direction of the system: instead of notes becoming writing, writing becomes notes, which then feed future writing. It is the same move as the public notebook, inside a private tool.

*The library.* The video also shows Kortex's library, integrated with Readwise, which connects books and sources (clipped web highlights, Kindle highlights, bookmarked tweets) that can be connected and tagged; a PDF reader (EPUB and article readers were in development); and the speed of his workspace of almost 1,300 documents, "faster than a 10 document Notion workspace." At the time, Kortex was pre-launch, with a waitlist, a free tier, and a free community and second brain course planned for September to November (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). These are product details, recorded here because they show the pipeline of section 16.2 (Kindle highlights to Readwise to the writing tool) as it existed in 2024.

#### The story of Cortex

Koe's ideas about note-taking cannot be separated from the software he built, and the software's trajectory is part of the evidence for how his views changed. Its history can be pieced together from passages spread over four years.

The origin, as he told it in 2026, lies in Roam Research. He had tried all the systems; Roam "completely changed how I write my newsletters. I built a knowledge base of very potent ideas, and then I was able to pull from those when it was time to write, because the research was already there. My note-taking was essentially like **preparative research**." Cortex, a few years later, was not meant as a second brain, but it became one, because he tried to put his writing process into software, and that process needed notes, connections and tags (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). In 2024 he gave the reason for building it: other apps "didn't fulfill my needs as a writer, creator and someone who values self-improvement knowledge and idea generation" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10).

The lineage of the method is documented in a 2024 aside: the idea-capture system from his free course (7 Days to Genius Ideas) was one of the initial ideas that led to his writing course, and "the system in that is being used as the foundation of our writing app" (**Source:** `The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md`, 2024-06-16). The sequence runs from a free lead magnet to a paid course to software, which is itself an example of the system-to-product process Koe describes in Part X.

The first users were students. At the end of 2023 Koe announced that Cortex, "the second brain software" he had been building, was nearing completion, and that version zero would launch to students of **Cortex University**, not to the public. The program had 40 people at the time, with cohorts limited to keep it impactful; he warned that it was very expensive and that the price was not on the landing page. Its goal was to build the best program on the internet and, within one or two years, compete with college-level programs, helping people "create their own career rather than be assigned one" as **synthesizers**, with a promise of becoming one in 90 days (**Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31). The synthesizer, the person who connects and makes sense of ideas across domains, is the subject of Chapter 18; that the note-taking software was launched inside a program to train synthesizers shows what the tool was for.

In September 2024 Koe described the product's ambition with a mixture of modesty and confidence. Cortex "isn't anything revolutionary or new; it's just a bit better than the products that other people have grown tired of and aren't the best solutions for writers, creators, marketers." Notion, Obsidian and similar apps (one name is unclear in the transcript) are useful for their use cases, but many note-taking apps are "just there for jotting down scattered ideas and having a place where things go to get lost," and don't help you serve your purpose. You have work to do, and its quality directly affects your ability to survive and thrive: "If your mind is the interface of anything that you do, Cortex is your second brain to do that better, and to store all of that in a way that's just ready to be distributed to other people." Even if it doesn't reach Notion's level (which he was "fairly confident" it could in five to ten years), the team had a fraction of the size and costs, had taken no venture capital, and could pivot and move fast with current technology: "even if we fail catastrophically, I think the failure landing point for something like Cortex would be at least eight figures annual" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md`, 2024-09-15). The phrase "**your mind is the interface**" condenses the premise of the whole product: if all of a knowledge worker's output passes through the mind, a tool that extends the mind improves all of it.

The app was also meant for more than writing. In 2024 Koe offered a Cortex template with sections for anti-vision, vision, weekly review and tasks, "like a planner that you can duplicate every day," adding that you can also just write the contents on paper (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10). In 2025 he described a "**life reset map**" workflow inside Cortex for people who don't know what their ideal life is: an interview asks key questions about your situation, skills and interests, and "spits out a first iteration of your ideal life with your vision, skill development tree, long and short-term goals," which you save as a document to reference or iterate on, with AI or not, as you go about life (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). Both are promotional mentions, but they show that the "second brain" was also a place for the direction-setting documents of Part IV (anti-vision, vision, goals), and the life reset map uses the same interview-then-output structure that section 16.4 describes for prompts.

The ending came in 2026: "Cortex inevitably failed... it didn't really fail, but it very painfully evolved into the version of Eden it is today," which, he said, was why he hadn't been posting much (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). The predicted floor of eight figures a year and the later admission of a painful evolution are not reconciled in the corpus. The product was renamed and redefined: Eden was described in 2026 first as an "AI canvas and drive" and then as an AI content strategist. The arc belongs to the case study of Koe's own trajectory in Part XIII; here it matters because his views on note-taking changed along with it.

#### 2025: store and retrieve

The first sign of change appears in a November 2025 video, "Kortex: The $3 Million Dollar Mistake," a conversation with his cofounders about what went wrong. "It's really hard to get across what a second brain is to anybody who's not in the productivity niche," Koe says: it makes sense as a concept but not as a process. Building community through writing cohorts and Kortex tutorials on YouTube generated support questions like "I have to use tags, I have to use backlinks, I have to connect things... I'm not really getting what I have to do." His cofounder Matt reduced the idea to its function: your first brain stores and retrieves information quickly; you store information so you can retrieve it for projects: content, books, research papers, video scripts, any creative or information endeavor. Koe adds: "it's a curated space," not the "water hose" of Google or AI, but your favorite ideas, the ones that mean something to you, saved for a later project. If they can't be found or used, "you're back at ground zero." Matt's formulation: "it's like a **search engine for your memories**": one shortcut to save anything without thinking, and then search by exactly what you remember (for example, "Dan Koe one person business") to get the exact moment you saved, "because the way that he worded it there is the way that... made it click for me." Compared to Kortex's manual tags and backlinks, Koe concedes: "it's not there" (**Source:** `Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md`, 2025-11-24).

The passage contains the diagnosis that drives the rest of the evolution. Users did not fail to understand what a second brain was for; they failed to sustain the maintenance it required. The function (store and retrieve) is simple; the 2024 implementation (tags, backlinks, connections kept up by hand) made the function depend on a discipline most people do not have. The phrase "search engine for your memories" belongs to Matt; "curated space" is Koe's addition, and it preserves the selection criterion from 2022: what goes in is what means something to you.

The same video addresses a second problem: **context**, which Koe uses in a specific sense. "If you use AI, you know that one of the big problems is context": giving the model the right information. His example: save the reels you want to emulate in a "best reels" folder, @-mention the folder in a chat, and ask "break down what works about all these reels." "You can't chat with a reel anywhere else, or a YouTube video"; where you can, it is usually only the transcript. The AI chat his cofounder Ari built for Eden also uses visual information: "break down the shots in this YouTube video and explain why it worked," "why is the hook in this reel visually appealing?" (**Source:** `Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md`, 2025-11-24). The capture system, on this view, is also the context supply for AI: what you have saved is what you can give the model to work on.

Koe then describes the workflow the new design enabled. The chat interface is "one-dimensional": one chat, copy-pasting in and out, new chats to clear context. A **canvas** (he compares it to Miro, mind mapping or a collage) lets you drag in PDFs, YouTube videos, reels and your own video files (he mentions using it as a Dropbox replacement: searching B-roll by shot, clipping it and sending it to an editor). For example, put a raw filmed video, one of your better videos, a few reels and your brand guidelines on the canvas, connect them to an AI chat as context, and ask it to "break down this video and replace it with something from this video"; any AI chat can connect to any item: "free form visual spatial AI." He uses it weekly for newsletters. He jots a small idea as a note and drags it next to where he writes; he adds an AI node for research. He takes fewer manual notes, because many of his ideas come from YouTube videos or audiobooks: he adds the video to the canvas, connects it to an AI chat and asks "what was the idea about this?" or "explain this concept to me in the way the video does." "I'm not asking the AI to write it all for me": he has a rough idea and sources, and he needs the information synthesized, which is different from finding the exact part. The result is an outline, research, a thrown-in idea and an image side by side (**Source:** `Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md`, 2025-11-24).

Compared with the 2024 weekly outline, the structure of the week is the same (an idea, research, sources, an outline, writing), but the labor has shifted. Manual notes are partly replaced by saving the source itself and asking an AI to retrieve the idea from it when needed. The line "I'm not asking the AI to write it all for me" marks the boundary that section 16.4 develops: AI retrieves and synthesizes sources; the idea and the writing remain his.

#### 2026: the idea museum and idea density

In January 2026, in a video for people with multiple interests, Koe describes the capture habit under a new name. "Step one is to build an **idea museum**. This is the most useful thing." It is what marketers call a swipe file: a document or a folder in Eden, Notion or Google Docs. (He describes Eden at this point as an intelligent drive that stores files, PDFs and videos, transcribes and downloads YouTube or social links, makes them referenceable with AI and lets you work in canvases or projects.) But the tool is secondary: "The point is that you need somewhere to jot down ideas as soon as they come to mind. This is a critical habit" (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20).

Step two is to fill it. "Have three to five sources of information that have high **idea density**," sources where you consistently think "Damn, I wish I wrote that." Do it "without judgment": don't let "it's been said over and over" or "too basic" cloud your mind; jot it down, then reframe it and post. The sources fall into three kinds:

1. **Old or little-known books.** Koe has five books he rereads over and over: "these are where the timeless principles live. They're untouched by trends."
2. **Curated blogs, accounts or books.** Farnam Street (which curates the best ideas for modern intellectuals), Navalism (which curates Naval's best ideas), the Maxwell Daily Reader (John Maxwell's best ideas, one day at a time for a year). They "do a lot of the heavy lifting" by exposing you to validated ideas.
3. **Heavy-hitting social accounts.** He keeps a list of about five; when he has nothing to write, he scrolls their pages, finds something he has an opinion on and writes about it.

He adds that you should also discover ideas yourself, in passing, while scrolling (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20).

Idea density is a term Koe applies in the same video to one's own output: "A metric to aim for in your content is idea density, which means that the amount of high signal or really good ideas is just everywhere, and that slowly increases over time with time and effort, and that's what creates a brand that's worth following and paying for, because you just hit the mark all the time" (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20). The two uses are linked: a creator whose inputs have high idea density can produce outputs with high idea density. The idea museum is where the conversion happens. The emphasis on old books that are "untouched by trends" repeats the rereading principle of section 16.2 from the creator's side, and the instruction to capture "without judgment" protects against the perfectionism that would reject a foundational idea for being too familiar, which matches the section 16.1 observation that foundational, evergreen topics "just work."

#### 2026: the second brain as a false god

The same month, Koe began to criticize the practice his company had been built on. Watching videos about managing multiple interests from creators he likes (he names Odysseas: "I love you"), he says he doesn't think they are on the right track. Speaking "as someone with a note-taking software," he doesn't think that creating a note-taking system to manage notes that "don't serve any higher goal in your life" is the answer, even if that goal isn't directly tied to money. And he explains why money still matters to the question: "If money is a **currency of agency**, expressing your agency, then money is important, because that allows you to discover something more meaningful and to choose that meaningful thing over the options that are available to you right now, in a culture that glorifies productivity as God" (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20). "Currency of agency" treats money as the means through which a person exercises the capacity to choose and act for themselves; Part XII develops the idea (Chapters 34 and 35). The criterion is the one from Chapter 8's goal hierarchy: a note-taking system is a means, and a means that serves no higher goal is a form of productivity worship.

In July 2026 the critique became categorical. Koe says he has never been the most organized person and that building a second brain or an idea system is "like a **false god**," because systems succumb to entropy and fall apart over time. However perfect your Notion system, it will fall apart; in a year you will have evolved, you'll try a new system, and the same will happen. "Just get over it. Embrace the mess. Embrace the chaos." His greatest success, he says, came from embracing the chaos (not completely) and jotting ideas in the same place so that they are at least recorded. Ideas get lost all the time, but that's not the problem: "If you need to remember it, then it's not important. And if it's important, you'll remember it." Writing an idea down is simply the act of putting thoughts into words (**Source:** `The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md`, 2026-07-27).

The argument applies the entropy model of Part III to the note-taking system itself. Any structure requires energy to maintain; a person who changes over time will stop maintaining a structure designed by an earlier self; the system decays. Rather than fight that decay, Koe proposes accepting it and lowering the requirement to a single habit: put ideas in one place. This directly contradicts his 2024 statement that "the more you keep up with connections and tags, the more connected your second brain becomes," and his 2022 "it's structured." He does not present the change as a correction; the evidence for it (users who didn't understand what to do with tags and backlinks, his own disorganization) appears in the 2025 and 2026 videos. The saying about importance and memory, which in section 16.2 justified not memorizing books, here justifies losing notes: the system is not meant to preserve everything, only to catch enough.

#### 2026: misused, not useless, and the second subconscious

In August 2026 Koe gave his fullest account of what went wrong and what he proposes instead, in a video titled "How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone." He begins with the waste. People capture ideas, take notes, hit bookmark or save, "and then never look at them again"; there is "a lot of gold that's just sitting there." "I'm not here to say that second brains are completely useless. I just think that they are misused." "Organizing your entire life inside of Notion is not going to help you very much, because you bookmark and you take notes just for them to never be seen again. So you quite literally wasted your time thinking you were learning, when you could have actually been learning in the first place, and engaging with reality and trial and error" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09; earlier in the same video he dismisses hoarded second brains more crudely).

The phrase "thinking you were learning" places the misused second brain in the same category as mental masturbation and summary culture: an activity that produces the feeling of progress without its substance. Its alternative, "engaging with reality and trial and error," is the build-to-learn principle of Chapter 14.

Then he renames the concept. "Rather than this being a second brain, I like to think of it as a **second subconscious**. Because when you create something, your subconscious spits out relevant ideas that contribute to the quality of the creation, but it's still limited in processing power. And sometimes it decides to just not help you at all, leaving you in a creative rut." What second-brain systems miss is that "they don't have a project to apply the ideas to. It doesn't spit out ideas when the time is right." Later in the video he adds that, used correctly, it is "an externalization" of the web of ideas that composes your worldview, "fuel for the things that you put in public... a layer between you and your published work" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

The renaming changes the model of what the system does. A "second brain" suggests a second place where thinking happens, organized like a library. A "second subconscious" suggests a store that works the way the subconscious does during creation: it offers relevant material while one is working on something, without being asked item by item. The subconscious already does this, but with limited capacity and unreliably; the external store supplements it. The definition makes the project essential: without something being created, there is nothing for relevant ideas to surface into. And the phrase "an externalization of the web of ideas" ties the system back to the model of reading in section 16.2: the store is the outside counterpart of the network that reading builds inside.

From this follows what Koe calls the most useful application of a knowledge system. Most note-taking and second-brain software "still require manual upkeep. Some of them have an AI chat tacked on... but they're missing the most useful application of them all, which is make it so your ideas don't disappear into the void, because your creative work depends on your ability to resurface them at the right time." The goal is to "just be able to drop an idea in and then find it when we need it, when we're writing, creating or just working on a project." The principle he states is to use AI to aid creativity without taking from it. He gives two options: Claude Code with Obsidian, or modern software such as MyMind or Eden that auto-tags, categorizes and embeds knowledge "in **meaning space**, so it can connect seemingly unrelated ideas to birth novel insights" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

#### The 2026 tools: a Claude and Obsidian setup, and embeddings

Koe describes both options in detail, and both are designed to remove the manual maintenance that sank the 2024 approach.

**Claude and Obsidian.** He summarizes what he presents as a popular setup:

1. Download Obsidian and Claude.
2. Open Claude Code or Claude Cowork and select your Obsidian vault folder (Obsidian stores files locally).
3. To create prompts or skills, "just ask it, 'Hey, how do I create a skill?... a good prompt.'" You are "not trying to one-shot things. Working with AI is as close to working with the process of trial and error itself... you still have to refine, refine, refine. And if you settle for slop or mediocrity, that's what creates slop or mediocrity."
4. Create a "save an idea" skill, which creates a note with a clear title in an inbox folder whenever you drop in an idea, a link or a post.
5. Create a "process my inbox" skill, which goes through the inbox, adds tags, moves notes to the right folder and adds backlinks to related notes. The two skills work in tandem so that the second brain grows and organizes itself; while writing, you can ask "what other ideas could work with this?"
6. Ask Claude to help define your taxonomy (tags and categories), which you review, approve and iterate on.

From then on you simply drop ideas from your phone or desktop: "add this to my brain." The scope is idea capture for creative projects (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

The setup keeps the 2024 structure (inbox, tags, folders, backlinks) but assigns its maintenance to an AI. The human tasks shrink to dropping ideas in and approving the taxonomy. The warning about "slop" applies the refinement principle of Chapter 15 to the system's design: an AI-maintained system is only as good as the iterations its owner puts into it.

**Embeddings.** The second option rests on a concept Koe explains for non-specialists. Keyword search finds most ideas, "but good writing and creative work come from novel connections... not keyword search or that AI brainstorming synonyms." Tools like MyMind and Eden categorize, tag and embed everything (searchable without using up one's Claude allowance, and Eden can also connect to Claude through MCP). They "can show you what's nearby... the novel connections or the novel relations. And that's because each item is embedded with 1,500 plus numbers that act as GPS coordinates for an idea. They don't need to keyword match." That lets you "drop an idea in, forget about it, and then be able to surface it while you're writing." He warns against building this yourself: it means maintaining "a vector database, an embedding API, indexing scripts, re-embedding when notes change, keeping the index in sync when files are renamed, and caching." "Everyone just thinks, 'Oh, I'm going to build my own app.'... No, you're not," except for simple local apps; a search for outliers, he says, would cost "10 to 20,000 dollars" to store and maintain. He notes that these features of Eden are free (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

**Complementary context:** An embedding is a numerical representation of a piece of text (or image) produced by a machine learning model, typically a vector of hundreds or thousands of numbers, arranged so that items with similar meanings lie close together. Searching by proximity in this "meaning space" finds related items even when they share no words, which is the property Koe's "GPS coordinates" image describes.

The embedding image explains why the second subconscious can work without maintenance. In the 2024 system, connections existed only if the user made them (backlinks, tags). In an embedded store, every item already has a position relative to every other, so connections can be computed when needed: an idea saved a year ago surfaces because it sits near the one being written about now. That is the technical counterpart of "resurface them at the right time." The cost figures and the advice not to build it oneself come in a promotional context and should be weighed accordingly.

A short passage from a 2026 video on strategic thinking shows the system at work in Koe's own research. "I read a lot of stuff, I save a lot of stuff, I highlight a lot of stuff, and then when I choose a topic, I just ask the AI chat to look through my library, and it can find novel relations and connections between all of the ideas I've saved and give me potential ideas that I can include in videos like these. The quotes that I used throughout each section were actually done via that process" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19). The process has two phases with a clear division of labor: the human reads, saves and highlights generously (curation); the machine searches the curated library for relations when a topic is chosen (retrieval and connection). The topic itself, and what is done with the connections, remain the author's.

#### 2026: curating the ideal mind and closing the loop

The August 2026 video completes the system with three steps that restate, in Koe's latest vocabulary, what a capture system is for. The first step is the setup just described. The second and third concern what goes in and what comes out.

**Capture the ideas that represent your ideal mind.** "Capture the ideas that best represent your **ideal mind**. Because when AI can create anything, curation matters more than ever. You need a curated space for ideas that spark inspiration. The opposite of an endless fire hose of noise we call a social media feed." What to capture: things "that form your worldview or are powerful enough to eventually form your worldview": "you listen to that podcast and you're like, 'Oh, wow, that's a really good idea.'... That's what you save." "That's what your fancy note-taking system was meant to be... You just never got around to solidifying those ideas as a part of yourself, usually through the act of thinking, writing, and sharing in public, so that you can receive feedback from the market and let the best ideas win" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

The "ideal mind" is the mind one would want to have; the curated store is a model of it built from outside. The argument for curation is an argument about scarcity: when AI makes generation abundant, the scarce act is choosing, and taste (Chapter 18) becomes the skill that matters. The final sentence names what most note-takers skipped: the ideas were collected but never made part of the self, which happens through thinking, writing and publishing, and through the feedback publishing produces.

**How to curate.** "You read more books. You let your curiosity guide you. You reject doomscrolling and embrace curated lists of authors and thinkers who are dedicated to producing good ideas. You make time for research, because as a writer or creator, that's 80% of your job." Then a clarification about AI, addressed to viewers who might take the video as anti-AI: "I'm not choosing one side... I'm not saying, 'Oh, you touch AI and then you're just brain dead.'... But I also didn't say that you shouldn't read or that you should outsource your creativity... It's just a tool. Stop... overexaggerating it in your head. You still need to do the things that lead to a good mind and life... And if AI is taking away from that, then stop using AI for that specific use case or change how you use it" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). The figure of 80% for research restates, as a proportion, the 2026 claim from section 16.1 that research is the creator's full-time job. The rule about AI is functional: judge each use by whether it adds to or subtracts from the activities that build a good mind.

**Close the loop.** "Step three is to build your ideal mind through the act of creation... We need to **close the loop**. We have the system for capturing and retrieving knowledge, but now we need the project to apply it to so that it doesn't go to waste." Without a project, "we still aren't capturing specific knowledge, because we don't have a reason to learn. We aren't giving our mind the frame from which it can effectively signal that ideas are important enough to capture." The goal is to be smarter, retain what we learn and sound articulate, "for superficial reasons, maybe, but also because the outcome of our lives depends on the decisions that we make and our level of smartness and ability to articulate our own value." The project can be anything (an image, a design, a Photoshop piece); Koe goes on to describe his own writing process as his example (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

The closing of the loop brings the section back to where it began, and to the causal chain of section 16.1. The consumer had "nothing to build, nothing to frame and guide learning." The 2026 argument says the same of a capture system: without a project, the mind has no frame for deciding what is important enough to capture, so it captures either everything or nothing useful. The project is what turns collection into curation, and curation into creation.

#### What changed and what stayed

The evolution can be summarized in a table, followed by what remained constant.

| Period | System | What the user maintains | What the system is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 7 Days to Genius Ideas; dissect and distill (seven fields) | Fleshed-out notes from "golden nuggets" | A database of intellectual property from which videos are assembled |
| 2023 | Database of big ideas (Notion + Readwise); synthesis software; social media as public note-taking | Copied tweets, synced highlights, public posts | Material to sprinkle into newsletters and landing pages; "creating rather than gathering" |
| 2024 | Kortex: Core Notes, elements, tags, connections, weekly outline | Tags, backlinks, elements, templates ("the more you keep up... the more connected") | Writing, persuasion, and a packageable database of intellectual property |
| 2025 | Store and retrieve; context folders; canvas with AI nodes | Saved sources; fewer manual notes | Retrieval for projects; context for AI |
| 2026 | Idea museum; second subconscious; Claude + Obsidian or embedding tools | Dropping ideas in one place; curating sources; a project | Resurfacing ideas at the right time; building the ideal mind through creation |

Across all five periods three things hold. First, the selection criterion: what goes in is what excites or resonates ("golden nuggets," "I wish I wrote that," "that's a really good idea"). Second, the purpose: the collection exists to fuel creation, not to be an archive; the 2023 phrase "creating rather than gathering" and the 2026 phrase "their collection of ideas was the fuel for their creations" say the same thing. Third, the dependence on a project: from the 2022 "place to write them down... so they fuel your work on your projects" to the 2026 "close the loop."

What changed is the theory of maintenance. In 2022 and 2024 the value of the system depended on structure kept up by hand; by 2026 Koe regards that structure as doomed by entropy and assigns it either to nobody ("embrace the mess") or to an AI (skills, embeddings). The two positions can apply to different people (structure for those who sustain it, mess for most), and the notes keep their value as raw material for creating and teaching, not as an archive. But Koe does not frame the change as a correction; it is visible only by comparing what he said in different years.

### 16.4 AI for Learning

The previous sections have already shown AI in supporting roles: a reading companion that holds the book, a digestion tool that connects an idea to one's life, a retrieval layer over a library of saved ideas. This section treats AI directly as a tool for learning. Koe's statements on the subject belong mostly to 2025 and 2026, and they share a premise and a warning. The premise is that AI, used well, restores a form of education that industrial schooling replaced: personalized guidance at the moment of need. The warning is that AI used badly becomes one more way to feel as if one is learning while outsourcing the thinking. Most of his method can be read as a set of devices for getting the first without the second.

#### The return of apprenticeship

Koe frames AI education historically, in a 2026 video on how he would build a one-person business if he started over. What AI offers, he argues, is not new. Before mass education, knowledge passed through apprenticeship. "A blacksmith didn't hand his apprentice a manual and just say, figure it out"; he worked alongside him, corrected his grip in real time and pointed out mistakes as they happened. Then education was industrialized to train thousands of workers quickly: the **lecture model**, with one teacher, many students and a standardized curriculum, efficient for scale, terrible for learning, and "great for indoctrinating." For the first time since apprenticeship, he says, we can have personalized, interactive guidance at scale. Koe calls this the **return of the artisan** or "the next renaissance": a loop back to more meaningful things that corrects the mistakes of industrialization. He anticipates that the idea won't click for those who see AI as doing everything and removing meaning, "but that is a choice" (**Source:** `How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md`, 2026-01-03).

The blacksmith image identifies the three features of apprenticeship that the lecture model lost and that AI, in his account, can restore: guidance happens alongside the work (not before it), correction happens in real time, and feedback is specific to the learner's mistakes. Those are also the features of learning that Chapters 14 and 15 emphasize: build first, struggle, get feedback, correct. The historical frame links the section to Part I's critique of industrial schooling and to the "second renaissance" of Part VII. The closing remark ("that is a choice") makes the same point as Koe's 2026 comment on curation: whether AI removes meaning or restores it depends on how it is used.

#### Ask at the moment of struggle

The most basic use of AI as a tutor is a question asked exactly when one is stuck. Koe gives an example from 2025. Building an image in Photoshop while knowing nothing about it, he tries tools and can't do what he wants, such as removing the background of an image. He asks AI "how do I remove the background of an image in Photoshop," gets options, tries them and succeeds, "and because I'm struggling and my brain wanted to learn that thing, it kind of locks it in into place a lot better." Then he continues and asks more: "what are selections and masks in Photoshop, how do I make a selection on this type of image." More recently, editing in Premiere, he pressed a key combination to open a floating AI pane, asked how to get through a problem and edited much faster "without having to go through 10 different articles on Google" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

The mechanism Koe points to, that an answer received during struggle "locks in" better, is the one that underlies his treatment of tutorials in Chapter 14. His earlier advice moved from watching tutorials first to starting a project and seeking help at the point of difficulty; in 2025–2026 AI takes the tutorial's place at that point. The claim about retention is based on his experience rather than on cited research, but it is consistent with the logic he applies throughout: the problem in mind acts as a lens that selects and fixes what is learned.

A 2025 video on gamifying life gives the practice a name and a more deliberate form. For beginners, "the best use case for AI is just to get unstuck." Whether he is in Photoshop or writing his daily word count (the transcript says "10,000 words a day" here and "1,000 words a day" earlier in the same video; the first figure is probably a slip), AI doesn't need to do the work for him. It can be "this creative sparring partner, a **thought partner**" to overcome creative blocks: "I'm trying to do this in Photoshop. What do I try? What are five different ways to do this?" He tries them and gets unstuck. "This isn't outsourcing your learning"; it is how you learn effectively, sped up. A further step turns the tutor on the learner's own ignorance: "Here's what I'm doing, here's where I'm at, here's where I'm stuck. Can you help me identify my own **knowledge gaps**, my blind spots, and maybe give me three questions to ask you to help me make progress?" Done consistently, he says, this makes you progress much faster than others (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08).

The distinction between getting unstuck and outsourcing is the central line of this section. Asking for five ways to do something leaves the choosing and trying to the learner; asking the AI to identify one's knowledge gaps makes it reveal what to learn rather than replace the learning. The knowledge-gap prompt is a machine version of what teaching does in Chapter 14: it exposes what one cannot yet explain or do.

#### Prompt engineering as a skill

Using AI this way requires a skill that Koe treats as a successor to an older one. A few years ago, he says, searching was a skill: knowing what to type into Google, Reddit or Stack Overflow. "Now that AI has all of that information, prompt engineering is becoming the new Google search in a sense." It is "one of the new modern skills that you can pair with any other skill to future-proof yourself to an extent." He supports this with a quote he attributes, hesitantly ("I believe it was the founder of Nvidia"): "Machines aren't going to replace people; people using machines are going to replace people." "If you learn how to use AI beyond just typing a sentence into it, you're setting yourself up quite well" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). The attribution is Koe's own guess and should be read as such.

**Prompt engineering** is a common term that Koe uses with his own emphasis. In an August 2025 video he describes what it is: "AI has become a programming language for language itself" (he is speaking of large language models). Prompt engineering lets you give hyperspecific instructions so that the output comes very close to what you would have done yourself. The comparison is to creative software: the tools of Photoshop, After Effects or Premiere are "just a way to make it more specific to the image that you're trying to create." If natural language can't do something, you may need another medium, but you can do a lot with it; people who think otherwise "just don't understand prompt engineering" (**Source:** `You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md`, 2025-08-17).

The analogy recasts prompting as craft rather than as a trick. Just as the buttons of an image editor exist to move an image toward the artist's vision, the details of a prompt exist to move an output toward the user's vision. The implication, which the rest of this section develops, is that the quality of AI output depends on the specificity of the vision and of the instructions, and therefore on what the user already understands.

In January 2026 Koe defines one specific kind of prompt as part of turning a course into an AI product. After creating the course like a help-center knowledge base, you write one or more **system prompts**. "A system prompt is a set of instructions you give to an AI before it talks to anyone. It defines the AI's personality, knowledge boundaries, and behavior rules." "You think of this as programming the AI's identity." For an AI writing coach, the components are:

| Component | What it specifies |
|---|---|
| Role | How the AI should act |
| Instructions | What it should do, step by step |
| Knowledge base | All of your frameworks, processes and examples |
| Boundaries | What it should and shouldn't help with |
| Personality | How it talks: tone, and how harsh or supportive it is |

The prompt is what makes your AI different: your specific knowledge, frameworks and voice helping thousands of people at once. Can people steal it? Probably, he says, but they could steal anything you build; that "is never a valid response" for not doing it, and most people don't do anything with what they take (**Source:** `How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md`, 2026-01-03). The definition belongs to Part X's discussion of products, but it is included here because it shows the most complete form of the idea that runs through this section: a prompt encodes a teacher. The phrase "programming the AI's identity" also echoes, deliberately or not, the language Koe uses for human identity in Part II.

#### Study partners: the strategic advisor, the study regimen and the discipline coach

The March 2025 video "How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI" builds a set of AI roles around a learning project. Koe presents three chats to "oscillate between" while building: "Now we have three things inside of here: we have the strategic advisor, we have the discipline coach and we have the study regimen. So those three things combined, while creating your project, that you can oscillate between, really enhance your learning" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

**The strategic advisor.** The prompt comes from a Twitter account he credits as "pollinator 3000." In his reading of it, the prompt says: "Act as my personal strategic advisor with the following context: you have an IQ of 180, you're brutally honest and direct, you've built multiple billion-dollar companies, you have deep expertise in psychology, strategy and execution... Your mission is to identify the critical gaps holding me back, design specific action plans to close those gaps, push me beyond my comfort zone... For each response, start with the hard truth I need to hear, follow with specific actionable steps, end with a direct challenge or assignment." To use it, paste it into an AI (he mentions Cortex's assistant, ChatGPT, "whatever") and say "review my life plan and help me achieve my goals." It "just rips you apart." He shows its verdict on his own life plan: "a scattered collection of thoughts, not a strategic road map"; prioritize ruthlessly; rewrite your goals; identify your bottleneck; systematize health and relationships; eliminate distractions; focus your projects; "by tomorrow, rewrite your life plan with one primary goal, revised secondary goals, clear defined SMART goals, identification of your bottleneck, a list of focused projects. Don't come back until you've done the work." You can keep talking to it as you learn (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

The prompt is borrowed, and Koe credits it; what he adds is its placement in a learning system. Its structure (hard truth, then actions, then a challenge) is designed to counter the tendency of AI models to agree with the user. That Koe shows it tearing apart his own plan is part of the demonstration: the advisor is useful because it finds blind spots its user cannot see.

**The study regimen.** The second chat builds a curriculum. Paste the study-regimen prompt into a new chat, rename the chat "study partner," and replace the topic (he uses "storytelling"). The output is a 60-day regimen. The prompt asks for one bestselling book, one technical book, multiple YouTube videos and "three to five interests that complement the main topic that I can explore as supplementary education for fun, because you don't always just want to... go through this boring grind... you want to learn complementary things that fill in the gaps of what you're learning." The regimen came out in four phases, each with action steps: phase 1, immersion and foundations (weeks one and two: read *Story*, with its sections on style, structure and substance, two or three chapters a day, take notes on key concepts, watch a YouTube video); phase 2, technical application (weeks three and four); phase 3, creative exploration; phase 4, refinement and practice. He says he has tested it multiple times and that it gives good information and good books. Notes can be taken in the chat or in a linked document, and then you ask it to "quiz me on @document" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

**Complementary context:** *Story* is most likely Robert McKee's *Story: Substance, Structure, Style, and the Principles of Screenwriting* (1997); the source names the book but not its author.

The regimen reproduces, in automated form, the immersion prescription of section 16.2 (a bestseller and a technical book) and adds what Koe calls supplementary education for fun: complementary interests that fill gaps and keep curiosity alive, in line with the 2023 warning against "the mechanical nature of prescriptions."

He shows the study partner at work with an example from his own learning. He has been listening to Brandon Sanderson's lectures on writing science fiction and fantasy "because I want to personally write a fable." He adds the video to his Cortex library to pull the transcript, asks the AI for the key points, goes deeper into specific timestamps, and, if he listened to the lecture on a walk, says "can you quiz me on these key points," answering the questions with the AI as study partner (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). The example combines three practices from this chapter: the walk with audio from section 16.2, the library of saved sources from section 16.3, and AI quizzing, which turns passive listening into active recall.

**The discipline coach.** The third chat is one to return to "whenever you need to start the task." The transcript does not describe its prompt, so its content cannot be reconstructed; only its function is clear: it helps with starting, the moment where, according to Chapter 11, resistance is greatest.

The three roles divide the work of a learner: the advisor addresses direction and blind spots, the study regimen addresses content and testing, and the discipline coach addresses execution. They are meant to be used "while creating your project," which keeps them inside the build-to-learn frame of Chapter 14 rather than turning them into a substitute for it.

In a 2025 aside Koe mentions two more roles from a free mini-course he taught in Cortex (but transferable to any AI app): creating a "**tribe of mentors**" "so you can enhance your thinking by talking to the people that you aspire to be like," and creating a "**focus coach**" that helps you get into flow when you sit down to work, along with practical uses such as scripting YouTube videos or newsletters and writing social posts that don't feel templated (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). The transcript gives no implementation details. A device Koe describes six months later, the intellectual sparring partner below, looks like a fuller version of the same idea; but he does not use the phrase "tribe of mentors" there or link the two himself, so the connection is this book's reading, not his.

**Complementary context:** *Tribe of Mentors* is the title of a 2017 book by Tim Ferriss collecting advice from many accomplished people; Koe uses the phrase without attribution.

#### Prompts as little employees

The study partners are prompts written to help with learning. In mid-2025 Koe begins describing prompts more generally as reusable assets. "The prompts that you create and reuse and refine over time are like your **little employees** now." If you don't want to learn copywriting but know you need marketing or sales, create prompts, "little employees," that do those things "90% of the way there, which is still much better than 0% of the way there" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md`, 2025-05-25).

Three months later he restricts the idea to a specific domain. "Outside of what you enjoy doing, I see prompts, prompt engineering, as creating **specialized employees**," not completely there yet, but most of the way. His own case: he doesn't like spending time on marketing, which he finds formulaic and not his art form, but he needs it to sustain his writing. His options are to hire a marketer "at absurd prices" or to create the process with AI "and probably get better results," by uploading the best copywriting books to an AI and turning them into a prompt that helps him create a landing page (**Source:** `You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md`, 2025-08-17).

The qualification "outside of what you enjoy doing" is important. Koe draws a line between one's craft, where AI should not do the work, and the necessary tasks around it, where a prompt can stand in for a hired specialist. For learning, the line has a consequence: the prompts that do tasks for you are for skills you have decided not to master; for the skill you are trying to master, AI should coach, quiz and question, not produce.

#### Becoming AI-first: from a reference to a metaprompt

The May 2025 video "The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First)" lays out how such prompts are built. Koe's starting point is that vague requests produce poor results. If Ali Abdaal, Alex Hormozi or Koe himself asked AI without direction "how do I create my next YouTube video?", the results would be worse than doing it themselves. Specificity comes from a reference. Take one of Ali Abdaal's highest-performing YouTube videos, give it to AI, and ask it to break down the exact structure: what worked, the psychological patterns, the structure of the whole video, every line and why it works. Then you have detailed instructions that can be turned into a prompt to recreate the video with your own ideas. He attaches a condition: feed it a video that didn't perform well, and your video probably won't either; results also depend on topics and video types. So the claim that "all of this content creator stuff is just going to be automated out of existence" misunderstands the process: "That's not how this stuff works" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md`, 2025-05-25).

The method is the anomaly research of section 16.1 performed by a machine: a validated example is dissected into the reasons it works, and those reasons become instructions. The condition shows that the human contribution has not disappeared but moved: the quality of the result depends on choosing the right example, which requires the judgment (pattern recognition, taste) that only the learner can supply.

A 2025 video on not needing to grind gives the same workflow as four steps:

1. Choose a task, for example making a better YouTube intro.
2. Find a YouTube video, PDF or reference text and have AI break it into actionable steps. (He looked up how to create a killer YouTube intro, found a video by April Lyn Alter, whose content he praises, put it into Cortex and asked it to break down how to create an effective intro.)
3. "I turn that into a meta prompt I can reuse over and over again,"
4. "and then I refine the prompt as I use it."

In Cortex this was done through a "write incredible AI prompts" chat: you say what you want, with details, and it creates a reusable, refinable prompt. "Start learning AI," he concludes (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04).

The fullest example in the AI-first video concerns landing-page copy. "One of the most valuable things that I have in my life right now is a very good **prompt that creates prompts**." The steps:

1. Get the how-to guide; in his case, a guide derived from *Breakthrough Advertising*.
2. Because he structures prompts in two phases, ask: "Imagine you were going to write landing page copy for me using all of these principles. What is all of the information you'd need to gather from me?" The AI lists the context it needs: product or service name, description, core problem, desired outcome and benefit, unique mechanism, unique selling proposition, target audience and so on. (The last two are direct-response terms: the unique mechanism is the specific way a product produces its result, the unique selling proposition the claim that distinguishes it from competitors; Chapter 27 develops the first, and Part XI treats the persuasion both serve.)
3. Turn both outputs (the how-to guide and the list of questions) into documents to reference later.
4. Go to his prompt generator ("write incredible AI prompts") and ask it to create a prompt that generates a landing page of 500 to 10,000 words for a digital information product, in two phases: phase 1, context gathering, following the "questions needed from user" document and asking the user two or three questions at a time "to prevent overwhelm"; phase 2, writing the copy using the "how to write copy" document.
5. The generator returned the prompt in a code block; he asked for Markdown.

The result: in a new chat, phase one interviews him (product, audience and so on), and phase two writes the copy, with a compelling headline, an opening lead, intensification of problem and desire, benefits and proof. Now he sends the prompt, gives his information and gets a first draft "better than what most people can create when they haven't learned anything." He summarizes what being AI-first means: figure out how to do something, write detailed instructions, turn them into a metaprompt, and refine it over time (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md`, 2025-05-25).

**Complementary context:** *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) by Eugene Schwartz is a classic of direct-response copywriting, known for its levels of customer awareness and market sophistication; Koe draws on it at length in Part XI.

The two-phase structure, gather context and then execute, is the same structure as the "make sense of a topic or idea" workflow in section 16.2 and the life reset map in section 16.3. It recurs because it solves the problem Koe calls context: an AI that does not know the user's situation can only produce generic output, so the first phase of any useful prompt is an interview.

#### Using AI "better than 99% of people": detailed instructions

In November 2025 Koe published his most complete method, in a video titled "How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life)." It refines the AI-first workflow into a sequence of steps, each with options, and it can be reconstructed in full.

**Step one: create detailed instructions.** "Step one as a whole is you need to create **detailed instructions** for the AI... This isn't the magic step yet, but this is the prerequisite." Whether the task is a YouTube script, a landing page or a stimulating conversation, you must tell the AI exactly what to do. There are four ways to get such instructions, used in different situations:

| Option | When to use it | How |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Write them yourself | When you already know how you do the thing | Deconstruct your own method into requirements, varied examples and an output format |
| 2. Ask AI for a detailed guide | Only for well-known, low-variation topics that require little creative thought | Ask for a comprehensive guide, then turn it into a prompt that interviews you |
| 3. Extract from an expert source | When you don't know what to do and a recognized expert does | Feed the source (PDF, video, website) to the AI and ask for an extremely detailed step-by-step guide |
| 4. Emulate an example you like | When generic output is not distinctive enough | Paste the example and ask for its structure, tactics and line-by-line breakdown, "as if you are teaching me" |

(**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27.)

*Option 1: write them yourself.* Koe's first serious prompt asked whether AI could replicate how he writes tweets. His first attempt was "write a viral tweet for me." Then: "here's actually how I write. Try emulating this." Over time the prompt grew, because, having written so many tweets, he could "deconstruct how I write the tweets, how I think about it, how I generate ideas, how I structure certain ideas" and give all those requirements to the AI. The structure is a list of requirements; post examples of different types (one-sentence posts, multi-line paragraph posts, listicle posts with bullets); and an output format, because otherwise "it's just going to give it to me in this weird output." The tweets come out pretty good, "but it's still generating. It's still guessing"; it doesn't cover the whole spectrum of how his mind works. And a warning: most people give one or two examples, "and then all of their tweets are just going to look the same... they're going to look homogeneous, and it's going to be an easy tell that you're using AI" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The option presupposes mastery: you can only write instructions for a process you can deconstruct. It also explains the value of variety in examples: the model imitates what it is shown, so a narrow sample produces a narrow, recognizable output.

*Option 2: ask AI for a guide.* The condition is strict: "the topic has to be relatively well-known. It can't have much degree of variation depending on the person" (unlike YouTube videos, where Abdaal and Hormozi work differently), and "this can't require much creative thought." His example is a customer avatar, a topic "talked about so many different times... There isn't a better way to create a customer avatar. Kind of sort of, but it doesn't really matter." The prompt: "give me a detailed guide on how to create the most comprehensive customer avatar in the world." That completes step one. Then you turn the guide into a prompt that interviews you to fill every area and generates a super-detailed avatar "that most people don't have." The advantage over a blank template ("what keeps the customer up at night?") and over manually trawling Reddit is that, while it asks questions, you can reply "what do you think? Go and research Reddit and tell me what they are." "I'm not just staring at this blank template" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The condition limits the option to cases where the AI's general knowledge is likely to match expert consensus.

*Option 3: an expert source.* "Find an expert source of information if you don't know what to do." For creating an offer, you could ask AI for a guide, "but we already know that Alex Hormozi is the expert on that and his methods work. So I could take his PDF, plug it into a chat, and then tell it to give me a detailed guide on how to create an offer," then turn that into a prompt that asks you questions and outputs your offer. The same works for landing pages, products and social posts, or for something unusual, such as turning two YouTube videos on personal branding into a personal brand coach that finds your content pillars, guides your posts and grades your writing (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). Koe also gives the wording he uses for extraction. Building that personal brand coach, he used a YouTube video (he loves a six-hour video by a creator whose name is transcribed as "Caleb Rston," but uses a shorter one to avoid clogging the context) with this prompt: "I want you to give me an extremely detailed step-by-step guide on how to build a personal brand in 30 days. You are the expert here. Give me the necessary education and steps." "When I'm trying to get the AI to break down the instructions of an expert source, like a YouTube video or a PDF or even a website, I tend to write something like this." The AI reads the transcript and produces "expert level instructions, pretty much summarizing the video, but in the form of an actionable guide" (day one, day two and so on), which he then takes into the chat with the meta prompt (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The phrase "you are the expert here" assigns the AI the role of the source's author, and the request for an actionable guide changes the format from summary to procedure.

*Option 4: emulate an example.* Plain AI landing-page copy works, Koe says, but is "still not that unique"; it sounds "like it belongs on a ClickFunnels landing page with a countdown timer." So he finds a distinctive page (his example is an "anti-metal" page with "a really cool storytelling structure that is very attention-grabbing and compelling"), pastes its content and says: "I love this landing page copy. Break down the overall structure, what psychological tactics it uses, why it works, then break down each line individually. Write this as if you are teaching me how to do it step by step." The result is a replication guide. Turned into a prompt, it quizzes you on your product, customer avatar, pain points and so on, and rewrites the page "with that structure, in my own words, and with my own product" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The phrase "as if you are teaching me" is the key: the breakdown is written as a lesson, so the user learns the structure while obtaining the instructions.

The four options form a decision sequence. Use your own method when you have one; ask AI only where its general knowledge is reliable; go to an expert when you don't know and someone else demonstrably does; emulate a specific example when you want something distinctive. In every case except the second, the instructions come from human expertise, the user's own or someone else's, which the AI merely transcribes into a usable form.

#### The meta prompt and the structure of a prompt

**Step two: the meta prompt.** Detailed instructions still lack "the personal context": how will an AI write landing-page copy "without understanding my company, my product, my customer avatar?" "This is where the magic happens." The **meta prompt** is "a prompt that helps you create a prompt, because most people suck at writing prompts, and prompts have a pretty predictable structure." It saves hours of writing and refining: "you start with this incredible first draft that you can then refine." He calls it "the bread and butter... the secret sauce" and tells viewers to save it; it is linked in the video's description, and its full text is not in the transcript. To use it, open a new chat and send the meta prompt; it asks "What is the topic or role of the prompt you want to create?"; then you supply the expert instructions and a description of the prompt you want (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27).

**Phases.** His request to the meta prompt for the personal brand coach shows how he structures prompts: "I want to create a prompt that coaches me through building a personal brand for 30 days. You will execute this in three phases." "This is how I like to create prompts, is I like to break them down into phases. And this does require some thinking." Phase one is **context gathering**: "break down everything you need from me in order to best build a personal brand... interview me to gather all of that information and ask one question at a time." This is "usually the first phase in any prompt you create," because otherwise how would the AI know how to coach you? Phase two is the **action plan**: output the 30-day plan based on your answers. Phase three is **coaching**: one day at a time. The result was a "30-day personal brand coach prompt" built around a five-pillar framework; used in a new chat, it starts with "question one of 15," then builds the strategy and coaches daily (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27).

The phase structure has evolved from the two phases of May 2025 (context, then execution, with two or three questions at a time) to three phases with one question at a time. The constant is the first phase: the interview that supplies context, which every one of Koe's AI workflows in this chapter begins with.

**Ask for clarifying questions.** Koe ends his prompt-creation requests with a sentence he recommends as a habit: "Please ask clarifying questions before creating the prompt so that it comes out the best it can." "This last sentence, this is something I like to do when I'm creating prompts, because then it asks me questions that will lead to a better prompt" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). In one example below, the clarifying questions it produced concerned depth, questioning style, response format, scaffolding level, domain flexibility and progress tracking. The habit applies the context-gathering phase to the creation of the prompt itself: before the meta prompt writes a prompt, it interviews its user.

**The recap.** Koe summarizes the method in a paragraph that is worth quoting whole: "Here's how you use AI better than 99% of people. First, you use AI to create or extract detailed expert level instructions. You do not allow the AI to guess what it should do. You create a new chat and send the meta prompt. Then you give details about what prompt you want to create. You add a context gathering phase if needed and an execution phase. And then you paste the instructions into the prompt and tell it what you want." "When you use AI this way, you are using AI to both learn and build at the same time... You are **orchestrating**. You're not guessing anymore." If you are already skilled, you do what you would do anyway, "but faster and potentially at a higher quality, because you can iterate through drafts faster" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27).

"You do not allow the AI to guess" is the principle that unifies the method. Generic output is what an AI produces when it guesses; detailed instructions remove the guessing about how, and the context phase removes the guessing about whom. "Orchestrating" names the user's resulting role: the person decides what is done, by which instructions, for which purpose, while the AI executes. The claim that one learns and builds at the same time connects the method to Chapter 14: reading the extracted instructions is learning, and using the prompt on a real project is building.

The same video records Koe's model preferences at the time, which illustrate how personal the choice of tool is. "I personally like to do most of this with Claude Opus 4.1." "I don't really like ChatGPT at all personally, just personal preference. But I also don't like Claude Sonnet 4.5, the newest model, because it just tells me that I can't do certain things. It doesn't allow me to be harsh. Like I say, hey, be as harsh as possible. And it's like... I don't feel comfortable doing that" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). In April 2025 he had used Gemini Flash for large contexts and said "I like Claude better." The preferences are dated and personal; the stated criterion (willingness to be harsh) matters more than the model names, because it connects to the strategic advisor's design: a tutor that will not criticize cannot find blind spots.

#### Prompts that make you think

The danger that Koe guards against throughout is that AI thinks in the user's place. Two of his prompts are designed to do the opposite: to make the user think.

The first is in the November 2025 video, where it is called a **creative thought partner**. Take a YouTube video on first-principles thinking (or on how Daniel Schmachtenberger or Naval Ravikant think) and ask AI to break down how they think; reading that guide, he says, teaches more than watching the video. But understanding a way of thinking is not the same as having it: "Most people can watch these videos... but then they still don't practice it... How are you going to lock that in as a mental habit if you don't actually practice it? Well, creating a prompt out of it is a way to practice it." His request to the meta prompt: "I want to create a prompt that helps me arrive at clear, novel insights through first principles questioning according to the attached guide. I want you to act as purely observational clear eyes that does not give me the exact answer but guides me to it... First you will ask what topic, idea or problem I want to discuss. Then you will ask one question at a time following the thinking instructions. Please ask clarifying questions before creating the prompt so that it comes out the best it can." The key line: "I want it to help me think, not do the thinking for me." "Habit formation comes through practice" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27).

The second appears three months earlier, in August 2025. One of Koe's favorite prompts acts as a very good thought partner. It is written so that it does not give answers but asks questions relevant to a topic, guiding him toward insights faster, because he encoded in it how he generates his best ideas. His reasoning: ideas come from thinking, debating, conversing and asking yourself questions, and self-questioning runs through a predictable series (what, why, how and so on), so it can be put into a prompt. He still goes on walks and listens to books and content, but the prompt creates a "guide for my mind," and it "drastically increased the quality of my writing and creativity." The limits he sets are explicit. All his newsletters and social content are written by hand; he changes and refines many of the AI's ideas; "the AI output is just a prototype. It's an experiment. It's a first draft." Pasting AI output directly "is going to suck," because you are not practicing taste; you have to pick and choose (**Source:** `You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md`, 2025-08-17).

The two prompts share a design principle: they reverse the usual direction of the exchange. Instead of the user asking and the AI answering, the AI asks and the user answers. The first encodes someone else's way of thinking (first-principles questioning) so the user can practice it until it becomes a habit; the second encodes Koe's own way of thinking so that he can apply it more consistently. Both connect to Chapter 17, which treats questioning as a form of thinking. And the August statement draws the line that Koe holds across his discussions of AI and writing: AI output is raw material to be selected and reworked, because the selection is where taste is practiced (Chapter 18), and taste cannot be practiced by someone who accepts whatever is generated.

#### The intellectual sparring partner

A related device, which can be read (the link is ours, not Koe's) as a method for the "tribe of mentors" mentioned above, appears in a video from November 2025. "I don't like just asking the base AI questions," Koe says; for deep knowledge, a model won't deliver unless instructed. "If your mind takes the shape of those that you learn from, I personally want to learn from these very high-level thinkers": Naval Ravikant, Daniel Schmachtenberger, Krishnamurti, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. For each, the prompt is: "Break down the entire worldview of the person, his core principles, how he thinks through problems, his main discoveries or insights, and all of the ideas that best illustrate his philosophy. This should be a comprehensive document, as if I am diving into the entirety of his mind." Web search should be enabled, or a podcast that overviews the person's worldview can serve as the source. Store each document in your notes or in a ChatGPT or Claude project, then ask questions. His test: "I'm struggling with how I should best manage projects for a software company with a small team." The output included a "meta problem perspective from Daniel Schmachtenberger," "leverage and long-term games," "flow state design," an "awareness-based approach" and a practical synthesis (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27).

The premise, "your mind takes the shape of those that you learn from," is the same as the reading principle of section 16.2, where reading is immersion in the thoughts of those who have lived the life you want. The **intellectual sparring partner** extends reading into dialogue: the distilled worldviews become a council that can be consulted on a specific problem. The value lies in the synthesis across perspectives, which is the generalist's operation of Part VII. The device inherits the limits of its sources: a worldview distilled by an AI is a summary, with the losses that section 16.2 attributes to summaries, which is presumably why Koe treats it as a supplement to reading rather than a substitute.

#### The prompt library

The method produces an accumulating asset. "Think of this as documenting your own processes with AI," Koe says. He keeps a library of prompts for specific things: "creating a coach, creating an advisor, creating a thought partner, being able to write landing pages, being able to do research." A **prompt library** has three effects, in his account. "You bring yourself to a higher level of thinking rather than a lower level." "You can refine and iterate on your processes in a tangible way... like having a list of instructions as a prompt that you can change as you get better." And "you reduce your cognitive load of just storing all of that in your head" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27).

The prompt library is, in function, a note-taking system for processes rather than ideas. Where section 16.3's second subconscious stores what one has found worth knowing, the prompt library stores how one does things, in a form that can be executed and improved. The first effect (a higher level of thinking) follows from the division of labor: once the steps of a process are written down and delegated, attention moves to deciding which processes to run and how to improve them.

Koe then applies the library to building a business. For a one-person business, "it's not as simple as just telling an agent to do it, or downloading a business software or business AI and having it do it for you. In fact, to build a business with AI, you're doing all of the same things that you normally would have done by yourself, but now you're doing it with this process. You're building a library of prompts that help you do the things you need to do in business well, like writing content, building a digital product, writing promotions, writing emails, crafting an offer, and writing landing page copy." The consequence: "you don't need to spend so much time learning the skills before you actually start building the business. You learn and do at the same time" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The phrase "you learn and do at the same time" is build-to-learn applied to AI: the prompts are both the tools for doing the work and the curriculum for learning it.

He sketches what the library might contain, by domain.

*Content.* (1) A personal brand strategy: find a YouTube video that teaches it and turn it into a prompt. (2) Content ideas: "Paste 10 high-performing content pieces into AI and have it teach you how to replicate them." (3) Newsletters: "Paste two to three newsletters you like and have AI break down their structure." With a caveat: "I don't personally recommend having AI write for you. So consider creating a prompt that guides you through the process or coaches you through the process... instead of telling it to write the thing for you" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The caveat is consistent with his repeated statements, in 2025, that AI cannot write for him; in 2026 he clarifies that what he rejects is using AI to articulate one's own opinions and beliefs, while factual material from others can be handled with AI. Part VIII returns to this distinction.

*Product, offer and copywriting.* For a digital product, preferably start from an idea you already want to build; "ask AI how those products are structured and how to build them in a way that ensures the buyer uses and benefits the most," and create a prompt that guides you through creating it section by section. For the offer: a customer-avatar prompt, and a prompt that guides you to "a compelling **offer blueprint**" (asking AI how Hormozi creates offers, for the instructions); then "use the offer blueprint for any of your other marketing materials," feeding it to the AI whenever product information is needed. For copywriting: take a respected book such as *Breakthrough Advertising* and/or *Great Leads*, upload the PDF and turn it into "a detailed actionable guide"; find a landing page (or an email or social promotion) whose structure you like, paste it and have AI explain why it works; combine the two and create a prompt that interviews you for the offer, the avatar and the context, and writes the copy. "That's four or five prompts that allow you to build a business" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The offer blueprint functions as a reusable context document: written once, it supplies the context phase of every later marketing prompt.

**Complementary context:** *Great Leads* (2011), by Michael Masterson and John Forde, is a copywriting book on the opening of sales letters, organized around six types of lead matched to the reader's level of awareness.

*YouTube.* Koe warns against asking AI to "create the entire video for me." A video needs "a compelling title... the key points, a gripping introduction, a full script, B-roll ideas, the video description, and then potentially a coach that walks you through the video creation process. All seven of those things can be turned into prompts," run each time for a noticeable increase in quality:

1. *Title:* find five to ten accounts in your niche, filter by most popular, copy 10 to 20 titles into the AI, ask for instructions on replicating them, and turn those into a prompt that takes your topic and outputs titles.
2. *Key points:* ask AI for a guide on outlining a topic into compelling key points "that keep the viewer engaged while ensuring that the video is novel and valuable."
3. *Introduction:* find a YouTube video that teaches good intros and turn it into instructions and a prompt.
4. *Script:* use a video on scripting, or a script you want to emulate, to make a guide, then a prompt that gathers the topic, key points and introduction as context.
5. *B-roll:* ask for best practices on B-roll and retention, and make a prompt that adds B-roll ideas for each line of the script, fed section by section.
6. *Description:* via the meta prompt, a prompt with three sections: a keyword-friendly brief description, your links, and chapters with exact, attention-grabbing, keyword-friendly timestamps.
7. *Coach:* a prompt that walks you through the creation process.

"You're off to recording a YouTube video like a pro in a day rather than 6 months" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). The workflow decomposes a complex output into components, each with its own instructions drawn from a separate expert source or example, which is the technique stacking of Chapter 15 applied to prompts. The promise of "a day rather than 6 months" should be read alongside the caveat that follows below.

*Selling prompts.* Koe adds that the skill can itself be a product. "I know of softwares out there that are literally personal brand coaches, right? They take an AI chat and they put a prompt like this in there... and they charge $30 to $50 a month. So if you can simply create a prompt around this, put it behind a paywall for 10 bucks and sell it, a lot of people will buy that." "Understanding this skill alone, just how to create prompts and selling the prompts, you can make a lot of money doing that" (**Source:** `How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md`, 2025-11-27). This is an opinion about a market opportunity, not a reported result; it connects to the system-prompt products of Part X.

#### What AI does not do

Koe's enthusiasm for AI as a tutor comes with limits that he states himself, and the section would be misread without them.

The first concerns time. In a March 2026 video on building a one-person business faster with AI, he says that "it takes about two to five years to get really good at those things: at branding, at content, at marketing, at sales." "But with AI, if we train them on the principles of experts who willingly put out their knowledge on the internet, like Alex Hormozi," a sales and marketing expert, and the many people who talk about branding and content, "why can't you take that knowledge and then have the AI help you through that process?" He then repeats later in the video that even with AI "it's going to take you 2 to 5 years to actually reach the point of being able to masterfully do all of this stuff" (**Source:** `How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md`, 2026-03-15). AI shortens the path by supplying expert guidance at each step; it does not remove the years of practice that mastery requires. This sits uneasily with promises elsewhere in his work ("a day rather than 6 months," "learn anything in two weeks"), and Koe's own figures for how long learning takes vary across videos. The likely reconciliation, which he offers in 2025, is that short timeframes refer to substantial progress on a specific output and long ones to mastery; the March 2026 statement is the clearest on the latter.

The second concerns the output. The thought-partner passage set the rule: "the AI output is just a prototype. It's an experiment. It's a first draft," and publishing it unchanged means not practicing taste. Across 2025 and 2026 Koe's position on AI moves from treating prompts as "little employees" that get 90% of the way, to "not completely there yet, but most of the way," to insisting that the output is only a prototype and that his own published writing is done by hand. The resulting position is stable enough to state: AI is useful for what is not one's art and as a partner in thinking, not as a substitute for understanding or for the practice of taste.

The third concerns the user's relationship to the tool. In the 2026 curation passage Koe said "it's just a tool," and gave a test: if AI is taking away from the things that lead to a good mind and life, stop using it for that use case or change how you use it. Read with that test, the devices of this section sort themselves. The reading companion, the knowledge-gap prompt, the study regimen with quizzes, the creative thought partner and the intellectual sparring partner all add to the activities that build a mind: reading, struggling, recalling, questioning, connecting. The little employees and the business prompts take over tasks outside one's craft, freeing time for it. What the test rules out is using AI to skip the struggle in the domain one is trying to master, which, by the argument of Chapters 14 and 15, is where learning happens.

This returns the chapter to its starting point. The consumer of section 16.1 was "used by the internet rather than the one who uses it." AI reproduces the same choice at a higher level: it can be used as a tutor, an advisor and a set of specialized employees by someone who directs it with a goal, or it can use its user, supplying the feeling of progress without the change of mind. Koe's methods, from the two layers of reading to the prompts that ask rather than answer, are designed to keep the user on the first side. Chapter 17 turns to what the mind does with the material gathered this way: how to think.

### Exercises

1. **Audit your inputs as a researcher.** List everything you consumed in the past three days (books, posts, videos, podcasts). For each item, say whether you consumed it as a consumer or as a researcher, using Koe's five operations (dissect, expand, summarize, connect, form a perspective) as the test. Then identify the goal or project that would have turned each consumer item into research. Where you cannot name one, what does that tell you about the causal chain in section 16.1 ("nothing to build, nothing to frame and guide learning")?

2. **Run a small anomaly study.** Choose three writers you admire for their articulation and find, for each, one post with at least twice their usual engagement. For each post, write three to five bullets on why it worked. Then compare your bullets across the three posts: which patterns recur? Explain what this exercise can and cannot tell you, considering factors Koe's method does not control for (timing, platform changes, topic).

3. **Read one chapter in two layers.** Pick a book that is slightly beyond your understanding. Read until you either reach confusion or reach an idea that makes you want to stop. If confusion, formulate the exact question you would ask a reading companion; if an idea, write how it connects to a current goal or problem in your life. Afterward, assess Koe's claim that "the purpose for reading is behavior change through identity change": what would have to happen for this idea to change your behavior, and over what time span?

4. **Evaluate the case against summaries.** Koe used summaries in 2023 as a filter for choosing books and condemned "bullet point summary culture" in 2025. Construct the strongest version of each position. Then state a rule that would let someone use summaries without falling into what Koe criticizes, and identify a situation in which your rule would fail.

5. **Diagnose your note-taking system.** Describe how you currently capture ideas (or why you don't). Place your practice in the table at the end of section 16.3: which period does it most resemble? Then test it against the three constants Koe keeps across all versions (selection by resonance, collection as fuel for creation, dependence on a project). Which constant does your system violate, and what is the smallest change that would fix it?

6. **Question the "false god."** Koe argues that every idea system succumbs to entropy and that one should "embrace the mess." Identify the assumptions in this argument. For what kind of person or work would a maintained, structured system still be the better choice? What would count as evidence, in your own experience, for or against his claim?

7. **Design a prompt that makes you think.** Choose a skill you want to master (not one you would delegate). Using the structure of section 16.4 (an instruction source, a context-gathering phase, and a rule that the AI asks rather than answers), write a prompt for a thought partner or study partner in that skill. Then explain, using Koe's distinction between getting unstuck and outsourcing your learning, why your prompt adds to your learning rather than replacing it, and describe how you would know if it started doing the opposite.

8. **Decide what to delegate.** List the tasks involved in a project you are working on. Sort them into your craft (where Koe would have AI coach, quiz or question) and tasks outside it (where a prompt could act as a "specialized employee"). Defend one borderline case in each direction, and say what you would lose if you delegated a task from the first group.
