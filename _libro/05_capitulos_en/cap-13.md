## Chapter 13 — Rest, Routine and the Maintenance of Order

Chapter 12 described how Dan Koe organizes the hours he works: one protected block, a priority ladder, deadlines that narrow attention, a morning ordered from low to high entropy, and a cutoff that forces the work to fit inside four hours. Several times that chapter pointed forward. The gym analogy claimed that cognitive work, like training, produces adaptation only when it is followed by recovery. The challenging time block worked only if there were ideas to execute, and deep work, Koe said, uses only what you already know. The "lie" in the four hours turned out to be the reading, walking and napping that he did not count as work but where the material for the work was produced. This chapter is about that other half.

It draws on three earlier chapters. From Chapter 5 it takes entropy, the "supreme law of the universe" in Koe's vocabulary, and psychic entropy, the tendency of a mind to fall into disorder unless something orders it; the last section of this chapter is the practical counterpart of that diagnosis. From Chapter 6 it takes the two brain networks behind the Focus Matrix (the task positive network for narrow external focus, the default mode network for open internal focus), which here become the mechanism that justifies rest. From Chapter 10 it takes the metaphor of life as a game with rules, which here becomes a definition of routine.

The chapter moves in four steps. Section 13.1 presents rest as half of the work: the distinction between productivity mode and creativity mode, the default mode network, Koe's definition of real rest against its popular image, the "dead stop" at the end of the workday, the examples of great creatives who worked little (Charles Darwin above all), the polarity of work and rest, and leisure as a mindset. Section 13.2 turns to routine and habit: routine as the rules of one's own game, lifestyle design as experimentation rather than copying, the claim that a few chosen habits create the future, Koe's own routine across several versions, the routines of guests he interviewed, and the first system he tells the reader to build. Section 13.3 presents the **Fill, Empty, Use** framework (fill the mind, empty it, use it) and its relatives: read, write, build, and the four modules of a creative life. Section 13.4 closes the part of the book on self-government with the practices that keep order from decaying: mental housekeeping, the hard reset, the week-long plan, nightly reflection and the weekly review.

### 13.1 Rest as Half of the Work

#### Productivity mode and creativity mode

Koe's argument for rest begins with a hard distinction. In a 2023 video he separates work and rest as two modes of mind: work is **productivity mode**, rest is **creativity mode**. He introduces them by returning to the Focus Matrix of Chapter 6, whose four corners (unconscious narrow, unconscious open, conscious narrow, conscious open) are "states of mind," not permanent conditions, and which mix into each other.

Productivity mode is narrow, focused attention, "fixated on solving the problem a specific task presents," and it is almost always accompanied by pressure or stress to perform. Koe illustrates it with the task positive network, active during attention-demanding tasks, including conscious attention to the external environment. Creativity mode is an open state. You are not fixated on a stress-related task; you may still be oriented to a task or a goal, but you are not actively attending to it; you are not thinking about work. You notice it "at the gym, in the shower, on a hike or walk," during rest. He immediately blocks the obvious misreading: "I'm talking about actual rest not laziness," not "sitting around drinking wine and shoveling ice cream down your throat." Creativity mode is illustrated by the default mode network, which activates when cognition shifts from outward-focused to inward-focused. Neither mode is bad; the point is to contrast and balance them (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18).

Two features of this distinction matter for what follows. First, the creative mode is defined by the *absence* of attention to work, not by the absence of a goal. The person on the walk still has a project; they have simply stopped steering toward it. This is the difference between Koe's rest and plain distraction, and it will return when he defines leisure. Second, Koe borrows the names of the two networks from neuroscience but uses them as an explanatory frame. Chapter 6 already gave the **Complementary context** on what these networks are; the claim that productivity mode is "almost always" stressful, for instance, is his description of how most people work, not a property of the task positive network.

#### The mechanism: the brain is active at rest

The step from the distinction to the argument is a single claim, repeated across the corpus in different words. In 2023: "when you are not working your brain is more active, do you know what that means? That means that you are going to solve more problems," and you will have more ideas and insights to channel into your work when you take more rest (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). The same year, in a video on the new economy, he gives the most technical version: work is necessary, "but rest and proper rest is going to become even more important," because of the default mode network, "a series of interconnected sections that activate as soon as people stop concentrating on external tasks and shift from outward focus to inward focused cognition. In short the brain is more active when you are not working" (**Source:** `Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md`, 2023-04-16).

In 2024 he compresses the mechanism into an aphorism that summarizes the whole section: "You can't identify new problems with a narrow mind. **Work contracts the mind, rest expands the mind.**" At rest, "your brain kicks on what's called the default mode network"; without technicalities, "your brain is as active, if not more active, when you are at rest than when you are at work," and it "connects ideas and it spits them into your conscious mind." Shower thoughts, the ideas that keep coming, are "the DMN." The conclusion is about leverage, not comfort: "If you want to consistently have the good ideas that lead to the highest leverage work and finding and identifying the problems that lead to that, then you need to rest" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). This is the seventh step of that video's system, and its placement is significant: rest appears as a step in a method for leverage, not as a reward for having completed the method.

The chain, reconstructed, has four links. Narrow focus executes known problems but cannot see new ones. New problems and new connections are what lead to the highest-leverage work. Those connections are made when attention is released from the task. Therefore the person who never releases attention will execute well on problems that may be the wrong ones. Chapter 12's priority ladder assumed that you know which project deserves the first block of the day; this chain explains where that knowledge comes from.

In August 2024 Koe returns to the mechanism through Alex Soojung-Kim Pang's book *Rest* (introduced in Chapter 12), reading a passage aloud: even in its resting state "the brain is plugging away at problems," examining and tossing out possible answers, looking for novelty. We cannot really control this process, Pang writes, but by learning to rest better we can support it, let it work, and notice when it has found something that deserves attention (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). Koe then adds his own layer. If you want rest, you have to take it; nobody will give it to you. Rest lets your mind "filter signal from noise," and **intentional rest** breaks the narrow-minded stress of **doing mode** that most of us are addicted to (doing mode is the chronic, always-on narrow state described in Chapter 6, a term Koe adapts from the same book). Walking, lifting, reading, leisure, anything other than giving attention to work, activates the default mode network. Then comes the line that reframes the four-hour day: by working two to four hours and resting the rest, "you're still working 12 hours a day, you're just not actually doing the work, you're enjoying life." He states its limit explicitly: this is only possible with knowledge, mental or creative work. What he optimizes for are "shower thoughts or moments before sleep with life-changing ideas"; if you are always in doing mode, you will not have the idea that lets you transcend and improve your work (**Source:** same file).

The line about twelve hours is the clearest statement of what Chapter 12 called the "lie" in the four hours. Koe does not claim that the creative worker labors four hours and does nothing else; he claims that the subconscious keeps working during the other eight, and that this unattended work is the more valuable part. The restriction to creative work is not a footnote. A cashier or a line cook does not get their output from shower thoughts, and Koe does not pretend otherwise; Chapter 6's distinction between mechanical and creative work is the boundary of this whole section.

**Complementary context:** "intentional rest" and the image of the mind working in the background are central to Pang's *Rest*, which draws on research on the default mode network and on the daily habits of scientists and writers. Koe's use of the book is mostly adaptation: he takes its thesis and examples and builds his own vocabulary around them ("doing mode," "work contracts the mind").

#### Shower thoughts, the bug and the sleepless night

Koe's examples of the mechanism are ordinary on purpose. The earliest, from 2022, is the programmer's bug. When a programmer hits a bug, or when you hit a problem in a project and cannot figure it out, take a break: go for a walk, rest and do nothing, go to the gym, anything not focused on work. Then, "like magic," like a shower thought, the solution comes; you write it down and go back to implement it. In the same passage he adds a second benefit that has nothing to do with creativity. Many people live in a permanent state of narrow focus, "and that is when stress becomes a thing," because narrow focus on something stressful is what stress is; building routines that encourage **open focus** helps you de-stress, which matters beyond work (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). This early passage already contains the two justifications of rest that the later corpus develops separately: rest as a source of solutions and rest as relief from chronic narrowness.

In 2025 the mechanism is tied to the project, which links it to Chapter 9 and to the dopamine of Chapter 11. With a project, "as you read books, study tutorials or have conversations, good dopamine will spurt into your brain to signal that information is important for the completion of the project. Your subconscious will munch on problems and send relevant ideas to your conscious mind. This is what creatives call shower thoughts. It's when the default mode network is active in your brain while at quote unquote rest." The examples are a walk or, more commonly for him, late at night while about to fall asleep during a project: ideas keep him up and he cannot stop typing them into his phone. "It sucks to not be able to sleep, but it feels so good because that's when the ideas come the most," when the brain is not in the task positive network (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). The passage adds a condition that the earlier ones leave implicit: the subconscious "munches" on the problems of a project you have. Rest without a project produces rest; rest with a project produces ideas for the project. This is why the chapter on the project comes before this one.

A secondhand anecdote, from 2024, serves the same point. Koe says he is writing a book on walking and that someone working with him mentioned a book (he does not know which) recounting that a discovery related to the atomic bomb came to Werner Heisenberg as he walked across the street. He draws the lesson: "the secret to the creative worker's ability to make massive progress in such short periods of time is that they have the ideas that spring up," together with the awareness to work on the right task with quality and grace (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). The anecdote should be read as Koe presents it: an illustration passed along without a verified source, not historical evidence.

The earliest passage of all marks a limit to Koe's own action-first advice. In 2022, discussing how to become a "value creator," he warns against piling up knowledge without application, and then immediately adds: "there's also a trap there." In value creation you need to make knowledge valuable, and only so many things can be practiced tangibly. Some ideas you must "munch on and contemplate": go on walks, "let your default mode network, let your subconscious mind bring ideas to your conscious mind so you have firepower to write with." Sit with a topic and think; contemplate, introspect, self-reflect: what is the true value in this message, and what has it helped me with in my life? (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31). Chapter 11 discussed a later reissue of the same idea. Here it matters as the earliest appearance in the corpus of the default mode network as a tool for writing: contemplation is not the opposite of application but one of its forms, and **firepower** (Koe's word for the stock of ideas that writing draws on) is what it produces.

#### "The most effective work you do is when you don't work at all"

In May 2025 Koe pushes the thesis to its extreme form in a video titled after the claim that you do not need to grind around the clock. Among its announced themes are "the 80-hour myth," why we are addicted to being busy, and what he calls the **creator's paradox**: "creativity is the result of not trying to be creative" (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). The paradox is the default mode network stated as a rule of conduct. If ideas arrive when attention is released from the task, then trying harder to have ideas, which means narrowing attention on having them, works against having them.

The video develops the paradox through the routines of admired creatives. Most of them, Koe says, had similar patterns: "intense focused work blocks followed by relentless rest involving complete disconnection from work." His example is David Ogilvy, the advertising executive, who believed in intensive research, absorbing as much information as possible, and then stopping and taking a break, trusting that his subconscious would do the work. Koe glosses: "Your mind is this problem-solving machine and when you leave it alone with information to munch on, it does work for you." He then paraphrases Ogilvy's own claim: big ideas come from the unconscious, in art, science and advertising alike, but the unconscious must be well informed or the idea will be irrelevant; you stuff the conscious mind with information and then unhook the rational thought process (**Source:** same file).

The Ogilvy passage adds the condition that keeps the creator's paradox from becoming an excuse. Not trying works only after trying very hard. The unconscious has nothing to work on unless it has been filled, and it produces relevant ideas only if what it was filled with is relevant. The same structure will reappear in section 13.3 as the order of the Fill, Empty, Use framework.

From there Koe draws on *Rest* again. The long walks, reading, research and leisure of the greats activate the default mode network: when you stop focusing on work, your subconscious continues. He cites studies, without naming them, showing that the brain is as active when not working on something as when working on it, and often works harder, more creatively and more effectively, popping ideas into the conscious mind as shower thoughts that bring novelty and innovation. Paraphrasing Pang, he adds that the complexity of the default mode network shapes the capacity for self-awareness, memory, imagining the future, empathy and moral judgment, and that a better-developed network means a better ability to model other people's minds. Then the conclusion: "the most effective and result-generating work you do is when you don't work at all." He knows how it will land. For those whose identity is attached to the grind it is a hard pill, and he imagines their mind reacting before they have thought about it, while his advice is simply: "you should go on a walk" (**Source:** same file).

The same video then marks the limit of focused work with a metaphor. When you are deeply focused on a string of tasks, your mind narrows as it does on a quest in a video game: you will not, and cannot, see outside the task or the game. Strategies, tools, tricks and hacks are usually created outside the game, before entering it; while you are in it, deep in the task, you are not thinking about them. The summary is a principle: "most great ideas come from **well-informed self-reflection**" (**Source:** same file). The metaphor connects to Chapter 10, where life is a game with a frame and rules. Deep work happens inside the frame; strategy is the activity of stepping outside the frame and redesigning it. Both are necessary, and they cannot happen at the same time, which is why the day needs both a protected block and an unprotected stretch.

It is worth stating precisely what status these claims have. That the default mode network is active at rest is a finding Koe borrows. That it is "often" more creative and effective than focused work, that the most result-generating work happens when you do not work, and that most great ideas come from well-informed self-reflection are his interpretations, supported by examples and by Pang's book, not by studies he identifies. The extreme formulation is also rhetorical: in the same corpus he insists that the morning block is non-negotiable. The claim is better read as "the value of the focused hours depends on the unfocused ones" than as "focused work is worthless."

#### From modes of work to modes of thinking

By August 2026 the distinction has shifted. Asked about productivity and creativity, Koe answers: "Both have their benefits... They're different modes of thinking. There's creativity and there's productivity. Creativity benefits from a more open focus, letting your mind be kind of chaotic and then picking certain ideas or pieces and synthesizing them together. And then productivity is more strategic, more focused, more hierarchical. But most people don't engage in productivity in that way. They engage in a very narrow state of stressful focus based on urgency or scarcity because they have to to survive. And that is not a good place to be in" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

Three things have changed since 2023. The modes are now modes of *thinking*, not slots in a schedule: work time and rest time no longer map neatly onto productivity and creativity. Productivity is redefined as strategic and hierarchical, which brings it closer to the planning of Chapter 8 than to the pressured execution of the 2023 version, where pressure was "almost always" part of it. And a third state is named that belongs to neither: stressful narrow focus driven by urgency or scarcity, which is what most people actually do. The 2026 view is that the typical worker is neither productive nor creative but surviving. The 2023 version is not retracted; it is refined, and the earlier talk of "pressure" in productivity mode is now attributed to survival rather than to productivity as such. Koe gives no reason for the change.

#### Overwork against human nature

The mechanism also grounds a stronger claim about the modern workday. In 2023: "Eight hour work days, endless screen time and little movement are antagonists to human psychology and physiology." Constant overwork destroys your chances of becoming more self-aware, harnessing your creative potential and creating a vision. The reason is again the default mode network, the network that turns on when you switch from outward-focused cognition (work) to inward-focused cognition during restful activities; hence, he says, he is "so big on working only four to six hours a day, preferably on the lower end," then stopping, focusing on other areas and incorporating more rest. You may not notice the damage, because overwork is normal to you; you think you are at your creative peak when you are nowhere near it, and you have to open your mind to the possibility of a better future. Control over lifestyle and schedule lets you "realign with your nature" and "feel superhuman" (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19).

The figure here is four to six hours, not four. Chapter 12 traced how the number moved: three to five hours as the "sweet spot" (below), four as the identity, twelve in seasons of intensity. The 2023 statement is useful because it shows what the number protects: not leisure for its own sake but the time in which self-awareness, creativity and vision are produced, three things that Parts II to IV of this book treated as foundations. The argument "you think you are at your peak when you're nowhere near it" is unfalsifiable from the inside, which is precisely its point: it is an invitation to experiment, not a proof.

#### What rest is, and what it is not

If rest is half of the work, the word needs a definition, and Koe gives one by contrast. The ninth of his Ten Commandments for focused work (Chapter 12 lists all ten) is "prioritize rest," and it opens with a sentence he presents as self-evident: "Quality work does not exist without quality rest, it's that simple." But rest, he adds, "is not the modern notion of self-care, the thing that is perpetuated by mediocre people online." It does not mean "Netflix, wine and bubble baths," which "have their place." His definition: "**Rest equals regenerating mental energy through non-work related activities**": going to the gym, going on a walk, socializing with friends, writing in your journal, reading a good book, trying a new hobby, "whatever takes your focus off of work and allows you to bring it more intrinsic to the things that you want to do." The last clause of the passage is garbled in both transcripts ("anything that doesn't support your work or mediocrity or a cheap dopamine addiction"); the most plausible reading is that rest is anything other than work, mediocrity or the cheap dopamine of Chapter 11 (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The 2023 video on the new economy gives the same contrast with a longer list and a purpose. **Proper rest**, he says, is not "stuffing your face with ice cream, chugging a bottle of wine and binge watching Netflix," but going to the gym, walks, reading, contemplating, visualizing, thinking, getting creative, journaling, brain dumping. Its function is to prime the mind for novel ideas, so that new language, beliefs, ideas and thoughts can "pave the road of thought." Through the internet, he adds, collective knowledge and the capacity for development keep increasing, and the practical upshot is that you can pursue meaningful work that unlocks "the parts of the brain that modern society has neglected" (**Source:** `Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md`, 2023-04-16).

The 2024 version, built on Pang's book, gives the definition its sharpest formula: "**If you want to get things done, work. If you want to get the right things done, rest.**" Rest is again defined against its popular image: it is not "wine and bubble baths nor margaritas on a beach." It is a necessity for creative work, "the type of work that actually leads to a good life," and, counterintuitively, it is how you get "**6 years of work done in 6 months**." He prefaces it with a warning: proceed with an open mind, because this path may threaten everything you believe to be true and destroy a sense of security in your future that "was an illusion to begin with" (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). Here the frame comes from *Rest*, and the phrasing is his adaptation of it. (What Koe later reuses without attribution is a different line from the same book, the one about the creative man who "doesn't work at all"; see "Rest, the secret of the greats" below.)

The formula condenses the chain of the previous pages. Work executes; rest selects. The right things are the new problems and connections that, according to the mechanism above, a narrow mind cannot see. "Six years of work in six months" is not a measured result but a description of what happens when the selection is good: the work done is less in quantity and more in leverage. Its rhetorical form (a large number in a short time) is typical of Koe's titles and should be read as such.

| Rest, in Koe's sense | What he excludes from the word |
|---|---|
| Gym, walks, hikes, runs | Netflix binges, wine, bubble baths (they "have their place," but they are not rest) |
| Reading a good book, contemplating, visualizing, thinking | Ice cream and a bottle of wine on the couch |
| Socializing with friends, time with loved ones | Margaritas on a beach as an image of the good life |
| Journaling, brain dumping, a new hobby | "Cheap dopamine" and the numbing of work you dislike |
| Shower, nap, doing nothing with a problem in the back of the mind | Busy work on the computer after the workday is over |

The criterion that unifies the left column is double: attention leaves work, and it goes somewhere that regenerates mental energy or feeds the mind. The right column fails one of the two tests: either attention never really leaves work (busy work), or it leaves for something that depletes rather than restores.

#### Mental metabolism

In 2025 Koe gives the recovery side of rest a name taken from the body. Describing his deep work routine, he calls the third step "to top this all off": **mental metabolism**. "If you think of your mind like your body, you just spent a few hours training to failure in the mental gym with deep work. You spend 95% of your mental energy and you need to eat. You need to recover and you need to do what's necessary to show up in peak condition for your workout tomorrow" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

The analogy is the one Chapter 12 used to justify four hours (nobody trains eight hours a day and expects progress), now extended past the end of the workout. "Training to failure" means the morning block is supposed to exhaust the available focus; "95%" is an image of that exhaustion, not a measurement. "Eat" covers two things the rest of this chapter separates: recovering (rest proper) and taking in new material (the "fill" of section 13.3 and the "leisure maxing" below). The corpus also uses "mental metabolism" in an earlier, different sense, for the limited rate at which the mind can digest new information (Chapter 10 discusses it as the "digestive system of reality"). The two senses share an image, the mind as a body that processes food, but answer different questions: the 2025 sense is about recovering from output, the earlier one about digesting input.

#### The dead stop

Rest has to start somewhere, and Koe's 2023 commandment says where: "When the 4 hours are up you stop working, **dead stop**." There is one exception: priority tasks that spill over, in which case "you work to systemize back into four hours" (the move Chapter 12 described as the forcing constraint). Busy work does not count as an exception. Going on the computer to do little tasks that can wait does not justify continuing: "you need to get into the habit of stopping work." The reason is presence. If you are constantly focused on work, "and in that stressed survival state, you are not going to open up your mind for creative problem solving or any of the spiritual things in life that make life good," like living in the present moment; "**if you're constantly focused on work, by definition you are not present.**" And if your business would "explode" unless you worked more than four hours, "that's a problem, fix it with systems" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The argument for the dead stop has two premises, and they come from different parts of the book. The creative premise is the default mode network: a mind that never stops working never enters the mode that produces solutions. The existential premise is presence, which links the commandment to the awareness of Part I and to the spiritual themes of Part XIV: a life in which attention is always on the next task is, by definition, a life lived elsewhere. The word "habit" matters too. Koe does not present stopping as a decision made each afternoon but as a habit to be built, because the pull of small tasks is constant and will win any daily negotiation.

The rule was later qualified. Chapter 12 documented how the four-hour day became a phase and an identity rather than a universal rule, with legitimate seasons of twelve-hour days. The dead stop belongs to the 2023 formulation; in the later corpus the gym takes over its function as a "work cutoff" (section 13.2), a physical event that marks the transition rather than a clock.

#### More rest, or better rest?

The early corpus says, in effect, the more rest the better: more rest, more problems solved, more ideas. In March 2025 Koe adds a correction that changes the emphasis without abandoning the thesis. The problem of modern life, he says, is that people work on simple tasks they hate all day, never challenge themselves creatively, and think they need a break. "I think most people would benefit from more work rather than more rest," because for most people "rest is synonymous with a pleasurable escape from boredom rather than a **deep recovery** session from stimulating work." Rest is not lying on the couch eating Cheetos and watching TV all day; that is **numbing** yourself from work you dislike. The result is two problems, not one: the work you don't like, which rest does not solve and which your version of rest makes worse, and a lack of periods of pure intensity toward a meaningful goal (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02).

The same year he states the consequence for burnout. Most people's idea of rest as recovery is self-destruction: lying in bed, eating Cheetos, drinking wine, "becoming a degenerate." That is not rest, he says; it is "a recipe for burnout, because you're not recovering for any meaningful work" (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29).

| | 2023: more rest | 2025: better rest, and more work |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | The brain is more active at rest; more rest yields more ideas and solutions | Most people would benefit from more work than more rest |
| Who it addresses | Creatives with demanding work | People whose work is simple, disliked and unchallenging |
| What rest means | Regenerating mental energy away from work | Deep recovery *from stimulating work*; otherwise numbing |
| The underlying problem | Overwork and doing mode | The wrong work, and no periods of intensity |

The two positions are compatible once the audience is specified, and that is the reading this book proposes: the value of rest lies in its quality and in having demanding work to recover from. Rest is defined relationally. It is the second half of a cycle whose first half is intense, meaningful effort; without that first half, what looks like rest is escape. This anticipates the polarity argument below, and it also explains why, in May 2025, two months after saying most people need more work, Koe could still say that the most effective work happens when you are not working. Both claims assume someone who has a project worth recovering for.

#### Rest, the secret of the greats

Koe's historical case for rest relies heavily on Pang's *Rest*, and he says so in the 2024 videos. In the seventh guideline of a February 2024 routine video, titled "rest, the secret of the greats," he reads a sentence from the book: "the clever man may work smarter not harder, they say, but the creative man doesn't work at all." He adds a confession of temperament: he has "always had some form of aversion towards Western work culture, which is just filled with 80-hour work weeks, high pressure environments and little time for rest and recovery... why would I want to waste my entire day knowing that 2 to 3 hours in my work quality would suffer, because **focus is finite**." This has become a talking point, he says, from him, from Pang and from people like Sam Altman "who are open about how long they work and how they use that work," and it is becoming well known in the creator economy, remote work and "the new economy" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The line about the creative man is worth tracking. Here, in February 2024, Koe reads it explicitly as Pang's. Ten months later, in December 2024, he opens a video with the same sentence and no attribution at all (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29); section 12.1 ("Optimize for creativity, not productivity") tells that later use. The position does not change between the two videos, but the reader should know that the sentence is borrowed.

The same passage gives the historical list and the prescription. "Ancient Romans, ancient Greeks, Steve Jobs, Charles Darwin and an infinite list of visionaries, strategists and innovators attributed their success to surprisingly low work times. Writers from multiple domains like Hemingway and Tarantino would spend the majority of their days lounging by the pool, spitting game with girls and doing everything aside from what they deemed work." The prescription: "work for one to 4 hours, then stop. This is arguably the most difficult part of your day" (**Source:** same file). The examples are presented as he states them; they are probably drawn from *Rest*, but he does not say so in this passage. The remark that stopping is the hardest part of the day connects back to the dead stop: the difficulty is not finding time to work but leaving work alone.

Six months later he goes further back, again reading from *Rest*. The ancient Greeks saw rest as a gift, as "the pinnacle of civilized life"; the Roman Stoics argued that you cannot have a good life without good work; virtually every ancient society recognized that both work and rest were necessary for a good life. "One provided the means to live, the other gave meaning to life." Koe calls this "absolutely huge," because people do not realize that work and rest are necessary counterbalances (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). In May 2025 he repeats the Greek half with a different word: to the ancient Greeks, "whom we reference as pillars of wisdom," **leisure** was "the pinnacle of civilized life," and work was necessary but secondary to leisure, an idea he says he has recently rediscovered for himself (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). The shift from "rest" to "leisure" is not a change of position; it reflects the fact that the later corpus develops leisure as its own concept (below). The source of the 2025 version is not named, though it is almost certainly the same book.

The most memorable item Koe takes from *Rest* is an anecdote about the scientist Watson. A contemporary said that Watson had time for girls and tennis because he was a genius; an observer (whose name is garbled in the transcript) suggested that perhaps he was a genius *because* he made time for girls and tennis. The lesson Koe draws, in the book's words: "**creative achievement needs to be approached obliquely**" (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). The transcript does not specify which Watson; the context suggests James Watson, co-discoverer of the structure of DNA. "Obliquely" is the same idea as the creator's paradox, stated by a third party a year before Koe coined his own name for it: what you aim at directly with narrow focus is not the creative breakthrough itself, which arrives from the side.

**Complementary context:** the Greek word usually translated as leisure, *scholē* (the root of "school"), referred to time free from necessary labor and available for learning, contemplation and civic life, which is why classical authors could rank it above work. The Stoics, by contrast, valued work done well as part of a virtuous life. Koe compresses both into the formula "one provided the means to live, the other gave meaning to life," which is Pang's summary, not a quotation from an ancient author.

#### Charles Darwin, the central case

Of all the historical examples, Charles Darwin is the one Koe returns to most often, and the details change from telling to telling. The first account, in June 2023, supports the claim that great creatives worked little. "Four hour work days, singular focus and meaningful work" are becoming the norm in small pockets of the internet. Ancient Romans and Greeks, Steve Jobs, Charles Darwin and an ever-growing list of strategists and innovators attribute their success to surprisingly low work times plus abundant restful activities like long walks. When we see a respectable body of creative work, we assume its authors worked long hours and obsessed into the night. Maybe they did, Koe says, but "obsessing and thinking and contemplating, that's not work, that's rest": it is the default mode network, inward-focused cognition bringing better and more impactful ideas into focused output. His conclusion: three to five hours of focused work paired with **active rest** "seems to be the sweet spot for most impactful creatives," and if they could change culture, corporate workaholics can too (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18).

The move in this passage is a redefinition. The usual objection to the short-workday thesis is that great creators were obsessive. Koe accepts the obsession and reclassifies it: thinking about a problem on a walk is not work in his sense, because attention is open and the default mode network is doing the work. The redefinition is coherent with his definitions, but it should be noticed, because it makes the thesis harder to refute: any hour of thinking that might count against it is counted as rest.

The 2023 video then gives Darwin's day, from a biographical account Koe does not name. Darwin arrived at his study at eight after his morning walk and breakfast; he worked for an hour and a half; he took a break to read mail and write letters; he returned for more serious work (experiments); "by noon Darwin would stop his work day and head out on a long walk"; after reading and answering more letters, he took a nap, another walk, and returned to his study for another work block (**Source:** same file).

In December 2024 Koe gives the most detailed version, now with a source. He picked up *Daily Rituals* by Mason Currey after seeing a graphic online about how creatives structure sleep, work, day jobs, food, leisure and exercise, and he listens to it on walks as inspiration to experiment with his own routine. Reading Darwin's day, he realized he already pulls a lot from it: a short walk at 7 a.m. and breakfast; the first and most productive work session from 8:00 to 9:30; a break to read letters and spend time with family; a second session from 10:30 to noon, after which "he considered himself to have done a good day's work"; a second walk around his "**sandwalk**," "a quarter mile gravel path that Darwin created specifically for thinking and reflection"; after lunch and the newspaper, rest while his wife read aloud; and at 4 p.m. a third walk, usually the sandwalk, sometimes with company. Koe's commentary: "Charles Darwin of all people worked surprisingly little for the amount that he accomplished... more than most of the productivity obsessed people today. What's the difference? Ideas." "Focused work sessions paired with regular walks allowed him to achieve a balance of intense intellectual work and periods of rest and contemplation." "Darwin wrote 19 books and developed his theory of evolution with this routine. Imagine if this guy had AI." He jokes that he is helping make "the idea guy" meme a thing (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29).

"What's the difference? Ideas" is the thesis of this whole section in three words. The same 2024 video ends one of its work blocks with a direct echo: after his second block, Koe says, "like Darwin I also feel like I've accomplished quite a bit and I don't need to worry about work the rest of the day" (section 13.2).

Two later tellings add a different lesson. In March 2025 Darwin illustrates the alternation of networks: he "worked on his projects in focused blocks, then went on long walks; on these walks his brain was at rest and potential solutions to problems pop into his head" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). In May 2025 the lesson is about time horizons. Darwin wrote 19 books, discovered the theory of evolution and changed the world while working four to five hours a day, followed by long walks, reading and leisure that fueled his mind with ideas. "The difference between you and him is that this was his life's work"; it did not matter how long it took. For you, Koe says, it is do-or-die: succeed in six months or quit and accept your fate forever, which is "laughable and backward," and the constant stress and survival state that it produces does not help (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). This is the life's work of Chapter 9 applied to the workday: short days are sustainable only inside a long horizon, and the short horizon is what produces the long, stressed days.

In a January 2025 interview Koe uses Darwin as one end of a spectrum. His "guilty pleasure," he says, is reading about the routines of creative people: how often they work, what they do at certain times, what they produced. At one end is Darwin, "who would work in like two-hour blocks separated by long walks, and he produced 19 books in his lifetime and developed the theory of evolution" (Koe assumes he worked alone). At the other is hustle culture: "super long days, you live in a box," the meme that "all dudes need is the TV and the lawn chair," get up, work, go to bed. He asks his guests where they fall (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md`, 2025-01-28). One guest's answer appears in section 13.2.

The figures vary between tellings, and the corpus records the inconsistency without explanation:

| Telling | Date | Darwin's work time | Lesson drawn |
|---|---|---|---|
| *The 4 Hour Workday* video | 2023-06 | ~1.5 h, then more work until noon, plus an afternoon block | Three to five hours plus active rest is the sweet spot |
| *Daily Rituals* reading | 2024-12 | 8:00–9:30 and 10:30–12:00 (about three hours) | "What's the difference? Ideas" |
| Interview with the Stan founders | 2025-01 | "Two-hour blocks separated by long walks" | One end of the spectrum, against hustle culture |
| *How To Learn Anything* | 2025-03 | Focused blocks, then long walks | Solutions arrive on walks |
| *Harsh Truth* | 2025-05 | Four to five hours a day | It was his life's work; no six-month deadline |

The number of books (19) and the theory of evolution are constant; the hours are not. The variation does not affect the argument, which needs only that Darwin's working hours were short relative to his output and alternated with walks; it does mean that no single figure should be quoted as Koe's account of Darwin.

**Complementary context:** Mason Currey's *Daily Rituals: How Artists Work* (2013) collects short accounts of the schedules of writers, composers, painters and scientists. The "sandwalk" was a path on the grounds of Down House, Darwin's home in Kent, which he walked daily and used for thinking. Koe's descriptions of Darwin's schedule match Currey's account in outline; the total hours differ depending on what is counted as work.

#### The polarity of work and rest

Up to this point the argument has been about creative output: rest produces ideas. A second argument runs alongside it and is about meaning. It uses a word Koe applies to many pairs, **polarity**: one thing cannot exist without its opposite. Its first application to work and rest is from 2022. Work is a necessary part of life and, for most people, consumes the majority of their waking hours; for many, it lacks interest and passion; therefore a large share of their waking life is spent on something they have no interest in. "Is this something that you want?" By polarity, work and rest do not exist without each other: if you only rested your whole life, you would not like it; there would be no balance, "no wave." "The rest loses meaning if work ceases to exist, and if rest ceases to exist then work lacks meaning." He says he personally does not plan to retire ("maybe my views on this will change"), and that even if he did, he would still build something. Since work and rest are both a must, the question becomes: do you want the work half of your life to lack interest or enjoyment? (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

The chain is worth spelling out because the conclusion is not the expected one. The premise (work and rest need each other) could be used to justify any job: since you will have to work anyway, accept it. Koe draws the opposite conclusion. Since work is unavoidable and occupies most of life, the quality of that work is the main determinant of the quality of life, and choosing it is the most important design decision available.

In a 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, Koe returns to the principle in its most abstract form, but the context matters. He is agreeing with Bush about never fully stopping work, and the "again" in his sentence points back to Bush, who had used the same word earlier in the conversation in a different sense. For Bush, **polarity** means "balance through extremes": avoid operating in the middle, and alternate total intensity with total shutdown ("any kind of intensity: bring it or totally shut it off"), at every time scale, from twelve weeks on and one week off to fifty minutes of work and ten of break. That is a guest's usage, and Chapter 39 (section 39.4) treats it, together with Koe's answer to it. Koe's own sentence takes the word somewhere else: "it's polarity again, where work and rest are on the same spectrum... one cannot exist without the other." Without the feeling of rest, work would become too boring, or "literally the word would not exist," because "they play into each other" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). The two senses should be kept apart. Bush's polarity is a schedule (how to distribute intensity and rest); Koe's, here, is a definition (each term draws its meaning from the other). The point of Koe's version is semantic as much as psychological: rest is defined by its contrast with work, and vice versa.

The evidence Koe offers is experience everyone recognizes. If you went on a two-week vacation, toward the end (he says "towards week three") you would want to get back to work, home and routine. Retirement as "just not working anymore" is "such a silly concept to me"; perhaps it should mean working on something you want to work on instead. "If your rest is eternal it loses its meaning" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31). In 2024 the same observation becomes a diagnosis. Work and rest counterbalance each other. If you go on vacation too long, you want to get back home and to normal life; you tire of a vacation after one, two or three weeks. The same applies to work, especially work you do not care about: if you work 40 to 80 hours a week and come home not looking forward to the next day, you crave rest. The result is a constant cycle: "I need a vacation," "I need to get back to work," "I need a vacation." That, he says, is not the point of life. The point is to understand both and "**create work and rest that no longer feel like work and rest**" (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

Immediately afterward he adds the clause that makes the principle demanding: you have to *create* them. "You need to create these things. You are never ever ever ever going to find them in a job or something else that someone assigned to you." He qualifies it in the same breath (this does not mean all jobs are bad; "just use your brain") and apologizes for getting heated, explaining that he is yelling at a camera, not at the viewer (**Source:** same file). The "never ever" is one of the sharpest statements of a position that, across the corpus, oscillates. Elsewhere in the same period Koe calls jobs "an absolutely incredible stepping stone," and over the years his tone moves between rejection of employment and acceptance of it as a stage; the problem he identifies is permanence and complacency, not the job as such. Chapters 2 and 29 trace that oscillation. In this chapter the claim should be read narrowly: work that no longer feels like work is something you design, and an assignment by definition was designed by someone else.

Two passages from 2024 describe what happens when the polarity is broken from the work side. In the summary of his book *The Art of Focus*, Koe reads a sentence from its chapter on "the new rich": "Nobody said they wanted to work 40 plus hours a week, numb their mind with mindless entertainment and put off their dreams just to wake up one day wondering where the time went, yet that's what everyone does." The word "work" triggers a cascade of stressful thoughts about tomorrow, forever. People do not realize that work is a necessary part of life: "it brings contrast to rest." Everyone wants to spend most of life "sipping coconuts on the beach," but people with endless free time are "arguably the most miserable," because "they don't realize that **work brings order to the mind**" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The phrase "work brings order to the mind" connects the polarity to Chapter 5. If the mind tends toward disorder and a goal is what orders it, then work, understood as sustained effort toward a goal, is not only a means of earning a living but an ordering structure. Endless free time is miserable for the same reason a mind without a goal is chaotic. This is the same claim, from the other direction, that section 13.4 will make about projects.

In September 2024 he describes the cycle in emotional terms. "It's a sad reality that the term work is now perceived as a curse": the mind "floods with familiar pasts and predictable futures of stress, overwhelm and anxiety." "When you're at work you crave to be at rest; when you're at rest you eventually crave to be at work; a disastrous cycle of never feeling like you are where you want to be; your mind is anywhere but the present moment." "People dream of the **perpetual vacation** we call retirement, but once they achieve that delusion it normalizes, as everything does, within a few weeks; you can't help but want to balance being with doing." Hence his conclusion: "it should be considered your full-time job to create work that feels like play, to escape the extremely narrow view that work is bad and money is evil" (**Source:** `You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md`, 2024-09-29). In 2025 he repeats it as a thesis about meaning: "you will not be happy in a state of perpetual vacation because **meaning is found in contrast**"; without work to balance rest, rest becomes meaningless, and after a month of vacation you want to get back home and to work. People who hate their jobs "throw the baby out with the bathwater," hoping never to work again, when "the problem is you're working on the wrong thing" (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29).

The "disastrous cycle" is the polarity seen from inside a badly designed life, and it shows why Koe treats the dead stop and presence as the same issue. A person caught in the cycle is never where they are: at work they think about rest, at rest they think about work. The remedy is not to balance the two halves of a bad arrangement but to change the work, so that rest becomes recovery from something worth returning to. The 2025 argument about Cheetos and burnout, above, is the same structure: rest that does not recover you for meaningful work is not rest.

On retirement the corpus is consistent. In 2022 he did not plan to retire, while noting that his view might change; in 2023 retirement as not working was "a silly concept"; in 2024 and 2025 it is a "delusion" that normalizes in weeks. The announced possibility of change has not materialized in the corpus.

#### Work as a means to leisure: Aristotle and the self-directed career

Koe twice grounds the polarity in Aristotle and the Greeks, in passages from 2023 that are almost identical. He calls them "predictions from the past." Aristotle condemned manual labor as harmful to body and soul; the ancient Greeks saw work as necessary, but "only as a means to leisure, creativity and contemplation," which they considered the keys to happiness. They needed the contrast: leisure cannot exist without work, it holds no meaning; "without work, leisure loses its meaning and vice versa," there must be a "push-pull." Koe then adapts the idea. Manual work is not inherently bad ("it's all subjective... based on a specific perspective"); but if you are "manually, robotically, animalistically completing tasks" day after day, you are not human for that portion of life: "completing mindless tasks for the sake of survival or status makes you no different than a robot or animal," and "if you are a robot, you're going to be replaced by robots" (**Source:** `Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md`, 2023-04-16; `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31).

The adaptation is explicit and should be kept apart from its source. Aristotle's condemnation of manual labor belonged to a society in which such labor was done by slaves and non-citizens; Koe rejects the class judgment ("not inherently bad") and keeps the structural point: work matters as a means to something that makes us human. His addition is the robot: what dehumanizes is not the hands but the mindlessness, and in an economy of automation, mindless work is also the work most exposed to replacement. Chapter 36 develops that economic argument.

In February 2024 he gives the Aristotelian version that he finds most useful. The massive evolutionary problem leading to this moment, he says, is labor. As he presents Aristotle, "the objective of work is usually to sustain our lives biologically," an objective we share with other animals, while "the objective of leisure can and should be to sustain other aspects of our lives which make us uniquely human": our souls, our minds, our personal and civic relationships. Leisure is therefore wasted if it is not used purposefully (**Source:** `Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md`, 2024-02-25). He then adapts it in his own direction. Work and rest should not be seen as distinct opposites but as complementary. Creative work is the future in a tech-enhanced society. "**Evolution is problem solving to reverse entropy**": humans have solved the problems that make life miserable since the dawn of time; we want to avoid suffering, so we have built technology that lets us do what nature calls us to do, which is to pursue personal development, expand our sense of self, and do meaningful work that lets others do the same (**Source:** same file).

The last sentence ties the chapter to the book's spine. Entropy (Chapter 5) is the tendency toward disorder; problem solving is the reversal of it; technology has taken over much of the problem solving that once sustained biological life; what remains is the problem solving that sustains what is uniquely human. Creative work, on this view, is not one career among others but the form work takes once survival is handled, and it is the kind of work that can be complementary to rest rather than its opposite. The sequence "work sustains biological life, leisure sustains the human" is Aristotle as Koe presents him; the claim that evolution is problem solving to reverse entropy, and that creative work is therefore the future, is Koe's.

The same thread reappears in 2025 under a name of his own, the **self-directed career**. Before industrialization, he says, free individuals were mostly artisans and farmers, and the mark of a free person was acting on one's own interest and doing many things throughout life: "Around 80% of free workers were self-employed versus a mere 10% today." Slaves, by contrast, were expected to perform one repetitive, mechanical task for life, "work that machines are going to do now." In the industrial age, with machines, "we just found ourselves trapped in Excel sheets and algorithms and factories doing this repetitive work" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md`, 2025-05-25). Koe gives no source for the percentages, and they should be treated as rhetorical support rather than established figures. The argument they support does not depend on their precision: the generalist who directs their own work is the historical image of freedom, and the specialist performing one task for life is its opposite. Chapter 20 develops the generalist; here the point is that the design of one's work, which the polarity made the central question of the good life, has historically been the privilege of the free.

#### Leisure: maxing, mindset and normie nights

The later corpus gives the rest half of the day its own vocabulary, under the word **leisure**, which Koe uses with a meaning of his own. The most vivid formulation is from his May 2025 deep work routine, as the step after mental metabolism: "The second half of my day is spent **leisure maxing**. I fill my mind with nutritious food. I **carb load the psyche** so it can perform its best. I go on walks, read books, talk to smart people, relax with my loved ones, and try to learn or do at least a few new things each week. New places, new experiences, new trends or skills. Something that keeps me fresh that isn't directly related to my work" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

The metaphor completes the gym analogy. Deep work is training to failure; mental metabolism is recovery; leisure maxing is eating, and specifically carbohydrate loading, the athlete's practice of filling energy stores before an effort. The food is "nutritious": the qualifier excludes the cheap dopamine of Chapter 11. And the last sentence sets a condition that recurs: the novelty should not be directly related to work. This is the continuation of the 2023 instruction to "fill your brain in the afternoons" (section 13.3), now framed as nourishment rather than as an activity on a schedule.

Earlier the same month Koe gives leisure its definition. People ask him what he does with the rest of the day, and he says he feels bad for those who think work is the only thing they can do. "Leisure is a mindset." You can write for fun, for leisure or for rest, but once the writing is tied to an obligatory outcome for work, it ceases to be leisure. He gives graded examples. A walk with an external goal of 3,000 steps still counts: that goal often frees the mind from thinking about the walk, which leaves more mental energy for the subconscious to work on problems. Reading a book becomes work once you analyze it and try to extract something. The gym, a run, yoga, a nap, cooking, cleaning, socializing: during them the mind may oscillate between work and rest, but the purpose of rest should be a lack of focus on work. For creative work, "these types of leisure" are often direct fuel, since creative work depends on the quality of ideas and most ideas do not come while working. And then the closing move: "**leisure is how you begin to create a life where work, rest and play blur into one**." If long walks count as work, "then I work all day every day"; even sleep contributes (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04).

The definition is subtle, and it creates a tension the corpus records. Leisure is defined by the absence of an *obligatory work outcome*, not by the absence of benefit to work. A walk can feed your work and still be leisure, as long as you are not walking *in order to* produce something. But elsewhere Koe recommends turning walks into games with rules (read a chapter, write sections of a newsletter) and, in March 2025, lists a thirty-minute walk with audio and notes among the "keystone habits" of learning (section 13.3). By the May 2025 definition, those walks are work, not leisure. Koe partly anticipates the objection with the remark that if walks count as work, he works all day every day, which fits his ideal of a life in which the categories dissolve. The tension is not resolved; the reasonable reading is that the same activity can be done in either mode, and that what matters for rest is whether attention is under obligation, not what the body is doing.

The earliest description of his evenings, from 2023, is less theoretical and more candid. His nights, he tells Dickie Bush, are "pretty unproductive," what he calls **normie nights**, something he would not promote. He has a hard cutoff around seven, sits on the couch and watches Netflix; *The Last Kingdom* gives him ideas, and story-based shows teach him story. When he feels he is watching too much, he reads in his room for an hour for a few nights: "I've become very in tune with my body." He does not talk about it much, he says, because it can be spun, and because there were times he should not have done it, since he could have been making progress. The same goes for video games: he played the new *Call of Duty* for a month, then "quickly realized, like, I'm not making progress... I need to cut this out immediately." Blue light from screens is the one downside he accepts; studies argue both sides, and in his own experiments he noticed no big difference. What he avoids are destructive evening activities: doomscrolling Instagram and eating right before bed. "I try to just avoid destructive things" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

The passage is a useful corrective to the more programmatic versions of rest. Netflix appears here as part of Koe's own evenings, the same Netflix that the rest commandment excluded from the definition of rest (while granting that it has "its place"). The difference is the frame: the evening comes after a morning of demanding work and is regulated by attention to the body and by a single rule, avoid what is destructive. Koe does not present this as a model; he presents it as what he does, with its admitted lapses (the month of *Call of Duty*) and corrections. It is experience, not prescription.

#### The ultimate goal: one evolving vision

The polarity argument and the definition of leisure converge in a sentence Koe offers in December 2024 as the goal behind all of it: "The ultimate goal is to have a life so beautifully designed that **work, rest and play cease to exist**. The entirety of your life is channeled into **one singular yet evolving vision**" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29).

The sentence does not contradict the polarity; it describes its resolution. Work and rest remain distinct as modes of attention (narrow and open, task positive and default mode), but they stop being experienced as opposed categories, one endured for the sake of the other. When the work is chosen and the rest feeds it, the walk, the book, the conversation and the morning block all serve the same vision, and the question "am I working or resting?" loses its force. The vision is "evolving," which links it to Chapter 7: it is not a fixed endpoint but a direction that changes as you do.

In January 2026 Koe states the same aim in the language of multiple interests. Recapping a video for people with many interests, he lists four tensions they feel: they have multiple interests but feel they cannot keep learning forever; they love interest-based self-education but must carve out time for it outside their career; they understand the need for self-sufficiency but feel they do not yet have value worth paying for; and they need to adapt fast because nobody knows the future. The question that resolves them is how to combine all your interests into "a way of life, not a way of work-life balance, but a **complete way of life**, a way of turning work into play" (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20).

"Work-life balance" assumes two opposed quantities to be traded against each other; a "complete way of life" assumes a single design in which they are integrated. The difference is the difference between managing the disastrous cycle and leaving it. This is the horizon against which the routines of the next section should be read: they are not schedules for balancing work and life but experiments in building a life where the balance stops being the question.

### 13.2 Routines and Habits

#### A routine contains the rules of your game

Section 13.1 ended with an ideal, a life designed so well that work, rest and play cease to exist as separate categories. This section is about the instrument of that design, the routine, and Koe opens his best-known routine video with a definition taken from the game metaphor of Chapter 10: "**Without rules there is no game, without a game there is no winning. A routine contains the rules for how you live your life.** The longer you play the better you get, and as you're getting better you often forget the rules and win anyways." And then: "Now is the time to stop playing the game society told you to play and start playing your own" (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19).

Each clause of the metaphor does work. Rules constitute a game: without them there is nothing to win, only activity. A routine is the set of rules for a life, which means a life without a deliberate routine is not free play but a game whose rules were written by someone else (the "game society told you to play," the default path of Part I). Practice internalizes the rules until they no longer need attention: you forget them and win anyway. That last clause anticipates the claim below that a refined system becomes "second nature," and it explains why routine is not the enemy of spontaneity. A rule that has become automatic stops costing attention, which leaves attention free for the game itself.

A month later Koe gives a second definition that connects routine to the theme of order: "**A routine is a set of practical goals that orders the mind.**" His example is a writer who moves or travels for an extended period. They go through a stressful acclimation period until their mind runs on new systems, and if they cannot write well outside their normal routine, they feel threatened "because who they are may die" (**Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The definition joins three earlier chapters. A routine is made of goals (Chapter 8), small and practical ones; goals order the mind (Chapter 5); and a routine that sustains an identity is defended like that identity (Chapter 3), so that losing it feels like a small death. The traveling writer's stress is conceptual survival applied to a schedule.

In a January 2025 conversation with Sahil Bloom, Koe answers people who say they do not need a routine, or do not have one: "the people that think they don't have a routine just have a **routine of not having a routine**... that's what our mind loves. It wants the routine." He states the danger in the same passage: "if you get trapped in a routine that either you didn't create or one that you just don't like, then yeah, that becomes a problem." And he gives the positive case: "most famous creatives, think of Darwin. How did he produce 19 books in his lifetime? It's because he had a routine that allowed his mind... opened his mind up to have the creative ideas, to not focus on the day to day" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). The figure of 19 books is Koe's; the point is that routine is what freed Darwin's attention for ideas. A routine removes daily decisions, and every decision removed is attention returned to the work and to the open mind of section 13.1.

Sahil Bloom, in the same conversation, supplies the objection and its answer from a third party. On social media, he says, "people equate structure with inauthentic." His example is Bryan Johnson: "everyone gets so mad at... all of the structure and routines that he has. And they're like, well, he's not living his life." Bloom spent time with him for his book: "He's like incredibly happy. He loves what he's doing... Immersed in it. It's just that it doesn't match the map that you have for what your life should look like... **Your map does not match his terrain**, if you will. And so you're taking your idea of the fact that structure doesn't create happiness, and applying it to someone who very much feels that structure creates happiness." His conclusion: "do what works for you... I'm not forcing anyone to do anything" (**Source:** same file). The idea belongs to Bloom, not to Koe, who uses it to support his own point that routine is not a cage unless it is someone else's.

**Complementary context:** Bryan Johnson is an entrepreneur known for a highly regimented longevity protocol he publicizes online. "The map is not the territory" is a phrase associated with the semanticist Alfred Korzybski; Bloom inverts its usual use, applying it to judgments about other people's lives. Koe, elsewhere in the corpus, criticizes the longevity fashion as a status game (Chapter 40); here he only uses the exchange about structure.

#### Lifestyle design: experiment, don't copy

If a routine is the rules of your own game, someone has to write them, and for the person who works for themselves that someone is no longer a manager. Koe makes the point in 2022, describing the one-person business: another important aspect of it is **lifestyle design**. Working for someone else, a manager manages you; working for yourself, "you can sleep until 1 pm," but will you get the work done? How will you systemize it, structure your days for maximum productivity and maximum creativity, and design the life you want "through trial and error, experimentation, self-reflection and understanding the problems blocking your quality of life"? (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md`, 2022-10-23). "Lifestyle design" is a common phrase that Koe uses in a specific sense: not the choice of a lifestyle from a menu, but the ongoing work of designing one's days by experiment, which becomes necessary the moment external structure disappears.

In December 2024 he states the principle that follows from it: "There is no right way to work." Alex Hormozi, he says (adding that Hormozi "recently went on vacation, I'm proud of him"), "does very well working 12 hours a day and having this hustle and grind mindset, but that's his identity, that's what brings him fulfillment; for a lot of people that's not theirs." Hormozi is very successful, "but so were some of the world's most famous creatives who barely worked at all." "The point is to experiment, to collect various perspectives, experience them and discard the pieces that don't work for your unique set of goals, interests and values. It's called lifestyle design for a reason; you don't simply copy someone else's design and expect it to make you rich. **How can you call yourself a creative if you don't apply the creative process to the main thing that influences everything else?**" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29).

This passage qualifies Koe's own criticism of overwork. Elsewhere in the corpus he calls sustained twelve- to sixteen-hour days "a sign of major dysfunction" and describes hustlers as performing distracted work as a status badge. Here, a twelve-hour day is legitimate if it is genuinely the person's identity and fulfills them. The corpus treats this not as a chronological change but as two registers that coexist: a general critique of hustle culture as a default, and respect for an individual choice that is genuinely one's own. The criterion that reconciles them is the one this chapter keeps returning to: whether the routine was designed or assigned. The final question, about applying the creative process to one's own life, is the strongest version of the argument. The routine is "the main thing that influences everything else," because it determines when and how all other work is done; a creative who copies it from someone else has outsourced their most consequential design decision.

A 2024 passage about the gym makes the same argument with a concrete case and gives a word, **optimal**, a meaning of its own. Starting in the gym, it is foolish to hop blindly between random machines hoping to enjoy it; first, research programs aligned with your life goals. For a busy person, a six-day, high-volume bodybuilding split probably will not be enjoyable, but a three-day minimalist program could be perfect. Some argue it is not optimal for muscle growth, "but your goal isn't only muscle growth: you choose the program that is optimal for your goals, not someone else's." Everyone follows "optimal" routines found online (the best gym routine for the most muscle, the best morning routine for the most work), but those attend to only one goal, and following them without goals of your own is the same as having society assign you one. Understand your values and goals, choose, then experiment with the process. A program that does not account for your life context will not be adhered to, "therefore it's not optimal"; something "less optimal, quote unquote," that fits your lifestyle "to perfection" builds more muscle "because consistency is key." "That's the entire game of life, is lifestyle design": picking the very few habits and routines you can perform day after day for the rest of your life "to become exponentially the person you want to become" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

"Optimal," in this sense, is relative to the whole set of one's goals and to one's capacity to sustain the routine. A program that maximizes one variable while being abandoned in three weeks is optimal for nothing. The phrase "consistency is key" deserves a note, because Koe's view of consistency changed. In 2023 he had said "consistency is overrated, persistency is underrated" and "persistency and iteration is greater than consistency and repetition"; in October 2024 he would say that consistency maintains progress but does not create it. The 2024 gym passage sits between these and uses "consistency" in a limited sense: adherence to a routine that fits. The two views are compatible once their scopes are separated. Repetition builds and sustains the base; iteration improves it; and neither is possible with a routine you cannot keep.

The same video turns the argument into a method. Once you have a program, you may dislike a certain exercise and start skipping leg day; "this is your chance to take it even further and make it unique to you." A disliked exercise is a problem, and "it's all just **problem solving all the way down, all the way up**." Research potential replacements to experiment with; now the gym has "something fun to try," you look forward to it, and if you don't like the new exercise you swap again. You are no longer hopping randomly between machines, which causes chaos in the mind; you walk in knowing what to do and what to try, with confidence, clear, not focused on other people's opinions. Koe calls this "check number two for getting into flow and actually enjoying the process" (the list of checks it belongs to is not enumerated in the transcript). You see progress faster, which is feedback, which brings more flow and enjoyment, so you become obsessed and stick with it, instead of going in blindly and hating the process. The same applies to reading (**Source:** same file).

The method is the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6 applied to a habit: a goal, an action, feedback, a correction. Dislike is treated not as a reason to quit but as data about a problem to solve. The connection to flow (Chapter 10) is explicit: clarity removes the chaos of choosing, and visible progress supplies the feedback that flow requires. A routine designed this way is enjoyable not because it was enjoyable from the start but because every point of friction was turned into an experiment.

#### Few habits, chosen: your habits create your future

Within a routine, Koe singles out habits, and his argument starts from arithmetic. You only have so much time in a day, so you can only have so many habits; therefore the habits you choose are of utmost importance, because they create your future and influence your quality of life. Most people are not in control of their habits, so their lives are on a trajectory they did not set, and changing who they are becomes increasingly hard. Habits, he adds, are not only physical but mental. Scrolling shrinks attention span because the mind gets into the habit of receiving novel information every thirty seconds; then you cannot focus on meaningful conversations, on building a business, or even on putting on your shoes for the gym. Your mind reacts with memes "others programmed into your mind"; you cannot take anything seriously, you think worthwhile things are a joke, you feel depressed and hopeless, but you cannot see a solution, because "your mind is too busy stuck **bouncing on the surface** to see one" (**Source:** `The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md`, 2024-06-16).

The solution follows in the same video, as a sequence. Reclaim your focus, so that you can implement new habits; implement new habits, because you can only choose a few; and make them "**high leverage, high impact, and highly enjoyable**." Chosen habits refocus attention on the depth of life. "Where you focus your attention programs your mind, creates who you are and determines your future." Walking, the subject of the video, is his example of a habit that "covers all bases": mind, body, spirit and business (**Source:** same file). The three criteria correspond to the constraints of the arithmetic. Because slots are few, each habit must have leverage (affect many areas) and impact (affect them substantially); because habits must last for years, each must be enjoyable, or it will not survive.

The ordering in the sequence is not incidental. Focus comes first, habits second. Chapter 6 recorded that in 2022 Koe said "habits aren't the most important thing," because focus is the only thing with root control, while other passages, including several in this section, put habits at the center. It is a change of emphasis for which the author gives no explanation; the reading this book adopts is that focus is the cause and habits are its sediment, what repeated attention leaves behind. The 2024 sequence states exactly that order: reclaim focus, then install habits.

How many habits? Koe gives a minimum in two forms. In 2024, as "the solution to chaos": "The solution to chaos is finally taking control of your life. You need **four habits**: one that builds your mind, one that builds your body, one that builds your business, one that builds your connection." He closes with a definition: "The good life is the process of becoming everything you could be" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). A month later, as a reason to read, the same list with "work" and "relationships" in place of "business" and "connection": "The best way to end bad habits is to replace them." You can only have so many habits: one for mind, body, work and relationships, "minimum." Audit your day: how many of the habits you perform unconsciously help your future? Observing society, he says, it is easy to assume close to zero, which is why most people are not happy and will be the same person in the same position in twenty years (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

| Domain | Example habits Koe gives (2023–2024) |
|---|---|
| Mind | Books, self-reflection, writing |
| Body | Gym, long walks, nutrient-dense food |
| Business / work | Skill acquisition, problem solving, self-promotion |
| Connection / relationships | Time with loved ones, conversations with smart people |

The examples in the first three rows come from a 2023 statement of the same idea, quoted below; the fourth row is filled from the leisure passages of section 13.1, since the four-habit lists name the domain without examples. Walking is Koe's favorite example of a single habit that touches all four.

The audit introduces the second principle: bad habits are ended by **replacement**, not by removal. Koe's example in 2024 is staring at a TV at night in "unnatural blue light." He speculates, in a passage he himself labels as exaggerated and a "deep topic," that night-time blue light combined with avoiding sunlight might contribute to skin cancer, and he tells the viewer to "go on an esoteric search for light health"; replacing the TV with reading, he says, could dramatically change your mental health and "potentially save your life" ("I know I'm exaggerating") (**Source:** same file). The health claim should be read as he frames it: speculation offered with a disclaimer, not a finding. The principle does not depend on it. A habit occupies a slot in the day; removing it leaves the slot empty, and an empty slot is refilled by whatever is most available, usually the habit you removed. Replacement fills the slot with something chosen.

An earlier video, from 2023, gives a list of swaps that show what replacement means in practice. Swap mindless scrolling for intentional learning: the same activity with a different intention, because "one has a goal, one doesn't." Swap video games for building new projects: "building projects is a video game in the real world." He is not telling you to quit video games, he adds; you need a **foundational habit of learning and execution**, and without it "you have no business doing other things." If your daily life does not include what brings you peace and progress, "you're dead." Swap sleeping in for a morning walk with a podcast, filling the mind with information that exposes you to new potentials and brings clarity and vision to your mornings. "Learning leads to awareness of opportunity, awareness of opportunity creates motivation"; continuous daily learning changes your decision-making over time (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23).

The scrolling swap is the clearest illustration of Koe's view of habits as intentions. Scrolling and learning can look identical from outside (a person reading on a phone); the difference is the goal, and the goal changes what attention selects, as the lens of Chapter 6 predicted. The video game swap repeats Chapter 10: a project has the structure of a game (quests, feedback, progress) with real stakes. The chain at the end, from learning to awareness of opportunity to motivation, inverts the usual order. Motivation is not the precondition of learning but its product.

In another 2023 video Koe restates the four domains with examples and adds a confession that matters for reading his advice. "**The difference between you and who you want to become are the habits that compose your lifestyle.**" Build your mind with books, self-reflection and writing; your body with the gym, long walks and nutrient-dense foods; your business with skill acquisition, problem solving and self-promotion. People who seem to have it all did not wake up one day with the best mind, body and business; they took tiny daily actions. Others will tell you to stop playing video games, going out and distracting yourself; Koe agrees, but he still plays video games about two to five hours a week (not habitually) and goes out with friends and stays up late every two to four weeks. He can do so, he says, because his priorities are straight, so he can think critically when the time comes (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30). The confession sets the scope of the advice: replacement targets habits, defined as what you do automatically and daily; an occasional evening of games or a late night out is not a habit and is compatible with straight priorities. His later statements on video games harden in tone (if you feel the need to defend them, they are probably not good for you), but the position, conscious moderation, remains the same.

Two short passages bracket the scale of habit change. On one side, in 2024, Koe declines to give small tips: "I could give you tips like go make your bed every morning or organize your bookshelf or clear off your desk, which I literally just did, but I'm not going to give you those, because that's not very big picture. That's good for just adopting as a habit, but it's not going to make that much of a difference" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). Small habits are not wrong; they do not have the leverage the arithmetic demands. On the other side, in 2023, he closes a video on escaping wage slavery with the instruction that summarizes the section: "Replace the habits that keep you enslaved with habits that will break you free. It won't happen immediately; be okay with that. **If you can stay on the path, you win**" (**Source:** `How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md`, 2023-08-01). The first passage sets the size of the habits worth choosing; the second sets the time horizon in which they pay off.

#### Bad habits compound: a disagreement with James Clear

In the 2024 reading video Koe adds a claim about the dynamics of bad habits, framed as a disagreement with James Clear's *Atomic Habits*. "Bad habits aren't just bad because they take away time from having good habits; they slowly erode every other aspect of your life. Bad habits aren't neutral in their effect, they're exponentially bad, just as good habits, when consistent, are exponentially good." He refers to the book's well-known graph: getting one percent better every day compounds to a large gain over a year, while getting one percent worse every day declines toward zero (0.99 to the 365th power is about 0.03). "I understand the math... I understand that you'd, like, plateau. I'd argue it's not that simple, it compounds." His example is weight: if you keep gaining weight, you do not plateau; you keep eating more until you decide to reverse it; it is the fit trajectory that plateaus. "I feel like it's kind of the opposite." The more weight gained, the harder it is to dig out, and the same holds for any life situation as you "tack on all of these responsibilities and little things" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04).

The exact disagreement is unclear in the transcript, and the corpus records it as ambiguous. Clear's declining curve flattens as it approaches zero, which Koe reads as a plateau; his objection seems to be that real bad habits do not level off in their effects but accelerate, because each one makes the next harder to reverse, while good trajectories (fitness, for example) eventually level off near a ceiling. If that is the reading, his point is not about the arithmetic, which he accepts, but about the shape of real-life decline: it is self-reinforcing. The practical consequence fits the rest of the section: the cost of delaying replacement grows with time.

**Complementary context:** in *Atomic Habits* (2018), James Clear illustrates "the power of tiny gains" with the calculation that 1.01 raised to the 365th power is about 37.8, and 0.99 raised to the 365th power is about 0.03. Koe accepts the illustration and disputes its implications for bad habits. Elsewhere in the corpus he also criticizes Clear's slogan that systems matter more than goals (Chapter 6).

#### The author's routine, version by version

Koe has described his own day many times, and the descriptions are useful precisely because they differ. Before reading them, one warning from the corpus applies. In the 2023 conversation in which both describe their routines, Dickie Bush frames the audience's questions this way: "I'm always wary of looking at people's current routines versus... I want to know what they were doing when they were at my stage." So both of them describe their routine from two years earlier and their routine now (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). The warning is Bush's, and it is sound: a routine is fitted to a stage of a business and a life, and the current routine of someone with a team, an audience and a product answers problems a beginner does not have. Koe's own versions should be read with that in mind; the earliest is the one closest to the stage of most readers.

**The early pattern and the March 2023 day.** In the same conversation, Koe says his routine has had "the same components for years," only reordered. Even at his only full-time job, where clients were assigned to him (the job eventually became remote), he pushed client work back and worked on his own projects for the first two to four hours; in college those hours were deep study. He used to go to the gym first thing; now he goes around 12:30. Then he gives his current day, with the reasons for each step (**Source:** same file):

1. Wake at 5:30.
2. Morning cardio: twenty minutes walking on the treadmill. It is a no-decision way to wake up ("throw on the clothes I took off last night, go get sweaty") and attention to cardiovascular health ("as bros we put that off for a long time"). Done by six.
3. Hygiene, shower, coffee.
4. First time block at home, 45 to 90 minutes, usually 60, around 6:30 to 7:30, "always based on the highest priority project." This is usually a **building** phase, not maintenance; at the time, it was his book, every morning.
5. Take the dog out, drive to a coffee shop around eight, order an Americano ("no decision fatigue, no calories"), and do the second time block: the newsletter and content outlining, a **maintenance** phase, which he describes as what he must maintain in his business and also enjoys.
6. Home by about ten; an hour-long walk (shirtless if sunny, for a tan), listening to an audiobook, a YouTube lecture or a podcast. The intention is not to generate ideas, but he often comes back with ideas written on his phone.
7. Second maintenance block: community check-in and email. "I have to do that last... if you do that like first thing in the morning it just opens way too many loops." The day, he says, is structured as "**slowly opening myself up to the world** as time goes on throughout the day."
8. The gym, right after, "to wash myself from that and take out any potential rage that emails may have caused."
9. Lunch at solid, healthy restaurants he walks to, one to two hours; back by three or four; check the community again; more walks, reading, YouTube, not working.

The logic of the order is the logic of Chapter 12's priority ladder, in the vocabulary of open loops. Building comes first because it is the most demanding and the easiest to displace. Maintenance follows because it is necessary but less fragile. Email and community come last because they open loops: each message is an unfinished conversation that will occupy attention, and opening them early would contaminate the blocks that need a closed mind. The gym marks the end of work and absorbs whatever stress the inbox produced. The reasons attached to small choices (the treadmill as "no decision," the Americano as "no decision fatigue, no calories") show the same principle at the smallest scale: remove decisions from the morning so that attention is spent only on the work.

**November 2023: nine blocks.** Eight months later Koe describes a different arrangement of the same components in his routine video, block by block.

The first block, at about six, is a thirty-minute walk outside "no matter the weather or how I feel," listening to a podcast or educational material, or planning the day on his phone. He aims for "15 to 20,000 steps a day just because of the stacked benefits." Walking "requires minimal effort but brings maximal results": it clears his mind, wakes him up, gets him away from distractions, "acts as a **creativity block**" (his term, defined in section 13.3, for time set aside to hunt for ideas outside the productive work blocks), keeps him lean, healthy and "slightly tan," and "reverses most of the damage done by sitting under life-sucking blue light." He mentions that he is currently experimenting: for the past few days, fifteen minutes each of meditation and of another practice the transcript renders unintelligibly (possibly journaling); that very day, he went straight into work. "I don't let my routine get stagnant... if I don't experiment then I eventually get **trapped in the known**... everything in life must evolve" (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19).

The second block is ninety minutes of focused work. "I have a list of recurring tasks and levers that I execute every morning": writing books, newsletters, content and marketing material. "I stack all of my priority tasks in this work block so I can complete them before most people wake up. This allows me to **gradually introduce entropy into my day** as work becomes less structured." The walk before and this block, he says, are "idea generation and writing," the source of "everything good that has come in my life"; the video itself is a written script (**Source:** same file). The phrase "gradually introduce entropy" is the 2023 form of the ordering by entropy that Chapter 12 traced to its 2024–2025 versions.

The third block is another thirty-minute walk, or a run. He runs three times a week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday) for about thirty minutes to meet "the general recommendation" for zone 2 cardio; the transcript says "15 minutes" a week and then "that's not 150 minutes," so the intended figure is probably 150 minutes, though it is ambiguous. He trusts his daily steps to cover the rest. On non-running days he walks (it is hard to write on a run), listens to podcasts, audiobooks, educational material or YouTube videos, and collects ideas on his phone with Cortex, his software (not yet released at the time): "a system for capturing ideas that actually have a use case, which is writing or creating" (**Source:** same file).

The fourth block is a second ninety-minute session, after a shower and breakfast, for less creative tasks: administrative work, client work, responding to clients and collaborators. "This is when open loops and distractions really start to take over, so this is why I have it later in my mornings, because at the very start of my morning I want to make sure I get everything I have to for the day done." Responding to people is "kind of necessary, but it's not as lever moving as writing content every single morning." Distractions in this block "can just be mitigated by another walk" (**Source:** same file).

The fifth block is calls, and it comes with a story about identity. Koe "used to despise calls": he removed them completely, stopped client work and all calls. With Cortex this "isn't possible anymore and I had to accept that even though it threatened my identity." "I adopted a person that doesn't take calls as my identity," so incorporating calls produced a lot of friction. "When I zoom out, I want Cortex to succeed more than I don't want to take calls, therefore I want to take calls." The calls include client calls and internal company, design, development and product calls; if he does not need his desk, he takes them on a walk, and if there are no calls, he walks. Cortex, he adds, was already making money through consulting clients (**Source:** same file). The story is a small case study of Chapters 3 and 4. An identity adopted deliberately ("a person who doesn't take calls") produced the defensive friction those chapters predicted, and the friction was resolved not by willpower but by zooming out to a higher goal that outranked the identity. It also shows that a routine is downstream of the goal: when the goal changed (building a software company), the rules of the game had to change too.

The sixth block, around one or two in the afternoon, is the gym: "the turning point where my day goes from work to rest. I know that I won't be able to operate my best after the gym, so I treat this as a **work cut off** time." For about six months he had trained every day; with the workload piling up, he was considering switching back to three days a week ("going to experiment with that this week"). "I don't need your training ideology... I've studied all of them. I like training every day" (**Source:** same file). Here the gym takes over the function of the 2023 dead stop. Instead of a clock that says four hours are up, a physical event ends the workday, chosen precisely because after it he could not work at his best even if he wanted to.

The seventh to ninth blocks complete the day. A long conversational lunch with his editor, to catch up, socialize, talk about things other than business and laugh. He and his friends keep their social circle "extremely small": he used to have many contacts, but "that seemed to always subtract from my life rather than add to it"; he is not closed to new people, but "I have to be very careful with who I let into my life." Around three or four, a nap, another walk or easy busy work, a slot "dedicated to **psychological recovery**... like in the gym you have to take rest, like with work you also have to take rest." Then dinner with a friend or staying in with his girlfriend (Netflix). Nice dinners are his main expense ("good food, good experiences"), and at good restaurants "it's hard to be unhealthy" (protein like steak or fish with potatoes, "a nicer way of preparing the dinner I would have prepared for myself"). This, he says, is the baseline he falls back to, apart from events like recording an audiobook, trips to the mountains or travel (**Source:** same file).

**A six-month flow state.** In January 2024 Koe describes the routine he followed during an earlier period he calls a six-month flow state, in a video on resetting one's life. He woke up, walked to a lake, sat on a bench in front of the pond, and read a book, highlighting non-stop. Back home he showered, walked ten minutes to a coffee shop and wrote for two hours: "those 2 hours every morning built the entirety of your life right now." Then home, breakfast (he was experimenting with different diets, and suggests "maybe that's a clue," because of the novelty and dopamine of experimenting), another walk, the gym in the afternoon, food, a bit of learning on YouTube, another walk, and in the evening catching up with friends, maybe a little more work, and a show (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07). The interesting detail is the hint about experimentation. Koe suggests that part of what sustained the flow state was the novelty of trying things, which is consistent with the dopamine of Chapter 11 and with his rule that a routine must not become stagnant.

**December 2024: the walk for intention.** A year after the nine-block video, the routine has changed again. Koe now wakes naturally around five. In warmer months he walks first; in winter (the transcript reads "winter ark season," presumably the internet phrase "winter arc") he goes straight to work. The morning walk has a new purpose: it is "a **walk for intention**," with no listening and no phone, "simply thinking what am I going to write about when I get back... I'm already writing in my head," warming up "to the writing process of flowing through ideas so I have the gunk out before I start writing." The first ninety-minute block is "my highest priority, highest lever moving task, usually something new": a book, a product or a business, "something that can be built once and then sold multiple times." It is not a permanent daily task but something done "in kind of like a sprint," placed first "so that I ensure that it's making progress, because since it's not a consistent part of my day... it's easy for me to fall off." Then only fruit until noon, a "modified intermittent fasting with just fruit" (his diet, he says, changes frequently) (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29).

The second block, 45 to 90 minutes, is for "high priority tasks that grow and maintain my work": writing one section of a newsletter (20 to 45 minutes), writing as many content ideas as possible (10 to 30 minutes) and crossposting to platforms (about 15 minutes), "highly dependent on how well my mind is turning out ideas." On Tuesdays he replaces this block with recording a video, a process he teaches in his course *2 Hour Writer*. When Cortex is launching or building, it takes more time; in maintenance mode, "my role at Cortex is the same as it is in my personal brand because it's a product": generating traffic, marketing, generating ideas and strategizing, "my main value source for Cortex." Then more fruit as lunch, and the remark quoted in section 13.1: "like Darwin I also feel like I've accomplished quite a bit and I don't need to worry about work the rest of the day" (**Source:** same file).

The second walk, at midday, is "usually shirtless if it's not too cold, because I want midday sun, which helps with sleep and light health." On it he consumes an audiobook, a YouTube video or a lecture: sometimes a rabbit hole on one topic, sometimes scrolling "until I find something interesting." "This walk specifically is when I can get my best ideas, because I can spend the most time focusing on a problem, because I don't have to focus on work anymore." "This is the walk that sets myself up for the next morning of work, because I'm capturing like crazy inside of Cortex capture connected to my projects." He contrasts this with Apple Notes or Notion, where "these little sentence-long independent ideas" are "not connected to your projects, they're not organized." After or during the walk come the admin tasks: emails, messages to partners or friends (**Source:** same file). The midday walk is the default mode network put to work by design: attention is released from work, the mind is fed, and the ideas it produces are captured into the projects that will use them the next morning. The day is a cycle, not a line.

Then the gym, which "marks a crucial distinction in my day from I'm working to I'm done with work if I want to be." The rest of the day is open: reading, a nap, more walking, errands with his girlfriend, dinner with a friend, meetings. "Sometimes I'm laying on the couch and I have an idea," and he writes for thirty minutes, goes back to the couch, takes a walk: "**it's called leisure for a reason**." "If I have nothing to do and I'm bored, then I'll probably just work more, but it's not a requirement... it's not the default thing I do." Dinner is around five, with a friend or his girlfriend or both; after that he rarely works unless something is pressing or he strongly wants to (he does get good ideas at night reading on his phone). And then the disclaimer that governs all of his routine descriptions: people want it in "a perfect little box," but "this is a guess at what my day looks like over the course of maybe 6 months"; "these things change, experimentation, they're supposed to change." "I can't give you the exact... this is what leads to making a billion dollars, because... you have to discover it" (**Source:** same file).

The versions can be compared directly:

Four kinds of change appear. The clock moves (5:30, 6, 5). The function of the morning walk moves, from waking up to consuming audio to, finally, a phone-free warm-up for writing, with the audio moved to midday. The gym moves from first thing to the end of the workday, where it becomes the cutoff. And attitudes move: from rejecting calls to accepting them, from fasting to eating breakfast. The reasons Koe gives are always the same two: experimentation, so as not to get "trapped in the known," and changing goals (Cortex). What does not change is the structure: the most demanding, newest work first, in a quiet window; maintenance and communication later; walks between blocks; a physical cutoff; an open afternoon. Section 12.5 reached the same conclusion about the ordering criterion. The step counts illustrate the difference between his practice and his prescription: Koe walks 15,000 to 20,000 steps, but in 2025 he calls 10,000 a marketing number and recommends 7,000 to 8,000 as a sweet spot for most people; Chapter 40 discusses walking in its own right.

One change is not settled. The word "sprint" in the December 2024 description of the first block sits on an unresolved tension about the scale of building. In February 2024 Koe had said that building "isn't a permanent thing": it "may be 6 weeks of working extra or working more on the weekends," something "you do maybe once or twice a year to reverse entropy of your business" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). A few weeks later, in April 2024, his routine gave building a permanent daily place: the first hour of every day "has to be building" (**Source:** `A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md`, 2024-04-02). The December 2024 version holds both at once: a daily first block, but devoted to a project worked "in kind of like a sprint," not a permanent part of his day. Koe does not explain the change. The two statements can be read as describing different scales (an intensive season of building against a daily block that keeps the building moving), but that reconciliation is this book's reading, not his. Section 5.5 sets out the February and April positions side by side.

The repeated disclaimer ("a guess," "supposed to change," "you have to discover it") is the methodological point of the whole series. Koe offers his routine as data from one experiment, not as a template. The section on lifestyle design gave the reason: copying a routine means copying a solution to someone else's set of problems.

#### Routines of guests

Koe's interviews include several guests describing their days, and they show the same pattern with variations. The ideas in this subsection belong to the guests; Koe's role is that of interviewer.

Justin Welsh, in a 2021 interview, says there are proponents of randomness and of structure, and he "lean[s] more towards the structural side." His days are "pretty similar every day": up at about six; an hour and fifteen minutes drinking coffee and talking with his wife on the patio ("my favorite time of the day"); at 7:15 a.m. Central Time, he posts on LinkedIn; then 45 minutes set aside for engagement, chatting and answering comments; then either a six-mile walk at Shelby Park or sixty minutes at the gym in East Nashville (on the elliptical, because of his back); then home to write for about an hour and a half ("I try and write an hour and a half every single day"); lunch; Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday afternoons reserved for advising clients (early-stage software companies); and Mondays and Fridays for working on business systems: "**eliminate, automate, delegate**" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20). Koe adopted the triad as "a big thing of mine," and Chapter 12 shows its descendant in his own "systemize, eliminate, delegate." The corpus also records that Welsh's slow morning coffee inspired Koe's own mornings at a time when he started each day forced and lost an hour on social media.

Dickie Bush, in 2023, says he prefers structure but that his business's growth and his move to Miami keep changing it. He wakes at five, foam-rolls, drinks water, takes supplements and immediately starts a ninety-minute work block, either creative work or immediately useful learning, avoiding consumption early. The night before, he writes down "what is the one thing I'm thinking about overnight that I can then do it first thing": he goes to bed with one idea, lets it marinate, and expresses it on waking, which "has always been... where I've had my most powerful idea breakthroughs." Then cardio (eight to ten miles of running a week, about three times, or yoga); his brain fires better after cardio, but he now does it after the block. He cooks and eats breakfast (new in the last six to eight months) and spends twenty minutes on his balcony in the sun. Tuesdays and Fridays are pure meeting days; Monday, Wednesday and Thursday mornings are kept as free as possible. He lifts at four in the afternoon, because he is stronger with meals in him (as a future father, he says, he would lift first thing in a home gym to check the box). He walks about 20,000 steps a day: anything that does not require the computer happens on a walk, including calls and almost all writing. He is an audio learner (two to three times speed, without a drop in retention); he used to see this as a deficiency, but now "I can learn and walk at the same time." He uses ChatGPT as a formatter: he writes in bullet points and asks it to turn them into paragraphs; "it'll just take those exact ideas... light formatting changes, it's saving me hours." He shuts everything off by eight and spends the last hour journaling or planning the next day, which he calls his biggest weakness; he plans to fill it with a hobby, learning to play house music, totally separate from work (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

Bush's practice of going to bed with one idea is the default mode network used deliberately at night: load the problem, release attention through sleep, collect the result in the morning. It is the same mechanism as Koe's midday walk, placed at a different point of the cycle, and it anticipates the evening "empty" of section 13.3.

John Hugh, co-founder of the creator platform Stan, answered Koe's 2025 question about where he falls on the spectrum between Darwin and hustle culture by describing his creative rhythm. Everyone has a different style, he says: morning or two in the morning, total quiet or not. In college he could not "rail an Adderall and write an entire essay the night before"; he would "write like two sentences every day on an essay for like three weeks." Translated to now: he starts the day without phone, Slack or email, and spends "the first two hours internalizing the idea that... I'm going to have **10 minutes of creative spark**, and that's going to be four weeks' worth of effort, but I just need to have the repetition of going about that every single day" (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md`, 2025-01-28). Hugh's account adds a temporal claim the others lack: the spark is brief and unpredictable, and the daily repetition exists to be present when it comes. It is the creator's paradox from the side of discipline: you cannot schedule the spark, but you can schedule the conditions for it.

|---|---|---|---|

#### Your first system, and the test of direct experience

The 2023 video that introduced the Ten Commandments ends with a practical instruction for turning them into a routine. Koe recommends writing everything in Notion, and starting now, because it takes time to learn its structure and you will refine things. Create a new page and write what you are going to do tomorrow in your four hours of work: "that is going to be your **first system**," that is, steps. The next day, come back and refine it: what didn't work, what do I have to do differently today? Do this for a week; note the common patterns; turn them into a weekly system; abide by it; and refine it with time (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued with numbered steps in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The method is minimal on purpose. It does not ask you to design an ideal routine; it asks you to write tomorrow, live it, and correct it. The system is not designed but extracted, from a week of observed patterns. This is the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6 in its smallest form, and it is the same structure as the week-long plan of section 13.4, which Koe introduced in the same week of January 2023.

The same video closes with a challenge that is also an argument about knowledge. Apply the information, don't just watch for learning or entertainment. Four-hour workdays are "more than possible; I've proven that, and there's many people that leverage the power of the internet to do that." But "you'll only be able to discover whether you can or not through **direct experience**; it doesn't matter what you think is possible." "If you think it's impossible, you don't have the awareness or experience that allows you to believe that it's possible and make it a reality for yourself." Write the ten commandments in Notion with your systems and start tinkering; gain clarity on the next day or week, and act (**Source:** same files).

"Direct experience" is a common phrase that Koe uses with a specific epistemic weight: it is the only valid test of whether a way of living works for you. The argument has a structure worth noticing. A belief that something is impossible is reinterpreted not as a conclusion but as a symptom, a lack of awareness or experience. This makes the belief impossible to defend in advance and possible to revise only by trying, which is exactly what the challenge asks. The risk of the move is that it can be used to dismiss any objection; its strength is that it shifts the question from opinion to experiment, consistent with everything this section has said about routines.

The corpus records how Koe's view of systems evolved after this. In 2023 the system was central: entropy explains why productivity systems exist, you build your first one in Notion, and refined systems become automatic and can be stacked. He also insisted, against a literal reading of James Clear, that "you don't have a system without a goal": goals create systems. By 2025 he adds that "all systems fall apart or they evolve" and that "systems don't last forever," like a Notion system you set up and forgot; even his own four-hour proposal, he says, is a system that breaks when it needs to; he tried the Pomodoro technique and other devices, but he now focuses on principles. The refinement does not contradict the first-system exercise. The first system is how you start; principles are what last; and the exercise was always meant to be revised, not kept. Section 13.4 makes the same point about the tools of review.

### 13.3 Fill, Empty, Use

#### Three activities to pursue goals at full force

Sections 13.1 and 13.2 described the day as an alternation: focused work, then rest that feeds the next session. Koe's most compact framework for that alternation is a three-part instruction he calls **Fill, Empty, Use** (in one transcript it appears garbled as "fill mtus"; the intended name is clear from other versions). Its earliest full statement is from March 2023, in a video on his deep work routine: "You need three activities to pursue your goals full force." One that **fills** your mind: "education, ideas and novel resources you can apply towards your goals; this will lead to intrinsic motivation." One that **empties** it: "you don't want to be trapped in a chaotic bubble of thoughts and useful ideas, that's exactly how you make zero progress; write things down." And one that **uses** it: "you need a vessel to focus your efforts with your ideas and the clarity to execute; build your future." The video focuses on the third, using the mind through deep work, and refers to an earlier video he calls "the three-step productivity framework" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The version in the summary of his book *The Art of Focus* (January 2024) adds the functions of each step. Filling the mind gives "**creative firepower**"; emptying it gives "space for **creative emergence**"; and then you focus that creativity into your work. He frames the three as things to incorporate into daily activities, building your own daily system with time and practice, and, unlike the 2023 video version discussed below, he does not assign them to times of day (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

Each step answers a specific failure, and the framework is clearest when read that way:

| Step | What it does | What it produces | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fill | Education, ideas, novel resources applied toward goals | Intrinsic motivation; creative firepower | Creating "on an empty tank": nothing to say, no motivation |
| Empty | Writing things down; reflection | Space for creative emergence; clarity | A "chaotic bubble of thoughts and useful ideas" that produces zero progress |
| Use | A vessel (a project, deep work) with the clarity to execute | Output; a built future | Ideas that never become anything |

The middle step is the least obvious and the most characteristic. Most productivity advice moves from input to output; Koe inserts a step between them whose function is subtraction. The reason is psychic entropy (Chapter 5): a mind full of unprocessed ideas is not rich but disordered, and disorder blocks execution as effectively as emptiness does. Writing externalizes the material, which both orders it and frees attention. The **vessel** is Koe's recurring term for the structure that receives effort (a project, a business); without it, as Chapter 9 argued, filled and emptied minds have nowhere to put their output.

#### The time-assigned version: the viral tweet

The framework began as a tweet that went viral, and the tweet assigned each step to a time of day. Koe quotes it in 2023: "**Fill your brain in the afternoons with books, learning and socialization; empty your brain before bed with journaling, planning and meditation; use your brain in the morning with creation, output and focus.**" Its success, he says, led him to create more content on lifestyle and productivity. In the same 2023 video he gives the most detailed breakdown of the three slots, each with three activities and a reason for each (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18):

| Slot | Activity | Its purpose, in Koe's words or close paraphrase |
|---|---|---|
| Afternoons: fill | Curiosity pursuit | Collect "kernels of truth" to integrate into your worldview |
| | Skill acquisition | Increase your value in a changing marketplace: "if you stop learning you die" |
| | Intellectual conversation | Avoid being trapped in a narrow perspective; test ideas with an open mind; avoid debate and argument. All three are "fuel for creative work" |
| Evenings: empty | Self-reflection | Notice mistakes, lessons and directions you would otherwise miss |
| | **Reality mapping** | Document what you learned that day and how it connects to other ideas, so you can create something of value |
| | Spiritual connection | Avoid being trapped in doing mode; embrace *being* to balance stress |
| Mornings: use | Focused work | Channel the discoveries and practice your craft |
| | Priority projects | Give direction; the only way to produce something you can pass to others and be paid for |
| | **Bursts of intensity** | Feel accomplishment first thing, so you can be at ease during rest knowing that what needed doing is done |

Several terms in the table are Koe's coinages. **Reality mapping** is his name for the evening practice of documenting and connecting the day's learning; it appears only in this passage. **Bursts of intensity** here means the morning's concentrated output, whose function is psychological as much as productive: it is what allows rest to be rest. That is the dead stop of section 13.1 seen from the other side. If the morning delivered, the afternoon can be free without guilt; if it did not, the afternoon is haunted by the unfinished.

The order of the cycle is significant. It runs afternoon, evening, morning, so that the day's intake is processed overnight and spent the next morning. The sequence is the Ogilvy pattern of section 13.1 built into a daily rhythm: fill the conscious mind, unhook it (evening reflection, then sleep), and collect the result in the morning's focused work. In December 2024 Koe quotes "this previous quote of mine" again and presents it as the starting point for a lifestyle that leads to more idea flow, which he calls "the most important thing in your life for being creative and therefore having the quality ideas to be more productive in a more efficient and leveraged way" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29). The sentence states the hierarchy of this whole chapter: idea flow comes first, and productivity is downstream of it.

#### The holistic daily routine: creativity, productivity and experimentation

In the March 2023 video, the tweet is presented as a **holistic daily routine**, and Koe attaches a principle to it: "You need dedicated time every single day for creativity, productivity and experimentation." You need the mixture: "it can't only be productivity without experimentation and being able to refine along the way, or you're just executing on tasks that have been laid in front of you" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The third term, experimentation, is the one that connects the framework to section 13.2. Productivity without experimentation is execution of someone else's plan, even if the someone else is your past self. The routine has to contain its own mechanism of revision, or it becomes the routine you did not choose and do not like, the danger Koe named in his conversation with Sahil Bloom.

#### Balance: anxiety and boredom

In August 2024 Koe gives the framework its mechanism, in the language of the skill–challenge balance of Chapter 10. The third step of that video is to install a creative daily routine with three habits: one to fill your mind, one to empty it, one to use it. "When you consume too much and create too little, you get anxious. When you create too much and consume too little, you get bored," because you have run out of fuel, you repeat old patterns, and you lose the challenge of the craft. He calls it "the fill empty use framework," a big-picture, sustainable routine that leaves room to experiment with the details: fill in the afternoon (books, podcasts, courses), empty in the evening (journaling, planning, meditation), use in the morning (writing, deep work, building), "or frontload all three first thing in the morning." The practical instruction: pull out paper, plan the three habits over a week, start, and if something doesn't flow, change it until it does (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

The diagnosis maps the two failure states onto the two imbalances. Excess input without output leaves the mind full of material it cannot discharge, and that pressure is felt as anxiety. Excess output without input exhausts the material, so the work repeats itself and loses its challenge, which is felt as boredom. In Chapter 10, anxiety and boredom were the two exits from the flow channel; here the same states are produced by the ratio of consumption to creation. The framework is a way of staying in the channel across days, not only within a task. The permission to "frontload all three" in the morning is new in this version: the time slots of the tweet were an example, not the principle.

#### Productivity and creativity: two sides of the same coin

The framework has a 2022 antecedent that shows where it came from. In a video on learning skills, Koe's second step is to schedule a **creativity block**. Everyone knows that scheduling time for productivity or deep work is important, he says, but people do not understand that productivity and creativity are "**two sides of the same coin**": you need creative things to implement through productivity. A creative trapped in "narrow focus hyper productivity" has to ask: are you hunting for ideas outside your productive work blocks, or are you "**creating on an empty tank**"? Do you value the spontaneous nature of creativity, do you have a balance, or are you "a slave to regimented work and productivity apps"? (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). The programmer's bug of section 13.1 comes from the same video, as the illustration of what a creativity block produces.

In June 2024 the same idea appears as a description of his mornings. The second point of that video is to balance productivity blocks and creativity blocks; he restates the fill, empty, use tweet and then describes how he personally oscillates "between two **macro states of consciousness** in the morning, which is productivity and creativity." He focuses for the first one to two hours; goes on a walk, takes a break, releases a bit and thinks about the intention for the next work block; goes into it; and repeats until the gym. The first block is very dedicated: no phone, straight to writing. On the walk he listens to things and collects ideas. Afterward come email and maintenance work, interacting with people in his business, then "full-blown" messaging during the day. The gym is a "hard cutoff and hard reset"; afterward he reads, does a bit of work if he wants, whatever he wants, because the two to four hours have handled everything. Throughout, he says, he is "releasing entropy" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`, 2024-06-02).

The June 2024 description shows the framework compressed into a single morning. Use (the writing block), fill (the walk with audio), and a light form of empty (thinking about the intention for the next block) alternate within hours instead of being spread across a day and a night. The phrase "hard reset" is used here for the gym as the transition from work to rest; the same words name, in section 13.4, a different practice (a thirty-minute reordering of one's life). The corpus records them as two senses of one name.

The earliest version of the morning sequence, from 2022, is what Koe called a "**three habit morning routine**": he walks outside in the sun, does his "creative habit" (tied to a free challenge he ran at the time, *Seven Days to Genius Ideas*, meant to help people become more creative), and then gets straight into focused work "because I have that inspiration, I'm remembering my why... I have purpose behind my action." In the afternoons he consumes information aligned with his curiosity and makes connections: "the passion is found at those intersections." He has a purpose, a big problem he is intentional about solving, which his curiosities and learning let him solve better: "I'm feeding off of the clarity that I have in order to gain more clarity for something that is bigger" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). The 2022 routine already contains the cycle (sun and walk, a creative habit, focused work, curious consumption in the afternoon), and it adds what the later versions take for granted: a purpose that makes the curiosity converge. Chapter 8 called this the purpose as a problem to solve; here it is the reason the afternoon's consumption is not random.

#### Keystone habits for learning: build, learn, walk

In March 2025 the framework reappears in a video about learning with AI, as three daily blocks. "The best way, in my opinion... is in three different focused blocks": thirty to ninety minutes of **building**, in which you build your project and search for information when you need it; thirty to sixty minutes of **learning**, in which you follow your study regimen and take notes, and review them by asking an AI to summarize them (he describes capturing notes in a chat with the AI's responses turned off, then asking it to summarize the chat); and thirty minutes of **walking**, listening to YouTube videos, audiobooks or lectures and jotting ideas on your phone. "Those are your **keystone habits**; that's when all of the learning happens." And then the condition that applies to every routine in this chapter: "Nobody is going to give you the time to learn and build what you want to learn and build; you have to take the time." Put them on your calendar, wake up an hour earlier or stay up an hour later, turn it into a ritual: "grab your coffee, pull up your outline, put on some focus music" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

The three blocks are Fill, Empty, Use rearranged around a project: building is use, learning with notes is fill and partial empty, and the walk is fill plus the default mode network. Chapter 14 develops the learning method these blocks serve (learning as you build). The sentence "nobody is going to give you the time" repeats, almost word for word, what Koe said about rest in 2024 ("if you want rest, you have to take it"). Both halves of the polarity have the same condition: they are taken, not given.

#### Read, write, build

In early 2024 Koe states a parallel triad that sorts the same three activities by the bottleneck they solve. A video on writing as a high-income skill opens with: "**If you don't have ideas, read. If you have ideas but can't articulate them, write. If you have ideas and the clarity to execute, build.** Expand, organize and focus your mind until your craziest ideas become reality" (**Source:** `This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md`, 2024-01-28). Two weeks later he offers a graphic as a phone wallpaper with the compressed form: "**read to expand your mind, write to organize your mind, build to focus your mind**" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11).

The two versions differ in what they emphasize. The wallpaper assigns a function to each activity (expand, organize, focus); the conditional version turns the triad into a diagnostic: find which bottleneck you are at, and do the activity that resolves it. The mapping to Fill, Empty, Use is direct (read is fill, write is empty, build is use), but the conditional form adds something the original did not have: an order of priority set by your current state rather than by the time of day.

In July 2024 Koe adds a claim about what the process feels like. In a step he calls expanding into a new level of mind, he repeats "read to expand your mind, write to organize your mind, build to focus your mind" and then says that feeling overwhelmed, or feeling that you are not learning, "is good": your mind is primed for pattern recognition. If you are in the unknown and the chaotic, but moving in the right direction, you will pick up the information you need to survive that path. Launch into the unknown; immerse yourself in the culture, environment and information related to what you are learning; condition your mind through repetition and exposure; slowly understand the lingo, the vocabulary and the skill set of those who succeeded. Let your mind expand through discomfort; "the worst thing you can do is quit when you're feeling **growing pains**." "You aren't seeing results because you aren't the person who would see results"; this process lets you become a new person "by letting your old version die," like leaving the old environment that fueled bad habits (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

This passage seems to contradict section 13.4, where overwhelm is a symptom of psychic entropy to be cleared by mental housekeeping. The corpus records the tension and its resolution by context. The overwhelm of progressive exposure to a new field, while practicing and building, is a sign of learning; the overwhelm of consuming without processing, or of launching too far beyond one's level, is disorder. Read, write, build contains its own answer: reading alone produces the bad kind; reading followed by writing and building turns it into the good kind. The sentence "you aren't seeing results because you aren't the person who would see results" connects the triad to identity change (Chapter 4): the activities are not only productive but transformative, because they install the identity that will produce the results.

#### The four modules of a creative life

The last revision of the framework, in December 2024, renames it, adds a fourth part and drops the fixed order. The question it answers comes from Chapter 12: "How do I make the most of that 1 hour of focused work? By designing a **creative lifestyle** and **titrating** the time spent on those activities up as you're able to transition to doing it full-time." "Four habits, think of these like modules in your day; you don't have to do them in order, you just have to do them": **clear**, **consume**, **create** and **connect**. "You clear your mind, you consume to learn something new, you create to piece that together, and you connect everything so that you notice those novel connections, which breed new ideas" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29). "Titrating" is Koe's term, taken from chemistry, for increasing the dose gradually; it is the same principle as "tapering up" from one hour in Chapter 12.

The first module, **clear**, is the old "empty," aligned with work. "My favorite way to do this personally is to align it with my work, so generating ideas for my work. You can do this by writing 5 to 10 ideas." If you don't know what to write, use it as journaling: reflect on your past or the past week, think of negative things, "experiences I don't want to experience again," and turn those into five to ten ideas; if you come up short, brainstorm on whatever comes to mind. "Think of it like a brain dump." In his planner the instruction reads "turn thoughts, reflection and experience into five to 10 ideas," and it is modular: sometimes he does it at night "so that you have something to wake up to and work on" (**Source:** same file). The move from "empty" to "clear" is instructive. Emptying the mind of negative experience is turned into production: the same reflection that would have been journaling becomes a list of ideas for work. This is Bush's practice of sleeping on one idea, generalized.

The second module, **consume**, is the old "fill," with a warning about how. "You can't create if you don't consume." But not "consuming endless content on social media like a mindless zombie": it means "learning, hunting for ideas for your work. **Like your ancestors did in the physical, you do in the mental and digital.**" Options: read a long book or a long article, scroll a curated social timeline for five minutes, study the course you bought and forgot, listen to a podcast or lecture. "Don't forget an absolutely crucial step: have somewhere to capture those ideas to the relevant project you're creating" (**Source:** same file). The ancestral image makes consumption an active, goal-directed search rather than passive reception, which is the distinction between cheap and meaningful dopamine in Chapter 11 and between scrolling and intentional learning in section 13.2. The capture rule is what turns consumption into input: an idea not attached to a project is lost.

The third module, **create**, comes with a metaphor that ranks the modules. "The core human desires are to create, expand and transcend; anything else is either a distraction or a lesser purpose that pulls you toward those desires." Creating serves "a crucial function of the mind that most people miss out on": "Everyone consumes information, they gain **mental fat**. Some people write down information or clear their mind, so they cut down mental weight. But few people synthesize that information into something that helps and impacts others. They have the strength, metaphorically and mentally, to save someone from a car crash. **Creating something is how you build mental muscle.**" Those who do not create miss out on "how they contribute to the world... a very crucial key to purpose and meaning." "You need a project to build. **A project is a structure for your mind to become a magnet for ideas**" (**Source:** same file).

The body metaphor ties the three modules together. Consumption without anything else accumulates fat: information that slows and overwhelms. Clearing reduces weight but builds nothing. Creation converts information into capacity, and capacity is measured by what it does for others (the strength to lift a car off someone). The metaphor is continuous with the gym language of this chapter (training to failure, mental metabolism, carb-loading the psyche). The project as "a magnet for ideas" restates the condition of section 13.1: the default mode network brings relevant ideas only to a mind that has a project for them to be relevant to.

The fourth module, **connect**, is new, and it is about what a project does to perception. "When you have a meaningful project to build, not one assigned to you by parents, teachers or society at large, you see life in a completely different way." Writing a book differs from writing a social post: "my mind expands to match the frame of that book. I notice more in my everyday experience, the information I consume and the conversations I have, because I have an **anchor for those ideas**." "My mind can zip through complex reasoning and make sense of ideas I couldn't before," articulating "novel lines of thought that AI can't, because I would have to train it on that same frame." "Most people don't optimize for pattern recognition, and by doing so they rarely become curious or obsessed; they haven't invested energy into a project that encourages their mind to take a new shape" (**Source:** same file).

Connect is less an activity than an effect: the expanded perception that a large, self-chosen project produces, in which everyday inputs start to fit somewhere. It is the goal as lens of Chapter 6 at the scale of a book. Koe's remark about AI is a claim about originality: an AI could produce lines of thought within a frame it was trained on, but the frame his mind has built through months of work on a project is his, and that is where its novel connections come from (Chapter 18 develops originality). The insistence that the project not be "assigned" repeats the polarity argument: only a chosen project expands the mind to its frame, because only a chosen project is something the mind keeps working on when unattended.

### 13.4 Maintaining Order: Hard Reset, Weekly Plan and Reflection

#### Entropy applies to the organization of your life

The routines of section 13.2 and the cycle of section 13.3 describe a life when it works. This last section is about what happens when it stops working, and why it inevitably does. Koe's answer is the law of Chapter 5. In a January 2023 video he again calls entropy the "supreme law of the universe," present in almost every situation, and the reason "you're in a state of chaos." "The organization of your life, and therefore your mind, tends towards disorder" if you do not perform what he calls **mental housekeeping**: writing down everything on your mind, restructuring and reorganizing it, "literally taking your mind, putting it on paper and ordering it in a way that makes sense," so that it gives you clarity to execute. Not doing this is dangerous, he says, because you can pick up bad habits along the way, and bad habits are hard to break (he defers that topic to another video or "a book on habit formation"). But eliminating them becomes much easier with clarity: "prioritizing clarity in your life" (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05; the same passage is reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The phrase "the organization of your life, and therefore your mind" carries an assumption worth making explicit: the structure of one's days and the structure of one's mind are the same structure seen from two sides. A cluttered schedule is a cluttered mind, and ordering one orders the other. Mental housekeeping is therefore not a productivity trick but an application of Chapter 5's thesis that clarity is order in consciousness. The link to habits completes section 13.2: bad habits are what accumulate in the gaps of a disordered life, and they are easier to replace once the disorder is visible on paper.

#### The invisible building

In November 2023 Koe gives the same idea its most memorable image. "When we don't have clarity on how to achieve a goal, our mind becomes disordered: we become overwhelmed, anxious and narrow-minded, because the challenge of the goal does not match the skill that we have. Our mind starts to decline into chaos. This is known as entropy." "Entropy is universal; it doesn't only apply to things like buildings that fall apart with time and less maintenance. **The structure of your mind or identity is an invisible building.**" The remedy has three parts: "You reverse entropy by making a goal conscious, creating a path to achieve it, and focusing your attention on priority actions that bring results as feedback." The priority actions "revolve around daily self-education and practice," so that you are exposed to information that creates a new identity, which lets you "perceive certain situations as advantageous towards your conscious goals" and "see problems in situations as opportunities." The lesson he draws is blunt: "you need a plan; there isn't any other way, because if you don't have one, society does, and they've been planning your life for decades." "Nobody wants to wake up 40 years later wondering where the time went" (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19).

The passage condenses most of the book into a maintenance problem. The invisible building is the identity of Part II; its decay is the entropy of Part III; the goal, path and priority actions are the direction of Part IV; the daily self-education and practice are the routines of this chapter; and the feedback is the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6. The diagnosis of overwhelm and anxiety as a mismatch between challenge and skill is Csikszentmihalyi's flow channel (Chapter 10). And the closing threat, that society has a plan if you don't, is the "assigned goals" thesis of Chapter 1. The image of the building explains why maintenance is never finished: buildings do not decay because they were badly built but because time passes. An identity that was ordered once will drift unless the order is renewed.

The corpus records a tension with this "you need a plan." In December 2023 and again in 2024 Koe says that "success is not planned, it is automatic," because a mind programmed with the right goals stores the information that leads to them. He does not reconcile the two formulations. They are compatible if read in sequence: the plan makes goals conscious and reprograms the identity; once the identity is programmed, success happens "automatically" in the sense that perception and action follow the goal without deliberate effort. The plan is the maintenance; the automaticity is what maintenance makes possible.

#### A project keeps you sane

In January 2024 Koe makes the remedy more concrete and more personal. Working on a personal project, he says, "is really the only thing that keeps me sane." When he gets overwhelmed or anxious, has negative emotions or thinks about meaningless things, it is usually due to "the concept of **psychic entropy**, or the mind tending toward disorder." He falls into a rut or a "season of overwhelm or anxiety" only because he lost focus, letting it split among other things. "In order to reverse entropy you need to order your mind; you need to focus on something that brings structure to your mind, and in most cases that is a project." And a project is not only computer work: your relationship, your life, your health, your business and its skill set can all be projects. At the time, his own were Cortex (his software), an education platform and community for creators, the promotion of his book, the planning of another book, and gaining a few pounds before cutting for summer (**Source:** `This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md`, 2024-01-28).

The passage reverses the usual relation between rest and order. The common assumption is that overwhelm calls for a break. Koe's diagnosis is that overwhelm, for him, is usually split focus, and the remedy is not less structure but more: a single project that gives attention somewhere to go. This is why the polarity argument of section 13.1 said that "work brings order to the mind," and why endless free time is miserable. A project is the structure that rest needs in order to be rest rather than drift. The inclusion of a body goal (gaining weight before a cut) in his list of projects shows how broadly he uses the word: anything with a goal, a path and feedback is a project.

Psychic entropy is Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's term; as Chapter 5 noted, Koe first attributed it to him and later used it as his own vocabulary, as in this passage, where no author is named.

#### Order is balanced with chaos

Maintenance is necessary because order does not last, and Koe insists that this is normal rather than a failure. In May 2024: "Order is balanced with chaos. Your mind isn't always going to be ordered, it's often going to be chaotic, and you need to be able to zoom out, create order, remind yourself of your goals and fill in the gaps that created the chaos in the first place." He names three constants: a constant reminder of your vision; constant self-education; and constant building toward your vision, so that you practice the skills to achieve it and identify new paths or gaps in your knowledge to fill. And then the diagnosis of self-discipline that justifies them: "**The reason people struggle with self-discipline is because they get distracted from what matters**: they forget who they want to become, they forget what they are capable of, they forget the impact they want to have" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12).

The redefinition of self-discipline as memory is the important move. A lapse in discipline is usually explained as weakness of will; Koe explains it as forgetting. If that is right, the remedy is not more willpower but better reminders: practices that bring the vision back into attention regularly. The three constants map onto the earlier sections. The reminder of the vision is the anti-vision and vision of Chapter 7, revisited; self-education is the "fill" of section 13.3; building is the "use." The phrase "zoom out" is the zoom of Chapter 6, here used as the first step of restoring order.

A year later he adds a line that connects order to novelty: "Your life loses the spark of novelty and pattern recognition when you have a vague or unconscious story you are living out. So **when you create the order your mind craves, chaos is much easier to keep at bay**" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08). The claim is counterintuitive: one might expect order to reduce novelty. Koe's view is that a clear story (a goal, a project, a game) is what makes things noticeable at all, because pattern recognition needs something to recognize patterns *for*. A vague story makes everything equally irrelevant, which is experienced as dullness, and dullness invites the chaos of cheap stimulation.

#### The hard reset: when to use it

The most fully specified maintenance practice in the corpus is what Koe calls a **hard reset**, "a process that I run through when my life gets a bit too messy." It first appears in January 2023 as a thirty-minute exercise, and it is reissued with small additions in 2024. He gives the conditions for using it precisely: when you start thinking back to when life was better; when things get bogged down and you don't feel like you're living the same life; when you're not making the progress you used to and you wonder what you're doing differently and why you feel this way; when "your routines start to fall apart and little tasks work their way in unconsciously," and you feel you have no time, or can't do the work you used to do well. The tools are minimal: paper or a notebook ("I recommend buying a journal," and always having one on hand), or his free printable planner (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The symptoms describe entropy at the scale of a life rather than a task. Nothing has gone catastrophically wrong; routines have simply eroded, and small tasks have crept in without being chosen. The nostalgic thought ("when life was better") is treated as a signal, not as an evaluation: it means the current structure has drifted from the one that worked. The name "hard reset" is also used in the corpus for the gym as the transition between work and rest (section 13.3); the two practices share a name but not a function.

#### Step one: put your mind on paper

The first step is observation. Pull out a piece of paper or a notebook and note two things: exactly what you are doing on a daily basis, "every single thing," as you go about the day (what you are doing, what you are thinking about, what you feel you have to do); and exactly how you feel during those times. Be very specific, because "you're putting your mind on paper, your mind is very complex, and **if you don't have accurate data on your paper, you're not going to be able to move on to the next step**" effectively (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The January 2024 version of the reset compresses this step into a ten-minute exercise. Use it, Koe says, when you constantly think back to a time when life was better, when routines fall apart, tasks fill your day and you feel nothing gets done. Write exactly what you do daily and how you feel in the morning, the afternoon and the night; take ten minutes; get specific. The purpose: "identifying **unconscious parasites**, time and energy suckers." He does all of this in his planner, which has daily, monthly and quarterly pages; the quarterly page is for mapping out your vision and "creat[ing] the game of your life" (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

The two versions differ in method. In 2023 you record the day as it happens, which produces real data but takes a day; in 2024 you reconstruct it in ten minutes, broken down by morning, afternoon and night, which is faster and organizes the data by energy level, the variable that Chapter 12 used to order tasks. The "unconscious parasites" are what the exercise is designed to expose: activities that consume time and energy without ever having been chosen, the "little tasks" that "work their way in unconsciously" from the list of symptoms. Recording how you *feel* is as important as recording what you *do*, because the parasites often reveal themselves as states (drained, scattered, anxious) before they are identified as activities.

#### Three reasons you aren't getting results

Before reorganizing, Koe offers a diagnosis of why results stop. There are three causes, he says, and the same three appear in all versions. First, feeling too pressed for time, which "is usually the cause of stress": you are in a "very **narrow state**" and feel that you have no time, "but in reality you're just sitting there thinking you don't have any time when you could actually be doing the thing that you're putting off." Second, not having the energy: living in that stressed state "takes a lot of mental energy," narrowing your focus on random thoughts or other things, precious mental energy that you were not structuring in a way conducive to results. Third, "maybe you've just stopped doing the things that get results." His business example: "hey, I'm not reaching out to get clients, why am I not getting clients? And it's like, you kind of are answering your own question there" (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21; restated in `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

The three causes form a sequence rather than a list. The feeling of having no time produces stress (narrow, unconscious focus in the terms of the Focus Matrix); stress consumes the mental energy that the work would have needed; and without energy, the actions that produced results quietly stop. The first cause is also a perception, not a fact: the time spent thinking that there is no time is time that could have been used. The client example is deliberately banal, because the third cause is usually invisible to the person inside it: they experience a lack of results without noticing that they stopped the activity that produced them. The log of step one exists to make that visible.

#### Step two: prioritize, remove, restructure

The second step is reorganization. Look over what you wrote and ask what you can change. Create your own structure: circle or write down the things that get you results, and **prioritize** them; **remove** the things that "snuck in there... and don't deserve to be there," which could be draining mental, monetary or even physical energy; and **restructure** specific tasks and obligations. The aim, in a phrase he repeats in every version: "**get the chaotic structure of your mind onto paper and reorganize it**" (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). In the 2024 version, the step draws on both the brain dump and the vision: look over your notes and your vision, prioritize what gets results, remove what "snuck their way into your day and don't deserve to be there," and restructure tasks and obligations "to free up more time" (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

The three verbs correspond to three operations on the log. Prioritizing selects (it is the priority ladder of Chapter 12 applied to the whole day). Removing subtracts the parasites. Restructuring moves what remains, because some obligations cannot be removed but can be placed where they do less damage, as Koe placed email at the end of his mornings. The addition of the vision in 2024 matters: without it, "what gets results" is measured against whatever goal was already operating, which might be the assigned one. With it, prioritization is measured against the goal you chose.

#### Step three: the week-long plan

The third step turns the reorganized structure into a plan. Take ten minutes and, on a new page, write every single thing you will do over the next week: your morning routine; the tasks you will complete during your focused work routine; other specific tasks and obligations, like meetings, and how they lay out in your day; what you will do in between; and your nightly routine. "**Just doing this alone reduces the friction that allows you to make better decisions**" (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05; reissued, together with the mental housekeeping passage, in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

Why should writing a plan reduce friction? The answer is in the default mode of a disordered mind. Each unplanned moment of the week requires a decision, and each decision, made in a narrow or tired state, tends toward the easiest option. A written plan converts those decisions into a single decision made in advance, when the mind is clear. The plan does not make the week rigid; it makes the default the chosen option rather than the available one.

Koe immediately warns that this is not a quick fix. Some people will write the plan and it will work well; others will have to experiment. While executing, "you're going to hit a problem, and then you're going to have to deal with that mentally, and you're going to have to go back to what you wrote down and change it for the next day" to something you think will work better: trial and error. Over the week, "you just get into this flow by solving the problems that come up along the way, and then you have a system that over the next month you can just do every single day," which becomes easier and eventually a habit. Preferably, he adds, the habit is doing the things that move you toward your purpose, your vision or "the highest version of yourself" (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The progression is the one section 13.2 described for routines: plan, problem, revision, system, habit. Its mention of "flow" is not loose. Solving the problems that arise in executing a plan is a challenge matched to one's skill, with clear goals and immediate feedback, which is the structure Chapter 10 identified as the condition of flow. Maintenance done this way is not a chore but a game.

#### The loop, and the lifestyle design process

The January 2024 version of the reset makes the loop explicit and calls it "one of the most important things I've ever done." You are in a chaotic spot because of entropy: routines and habits tend toward disorder unless you put energy into maintaining them. Write every single thing you will do next week (which reduces the friction of making better decisions): morning routine, focused work routine, other tasks and meetings, nightly routine. You will experiment; if your routine does not flow, there is a problem, and you go back and change what you wrote: "**this is why we write it down, so you can go back and change it.**" Repeat week after week until it gets better: that is how you refine a system, make it second nature and "become a new person." The loop: map your week, stick to the plan, identify problems, experiment with solutions, and repeat until you reach your ideal future. It gets more efficient with time; making this mental housekeeping regular is what makes new skills second nature. He did it himself, he says, the same day, in the weekly section of his planner (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

The book summary of the same month gives the whole procedure under the name of **lifestyle design**, with an extra audit step and an explicit goal (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18):

1. Pull out a notebook.
2. Write down exactly what you are doing, and why, at every minute of the day.
3. Write how you feel in the morning, the afternoon and the night.
4. Prioritize, remove and restructure your day so that you feel better and have more time; "this requires honesty with yourself."
5. Create a week-long system "**to reverse entropy in your daily routine**": plan your morning routine, focused work routine, other tasks and meetings, and nightly routine.
6. Over the next week, return to the notebook and iterate on what does not work, to ensure maximum mental output, creativity and life enjoyment.

The "why" in step 2 and the "honesty" in step 4 are the additions that matter. Recording *why* you do each thing exposes activities that have no reason, or whose reason is someone else's goal. Honesty is needed because the parasites are often things one enjoys or identifies with, and removing them meets the same resistance as any identity change.

The versions of the reset can be compared:

| | Hard reset (2023-01; reissued 2024-03) | Seven-day reset (2024-01) | Lifestyle design process (2024-01, book summary) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Life "a bit too messy"; routines fall apart; little tasks creep in | Same symptoms | Presented as the general method of lifestyle design |
| Observation | Log what you do and feel, as the day goes | Ten-minute reconstruction by morning, afternoon, night; "unconscious parasites" | Every minute: what and why; feelings by time of day |
| Diagnosis | Three causes: no time, no energy, stopped doing what works | Same three causes | — |
| Reorganization | Prioritize, remove, restructure | Same, informed by the vision | Same, "with honesty" |
| Plan | Ten-minute week-long plan | Week-long plan with an explicit loop | Week-long system "to reverse entropy" |
| Iteration | Not a quick fix: trial and error until system, then habit | Map, stick, identify, experiment, repeat | Iterate over the next week |

The procedure is stable; the 2024 versions add the vision as input, the explicit loop, and the "why."

#### Reset from scratch

In October 2024 Koe proposes a more radical variant. Tomorrow when you wake up, start completely new: everything you have done before does not matter; be intentional about doing different things from the second you wake up, because your habits, choices and decisions got you where you are. He clarifies that not everything you currently do is bad; this is a way to reset completely: "you need to **drop everything and see what sticks**... do nothing, and that alone will shine light on what your priorities are." Then fill your time with learning, building and experiencing as much as possible (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10).

The method inverts the hard reset. The hard reset starts from the current day and subtracts what does not belong; the reset from scratch starts from an empty day and lets what is essential return on its own. What comes back first, unbidden, is either a real priority or a strong habit, and the exercise makes it possible to tell them apart. It is the experiment of the dopamine detox (Chapter 11) applied to the whole routine: remove everything, and the things you miss reveal what matters.

#### Nightly reflection: closing the loops

The daily counterpart of the weekly plan is reflection at night, and in October 2025 Koe calls it "arguably the most important one that nobody does," the seventh and last step of his thirty-day dopamine detox protocol (Chapter 11 presents the protocol as a whole). Your brain constantly tries to process experiences; without dedicated time, "it hijacks your attention at the worst possible moments." "This is the **Zeigarnik effect**. Your brain holds on to incomplete tasks and unprocessed experiences, creating tension that drains your cognitive resources." Reflection does several things at once. It closes open loops. It is "an act of metacognition, or thinking about your thinking, the highest form of human intelligence." It builds a feedback loop: after thirty days you have "30 data points about what actually moves your life forward." "**It transforms mistakes into data, rather than regrets.**" And it reduces decision fatigue for tomorrow. How to do it: set a non-negotiable alarm for 9:30 p.m., or thirty minutes before bed; use the **3-2-1 framework**, "three wins, two lessons, one intention for the morning"; use a physical notebook, to avoid digital rabbit holes before bed; and start with two minutes if ten feel like too much (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26).

Reflection here is the evening "empty" of section 13.3, given a mechanism and a format. The mechanism is the open loop: unprocessed experience occupies attention, the "RAM" of Chapter 6, until it is closed, and writing closes it. The format is minimal: three wins (evidence of progress, which feeds motivation), two lessons (mistakes converted into data, which feeds correction), one intention (a goal for the morning, which gives the default mode network something to work on overnight, as in Bush's practice of sleeping on one idea). The thirty data points are the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6 accumulated: a month of reflections is a record of what actually moved life forward, against which the vision can be checked. The physical notebook is a small application of Chapter 11's environment design: the phone at night is the gateway to the cheap dopamine the protocol is meant to remove.

**Complementary context:** the Zeigarnik effect is named after the psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik, whose experiments in the 1920s suggested that interrupted tasks are remembered better than completed ones. Koe uses it in two opposite directions: elsewhere, to stop a work block before finishing so that the mind keeps working on it (Chapter 12); here, to explain why unprocessed experiences drain attention and must be closed at night. The research on the effect is mixed; Koe uses it as an explanatory frame.

#### The weekly review

At the scale of the week, Koe's practice is a review. In October 2024 he describes it: at the start of each week he does a **weekly review** to refine his system, asking four questions. What went well last week? What didn't go well? What am I grateful for? What are my focus projects? (A template was available in Cortex.) He gives three reasons. It is a weekly way to shine light on what to add to your vision or anti-vision. It clarifies and evolves the goals you pursue, so that "the journey can take shape with the shape of the destination." And it shows the minor changes that improve progress over time. "If you were to just focus on this weekly review the entire time and iterating on your vision, then you're going to make it pretty far" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13).

The four questions cover the past (what went well and badly), the emotional register (gratitude) and the future (focus projects). The reasons connect the review to Chapter 7: the anti-vision and vision are not written once but extended every week with what the week revealed. The phrase "the journey can take shape with the shape of the destination" restates Chapter 6's principle that the destination determines the journey, with a dynamic twist: the destination itself is refined weekly, and the journey follows.

In December 2024 Koe shows the tool he was using at the time, a weekly creative planner template in Cortex, in a walkthrough partly shown on screen (details beyond what he narrates cannot be reconstructed from the transcript). You duplicate the template into your workspace and duplicate it again each week. Each day is an "element" (a sub-note in the software). Inside each day are four parts: a **brain dump** ("clear your mind, turn thoughts, reflection and experience into five to 10 ideas," at any time of day, since it is modular); **learning**, where you link the sources you are consuming (his example is a book by David Deutsch he was reading, stored as a PDF in the software's library) or create documents such as "notes on creativity"; **projects**, what you are working on (his example: a newsletter on AI with its outline, and a book, newsletter and social posts for the week, also favorited for quick access); and **tasks**, the structure of priority tasks, walks and meals. Weeks are organized into monthly folders ("planning December 2024"). Ideas captured on the phone can be sent to a specific document (for example, the page for the second week of December), and for his book he saves ideas to each chapter every day as they come to him while consuming: "If you write newsletters or social posts and you're not capturing ideas to where you're going to write those things, you're missing out" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29).

The template is the four modules of section 13.3 given a container: the brain dump is "clear," learning is "consume," projects are "create," and capturing ideas into the documents where they will be used is the practical form of "connect." It is also, unavoidably, a demonstration of his product, which should be noted: the structure he recommends coincides with the features of the software he was selling.

In August 2026 his advice about tools reverses. The way to practice adaptability, he says, is "review, reflection, and prediction. You need to schedule this weekly. You need to review your week, see if you're on track, reflect on your progress, and predict where you're heading." But no fancy template: "just keep a piece of paper by your computer. I have this little tiny notebook where I just jot things down, because I understand that if I try to keep up with a note-taking system, I don't keep up with it. That's not how your mind works. **It kind of has to be messy.** You have to embrace just putting an idea down and letting it get lost. And if it's important, you will remember it." Apple Notes, a notebook, anything: "just get in the habit of writing things down" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

| | 2024 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Practice | Weekly review: went well, didn't, grateful, focus projects | Weekly review, reflection and prediction |
| Tool | Cortex template; weekly creative planner with daily elements, monthly folders, ideas captured to specific documents | A tiny notebook by the computer, Apple Notes, anything |
| View of structure | Capture everything to where it will be used | "It kind of has to be messy"; let ideas get lost; the important ones return |
| Reason given | Ideas not captured to projects are lost | "If I try to keep up with a note-taking system, I don't keep up with it. That's not how your mind works." |

The content of the review is stable; the tool and the philosophy of capture change. The corpus classifies this as a correction of personal practice, from a structured system tied to his product to minimum friction, and Koe's reason is a claim about how the mind works. There is also a third position, recorded elsewhere: in a 2026 creativity protocol he tells people not to write ideas down at all for its duration ("if it's important it will come back"), while admitting that this is "antithetical to everything that I preach" and limiting it to the protocol. The three positions can be read as a progression in where trust is placed: first in the system that stores ideas, then in a light habit of writing things down, and in the protocol, for a limited time, in memory itself. What survives every version is the review: the weekly act of looking back, checking the direction and correcting it. That is the same conclusion section 13.2 reached about systems. Principles last; tools are experiments.

#### Maintenance as the last practice of self-government

The four sections of this chapter form a single cycle, and the cycle closes the part of the book on self-government. Focused work narrows the mind and spends its energy (Chapter 12). Rest opens it, lets the default mode network connect what the work gathered, and recovers the energy, provided the rest is real and the work was worth recovering from (13.1). A routine holds the two in rhythm, as the rules of a game you chose, built from a few high-leverage habits and revised by experiment (13.2). Within the routine, the mind is filled, emptied and used, so that it neither overflows into anxiety nor runs dry into boredom (13.3). And because every order decays, the whole arrangement is maintained by regular acts of putting the mind on paper: a nightly reflection, a weekly review, a week-long plan, and, when life drifts too far, a hard reset (13.4).

The common assumption beneath all four is the one Chapter 5 established: disorder is the default, and order is something done, not something had. What this chapter adds is that the doing includes not doing. Rest, leisure and reflection are not breaks from the maintenance of order but part of it, because they are where the mind does the work of connecting, processing and closing that focused attention cannot do. Part VI turns from governing attention to using it: how the mind learns, and how it thinks.

### Exercises

1. **Audit your rest.** List everything you did in the last seven days that you would call "rest." Sort each item using Koe's double criterion from section 13.1: did attention leave work, and did it go somewhere that regenerated mental energy or fed the mind? Which items were "deep recovery" and which were "numbing"? Then apply his 2025 correction to yourself: is your problem too little rest, or work that gives you nothing to recover from? Defend your answer with specific evidence from the week.

2. **Reconstruct the argument, then test it.** In your own words, rebuild the chain that leads from "work contracts the mind, rest expands the mind" to "the most effective and result-generating work you do is when you don't work at all." Identify which links are borrowed findings, which are Koe's interpretations, and which are rhetorical. Then describe one kind of work for which the conclusion would clearly fail, and explain which link breaks.

3. **Compare two routines.** Choose two of the routines in section 13.2 (one of Koe's versions and one guest's, or two of Koe's versions). For each element that differs, state what problem the difference solves for that person at that stage. Then, following Dickie Bush's warning, describe what the routine of the person you are studying probably looked like at your own current stage, and what in it you could test next week.

4. **Design a replacement, not a removal.** Pick one habit you would like to end. Using the four domains (mind, body, business, connection) and the criteria "high leverage, high impact, highly enjoyable," design the habit that will occupy its slot. Explain why you expect the replacement to survive past the third week, and what you will treat as a "problem to solve" if it does not, in the sense of Koe's disliked gym exercise.

5. **Diagnose your ratio.** For one week, keep a rough record of how much time you spend consuming, clearing (writing, reflecting) and creating. Using the anxiety–boredom diagnosis of the Fill, Empty, Use framework, interpret what you find. Then decide which version of the framework fits your life better (the time-assigned tweet, all three in the morning, or the four modules in any order) and justify the choice by your circumstances, not by preference.

6. **Run a hard reset on paper.** Carry out steps one and two of the hard reset: log what you do and how you feel in the morning, afternoon and night, then circle what gets results and mark the "unconscious parasites." Which of Koe's three causes (no time, no energy, stopped doing what works) best explains your current situation? Is any item on your list something you identify with, so that removing it would feel like a small loss of self? What does that tell you about the "honesty" step?

7. **Question a claim.** Koe says that people who believe the four-hour workday is impossible "don't have the awareness or experience that allows you to believe that it's possible." Evaluate this argument. What does it get right about the relation between belief and experiment? In what way could the same form of argument be used to dismiss any objection, and how would you tell a legitimate objection from a lack of experience?

8. **Choose your review tool.** Koe moved from a structured weekly template to a "messy" notebook, and in one protocol to writing nothing down at all. Considering your own record with note-taking systems, decide which approach you will use for a weekly review of what went well, what didn't, what you are grateful for and your focus projects. Explain what would have to happen for you to conclude, after a month, that your choice was wrong.
