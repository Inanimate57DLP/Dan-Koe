## Chapter 7 — Anti-Vision and Vision

Part III ended with a conclusion that turns the whole system toward a decision. If attention is the only thing a person truly controls (Chapter 6), if the mind is a cybernetic machine that steers toward whatever target it is given, and if the goal works as a lens that decides what registers as relevant, then the most consequential choice in a life is the choice of target. Chapter 1 had already stated the uncomfortable corollary: you are always pursuing some goal, and if you do not choose one, one is assigned to you. Part IV is about making that choice deliberately. This chapter covers its first layer, the one Dan Koe places before goals, plans and projects: the **anti-vision** and the **vision**, the two poles of the frame from which every later decision is made.

The chapter moves in five steps. Section 7.1 starts where Koe starts: with a brutal look at the default trajectory and with the anti-vision, a written picture of the life you do not want, used as a source of energy and direction. Section 7.2 turns to the positive pole: intention as "what you are stretching towards", and the vision as a frame of reference, a lens and a filter. Section 7.3 gathers the many protocols Koe has published for writing both, from a thirty-minute notebook exercise in 2022 to a one-day protocol and an AI interview in 2025. Section 7.4 deals with a problem that the protocols cannot fully solve and that Koe has answered in contradictory ways: what to do when you do not know what you want, or cannot know it. Section 7.5 examines the size of the target: why Koe recommends "big irrational goals", how that recommendation coexists with the advice to aim "just above your level", and how it applies to money. The chapter prepares Chapter 8, which breaks the vision down into a hierarchy of goals, a plan and daily levers.

### 7.1 Brutal Awareness and the Anti-Vision

#### The default daily loop

Koe opens several of his most important pieces with the same list, almost word for word. "Wake up, hit snooze four times, stare at your phone, roll out of bed, make coffee, sit in traffic, eight hours of unfulfilling work, sit in traffic again, argue with your significant other, walk the pet, watch TV, pass out, repeat." In a 2023 video he follows it with a provocation: "This should scare the [hell] out of you, yet this is the state of 95 percent of the population," including many of the people who are trying to escape it (**Source:** `Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md`, 2023-04-16). The same list opens the first chapter of his book *The Art of Focus*, where it describes "the state of the modern world": humanity is conditioned to follow a default path set by society, and "the 99% of people that do what they're told, question nothing and pass down what they know lead to a population that can't think for themselves," producing "a dull, mechanical and mindless life" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The list is not decoration. It is the first concrete example of an anti-vision in the corpus, and Koe says so explicitly about his own life. From sixteen to twenty-two he did everything he could to avoid a nine-to-five job, obsessed with building businesses and learning skills: "that was my anti-vision, it was the bane of my existence to have a nine to five job." When he eventually did take a job, the list "became my **default state of living**; everything revolved around my work whether I wanted it to or not" (**Source:** `Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md`, 2023-04-16). The phrase "default state of living" names what the anti-vision is made of: not a dramatic catastrophe, but the ordinary loop that a life settles into when nothing else is chosen. It is the daily-life version of the default path described in Chapter 2.

The percentages deserve a note before going further. The loop belongs to "95 percent" of people in 2023 and to "99%" in the 2024 book summary, and elsewhere in the corpus the figure moves between 90, 95, 99 and "less than 1%" depending on what is being measured. None of these numbers is a statistic. They are rhetorical estimates drawn from the author's observation, and they do not reconcile with each other (the inconsistency is documented across the whole corpus). What they communicate is a judgment of scale: the loop is the norm, not the exception.

A 2024 video explains why the loop is so hard to escape even for people who try. People latch onto random ambitions for direction: some followers, a random online business, a girlfriend to escape loneliness. Building a business seems worthwhile "because at least it differs from the dead-end path" that "our botched culture" set for us. No wonder, Koe says, that most people fail to start, question everything and struggle, and end up accepting that they were "meant to be average": snooze four times, walk the dog, commute, pretend to enjoy coworkers and care about tasks, commute home, argue, television, pass out, repeat. The diagnosis is in one sentence: "you're trying to achieve Freedom with a mind that was conditioned to be a servant," like trying to fit a square block into a circular hole (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15). The argument links this chapter to the diagnosis of Part I. The problem is not the absence of ambition; it is that the ambition is pursued by an identity (Chapter 3) shaped by conditioning (Chapter 1) to follow instructions. A goal borrowed at random gives a direction but does not change the mind pursuing it, and the mind reverts to its default loop. This is why Koe's answer does not begin with a new goal. It begins with seeing the loop clearly.

#### Brutal awareness: the two things

The starting point Koe prescribes appears in its canonical form at the beginning of a December 2022 video that later became one of his best known: to get ahead of 99% of people in six to twelve months, first "become brutally aware of two things. The first is what you don't want. The second is where you will end up if you keep doing the same things in life" (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25; the passage is repeated in the 2024 compilation `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`). **Brutally aware** is Koe's term for a complete, unsoftened awareness of what you do not want and of where your present actions lead; it is the first step of the anti-vision.

The two objects of awareness are different. The first is static: an inventory of what you reject. The second is dynamic: a projection of your current trajectory. The second is what gives the first its force, because it converts a list of dislikes into a forecast. Koe's method for producing it is observational: look at "the masses" and see where "mindless action" leads. "It's not pretty at all." The observation must be deep. You have to sit with your thoughts, think them through, and refuse to close your mind and accept surface appearances as reality, because "closed-minded surface level living" is precisely what got you into your current situation. The cure for shallow thinking cannot itself be shallow (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). **Mindless action** here names action taken without deep thought, the kind that by default (by entropy, in the vocabulary of Chapter 5) produces the life you do not want.

Two qualifications accompany the prescription. First, Koe is explicit that he is not dismissing vision or "grand ideas for your future": "those things are good, they are not bad." The anti-vision is offered as the best way to create a vision, not as a substitute for it. Second, he asks the listener not to label what he says as good or bad but to "test it through your direct experience." The request is characteristic of the corpus: his frameworks are presented as hypotheses to be tested on one's own life, not as doctrine.

The 2022 video places the two acts of awareness at the head of a sequence: realize what you don't want (the anti-vision), create a vision and a strategy, "disappear" from what does not belong in your life, make a habit of the boring fundamentals and prioritize long-term mastery. Chapter 4 (section 4.6) treated this sequence as the earliest of Koe's reinvention protocols, the "origin story"; this chapter develops its first two elements.

In *The Art of Focus* the same prescription appears with an addition that matters for section 7.4: understanding what you don't want provides insight into what you do want, and the process is iterative. What you think you want can change in a year, a month or a week, "but you'll never realize that without making mistakes"; "mistakes are your compass toward a better life" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). And in an October 2024 video he places an emotional step before the cognitive one: "the first step out is to become disgusted with how far you've dug yourself in." Only then come the two acts of awareness (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10). The order matters. Awareness alone can remain intellectual; disgust is what turns it into motivation. The whole of this section is, in a sense, an explanation of why Koe thinks negative emotion is the right fuel.

#### Why start with what you don't want: the asymmetry of knowing

Why begin with the negative pole rather than the positive one? Koe's answer is epistemic, and it is the justification for the entire method: "it's easier to know what you don't want from experience and observation than it is to know what you want from imagination" (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25; repeated in the 2024 compilation). Knowing what you don't want draws on two concrete sources: what you have lived and what you have seen others live. Knowing what you want draws on imagination, thinking and "grand ideas for your future", which are by nature less tested. He is "very big on creating a vision," he says, but the best way to do it is by also creating an anti-vision. The vision is grounded in its negative counterpart.

An earlier version, from a 2022 video on reinvention, states the same idea as a heuristic taken from one of his tweets: "if you don't know what you want, understand what you don't want and work in the opposite direction." The rationale he offers is what he calls negativity bias: it is "extremely easy to become aware of the things you don't want in life because you don't know what you want until you've experienced it." You can therefore base your problem solving on what you have already experienced and know you don't want. He adds that this happens in "developmental stages": as you become more aware and realize "what it is not," you can start working toward "what it is" and stack small wins (**Source:** `Why You Can’t Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02). His example is small and physical: making eye contact with someone on the street, feeling something you did not like, and then going to learn about confidence. The negative experience defines the problem; the problem points to a skill.

**Complementary context:** "Negativity bias" is a term from psychology for the well-documented tendency of negative events, emotions and information to weigh more heavily in attention and memory than positive ones of equal size (reviewed, for example, by Baumeister and colleagues in "Bad Is Stronger Than Good", 2001, and by Rozin and Royzman in the same year). Koe uses the term without citing a source and stretches it slightly: the research concerns the greater salience of the negative, while his claim is that this salience makes the negative a more reliable guide to direction. That second step is his own inference.

A 2023 video gives the strongest version of the asymmetry, framed as a matter of emotional state. At a low point, Koe says, "it is so much easier to become directly conscious of that which you don't want in life, your anti-vision," than of a life you have never experienced ("this life of a billionaire"). You are experiencing everything about the low point directly, which gives you a direction (the opposite one) and a potent negative emotional state you can leverage toward goals set from the anti-vision. He presents this as an example of **transmutation**, channeling anger toward a goal (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11). A billionaire's life is pure imagination for most people; the low point is data.

Koe's account of his own identity follows the same two-sided observation. Describing the first guideline of his productivity system ("identity, or solving productive problems"), he says: "Ever since I can remember I've always had the goal of working 4 hours a day; a 9-5 job was the bane of my existence. I observed society and realized the life I didn't want. I observed the successful and realized the life I did want. Over time that perspective shaped my identity. I was attached to that desire of not working a job." And he adds a remark aimed at a common spiritual teaching: "Most people tell you desire is bad, but not if the outcome is positive" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). Two points follow. First, the anti-vision and the vision are both products of observation: one of the many, the other of the few who succeeded. Second, the attachment to a desire, which much of the contemplative tradition treats as the root of suffering, is here judged by its result. The criterion is pragmatic, and it coexists in the corpus with his praise of flow as a loss of self (Chapters 10 and 39). He does not reconcile the two.

The asymmetry also has a temporal form. A January 2023 video describes constant self-reflection on what you don't want as "a self-corrective process": you dig into past experiences "to get hints at your ideal future," it does not happen overnight, and the practice works "like your compass going into the future." That is how you gain clarity. "You can't just imagine what you want without knowing what you don't want" (**Source:** `How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md`, 2023-01-12). The compass metaphor recurs throughout this chapter, and in section 7.4 it becomes the **self-corrective compass**. Its logic is cybernetic in the sense of Chapter 6: you cannot steer toward a target you cannot see, but you can always detect that you are heading somewhere you don't want to go, and correct.

The clearest statement of that cybernetic logic comes in 2026. "If you consciously create your own frame by knowing exactly what you don't want out of life and comparing it to an evolving vision of what you do want out of life, you are only then able to sense when you are off track, notice the error, correct the direction you were heading, and learn as a byproduct." He repeats: "That is the key. That is literally success." The method is concrete: what do you hate, what experiences have you had; "create an image of a person in your head that you would despise being like, and now aim in the other direction. What does that person look like? What is their potential?" The positive image is imaginary, so you work toward it, "but you have to refine it like a sculpture over time by comparing it to what you don't want or where you are now," and slowly "you eventually become the vision that you had" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). The passage contains the whole architecture of the chapter in compressed form: two poles (what you don't want, an evolving vision), a comparison between them and the present, error detection, correction, and learning as a byproduct. The sculpture image recalls the marble and the sculptor of Chapter 4: the vision is not a blueprint executed once but a form refined by repeated comparison.

By 2024, however, Koe's tone about not knowing what you want had hardened. He says, "not to sound arrogant," that he never had a problem knowing what he wanted. When people say "I don't know what I want," he thinks they mean "I don't want to do the work it takes to get what I want." "It's not that you don't know what you want, it's that you know what you don't want already, meaning you know what you want and are hiding from what it takes to get away from it." He always knew, "because it's extremely simple to observe society and discern good from bad" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). The 2022 heuristic granted that someone might genuinely not know and offered the anti-vision as a way forward; the 2024 statement treats not knowing as evasion. In 2025 he will go further in the opposite direction and claim that nobody can ever know exactly what they want. Section 7.4 sets out the three positions side by side; for now it is enough to note that the anti-vision serves all three, because in each of them what you don't want remains the accessible starting point.

#### The anti-vision defined

Koe claims the term as his own. "One concept that I've coined that has helped many is the anti-vision. In brief it's how you realize what you don't want" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). Across the corpus, its definition accumulates several layers, and it is worth assembling them before turning to how it works.

The first layer is the content: "a negative thing, something that you despise, something that will get you angry" (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25). It is a picture of the future you despise, written down. The second is the function: "negative energy is very very potent; it can be used and transmuted to actually achieve what you want in life." The third is the test: when you read it, after creating it or while writing it, you "should feel uncomfortable, very uncomfortable with the possibility of where you could end up in life if you do not take control of your life." That discomfort is what you use "to laser in on one big goal." The fourth layer is a remark about the anti-vision's nature that is easy to miss: its content is "usually meaningless," because it "comes from mindless action, from not doing anything and letting entropy take hold," dragging your life into chaos. The vision, in contrast, "should be meaningful" (**Source:** the 2022 video and its 2024 compilation, `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`). The asymmetry is precise: the anti-vision does not have to be designed, because it is what happens if you do nothing; the vision has to be created, because it is what entropy will never produce on its own. This is the link with Chapter 5. The anti-vision is the entropic trajectory made visible.

A 2024 video adds the layer that ties the anti-vision to the model of the mind developed in Parts II and III. Since goals and problems compose your worldview, Koe argues, you should make them conscious, and "we start our story with an anti-vision": "the bane of your existence, the place that you don't want your life to end up; a **positive fear mechanism** to kick you in the right direction; the first polar end of the worldview you're creating." The worldview has two poles, anti-vision and vision, and inside them another layer: the goals and the problems you need to solve to reach them. Together they frame perception and let you notice information. "If vision and anti-vision are off, absent or assigned," then what you notice, which programs who you are and drives your actions, "isn't your own nor beneficial" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). This is the identity-as-filter chain of Chapter 3 (worldview, perspective, perception, choices) with its outermost layer specified. A "positive fear mechanism" is fear used deliberately to push you in the right direction; the phrase is a functional synonym of anti-vision, and it reappears in the "12 rules" video of the same year: "the bane of your existence, the life that you do not want to live, a positive fear mechanism that kicks you into action" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14).

The same 12-rules passage adds the last layer, the one that connects the anti-vision with problem solving and with business. You will not get rid of the things on your anti-vision in an instant; "you are meant to identify them as problems to be solved. This is what you do in business when you create a product": identify a problem, create a solution. "You can't change unless you are aware of a problem and you aren't going to change unless you are brutally aware of how that problem impacts your life." The anti-vision is therefore not only a source of emotion but an inventory of problems. Each item is a problem; each problem implies a goal; each goal implies the skills and solutions you will need. The analogy with product creation is not incidental. Chapter 28 will develop Koe's principle "solve your own problems and sell the solution," and the anti-vision is where those problems are first written down.

The layers can be summarized as follows.

| Layer | What the anti-vision is | Source period |
|---|---|---|
| Content | A written picture of the life and future you despise | 2022–2025 |
| Origin | The default result of mindless action and entropy; "usually meaningless" | 2022, 2024 compilation |
| Function | A source of potent negative energy to be transmuted into action | 2022–2025 |
| Test | It should make you feel very uncomfortable | 2022–2025 |
| Mechanism | A "positive fear mechanism"; the "bane of your existence" | 2024 |
| Structure | The first pole of the worldview; with the vision, it frames perception | 2024 |
| Use | An inventory of problems to be solved, as in product creation | 2024 |

One limit appears as early as 2023, in the same passage where Koe claims the term. The anti-vision does not mean you can get rid of what you don't want immediately: "you can't just quit your job and move out of your hometown if you've built an entire house of responsibilities around that," and there is more on the line than money. "Be smart about this, this is a long-term game" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). The **house of responsibilities** image explains why: a life is a structure in which jobs, places and relationships support each other, and removing one load-bearing wall without a plan can bring the rest down. The anti-vision supplies direction and energy, not permission for rash exits.

#### Transmuting negative energy

The central claim about the anti-vision is energetic: negative emotion, properly directed, is fuel. Koe illustrates it with a personal habit. Warming up on the treadmill at the gym, he thinks about what his life would be without the gym, where he could end up if he does not put everything into the workout, "and somehow I just get very very pissed and I can feel that energy" (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25; repeated in the 2024 compilation). The example is small, which is its point: the anti-vision is not reserved for life decisions; it can be summoned in a few seconds to charge an ordinary task.

From the example he draws a principle: "being pissed isn't bad. Negative emotions aren't bad. They exist, they are what they are, and they should be used, not suppressed, but used for good" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). Negative emotion is treated as a neutral resource whose value depends on where it is channeled. This connects with the "emotional alchemy" of Chapter 4 (section 4.2), where the formula was "emotional energy is the fuel and intentional effort is the vessel." The anti-vision is the device that produces the fuel on demand. (Elsewhere in the corpus the same author also recommends releasing negative emotions as obstructions so that something else can emerge; both versions reject suppression, but they disagree on whether the emotion should be used or let go. He does not reconcile them, and they may simply belong to different moments.)

In 2024 he states the orienting function in its simplest form. If you are unsure how to orient yourself positively, "deeply understand what you don't want in life — an anti-vision that brings clarity to your vision." If it is a job you despise, your aim is to create more enjoyable work and learn everything necessary to achieve that; "anything else is a distraction." If it is being overweight and unconfident, your aim is the opposite. "Get mad at yourself" (**Source:** `The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md`, 2024-10-06). In the same passage he offers two quotations that, in his words, "explain this quite well": "that which you don't hate, you tolerate," which he attributes to Malcolm X, and "no tree can grow to Heaven unless its roots reach down to Hell," which he attributes to Carl Jung. He does not elaborate. He uses them as support for confronting what you don't want as the source of direction.

**Complementary context:** The Jung line appears in *Aion* (1951), where Jung himself introduces it as a saying ("No tree, it is said, can grow to heaven unless its roots reach down to hell"); in Jung's work it concerns the integration of the shadow, the rejected parts of the psyche, rather than motivation. The Malcolm X attribution is widespread in quotation collections; Koe gives no source for it. Both quotations are used by Koe as rhetorical support, and the meaning he extracts (that hatred of the unacceptable is what prevents tolerating it, and that growth upward requires confronting the dark) is his reading, not an exegesis.

The most vivid image for the energy is the **slingshot**. In the October 2024 video, after the disgust and the two acts of awareness, the exercise is: sit with a notebook, get specific, write every single thing you don't want "and why"; you should feel uncomfortable. Then "use that potent negative energy to slingshot in the opposite direction," commit that it will never happen again, and laser in on one meaningful goal. "If your friends and family don't think you can achieve that goal, even better" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10). A slingshot works by pulling back: the further the band is drawn in the wrong direction, the more force is released in the right one. The image explains why Koe wants the anti-vision to be vivid and specific: a weak pull produces a weak launch.

A companion image is **gravity**, which adds a positive pole to the push. In a 2024 video on self-discipline, Koe says you have "only one option": "feel deep into your situation, realize where your life will end up if you keep going down this path, become absolutely fed up with the lack of progress you're making, use that negative energy to laser in on the meaningful goal that you've been putting off." That goal is "that nagging inner voice that is asking you to do more with your life." "Stop avoiding it. You have to add to the gravity of the life you want to achieve." The structure is a pair: here is where you don't want to be, here is where you want to be. "The more you realize where you don't want to be, the more powerful this becomes, and it starts to pull you towards it because the other one is pushing you away." And the condition of change is a balance of pains: "The pain of not achieving your goals must outweigh the pain of living a comfortable life. You must remove the distractions numbing you from the pain of becoming who you were meant to be" (**Source:** `It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12). The anti-vision pushes; the vision pulls; and the strength of the push increases the strength of the pull. "Gravity", in Koe's vocabulary, names the attraction of a goal, which grows with the energy and attention invested in it and with what you want to avoid. Distractions appear here not as temptations but as anesthetics: they numb the pain that would otherwise power the change.

In May 2025, the same mechanism is placed at the start of his "gamification" protocol, with an instruction that sounds paradoxical: "Step one: give yourself permission to allow your life to get worse." The explanation is that you feel lost, dull or lifeless because you lack a clear goal, but you don't know what you want; with a turbulent mind, a clear goal is the last thing you can think of, and you will make excuses about any candidate. "Goals don't exist in isolation. A strong purposeful goal is the result of the exact opposite: a negative outcome that you will fight tooth and nail to avoid. And once you've experienced that outcome firsthand you'll do anything to not relive it. You need a problem to solve. You need an enemy to attack." When you have something to avoid, your goal gains gravity (it pulls you) and clarity. The exercise is a single question: "if I keep doing the same things, where will my life end up?" Sit with it and let it consume your mind; then your mind becomes "hungry to learn, experiment and grow" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08). Koe calls this first step "collect vision." "Permission to allow your life to get worse" does not mean seeking misfortune; read in context, it means allowing yourself to see and feel how bad things could become instead of protecting yourself from the thought. The enemy is the anti-vision personified.

A last function of the anti-vision is perceptual. Having created a specific anti-vision, Koe says in 2023, "you've primed your mind for **pattern recognition** so that you can spot opportunities that will aid in you actualizing the vision for your future," or realize "this is not what I want to do." His example is mundane: "I'm not going to put this in my grocery basket because I'm only now aware of it," having realized that it damages the health you want in your vision (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). Pattern recognition, in Koe's use, is the mind's capacity to pick out of the environment what serves the goal or the anti-vision. This is the goal-as-lens principle of Chapter 6 applied to the negative pole: an anti-vision is also a lens, and it makes threats visible that would otherwise pass unnoticed.

#### Marinating in the pain

Koe's language for how to generate the energy has a culinary metaphor at its center. In a January 2023 video he says you have to "sit and understand how that problem or pain is spreading into the rest of your life"; you have to become conscious of it "in order to get absolutely fed up with where you are." Then that energy, that purpose, will fuel you in the morning and make you want to get out of bed. "You have to sit with it and marinate in it and stop ignoring the pain in your life" (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). **Marinate** here means to stay with a problem long enough to see how it extends through the rest of your life, until you are fed up; the energy that results becomes fuel.

The version preserved in the 2024 compilation adds an example. If you are overweight and it dampens your life (you can't walk downstairs comfortably, can't keep up with friends, can't do certain jobs that would make you more money), "then that is your purpose," your "North Star at the moment," and all actions align with it: the food you eat, whether you walk or sit on the couch (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The example shows the mechanism of marinating. A single problem (weight) is not motivating in isolation; it becomes motivating when you trace how it spreads: into mobility, friendship, income. The tracing is what turns a fact into an emergency. The **North Star** here is the purpose of the moment, not a lifelong vision; Koe uses the same term elsewhere for the distant vision, and the two uses should not be confused.

One terminological caution: in another passage of the same 2024 compilation, "marinating" has the opposite sense, a passive state of sitting in a problem without moving, which causes pain. The approving sense (sit with the pain in order to act) and the disapproving one (sit in the pain instead of acting) coexist in the corpus. The difference is the destination: marinating is useful when it ends in being fed up and acting, harmful when it becomes a place to stay.

In daily life, the raw material comes from observation. A 2024 instruction lists concrete settings: a walk, the grocery store, a school campus, work; there is "plenty of opportunity to observe what you don't want in life so that you can move in the opposite direction." Then sit with your thoughts, since it is easier to know what you don't want from direct experience than what you want from imagination (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10).

Another 2023 passage insists on specificity. Low points feel the same but are different situations, so you have to become aware of yours. A financial low point sounds like this: "I'm broke, I never want to be broke again in my life." From it you set a goal: get a better job, acquire the skills for a higher-paying job, or build a business and test it. It takes time; perhaps you go back to college. Throughout, you remain conscious, because you will hit new low points, and you register them as problems so they don't go "under the rug," correcting your actions over time. The aim is not to be fed into "the societal machine" via college, nor to build a business "for the sake of freedom" that becomes "a new nine to five." You can pivot along the way (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11). The passage adds two refinements to the anti-vision. It is not a single event but a recurring practice, since each stage has its own low point; and the opposing goal can itself produce a new version of the anti-vision (the business that becomes a job) if awareness lapses. The second refinement anticipates Chapter 30, where Koe describes client work as "the new 9-to-5."

The 2026 version of the instruction is the most direct, and it reads almost like stage directions. "The way you become obsessed is to look at your current actions and realize where your life will end up if you don't change. And for most people, that outcome is not pretty at all. Sit with that image. Actually sit with it. Pause this video, think, and let it fill you with this potent emotion and redirect that emotional energy toward a better direction." Such moments, he says, changed his life: "you just get filled with ... this divine energy ... you get choked up and you just want to attack whatever goal you have." "Only then can you actually align the goals you pursue with the aim of your future. Only then does a useful learning process start, the opposite of schooling where your learning is determined by the direction and goals society sets for you" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). The last sentence is the bridge to Chapter 14: learning becomes useful when it is directed by a self-chosen aim, and the anti-vision is what makes such an aim urgent.

The most elaborate version of the practice is a mental exercise Koe calls dancing between the worst case and the best case. It appears first in a 2023 video, where it follows the "Focus Formula" (the purpose-process-priority framework treated in Chapter 8, section 8.2; "process" became "path" only in later versions, as section 7.2 notes): internalize the image of your purpose as "your reality," sit with it, marinate in it, and "understand the good and bad of not achieving it." Ask what will happen if you don't achieve this purpose ("it's not pretty at all"), and follow the rabbit hole into the worst possible scenario of where your life could end up. Then the flip side: the best case if you actualize the purpose, and the next, and the next: where will you be in life? "Then dance between those two as you're going about life" (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). The version kept in the 2024 compilation adds a tool: when something pops up to distract you, "filter it through a **purpose filter**": is this important, and how will I act on this problem? "Typical distractions like social media, the news and other things, they don't [expletive] matter in your life," yet they matter to many people, who build identity around them, "because they do not have a purpose to bring their attention back to," no hierarchy of goals to structure attention (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The dance keeps both poles alive at once; the filter applies them to the stream of daily inputs. Without the poles, there is nothing to filter against, and distractions become the default organizer of attention, which was the thesis of Chapters 1 and 6.

#### Reject the average life and fabricate a rock bottom

In September 2025 Koe placed the anti-vision at the very top of his "12 rules," and justified the position. Rule one "absolutely has to come first," because everything else is downstream: "reject the average life. Reject being regular. Reject being boring." "Life is a series of decisions, and the single decision that determines all other decisions is to vehemently reject the trajectory you are set on at birth." "When you truly despise the outcome of being like everyone else, you begin to form an anti-vision." He adds two warnings. If the exercise did not work for you before, you may not have been in the life situation in which it would work. And it is not a thirty-minute exercise for "a quick dopamine hit"; "this is a lifelong thing" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The phrase **reject the average life** names the decision; the anti-vision is its written form. The claim that this decision "determines all other decisions" is the strongest formulation in the corpus of the idea that direction precedes method: without the rejection, every later choice is made inside the default trajectory. The remark about the "lifelong thing" revises his own earlier framing, in which a vision could be drafted in thirty minutes (section 7.3 traces this change).

Nine months earlier, in a video on making "the greatest comeback of your life," Koe had described a more abrupt use of the same rejection. The best periods of his life, he says, came after being fed up with his lack of progress; he would try anything, and what others call desperation he calls "a mind that's hungry to learn, grow and evolve." The goal is not to find something that immediately eases the pain, but to accept a radical shift in how you live: your actions compounded to where you are; keep doing them and you stay. "The only real change is behavior change." So say goodbye to what you hold tightly that you think "aren't that bad," since you clearly can't manage them. Become disgusted. "You must fabricate a mental Rock Bottom": write down everything you don't want, "the stupid mind, the pudgy body, the sluggish energy, the dead-end relationships," and reject them. Be extremely negative, because negative energy is more potent than positive, but channel it into forging a new identity. Not soft habit changes: "completely flip the switch overnight." And a criterion: if you gain absolute awareness of what you don't want, you will never do those things again; if you still want to, you lack absolute awareness of where they lead (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15).

A **mental rock bottom** is a low point manufactured on purpose by writing out everything you reject, so that the change can happen at once. Chapter 4 (section 4.2) traced how Koe's view of rock bottom moved from an external event (2022) to an internal state that can be provoked (2024–2025) to a precondition that is "hard to fake" (2026). The 2024 passage sits at the most voluntarist point of that arc. Its criterion of "absolute awareness" is a strong claim: it implies that relapse is always evidence of incomplete awareness. That is not something the author demonstrates; it is a definitional move that makes the claim hard to falsify. The 2026 refinement in Chapter 4 offers a more realistic account of why awareness so often fails to be "absolute": the identity doing the exercise has a survival interest in not seeing.

The overnight register also sits in open tension with a gradual one. In 2023 Koe compared change to sculpting marble with "small precise taps"; in 2024 he asks for "completely flip the switch overnight." Chapter 4 (section 4.3) documented this as one of the corpus's unresolved tensions. A plausible reconciliation, consistent with what the author says in different places but not stated by him, is that the decision and the shift of identity can be abrupt, while the consolidation of new habits is gradual.

A 2024 passage on money and purpose places the same sequence in a developmental frame. "You escape the survival stage by becoming deeply aware of the beliefs that are holding you back and becoming absolutely disgusted with them. You hit a metaphorical rock bottom in your mind and create this anti-vision for your future, so then you can orient yourself with a vision for the future and start to align your actions and problem solving with that." And he normalizes the modest content of this first stage: "Most of the time in this stage it's just making more money, and that's okay; you have to, to advance to the next levels of purpose" (**Source:** `You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md`, 2024-09-29). Here the anti-vision is the exit from the "survival" level of purpose, the first rung of the ladder that Chapter 9 develops (survival, status, creativity, contribution). The point matters for the reader who finds the anti-vision exercise too materialistic: Koe expects the first anti-vision to be about money, bodies and jobs, and sees no problem in that, because superficial problems are portals to deeper ones (Chapter 9).

A reading key belongs here. In the 2024 compilation, explaining what he means by "disappear," Koe says: "much of what I say is metaphorical and exaggerated to get the essence of the message across" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). He makes the same point later about the word "slave," which he says should not be taken literally. The remark applies to much of the language in this section: "fabricate a rock bottom," "get mad at yourself," "allow your life to get worse," "flip the switch overnight." These are intensifiers meant to communicate the force of a decision, not literal instructions to manufacture suffering. Reading them literally produces a caricature; ignoring their force misses the point. The author himself supplies the key.

#### The limits: both poles, and the energy to sustain the system

Koe's account of the anti-vision includes its own corrections. The 2025 version of rule one adds: the anti-vision creates "this potent negative source of energy that can lead you toward the positive." "You don't just think positively and hope that you move towards it. Sometimes that works, sometimes it doesn't. It's not a sustainable tactic. But you can round that out by having a negative side too." And then the limit: "But if you get absorbed in the negative, then obviously your life is going to end up negative. So you need both." The negative is the foundation, not the destination (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The emphasis shifts across the years: from 2022 to 2024 anger and disgust are presented mainly as fuel; in 2025 the stress falls on the balance of poles, and in the same year Koe warns not to try to sustain the "high" that comes from pursuing frightening goals (section 7.5). In his December 2025 protocol he even notes that the anti-vision questions may not fully apply if you are "just at the beginning of your own book" (section 7.3). The direction of the refinement is toward balance, and the reason he gives is simple: negativity alone produces a negative life.

A 2024 passage explains why the anti-vision needs a system and why that system consumes energy. It is a chain of five steps. (1) To "disappear" is to "break your addiction with feeling like [expletive]." (2) That feeling has many causes, but focusing on the mental ones rather than physical ones like nutrition, it comes from the mental energy "you are expending on people and things that don't give a [expletive] about you." (3) That freed energy should be redirected into your vision for the future. (4) You have created a system, the plan, that prevents entropy: "the thing that will take you from anti-vision to vision." (5) To maintain a system that prevents entropy you need energy, in this case mental energy, "to actualize or manifest that vision, if you want to call it that" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The argument joins this chapter to Chapter 5 and Chapter 11. The path from anti-vision to vision is a structure against entropy; structures against entropy cost energy; and the energy has to come from somewhere, which is why "disappearing" from what drains it (the subject of Chapter 11's protocols) is not a lifestyle preference but a requirement of the system. The hedge "if you want to call it that" around "manifest" is typical: Koe borrows the vocabulary of manifestation while describing a mechanism of attention and effort.

#### The origin story and a validated idea

The 2022 video that introduced the anti-vision ends with a summary that Koe presents as universal: realizing what you don't want, creating a strategy, launching yourself in the opposite direction and prioritizing long-term success in mastery "is the origin story; you will hear this in every origin story of a successful person. So flip the switch inside of you," he says, with the new year approaching, and "build a foundation that will carry you into the life you want" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21, from the 2022 video). Chapter 4 already noted that "every origin story" is a generalization from the stories Koe knows, not a survey. Its value is as a template that tells the listener which part of their own story they are in.

The fate of that video is itself a case the author uses later, in a different context. In 2026, explaining how to grow an audience, Koe recounts that he wrote a tweet "in 2019 or 2020, I think it's 2019" whose first line was "how to get ahead of 99% of people": "already a validated idea." In 2023 (the transcript's dating; the video itself is dated December 2022 in the corpus) he turned it into the YouTube video "How to get ahead of 99% of people in 6 to 12 months," which became his highest-performing video at 1.7 million views. Then Mark Manson, the author of *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F\*ck*, made a video with the same title but a different timeframe, which reached around 4.5 million views; then Alex Hormozi made one called "how to get ahead of 99% of people"; then Tom Bilyeu retitled one of his videos to include the phrase. "Four massive creators who just prove what I'm saying." They are not the same video, he notes: his covers "the process of creating an anti-vision and a vision," while Manson "has a completely different view" (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md`, 2026-05-14). The anecdote belongs to the chapter on growth (Chapter 26), where it illustrates validated ideas; it is recorded here because it shows that the anti-vision reached its largest audience through this framing, and because the same promise ("get ahead of 99%") recurs in the protocols of section 7.3. The view counts are the author's figures at the time of speaking.

#### The author's own anti-vision as engine

Koe uses his own life as the main evidence that the method works, and the stories are worth reading as a single case.

The simplest derivation appears in the 2024 "12 rules" video. "I know that I don't want a job I hate, work I hate, a body I hate, a partner I hate, a mind that hates me." From that alone, he says, it was easy to figure out what to do: become an entrepreneur no matter how many times he failed ("it took seven failures"); gain the power to get rid of work he didn't want to do "with writing and social media for leverage"; go to the gym and work on nutrition for his body; allow those three to open more opportunities in every domain; and then "let that path create a peaceful mind from the **progressive overload of responsibility**": "weights feel lighter as you get stronger." Everybody, he adds, knows that some form of this path is what they should do to avoid being stuck in a life they don't want (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). The structure is the method of this section in miniature: five negations, each converted into a direction. The phrase "progressive overload of responsibility" borrows the training principle of gradually increasing load (Chapter 15) and applies it to life: as you take on more responsibility gradually, the same burdens feel lighter. The claim that "everybody knows" the path is a rhetorical generalization; what the passage shows is how mechanically an anti-vision can generate a plan once it is specific.

The positive pole of the same life appears in a 2025 video. "As corny as this may sound," Koe always wanted to become "a force to be reckoned with," "an absolute unit of an individual," not just a muscular body but developed in every domain. "I wanted to become **multi-dimensionally jacked**. I wanted to max out all of my stats. I didn't want to be an NPC. I wanted to be a level 100 player. All areas of the map unlocked. Maxed out physicality, intellect, and professions. Bank overflowing with gold." Mind, body, spirit, relationships, money. The desire drove his path: as a teenager, obsessed with fitness; then absorbing as much knowledge as possible; then wanting freedom and failing at multiple business models until one worked; then spiritual and philosophical stints that gave him a deeper perspective on "superficial" things like fitness or money. That is why he wrote *Purpose and Profit*, "because most people think the two don't go together" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19). The video-game vocabulary (stats, NPC, level 100, map) anticipates the metaphor of life as a game in Chapter 10, and "multi-dimensionally jacked" is developed as holistic development in Chapter 40. Here it is the content of a vision: not one goal but a maximized character.

Koe explains why he keeps talking about vision with a statement about his own experience: "at every point in my life where I have made substantial progress in a short period of time while enjoying every second of it, I was hell-bent on actualizing a vision for the future that I held in my mind." People who have experienced this know its power; he writes about it to understand and dissect it, and to piece together a way for others to develop a vision (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). The claim is autobiographical, and he presents it as such: it is the observation that motivates the theory, not a proof of it.

Two childhood memories show the vision in its earliest form. At around twelve ("plus or minus"), Koe remembers sitting in the back seat of his father's old Chevy Blazer with a notebook, drawing to "tap into whatever creative ability I had at that time." Most drawings were humanoid figures: robots or muscular men, some with halos, some with horns. He reads this as evidence of an ability every child has, "to just create something that mimics life." As he grew up, his many notebooks gained a new purpose: "they were less about drawing imaginary figures and they became more about creating or visualizing imaginary futures" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). He uses the memory to introduce the practice of writing out goals and visions: visualizing a future is the same creative act as drawing a figure, applied to one's own life.

The second memory is of a place. As a child he visited his sister, who lived in Texas in a high-rise apartment on a big-city strip with shops downstairs. He was "just a kid sitting at home in my room playing video games," and he "would consistently imagine myself living in a location like that": being able to walk anywhere, having things close, a nice environment "with trees right outside of my window rather than a desert," since he lived in Arizona. At the time of the video he lives in Texas, on a street with many shopping malls; he walks to a coffee shop every morning, walks to the gym, barely has to drive and has great food around him. He calls it "mind-blowing to think about how that happened," expresses gratitude, and offers it as an example of a repeatedly imagined future that was later actualized (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). The story is an anecdote, and it should be read as one: it shows what the author believes about imagination, not that imagining a place causes you to live there. The causal claim he makes in the same video is more modest and is discussed in section 7.2: a future you have not first imagined is very hard to create.

Finally, in 2023, Koe describes his "ideal future" and says it is roughly what he already lives, "because that's what you do: you just live your ideal future and eventually the amount of time you get to spend on that ideal future becomes, like, fully actualized." Its components: four-hour workdays; writing every morning for about two hours; going to the gym; building side projects rather than businesses, among them the "Mastery facility" (a co-working space with a gym and other things), expanding Modern Mastery and building his software; doing fun things; and eliminating what he doesn't want to do. He adds that he is thinking ten to twenty years ahead (**Source:** `The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md`, 2023-03-12). The **ideal future**, in this usage, is not a distant destination but a lifestyle that one begins living partially now and expands until it fills the whole of one's time. Chapter 8 (section 8.5) develops this practice of bringing the ideal future into the present. Some of the projects listed did not materialize in the form described, and Koe's later move from a one-person business to a startup with a team (Chapter 37) shows that his own vision kept changing, which is the point of section 7.4.

### 7.2 Intention and Vision as a Frame of Reference

The anti-vision supplies a direction away from something. A direction away is enough to start moving, but it does not say where to go, and it cannot by itself decide between two paths that both lead away from the life you reject. That is the job of the vision. This section begins with the concept Koe places underneath it, **intention**, and then develops the vision in four aspects: why it is necessary, how it relates to imagination, how it forms (with the anti-vision) a frame of reference, and how it works day to day as a lens, a filter and an anchor. It ends with the caveats Koe attaches to all of this: clarity comes and goes, arrival does not feel like arrival, and visualization without action becomes a trap.

#### Intention: what are you stretching towards?

Koe defines intention through its Latin root, and he does so repeatedly from 2022 to 2025. In 2022: "that's almost quite literally what intention means from the Latin root: to stretch towards something": to pay attention to something in the future, to focus on it, and to allow your actions to follow what you are focused on and stretching toward (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). Being intentional is therefore defined as directing attention at a future target so that action follows. The definition sits exactly on the joint between Chapter 6 and this chapter: attention is the control, the future target is the vision, and action is what follows when the first is aimed at the second.

Later in 2022 he turns the definition into a question that serves as a tool. "The word intention, from the Latin word *intentio*, is stretching or purpose." So: "what are you stretching towards?" That is what you can bring your focus back to "in order to gain a better perspective and accurately perceive a situation." "This is how you live a life of purpose rather than a life of meaninglessness": intention, stretching toward something, "implies that there is a why behind your actions." His example is a bodybuilder striving toward a better physique (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16). The question has two uses. It is a reset (when attention scatters, ask what you are stretching toward) and a test of meaning (an action with nothing it stretches toward has no why).

In 2024 the etymology produces a new metaphor. "In Latin *intentio* means to strain, stretch or stretch out." To live an intentional life is to ask what you are stretching toward, what you are struggling for. Like a muscle that is stretched and strained to build endurance and strength, "living with intention is how you build muscle in the mind by simply engaging with life," which makes life more meaningful. The metaphor leads to a claim about learning: when you stretch toward a goal you choose, "your mind demands nutrition to fuel that growth; then and only then can books help you." "If you aren't doing anything unconventional with your life, reading won't help you much." At minimum, the intention to change your life must be there: "intention signals a mind that's ready to receive instead of react" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). The muscle image extends Koe's habit of describing the mind in the vocabulary of training (Chapter 15, "mental bodybuilding"), and the claim about books anticipates Chapter 16: reading is nutrition, and nutrition is useless without the training stimulus. The phrase "ready to receive instead of react" names the difference between a mind with intention, which takes in information as material for its aim, and a mind without one, which can only respond to whatever arrives.

A 2023 statement makes the link to information explicit. "When you have a goal that is top of mind you begin to perceive situations with intention." "Intention equals what you are stretching towards." Information becomes more relevant and applicable to your life, and (the transcript is slightly garbled at this point) it takes you out of "the mindless distraction and consumption cycle" (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). And a 2025 statement makes the link to neuroplasticity, in Koe's informal register: "Intention is what matters here. Intention is what are you stretching towards? That's the Latin origination of it. What goal are you achieving? Is that top of mind? When you do something intentionally, you're thinking about what you're going to do. Does it help me achieve my goal? No. Don't do it. Intention is you giving a direction for your mind to rewire itself toward" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). Across four years the definition remains stable; what changes is the metaphor attached to it (a target, a muscle, a rewiring direction).

**Complementary context:** The Latin noun *intentio* derives from the verb *intendere*, "to stretch out, to strain, to direct (one's attention or a bow) toward." Koe's etymology is therefore accurate in substance; in medieval philosophy *intentio* also came to mean the mind's directedness toward an object, a sense close to his own use. He treats the etymology as a starting point for his own definition, not as a historical argument.

The earliest passage on intention explains what writing goals down does. Self-improvement people, Koe notes in 2022, are "very big on writing out goals, writing out their vision for the future." What they are actually doing is creating clarity, so they are able to act "whether it be consciously, subconsciously or even sometimes unconsciously in alignment with that vision for their future." They are "**planting a seed** in their head that their actions can follow." The counterfactual is the important part: if you don't do this and aren't being intentional, you aren't stretching toward anything, so your actions have nothing to follow (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). The claim that actions follow a written vision "unconsciously" is presented as an explanation of a common practice, not as a demonstrated mechanism; its plausibility rests on the model of the goal as lens in Chapter 6 (a goal held in mind changes what is noticed, and what is noticed changes what is done).

#### Stacking whys and the intrinsic philosophy

If intention implies a why, the next step is to multiply the whys. In 2023, describing his daily routine, Koe says: "Every aspect of your routine should be intentional. Intention equals what you are stretching towards. The more reasons or whys you can stack behind your actions, the easier it becomes and the more beneficial it is." You discover these whys "by having a goal to apply your self-education to." His example: committing to a gym habit, then educating yourself about habit formation and training, gives you the reasoning behind the habit, "if and only if I am building in reality." He immediately qualifies his own example: his routine "may not be feasible for you"; it is "the result of 10 plus years of experimentation" to find what he deems enjoyable and conducive to his ideal future, and listeners should take bits and pieces as inspiration to test (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19). **Stack whys** is Koe's term for accumulating reasons behind an action until it becomes easy and self-sustaining. The mechanism has a condition: the whys come from self-education applied to something you are actually building. Reasons collected without practice remain abstract; reasons discovered while practicing attach to the practice.

In 2024 the same mechanism produces a larger structure. "You don't develop an **intrinsic philosophy** in one day; you develop it through conversations, reading, listening and filtering reality until you stack enough whys to hold you accountable. You will fail, and that is the only way to realize what you need to do next" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). The intrinsic philosophy is the personal set of reasons that holds a person accountable from the inside; it is built slowly, by filtering reality through one's aims, and it is corrected by failure. Chapter 4 (section 4.4) described "hunting and stacking whys" as part of reprogramming identity; Chapter 17 develops the idea of creating one's own philosophy. Here the point is narrower: the vision is not only an image but a growing set of reasons, and those reasons are what keep the image active when motivation fades.

Koe sometimes generates whys by interrogation, in a deliberately provocative register. In a 2023 video he asks: are you advancing humanity by handing out quadruple-pumped caramel macchiatos at Starbucks, or making humanity sick and overweight? Why haven't you begun a more purposeful career or business? Do you know why you're putting that food in your mouth, and do you understand how nutrients interact to create health? Why haven't you educated yourself on nutrition and training? "If you live in your body, it should be your full-time job to learn about it." Why are you going through the motions with a partner who was easy to get, and do you see forty more years with them? Why haven't you improved your social skills and personal development to attract a better partner? Many people, he says, become "incels" because they don't see the tiny choices that lead toward an ideal outcome and close their minds. "You only have one shot"; only you can stop yourself from a meaningless existence (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22). The questions are harsh, and some of their assumptions are contestable (the barista example implies a judgment about the worth of service work that the author does not defend). Their function in the argument is to apply the "why" test to the four domains where the default loop operates (work, food, body, partner) and to show that most daily choices in those domains have no why at all. That absence is what the anti-vision exposes and what the vision is meant to fill.

A gentler version, from 2022, closes a video with three questions meant for every domain at once, mental, financial, spiritual, relational and physical: "What is your vision for the future, how are you going to get there, and why are you acting in a specific way, in a conscious way, on a day-to-day basis?" (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16). The three questions correspond to the three layers this Part builds: vision (this chapter), path (Chapter 8) and the daily why (intention).

#### Why you need a vision

Koe's statements about the necessity of a vision are among the most categorical in the corpus. In a 2024 video on leverage, step one is to "choose a vision, not a skill": "If you don't have a vision you are lost; you can't create outcomes, so you are doomed to **mechanical living** and determined outcomes." And: "Every decision you make in any domain of your life must be filtered through your vision; that is how you bring meaning to your actions and minimize distractions" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). "Mechanical living" is life lived without a vision, in which outcomes are determined by forces you did not choose; it is the opposite of "creating outcomes." The phrase "a vision, not a skill" is a pointed reversal of the usual career advice, which begins with what you are good at. For Koe, the skill is chosen by the vision, not the other way around (a principle Chapter 20 develops in the definition of the generalist as someone who learns whatever their goal requires).

In 2026, in a video on strategic thinking, he builds the necessity of vision into an argument about decisions. The chain has three steps. First, without a strategy and strategic thinking, "every single decision you make in life becomes arbitrary. It doesn't matter. It is quite literally meaningless because it's not aimed in a specific direction." He admits: "I'm being hyperbolic, but that's for a reason." "If the outcome of your life is the accumulation of the decisions you make, then this is arguably one of the most important things you could ever do" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19). Second, strategy begins with vision. Principle one opens with a quotation from the X account "orange book": "No one ever suddenly gets lucky. If the stars suddenly align for you, you actually align them, consciously or not, by choosing where to live, who to learn from, who to marry, who to befriend, what lifestyle to maintain, what risk to take, and what vision to believe in." Koe continues in his own voice: "If you don't have a vision, you're lost. You do not have intention behind your actions because you don't know what your actions are leading to." And the image: "You're holding 10 darts in your hand, and those represent the 10 bold decisions that will determine the outcome of your life, but you don't even have a dartboard to aim at." Third, the definition: "A vision is the ultimate outcome that you're aiming for in life. It is not a vague wish. It must be as specific as it can possibly be. It must be compelling enough to motivate and guide every action, because until you're clear about what you want, you can't have a strategy at all. Once you are clear, you can reverse engineer every step and iterate as you go" (same source).

The quotation about luck belongs to Orange Book, a pseudonymous X account known for short stoic aphorisms; Koe takes it as the base of his principle and adds the darts image and the definition himself. The darts image is precise about what is at stake. The ten darts are not daily actions but bold decisions (where to live, whom to marry, which risk to take), few in number and largely irreversible. Without a target, they are thrown anyway, because not deciding is also a throw.

The 2026 definition explains what a specific vision makes possible. It lets you "evaluate every daily decision against it": scrolling for two hours "probably doesn't move you toward that, and without vision you wouldn't even register that as a problem." It lets you course correct, know which tactics apply to you, and avoid "the distraction rabbit hole on social media of people trying to tell you their business model is better." Every piece of content, every platform choice and every monetization decision "can be evaluated against the intent." His examples of sufficiently specific visions are revealing: "emotionally resilient or deeply self-aware and financially independent by 35," and "a 50,000 follower audience or newsletter in the personal development space that generates enough revenue to quit your job in 18 months" (same source). The second example shows how far "specific" goes in 2026: a number, a domain, a revenue function and a deadline. That is much closer to what earlier chapters would call a goal than to the "evolving image" Koe described in 2024. The tension between the specific vision of 2026 and the "vague idea of a good life" he accepted in 2025 is taken up in section 7.4. Koe's own reason for the 2026 emphasis is the turn toward strategy: without clarity, no strategy is possible.

The remark about not registering two hours of scrolling "as a problem" is the most important consequence. It applies the principle of Chapter 6 (section 6.6) that problems exist only relative to a goal: without a vision, the same two hours are neutral, and no amount of willpower can fight what does not appear as a problem.

A 2023 framework describes what happens to people who have no vision. Without one, Koe says, you find solace in three things. (1) **The societal machine**: the clear path of school, job and retiring happily is enticing, so most people relinquish their freedom to choose, their free agency and autonomy, and do what they are told. (2) **Momentary pleasures**: deep down we know we are not making the right choices; "our future self is looking down on us"; it makes us feel bad, but we don't know how to change, so we get trapped in a mental loop of not knowing what to do, and to numb that pain we drown it in momentary pleasures, bingeing Netflix or going out drinking, to escape confronting our problems. There is nothing wrong with these things in themselves, he adds, "but much can be wrong if you aren't conscious of your choices." (3) **Modern distractions**, which are not only digital: "distractions are not just digital; distractions are every single thing that does not align with your purpose or vision for the future." Without a vision you cannot filter distraction from what is worth focusing on. The catalyst of all three is you: your thoughts, beliefs and identity, forged over time, keep the mind narrow and focused on the known rather than the unknown (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22).

| Without a vision, you find solace in... | What it offers | Why it traps |
|---|---|---|
| The societal machine | A clear, pre-set path (school, job, retirement) | You give up the freedom to choose and do what you are told |
| Momentary pleasures | Relief from the pain of not knowing how to change | It numbs the pain that could drive change |
| Modern distractions | Something to attend to | Without a vision, nothing can be classed as a distraction |

The framework adds a precise definition of distraction: anything not aligned with your purpose or vision. That definition presupposes a vision, which is why a person without one cannot, strictly speaking, be distracted; they can only be occupied. Chapter 5 noted that Koe later narrowed the definition to whatever pulls you away from a work block, after which the same activity may be legitimate. The two definitions apply at different scales: the narrow one to the working day, the broad one to the direction of a life.

The 2024 version of the argument adds the voice of the artist and two borrowed concepts. Koe explains that he repeats "you need a vision" in every video because the idea needs reminding and cultivating; if it "just goes through your head and does not stick, maybe it's time to rethink that." "The world's greatest artists, founders and creatives are all visionaries; they have an evolving image of what they want the future to look like." "Your vision is your life's work; your vision is the massively transformative purpose that Steven Kotler illustrates as a step to achieving the impossible; your vision is your life's task, as Robert Greene would point to as a key to the good life." How do you find it? "You discover what you want by realizing what you don't want and revolting against it." And he adds a warning about its intermittence: "Your vision will come and go. You felt its power before. When your vision is clear, use that energy; when your vision is foggy, keep an open mind for the lessons that life is trying to teach you." "My vision isn't always clear... it's the cycles of life" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`, 2024-07-13).

**Complementary context:** The "massively transformative purpose" (usually "massive transformative purpose", MTP) is a concept that circulates in the work of Peter Diamandis and Steven Kotler (*Bold*, 2015) and in Salim Ismail's *Exponential Organizations* (2014); Kotler includes it as the first step of the goal-setting sequence in *The Art of Impossible* (2021), the book Koe appears to refer to with "achieving the impossible." Robert Greene's *Mastery* (2012) begins with discovering one's "Life's Task," an inner calling that the rest of the book treats as the precondition of mastery. Koe does not develop either concept; he equates both with his own "vision" and "life's work." The equation is an adaptation: Kotler's MTP is a large, outward-facing mission, Greene's Life's Task an inner calling discovered from early inclinations, and Koe's vision an evolving image of one's own future that begins, as this chapter shows, from what one rejects.

The 2024 passage contains three ideas worth separating. The first is that great creators are "visionaries" with an *evolving* image; the adjective prevents the vision from being read as a fixed plan. The second is the method of discovery, "revolting" against what you don't want, which restates section 7.1. The third is the cyclical nature of clarity, which anticipates the "hopeful delusion" discussed below and the life cycles of Chapter 39.

Two summary statements from 2024 frame the vision at the widest scale. The first closes the "12 rules" video: "Every successful interaction with reality starts and ends with a clear image of what they want, clarity on how to achieve it, and creative execution to acquire rare results" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). The three elements (image, clarity on how, creative execution) map onto this chapter, the next chapter and the rest of the book. The second appears in a video about writing, and it is the most philosophical. You need to persuade more than people: you need to persuade reality itself to "conspire in your favor." "Creating your ideal life is **persuading reality**: you can't force reality, you can't deceive reality, but you can persuade it," which he calls "a sign of truth that you can find through experience." You know inherently that you need to persuade people to get what you want; the only thing stopping you is beliefs programmed by people who have not done what you want to do, to whom you unconsciously give too much attention. "If this doesn't make sense yet, the rest of the video won't help" (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). The phrase is a metaphor, and an unusual one: it extends the vocabulary of persuasion (Chapter 32) to one's relationship with reality. Read in context, "persuading" reality seems to mean acting in ways that work with how reality responds (through repeated, adapted attempts) rather than forcing outcomes or pretending; but the author does not define it further, and it should be taken as an evocative image rather than a claim about how the world works.

A 2023 passage makes the escape route explicit in economic terms. "The second point is that you can bring a conscious vision into reality. The only way to escape is to build your own thing." But this is increasingly difficult "because most people are more than just wage slaves; they're slaves to many different things in their lives, whether it be addictions, their phone and other things like that." And the timescale is long: "it's taken you 20, 30 and even 40 years to dig yourself into where you are now; it's going to take some time to dig you back out" (**Source:** `How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md`, 2023-08-01). The passage links the vision to Chapter 2's critique of employment and to Chapter 28's argument for the one-person business. Its claim that building your own thing is "the only way to escape" is one of several formulations about entrepreneurship that the corpus states with different strengths (from "the only way" in 2023 to "I don't even recommend starting a business" in 2024, and back to the life's work that "will require you to start a business" later in 2024); Chapter 28 examines that variation. The final sentence counters the promises of fast change made in many of Koe's titles: the trajectory took decades to dig and will take time to reverse.

#### Imagination first, or experience first?

The most important change in Koe's account of the vision concerns its source. In June 2022, in the video that introduced the childhood notebooks, he states a principle drawn from what he calls "awareness begets awareness": "you cannot create a positive future reality for yourself if you have not first imagined it." Without ordered consciousness and clarity on the future you are trying to actualize, it will be very difficult for that future to be "manifested or created," or for your actions to lead there. "It has to be imagined." And the principle works in reverse: you can only create a negative reality for yourself "because you have already imagined and then experienced something." The point leads him into what he calls one of the biggest problems humans face, psychological time (Chapter 2) (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

From December 2022 onward, the emphasis is reversed. The video on getting ahead of 99% of people (section 7.1) makes experience the strong source and imagination the weak one; a 2024 video repeats that "it's easier to know what you don't want from experience than it is to know what you want from imagination" and makes the anti-vision step one; and in 2026 Koe says that "vision is very difficult to create just by thinking about it or imagining it" (section 7.3). The order becomes anti-vision first, vision second.

The two positions are less opposed than they look. The 2022 principle is about necessity: a positive future must be imagined to be created. The later principle is about method: imagination is hard to do well unaided, so it should be anchored in what experience has already shown you don't want. The vision remains an act of imagination in both; what changes is where imagination starts. Koe does not comment on the change himself; the reason he gives for the later position is the asymmetry of section 7.1.

#### The frame of reference

In *The Art of Focus* (2023, summarized by the author in 2024), the anti-vision and the vision appear as the first element of a framework the book calls **The Formula**. "To do this well you need: an anti-vision and vision to create the frame of your perspective; a purpose to provide a lens to perceive situations and identify problems; a path to bring clarity to your education, skill acquisition and building; and a priority to focus on every day and ground your attention." "All of these create a mind that is protected from distractions and expands with each level you reach" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

| Component of The Formula | Function | Developed in |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-vision and vision | Create the frame of your perspective | This chapter |
| Purpose | A lens to perceive situations and identify problems | Chapter 8 (8.6) |
| Path | Clarity on education, skill acquisition and building | Chapter 8 (8.2) |
| Priority | A daily focus that grounds attention | Chapters 8 and 12 |

The Formula is one of several repackagings of the same direction framework across the corpus: a "creation pyramid" in 2022, a purpose-process-priority "Focus Formula" in 2023, The Formula of the book in 2024, a "hunting skill set" later in 2024 and a "code" in 2025. The progression is refinement rather than replacement: "process" becomes "path," and the anti-vision is placed in front. The 2025 "code" is worth looking at, because it states what the frame of reference is.

In the "12 rules" video of September 2025, Koe describes how a commitment to excellence created a vision for him, and then defines distraction in terms of a frame. "If I trained my mind to stay within that frame, if I didn't get distracted — that's what distracted is. It's breaking outside of your frame of reference and not bringing yourself back in." The frame is defined as follows: "Your frame of reference, how you interpret and see the world and make decisions, is formed: one half is your vision, commitment to excellence, and the second half is your anti-vision, which is moving away from mediocrity." "That's the outer frame of the code that you live your life by." If you hold it, "your life will end up great no matter the steps you take," because the steps go from anti-vision "all over the place" toward the vision: "you're not going to hit an exact spot, but you're just moving in the right direction" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The **frame of reference** is the joint structure of the two poles: how you interpret the world and make decisions. Its two halves are named by attitude (excellence, mediocrity) rather than by content, which makes the frame portable across domains. And distraction is redefined in its terms, not as a particular activity but as a movement: leaving the frame and failing to return. This definition is compatible with both earlier ones (anything not aligned with the vision; whatever pulls you from a work block) and explains them: an activity is a distraction when it takes you outside the frame without bringing you back.

The claim that life "will end up great no matter the steps you take" should be read with its qualifier. It does not promise a destination; it promises a direction ("you're not going to hit an exact spot"). The image is again cybernetic: the steps wander, but the frame keeps correcting them.

#### The vision as lens and filter

The vision does not only define a destination; it changes perception now. Koe's 2023 definition: "Your vision is your frame. It is what you hold in the back of your mind to guide your daily actions. It is the lens from which you view your goals." And from the vision and the goals together, "those are the lens that help you view and perceive daily situations so that you can make better decisions." "It's not about having long-term or short-term focus, it's about having both. Zoom out." "Your life is a culmination of your choices, and if you want to make better ones you must expand your mind, consciousness and focus beyond your worries" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). The structure has nested lenses: the vision is the lens through which goals are seen; vision and goals together are the lens through which daily situations are seen. "Zoom out" refers to the zoom of attention of Chapter 6 (section 6.3): the vision is what you see when you zoom out, and it is what makes zooming back in useful.

The same year, in a video on focus, Koe gives the instruction that produces this lens. Step one is to zoom out and gain perspective. Think about your ideal future (have you ever? when was the last time?). Pull out a notebook and describe it in detail: "spend 30 minutes writing what you think you want" in mind, body, spirit and business, and how those will beneficially affect every area of your life. Then contrast that life with the one you have now. This is your vision, "a big umbrella" (the transcript reads "big medical"; "umbrella" is the most likely reading) that houses the rest of your goals and gives you a lens or frame through which to view daily situations and focus attention on the right things. Without a vision or goals top of mind, you cannot make better decisions, because you don't know which decision leads toward the goal (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11). The contrast with the present is the same operation as the anti-vision, run from the other side: the anti-vision compares the present with the worse future it is heading toward; this exercise compares the present with the better future it could be heading toward.

What does seeing through the lens look like? Koe's example, repeated in two videos, is ordinary. "When you view your daily situations from the lens of your future self or from the lens of the goal ... you are going to see life in a completely different light." Scrolling social media, a post you normally wouldn't stop on will stop your scroll and help you actualize the goal: "a fitness post is going to grab your attention more than some mindless cheap entertainment post." In conversations, "you're going to listen from the lens of your goal and be able to filter what they say" to spot what helps you move toward it (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The **lens of your future self** (or "lens of the goal") is this changed perception. The example matters because it shows the lens working on exactly the inputs that Chapter 1 described as programming the mind: the feed and the conversation. A vision does not remove those inputs; it re-sorts them.

The lens becomes a decision rule when it is used as a filter, and Koe is careful about how to use it. "Use the vision as filtration": say no to every opportunity that doesn't align. But be realistic: don't say no just because you can't see the connection or simply don't want to do it. "You're going to also say yes to everything that may lead to your vision," including things you don't want to do now but would want to do when you zoom out and adopt the perspective of your ideal self (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). **Filtration**, or the **purpose filter**, names this practice of passing opportunities, thoughts and distractions through the vision. The qualification protects against a misuse: a filter applied by present preference rather than by the vision would simply rationalize avoidance. The test is not "do I want to do this?" but "would my ideal self, zoomed out, want me to do this?"

A 2023 statement gives the filter its simplest form. "The first thing you do to start making better decisions is to bring your purpose to the top of your mind." This filters "signal importance from noise distraction": if it isn't conducive to achieving your purpose, it isn't important, "unless you've been convinced that it is by someone else" (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). The final clause is the bridge to Part I: much of what feels important was made to feel important by someone else (advertisers, institutions, the people around you), and the filter is how you notice it.

The filter can also be applied in writing, at the moment the anti-vision's energy is highest. In the 2024 exercise that begins with the slingshot (section 7.1), Koe continues: in the same notebook, right now, write exactly what you are going to do to achieve the meaningful goal; what skills you need to learn; why you want to achieve it in the first place; and your commitment to creating a better life. "Filter all thoughts, advice, opinions and emotions through your vision; channel the entirety of your life into it; start completely new." The last item: write all the potential distractions standing in the way of that goal (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10). The sequence is a small plan (action, skills, why, commitment, obstacles) written while the emotion is still active, so that the energy has somewhere to go.

The deepest reason for the vision, in Koe's earliest account, concerns how small wins accumulate. "One of the pillars" of his philosophy is creating a vision for your future. Just assuming or outlining what you think you want lets you connect what you learn while solving your problems; you become "directly conscious of more when you have a vision, goals, priorities to actually apply that to." When you discover problems by determining what you don't want and working in the opposite direction toward a vision, you need to know which priority task to do right now to solve the problem "for good in alignment with your vision." You could solve it in a completely different way, but if it is not aimed in the right direction, "you're not stacking these small wins up vertically, rather than to the side where it's just going to fall down again" (**Source:** `Why You Can’t Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02). The image is of a tower of blocks. Each solved problem is a block; without a vision, blocks are placed beside each other, each one a separate fix, and the structure collapses; with a vision, each block rests on the previous one and the structure grows. **Stacking small wins vertically** is Koe's term for this aligned accumulation, and it is the vision's practical payoff: it turns a series of solved problems into progress.

#### The vision as anchor and as a house

Two metaphors explain how the vision holds attention. The first comes from the 2024 compilation. Writing your goals down is important, Koe says, "and most people don't understand why because they've never done it." It is "like an anchor." He refers to a video in which he argued that you should learn and build in unison ("you need something to build so that you have something to apply your learnings to"), after which "your awareness starts to register more opportunities for what you're building." The reason he gives is the core of Chapter 3: "humans, compared to animals that survive on a physical level, we survive on a conceptual level." We try to survive beliefs and things we identify with, so "we would try to survive a project or... our vision for the future." Because it is top of mind, we notice things we can apply to the vision, clarify it further and keep striving (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). This is a deliberate use of the **law of conceptual survival** (Chapter 3, section 3.4). The mechanism that normally defends the old identity, and that Chapter 4 showed sabotaging the anti-vision exercise, can be recruited: once the vision is written and identified with, the mind defends it as it defends any concept of self, and that defense shows up as heightened attention to whatever serves it.

The second metaphor is from 2025, where the vision is the first of three steps (vision, clarity, identity; Chapter 4, section 4.6). "We need an **attention anchor**" that pulls your attention away from doubt and insecurity: "you need a sturdy mental house that you built, not someone else," a house you can decorate and live in when outside conditions get harsh. It cannot be any old goal, or a New Year's resolution set because everyone else is setting one, or a goal born of a tiny bit of motivation: "it must have gravity, it must be meaningful," something you want to dedicate your life to (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). The **sturdy mental house** adds two features to the anchor: ownership (you built it, so it is not an assigned goal) and shelter (it is where attention can retreat when circumstances are bad). The requirement of "gravity" connects with the push-and-pull of section 7.1: a vision without gravity cannot compete with doubt.

#### Holding the frame every day

A vision that is written once and forgotten does not function as a frame. Koe's instructions on holding it are concrete. In 2024: "Every day zoom out and remind yourself of what you don't want." You don't need to focus on what you want, "because that will make itself apparent through your choices." Hold that frame at the top of your mind; don't let distractions penetrate it. Whenever a choice comes up, zoom out and align: "will this benefit the future I'm trying to create?" Then be decisive. "Allow failure into your life so you can correct your behavior the next time around." "Awareness is a cure," he adds, but without a vision you may accept a quest that society assigns, which almost always leaves you without control over the level of your goals and problems (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The instruction is notable for what it says about the positive pole: in this version, the daily practice centers on the anti-vision, and the vision is expected to emerge from choices. It sits in tension with the statements, in the same year, that without a vision "you are lost" (section 7.4 returns to this). The "quest society assigns" is the video-game vocabulary of Chapter 10 applied to the assigned goals of Chapter 1.

In 2026, in the video on strategic thinking, Koe gives a five-part execution sequence that begins with holding the vision and ends with a goal hierarchy. (1) "Practice holding your vision in the back of your mind. Write it down somewhere and look at it every morning. It must become a part of who you are through repeat exposure." (2) "Take note of what you learn from studying your opponents or yourself. Meditate on it, contemplate it, let it marinate in your mind so that it can start to make connections." (3) "Identify the primary resource that you can leverage": for young people, time and freedom; for some, capital; for others, an internet audience; for more, "a weird set of interests and knowledge"; for many, AI. "AI gives high agency people the ability to do so much more, and it gives low agency people a reason to play victim and complain." The trap for high-agency people is unlimited options: "you can do anything with AI, but that doesn't mean you're going to get anywhere," which is why concentration of force matters more than ever. (4) "Choose your **concentration of force play**. Where is the single highest leverage point? The one that would unlock progress in more than one domain, the thing that makes everything else easier." (5) Then a "soft plan, but... crystal clear": reverse engineer the vision into a hierarchy (goals, 90-day objectives, weekly priorities, daily habits), because daily actions often won't visibly contribute to the vision, so "you have to break it down and see the entire system." "Every step should trace back to the intent or vision. If it doesn't, set it aside" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

| Step | Instruction | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hold the vision; read it every morning | Identity through repeated exposure |
| 2 | Note and contemplate what you learn about opponents and yourself | Connections ("marinate") |
| 3 | Identify your primary leverageable resource | Realism about starting conditions |
| 4 | Choose the concentration-of-force play | One point that unlocks several domains |
| 5 | Reverse-engineer a hierarchy: goals, 90-day objectives, weekly priorities, daily habits | Traceability from each step to the vision |

The sequence connects this chapter to the next three. The hierarchy in step 5 is the subject of Chapter 8; the leverageable resources and the concentration of force reappear in Chapters 25 and 34; the claim about AI and agency is developed in Chapters 35 and 36. Its central rule ("every step should trace back to the intent or vision") is the filter of this section made into a planning constraint. The phrase "soft plan, but crystal clear" names a balance Koe returns to often: the plan must be clear enough to act on and soft enough to change.

The two instructions, "look at it every morning" (2026) and elsewhere "set a 10-year goal ... then forget about them all" (2024), seem to conflict. Across the corpus, statements urging the reader to hold goals loosely and statements urging daily reminders coexist without a stated resolution. The most consistent reading, compatible with both, is that the vision (the frame) is held daily while specific goals are held loosely and revised; Chapter 8 discusses the goals side of this.

#### Clarity oscillates; arrival is quiet; visualization is not action

Koe attaches three caveats to the vision, and they keep the concept honest.

The first concerns clarity. In 2024 he writes that you don't need to know what your life's work is right now, and that realistically you don't maintain 100% clarity on it. At times it is crystal clear, "but this is often **hopeful delusion**, which isn't bad but necessary for intense bursts of progress"; at other times you have no idea what you are doing but still move toward a positive aim. Your life's work is not "a static sentence that you can articulate to others ... that's impossible"; it is "in the unknown," and by the nature of the unknown, it is unknown: "if it were known, you fell off your path to find security in someone else's." You create it through experimentation and evolution; your brand, content and products will change as you discover new goals, "but you won't discover those goals unless you start and pursue your current ones" (**Source:** `The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md`, 2024-10-06). "Hopeful delusion" is temporary, partly illusory clarity, and Koe values it for what it does (it powers bursts of progress) while admitting what it is. The argument that a fully known life's work would mean you had taken someone else's path is the most philosophical justification in the corpus for not knowing: certainty is borrowed, uncertainty is a sign that the path is your own. Section 7.4 builds on it.

The second caveat concerns arrival. "You're not going to know what you're doing for the majority of the time; I still don't know what I'm doing," Koe says in 2024. He is in a much better place financially, mentally and physically, and a vision is important, but "it's not like you have a vision for your future and then you get there one day and you're like, I achieved it all." You feel the same: same body, senses and perceptions, just in a better place, much calmer and more stable, "not acting out of survival as much." He attributes much of that to writing and creating value for himself and others; it has been a personal growth journey (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). The remark corrects a possible misreading of everything above. The vision is not a promise of transformation into a different person or of a felt moment of completion. What changes, in his experience, is the baseline: less survival mode. That fits the claim in Chapter 39 that happiness is not a destination, and the claim in Chapter 4 that the self evolves rather than arrives.

The third caveat concerns visualization. In 2025 Koe warns: "Dopamine spikes more when you're in pursuit of a goal rather than when you achieve it. But that's exactly where it gets dangerous, because you can visualize your goals all day," which "definitely helps with learning," "but you have to match that visualization with a level of challenge in reality to act and solidify that knowledge." "In other words, it's **being in the game**. You have to be in the game if you want your visualization or your imagination to take any kind of form in reality." Otherwise, "that's where **mental masturbation** comes into play, which is the act of telling everyone about your goals but never actually doing anything about it, because you already got your dopamine hit from telling people about it" (**Source:** `What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md`, 2025-08-03). The warning turns the mechanism of the vision against itself: the same anticipatory reward that makes a vision energizing can be consumed without acting, by imagining or by announcing.

**Complementary context:** Research on dopamine, notably Wolfram Schultz's work on reward prediction, found that dopamine neurons respond strongly to cues that predict reward and less to the reward once it is expected, which is the basis of popular statements that dopamine is about "wanting" and anticipation more than "having" (a framing Koe also takes from Lieberman's *The Molecule of More*, Chapter 11). Koe applies this to goal pursuit without citing a study. The further claim, that announcing a goal can substitute for pursuing it, resembles findings reported by Peter Gollwitzer and colleagues (2009) that publicly stating identity-related intentions can reduce follow-through; Koe does not cite this work, and his account is offered as observation.

The three caveats complete the definition. The vision is a frame and a lens that is held daily; its clarity oscillates; reaching it feels like a calmer version of the same life; and it works only when it is matched by challenge in reality. The next section turns to how Koe has asked people to write it.

### 7.3 Writing the Anti-Vision and the Vision

Koe has published more protocols for writing the anti-vision and the vision than for almost any other practice in the corpus. They differ in length, in the questions they ask and in the tools they use (a notebook, a running note, a template in his own software, an AI interview), but they share a structure: write the negative pole first, feel it, derive the positive pole from it, compress both into something you can carry, and connect them to goals and a plan. This section reconstructs the main versions roughly in the order they appeared and then compares them. The reason to look at all of them, rather than choosing one, is that their differences record what Koe learned about why the exercise fails.

#### Why write it at all

The earliest justification for writing is a metaphor from 2022. The best way to create a system, Koe says, is to contemplate and visualize a goal you want to achieve, to open your mind to having a vision and to gain excitement for the future, rather than being closed-minded about one little problem. "Sit down for 30 minutes, today, tomorrow, as soon as you possibly can, right now, and just start drawing, start writing": "what do I want for the future?" If these thoughts stay in your head, "you're not going to figure it out in your head; you just frankly aren't." The image is a water filter or spigot: "before clear water comes out you have to push out all the dirty water." Writing opens your mind and "takes those open loops out of your head," so you stop thinking about them compulsively over and over (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16).

The spigot explains why the first pages of the exercise are usually disappointing and why that does not matter: the first things that come out are the stale, borrowed, anxious thoughts that have been circulating for a long time. Only after they are out does something clearer appear. The reference to **open loops** connects the practice with Chapter 6 (section 6.1): unresolved thoughts occupy the limited "RAM" of conscious attention, and writing them down frees it. Writing is therefore not only a record of the vision but a way of producing it.

#### The thirty-minute notebook: becoming a mad scientist

The first full protocol appears in the December 2022 video and is repeated in the 2024 compilation, with the compilation version adding a final step about planning the week (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). Its steps are:

1. Sit down with a notebook for thirty minutes.
2. "Become a mad scientist" and "draw out everything, your entire future."
3. Start with the anti-vision: "write down every single thing that you do not want in this life." The guiding questions are what you do not want to look like, what you do not want your future to be, and what you do not want your day to look like on an everyday basis.
4. From that, the vision becomes much clearer, "because it's just the opposite of those things."
5. Check the emotional reaction: reading or writing the anti-vision should make you feel very uncomfortable. That is the test of success.
6. With that discomfort, "laser in on one big goal," your vision, which should be meaningful.
7. In the same notebook, create a plan and strategy for exactly what you will do day to day and week by week ("every single thing you're going to do for the next week"), getting "extremely specific" in order to "create the order so you do not be a victim of entropy."

The **mad scientist** is Koe's name for the attitude the exercise requires: experimental, uninhibited, drawing out possibilities as if the future were a laboratory. (He uses the same term elsewhere for treating life as an experiment of trial and error.) Three features of this first version persist in all later ones. The negative pole comes first. The emotional test (discomfort) is the criterion of a good anti-vision. And the vision is not left as an image but is immediately narrowed to one goal and a weekly plan, because, in the vocabulary of Chapter 5, order has to be created against entropy. The phrase "it's just the opposite of those things" is the simplest statement of how the vision is derived: by inversion. Later versions complicate this, because the opposite of what you don't want is not always what you want (section 7.4).

#### The question pairs

In *The Art of Focus*, the open instruction ("write everything you don't want") becomes a list of paired questions, one negative and one positive for each domain (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). The pairs are reproduced in the table.

| Domain | Anti-vision question | Vision question |
|---|---|---|
| The day | What does an unfulfilling day look like to you? | What does your ideal day look like? |
| Partner | What kind of person would you avoid as a significant other? | What is your ideal significant other? |
| Self | How do you not want to look, feel and act? | How do you want to look, feel and act? |
| Money | How much money is too little to sustain your lifestyle? Why is it too little? What won't you be able to do with that money? | (implied by the negative: the amount that would sustain the lifestyle you want) |
| Environment | What environment would you hate living in? Why? | What is your ideal environment? |

The instruction that accompanies the list is to get specific, write the answers in a journal or notebook and add to them as inspiration strikes. Two details of the list are worth noting. The money question is asked in negative form ("how much is too little?") and followed by two "why" questions, which forces the answer to be grounded in what the money is for. And the pairing itself makes visible what the 2022 protocol left implicit: each negative answer suggests a positive one, but the positive one still has to be written.

A 2024 version of the negative list, presented as "step one to becoming valuable," adds social and systemic questions. "Step one is to unveil problems with an anti-vision. It's easier to know what you don't want from experience than it is to know what you want from imagination, so create an anti-vision for your future; keep a running note that you will add to as you observe life." The questions: What do you not want to look like? How do you not want to feel? What does your worst day look like? What qualities do you not want in your friends, customers, partner and co-workers? How much money isn't enough to survive? And the last: how does everything above spread into each area of your life and prevent you from doing what you want? "Hit on the things that matter: health, wealth, relationships and happiness" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). The final question imports the "marinate" practice of section 7.1 into the questionnaire: each item is followed into the rest of life. The four domains named at the end are the **Eternal markets** of Chapter 19 (health, wealth, relationships and happiness), and the title of the section ("becoming valuable") shows why: for Koe, the anti-vision is also the first step of finding problems worth solving for others.

The positive questionnaire has its own versions. In 2023 Koe asks: If you had all the money in the world, what would your average day look like? What environment do you want to live in, and in which specific location? Do you want to travel? Do you want a family, and what do you want that life to look like (visualize an average day of family time)? How long a workday do you want? If you could do anything, what would you do for work? How do you want to look and feel: describe your body, your energy levels and how you present yourself to the world. What does your ideal day look like? Map out every hour. And list anything else that comes to mind about a specific future you want to build (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The "all the money in the world" condition removes the most common excuse for not imagining (lack of means) and isolates preference; mapping the ideal day hour by hour forces the vision down to a level of detail at which it can be compared with today.

In the same video he strengthens the vision with its opposite and with images. To make the vision more potent, turn it into an "antivision," completing the frame of your ideal self, so that you have "two things to bounce between": "multiple anchor points to observe situations from." The steps: (1) what is the bane of your existence? (2) write the opposite of every vision question. For "even more firepower," create a **vision board**, images collected in scrapbook software or on a wall, to make the future more tangible (**Source:** same video). Notice the order in this 2023 version: vision first, anti-vision second, derived by negating the vision questions. It is the reverse of the order in his other protocols, which shows that the order was not fixed even after December 2022. What remains constant is the pairing: two poles serve as **anchor points**, reference positions from which situations can be judged.

The 2025 positive questionnaire adds a "why" to every answer. Starting with his "simple life reset planner" (or a notes app, or paper): How do you want to look, speak and be perceived? Why? What environment do you want to live in? Why? "If money is a reflection of the impact you have," how much money do you want to make? What can you see yourself getting really good at and being known for? Why? What do you want your average day to look like, down to the detail? Why? It is a starting point, he says: "add, subtract, act and refine" as you go (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). Two changes are visible. The "why" after each question applies the "stack whys" principle of section 7.2 to the vision itself: a wish without reasons has no gravity. And money is reframed as "a reflection of the impact you have," which turns a number into a question about value created (Chapter 31). The phrase "add, subtract, act and refine" summarizes the iterative stance of section 7.4.

#### From one sitting to a running note

A July 2023 video replaces the single sitting with a five-step process that runs for years (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). It is Koe's most systematic account of how an anti-vision is built.

1. **Observe society as a habit.** Pay close attention to the people around you. Why do they do what they do? What is it leading toward? Are they someone you want to be? It can be as simple as observing the groceries in people's baskets or their bodies and realizing you don't want to end up "slow, lifeless, groggy all the time."
2. **Reflect on your past.** What experiences do you never want again? What were the lowest lows, what caused them, and have you done anything to prevent them? Make it a conscious practice.
3. **Make a list of things you don't want.** Pull out a notebook and get specific; "this list should make you feel uncomfortable." Keep it somewhere safe and add to it, because not every idea comes in ten or twenty minutes; they come over "years and years" of experience.
4. **Realize where you will be if you keep doing the same thing.** Look at your habits: where will your life end up with the same lifestyle in ten years? Do you want that?
5. **Transmute that energy into a passionate vision.** What will you have to learn, build and execute daily to avoid your anti-vision?

The process separates the sources of the anti-vision (others, the past, the present trajectory) and gives each its own step. Step one is the habit of observation already described in section 7.1; step two turns memory into data; step four is the projection that the 2022 protocol called the second act of brutal awareness. Step five defines the vision functionally, as what you will have to learn, build and execute to avoid the anti-vision, rather than as an image. And step three changes the timescale: the list is never finished.

The **running note** is the format that follows from this: a note that grows over time with experiences you don't want to repeat. In 2024 Koe specifies its contents twice. In the "12 rules" video: experiences you don't want to repeat, material you don't care to learn, work you don't care to complete, arguments you don't wish to have; section 7.1 took up the other half of that instruction, identifying these things "as problems to be solved." And in the video on life as a game: experiences you don't want to relive or have, things you don't care to learn, places you don't care to live, with the addition that "you must experience life to figure these out — you have to fail more, get uncomfortable more," and then work in the opposite direction. They won't vanish instantly, but writing them down and holding awareness of them lets your actions self-correct (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The note is not only a record of past pain but a reason to seek new experiences, since failures and discomfort are how new entries are found.

The 2025 "12 rules" video compresses the practice into four habits. "Every time you experience something you dislike, write it down. Go on a walk and think about where your life is heading if you keep doing the same things. Reflect on your past and note what you never want to experience again. Gather all of the data points you skipped over while you were under the spell of someone else's structure." Once the anti-vision is "at least somewhat clear" and potent ("you feel it"), you can stop and consider each decision, "because now you have something to work away from." Simply making decisions that move away from it moves you in a better direction; a vision makes the direction more accurate (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The phrase "data points you skipped over while you were under the spell of someone else's structure" describes the anti-vision as a recovery operation: the evidence of what you don't want was always there, but a borrowed structure (school, job, family expectations) taught you not to register it. The remark about accuracy states the division of labor between the poles precisely: the anti-vision alone gives a direction ("away"); the vision narrows it.

The longest version of the contemplative timescale comes in August 2025. "Step one is to write out everything you hate about your current life. And I don't want to hear about positive thinking right now, because you're wrong." Include "exactly what kind of life you will have if you don't change those things": the job you hate, things from your childhood you don't want to experience again, anything that comes to mind. Doing it at once takes fifteen to thirty minutes, which is "not enough time to really dig deep and excavate the painful experiences." So keep the paper accessible, on the desk or the phone, "then you take a week and you just contemplate it": on walks, even during conversations. Notice the things in your life you don't want; dig deep and research them further: "Where is this leading my life to? What are the negative aspects of these things?" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). The verb "excavate" explains why time matters: the most important items on an anti-vision are often painful memories that the mind does not offer up in a single sitting. The week of contemplation is a deliberate extension of the marinating practice. The dismissal of "positive thinking" should be read alongside the September 2025 statement in section 7.1 that "you need both": the point is not that positive thinking is wrong in general, but that it is the wrong tool for this step.

#### Tossing an anchor into the future, and the five things on paper

A November 2023 video gives the bridge from anti-vision to vision a name. "The first step to changing your life is to become brutally aware of the problems that make you want to change your life." "The big problem is a lack of awareness of your problems." So "sit and become negative for a while; let your mind run wild to create an anti-vision for your future: what is the worst case scenario if you continue with the same mental, physical, financial and relational actions you are taking?" "Then use that as a place to **toss an anchor into the future**: create a big vision-generating goal that encapsulates each domain of life." The next step is to bridge the gap with self-education and skill acquisition (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19). The anchor image is worth reading closely. An anchor thrown forward is something you can pull yourself toward. The vision is thrown from the anti-vision ("use that as a place"), lands in the future, and the rest of the process is pulling yourself toward it. A "vision-generating goal" is a single goal large enough to imply a vision in every domain. Section 7.5 develops the related image of tossing an anchor "into the unknown."

In January 2025 the protocol becomes a one-page structure of five items. Write on paper: (1) the anti-vision, your picture of the life you despise; (2) the vision, your picture of your ideal future; (3) **constraints**, what you aren't willing to sacrifice to reach your vision; (4) a ten-year goal; (5) a one-year goal. (He offers a worksheet through his newsletter.) It is not typical goal-setting, he says, because goals are aims that evolve. "You are creating an outline for the story that you plan to write": if you already have the ideas, start writing; most people don't have ideas top of mind and need the outline or structure to navigate creatively (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12).

The five items add two things to earlier versions. The first is the constraint: the vision is bounded by what you refuse to sacrifice for it. The concept has several names in the corpus, which refer to the same content: "anti-goals" (2024), "constraints" (2025-01), "rules of the game" (2025-05) and "anti-standards" (2025-09), the last renamed, in Koe's words, "for the sake of cohesiveness" with his rule about standards. Chapter 8 (section 8.4) develops it. The second is the metaphor of the outline. The written vision is to a life what an outline is to a piece of writing (Chapter 24): not the finished work, but a structure that makes creative navigation possible for someone who cannot hold the whole in their head. The metaphor anticipates the treatment of life as a book in Chapter 19 ("book to brand").

An October 2024 video places the writing inside a larger sequence, "think bigger and act smaller." Having thrown yourself into the unknown, you are in chaos and can't make sense of everything; it is up to you to create order. New ideas around you are building blocks for your vision, laid day after day. "You need a vision; without one, by definition, you are lost." Take a piece of paper: write everything you hate and don't want (the anti-vision) and everything you want: the body, the lifestyle, the location, the mind, the money, anything. "Don't worry about being shallow, because you can create meaning from it later." He mentions a template in his software (Kortex, spelled "Cortex" in the transcripts) that maps anti-vision, vision and priority tasks, and notes that in his Writer's Bootcamp program the anti-vision and vision become your brand vision: "that's how you turn yourself into the niche." You'll revisit it weekly; the vision becomes clearer; and you wait for "the click" that launches you into a season of intensity (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13).

Three points in this passage connect it with later chapters. The permission to be shallow ("you can create meaning from it later") is the principle of Chapter 9 that superficial goals are a legitimate starting point. The conversion of the personal vision into a brand vision is the thesis of Chapters 19 and 27: the person is the niche, and the brand is an extension of the person's vision. And "the click" is the moment of readiness that Chapter 4 (section 4.2) described as the high-gravity reason that "is very difficult to force": the written vision prepares for it but cannot guarantee it.

#### Inversion: the post-mortem and the minimum viable vision

The 2026 video on strategic thinking gives the most explicit methodological reason for starting with the negative. "Vision is very difficult to create just by thinking about it or imagining it. It's very difficult to think about what you want and actually know whether or not that's what you want. So instead, it's easier to start with inverting it." The steps are to be written now and iterated over time (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19):

1. **Anti-vision**, one paragraph: "exactly what you don't want the outcome of your life to be."
2. **Post-mortem**: "reverse engineer why that anti-vision would have happened in the first place, and what mistakes were made, and how to avoid them," "writing out every single thing that went wrong so that you can avoid it."
3. **Minimum viable vision (MVV)**: "one paragraph, one idea of what your vision is right now," because the vision "does evolve over time as situations change... you can't improve what doesn't exist."

The post-mortem adds causal analysis to the anti-vision. Earlier versions asked where your life would end up; this one asks why it would end up there, which mistakes produced it, and how to avoid them. That turns the anti-vision from a description into a diagnosis, and each identified mistake becomes a rule or a constraint. In a March 2025 video the equivalent step is called a "premortem"; the 2026 name "post-mortem" has the same logic (imagine the failure and work backward from it), and the change appears to be only of name, possibly a slip.

**Complementary context:** The "premortem" is a decision technique described by the psychologist Gary Klein (*Harvard Business Review*, 2007): before a project begins, a team imagines that it has failed and writes down the reasons, which counteracts overconfidence. A post-mortem, in ordinary usage, is the analysis of a failure after it has happened. Koe's exercise is a premortem in structure (the failure is imagined in advance) whichever name he uses. "Minimum viable vision" adapts the "minimum viable product" of Eric Ries's *The Lean Startup* (2011), the simplest version of a product that can be tested with real users and improved.

The **minimum viable vision** is the concept that resolves a practical problem the earlier protocols left open: what to do when the positive pole will not come into focus. Koe's answer, which he had already used under the name "vision MVP" from 2023 onward, is to write the smallest version that exists ("one paragraph, one idea") and improve it, on the product logic that "you can't improve what doesn't exist." The MVV is to the vision what the thirty-minute session is to the running note: a first iteration that makes iteration possible. Chapter 29 (section 29.2) places it in the family of "minimum viable" ideas Koe applies to goals, offers and businesses.

Inversion is also Koe's method for finding a meaningful project, which Chapter 9 treats as the practical vehicle of change. "How do you find this meaningful project? Honestly, it just takes a bit of floundering, which is very unpleasant, but it does help if you flip the problem on its head, you invert it. So what you do is you think deeply about all of the meaningless projects, tasks, and activities you currently tolerate to fill your time. Because if you aren't engaged in something meaningful, where do you think your life will end up? If you cultivate this deep awareness of what you don't want in life, that starts to create your frame. You start to see it everywhere. And once you see it, it's much easier to start moving in the opposite direction" (**Source:** `If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md`, 2026-03-28). The object of the anti-vision here is not a future but the present: the projects and activities you "tolerate." The word recalls the Malcolm X line of section 7.1 ("that which you don't hate, you tolerate"), and the passage shows its practical meaning: tolerated meaninglessness is invisible until it is listed. "Floundering" is admitted as part of the process, not a sign that it has failed.

#### The most powerful visualization, and its anchors

The May 2026 video on reinvention contains what Koe calls, in his opinion, "the most powerful visualization exercise." It belongs to the second of four patterns for recreating yourself, "become brutally aware of who you don't want to become" (the patterns are listed in Chapter 4, section 4.6). For the next day, look at all your current actions: when you wake up, during the day, at night. Then sit with pen and paper and write exactly where your life will end up if you keep doing the same thing. If you are honest, the answer "preferably would disgust you." "That disgust can be used as this ultra-potent fuel to slingshot you in a positive direction for quite a long time until you need a new reason." And none of this is permanent: "You aren't going to find your quick little fix... Nothing is permanent" (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02).

Two refinements distinguish this version from the 2022 "where will you end up?" question. First, the projection is based on a logged day of actual actions rather than on a general sense of one's habits, which makes it harder to soften. Second, the fuel is explicitly temporary: it lasts "quite a long time until you need a new reason." This matches the first of the four 2026 patterns ("a reason with extremely high gravitational pull, and when that fades, you must find another") and the 2025 warning not to try to sustain the high of a frightening goal (section 7.5).

The same passage adds a method for making the projection honest. What helps is grounding the exercise in reality: "you have to observe society. You have to observe the people around you. That's very potent. You have to find an anchor for where your life is going to end up." The anchors could be your parents, a random person at the grocery store, a person at the gym; if you live in a city, "just go walk out in the city and look at how lifeless people look" (**Source:** same video). An **anchor**, in this use, is a real person who shows where a given trajectory leads. It replaces imagination with observation at the point where imagination is weakest: in projecting one's own future decline. The instruction is blunt, and its judgments of strangers' lives are the author's; its logic is the asymmetry of section 7.1 applied to the projection step.

The same video also explains why this exercise usually fails, and that explanation was treated in Chapter 4 (section 4.2): "the self doing the exercise is the self being evaluated," and the current identity has a survival interest in not seeing its trajectory clearly. For the exercise to work you must be able to receive the honest answer, which usually requires a pre-existing "crack." The anchors are, in part, Koe's answer to that problem: other people's lives are harder to rationalize than one's own. But he does not claim they solve it, and the 2026 account remains more cautious than the 2022 one about what any written exercise can achieve on its own.

#### Templates and the AI interview

In 2025 the protocols move into software. In March, in a video on learning faster with AI, Koe describes a "life plan" template (available in his software, Kortex, but usable anywhere) whose first step is to map your ideal life. Its sections are: (1) dissect your current day, the activities you do each morning and so on, and how you feel (he says this part isn't too important and can be deleted); (2) the important section, "what do you not want out of life and what do you want out of life," where you must get specific, "or else the AI prompt is going to yell at you"; (3) generate your own goals, because "a higher hierarchy of goals frames your mind for what you are going to learn"; without written goals you won't know how to apply what you learn, since you only have the default goals of school and job, and your mind only picks up information that fuels those; (4) the skills and knowledge you need to acquire to achieve them, which shapes what you decide to learn; (5) the distractions standing in the way. Beyond that, each week you duplicate a review section (what went well last week, what didn't, gratitude, goals for this week, connections) and add daily tasks for each day (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). The template integrates this chapter with the next two: the anti-vision and vision (here), the hierarchy of goals and the weekly review (Chapters 8 and 13), and the skills that the goals require (Chapter 14). Its rationale is the one Koe gives for the whole of Part VI: goals frame what you learn.

A month later, Koe explains why he went further and replaced the template with an AI interview. His earlier "simple life reset planner" broke down your average day, anti-vision, vision, hierarchy of goals, priority tasks and a daily planner. "The problem with templates, especially when they're like contemplative templates, is that most people just get stuck, because it's a blank page. It's difficult for people to guess what they want out of life ... because that's in the future." "The best thing you can do ... is to at least get the first iteration of your vision and goals out on paper so that then you have an anchor, so when you go about life you can decide, okay, yes I do want this, I don't want this, and you can refine it with time." So he turned the planner into an AI "**life reset map**": about ten minutes of interview questions, after which the output is saved as a document with a vision statement; a hierarchy of goals by career, location, relationships, lifestyle, living situation and personal growth; a skill and knowledge development plan (for example, basic digital creation skills, self-management for independent work, books to read); a daily structure (a potential routine); an implementation strategy that scales difficulty; accountability methods; and next steps (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20).

The reasoning is the most self-critical in this section. The blank page, which the 2022 spigot metaphor treated as something to push through, is now recognized as the point where most people stall. The fix is not to change the content of the exercise but to change its form: an interview asks one question at a time and supplies structure that a blank template leaves to the user. The goal remains the same as the MVV's: a first iteration that serves as an anchor for later refinement. (The product context is relevant: Kortex is Koe's own software, so the move to templates and AI tools coincides with his commercial interests; the reasoning he gives for it stands on its own and should be judged on that basis.)

#### The one-day protocol

The most elaborate version of the exercise is the protocol in Koe's December 2025 video "How to fix your entire life in 1 day," which distributes questions over a single day. Its first part is the anti-vision, defined here as "a brutal awareness of the life that you do not want to live," so that negative energy can be aimed positively (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). The questions are:

- If nothing changes for five years, describe an average Tuesday: where you wake up, how your body feels, your first thought, who is around you, what you do from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., how you feel at 10 p.m.
- The same for ten years: what you have missed, which opportunities have closed, who has given up on you, what people say about you when you are not in the room.
- At the end of a life in which you lived the "safe version" and never broke the pattern: what was the cost? What did you never let yourself feel, try or become?
- Who in your life already lives that future (five, ten, twenty years ahead on the same trajectory), and what do you feel when you think of becoming them?
- What identity would you have to give up to change? What would it cost you socially?
- What is the most embarrassing reason you haven't changed, the one that sounds weak, scared or lazy rather than reasonable?
- If your current behavior is self-protection, what exactly are you protecting, and what is it costing you?

Koe adds a condition: if you are "just at the beginning of your own book," the questions may not fully apply yet; if they do apply, you will feel deep disgust or "dis-ease."

The questionnaire gathers almost every earlier element into one instrument. The "average Tuesday" makes the projection concrete in the way the 2026 logged day does. The ten-year version adds the social dimension (who gave up on you, what is said in your absence). The "safe version" question extends the horizon to the whole of life and reframes safety as a cost. The question about who already lives that future is the "anchor" of the 2026 video. And the last three questions are new: they turn from the future to the obstacle, asking what identity and social cost stand in the way, what embarrassing reason explains the delay, and what the self-protection is protecting. These are the questions of Chapter 3 (identity defends itself) and Chapter 4 (change is identity change) addressed directly to the reader. The **average Tuesday** and the **safe version** are Koe's names for two of these devices: the ordinary day as the scale of projection, and the cautious life as an anti-vision of its own.

The protocol's evening section synthesizes the day "to make [the insights] known, integrate them and act," "solidifying a new level of mind or new stage of development"; this, Koe says, is how you enter "a season of progress." The questions: After today, what feels most true about why you've been stuck? What is the **actual enemy**? Name it clearly: "It's not circumstances, it's not other people, it's the internal pattern or belief that has been running the show." Write one sentence capturing what you refuse to let your life become (the anti-vision compressed; it should make you feel something). Write one sentence capturing what you're building toward, knowing it will evolve (the vision MVP) (**Source:** same video). The compression to one sentence each is the end point of the protocols' evolution: from a notebook of everything you don't want to two sentences you can carry. And the "actual enemy" relocates the antagonist. In the 2025 gamification protocol of section 7.1, the enemy was the negative outcome; here it is an internal pattern. The two are compatible (the pattern produces the outcome), but the shift moves the focus from the feared future to its cause in the present self, which is where the post-mortem of 2026 also looks.

Three trends run through the table. The first concerns time. The exercise begins as a thirty-minute session (2022), becomes a list completed over "years and years" (2023), a week of contemplation (2025-08) and finally "a lifelong thing" (2025-09). The trend does not invalidate the short versions: thirty minutes produce the first iteration, and the maturation is lifelong. Koe's own reason for the 2025 reframing is that the exercise is not for "a quick dopamine hit" and that, if it did not work before, you may not have been in the right life situation.

The second trend concerns the obstacle. The early protocols assume that writing is enough; the later ones keep discovering reasons it is not: the blank page (2025-04), the inability to excavate painful experiences in one sitting (2025-08), the self-protective identity that softens the projection (2026-05). Each new version is partly a response to a failure of the previous one.

The third concerns compression and precision. The output moves from a notebook of everything to paired questions, then to a one-page structure, then to one sentence each, and finally to one paragraph plus a causal analysis. The vision, meanwhile, moves from "the opposite of those things" (2022) to a minimum viable version that is expected to change. That expectation is the subject of the next section.

### 7.4 You Will Never Know Exactly What You Want

Every protocol in section 7.3 assumes that, with enough honesty and enough writing, the positive pole will come into focus. Koe's own statements about whether that assumption holds are among the least consistent in the corpus. Sometimes he grants that a person may genuinely not know what they want; sometimes he says that "I don't know" is an evasion; and in 2025 he says that nobody can ever know exactly what they want, because what is wanted lies in a future that does not exist. This section sets out the three positions, then reconstructs the method that all three share in practice: a subtractive, experimental path in which action reveals what you don't want, exposure reveals new possibilities, and goals are picked, pursued, pivoted and sometimes abandoned. It ends with Koe's advice for the person who is simply lost, and with his own history as the case that illustrates the method.

#### Three positions on not knowing

The positions can be dated.

| Period | Position | Representative statement |
|---|---|---|
| 2022–2023 | You may genuinely not know; work from what you don't want and from experience | "If you don't know what you want, understand what you don't want and work in the opposite direction" (2022-07); "just pick something that sounds meaningful" (2023-07); you don't know because you haven't pursued anything (2023-12) |
| 2024–2025 | You know, or know enough; "I don't know" is evasion or excessive demand | "It's not that you don't know what you want, it's that you know what you don't want already" (2024-04); what you want "will make itself apparent through your choices" (2024-07); "you already know what kind of life you want to live" (2025-05) |
| 2025-09 | You can never know; it is in the future | "You will never know what you want. It's in the future. It doesn't exist. It's imaginary." |

The first position was quoted in section 7.1 (the 2022 heuristic) and is developed below through the 2023 statements. The second appeared in section 7.1 (the 2024 claim that "I don't know" means "I don't want to do the work") and in section 7.2 (the daily instruction to remind yourself of what you don't want, since what you want "will make itself apparent"). A May 2025 statement gives it its mildest form. "Within a few weeks you should have some idea of the life you want to live." Many think they need an absolutely clear goal or vision, "when you already know what kind of life you want to live": you don't want a bad life, you want a good one, with some image of it. "You can think of a very vague idea of a good life ... and just start working in that direction." "It's a general aim. You don't even need to know what to do or what you're doing as long as you're going in that direction rather than the opposite" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08). A **general aim** is a vague idea of a good life, sufficient as a direction.

The third position arrives four months later, in the "12 rules" video. "You don't know what you want. That's the problem. You don't realize that you will never know what you want. It's in the future. It doesn't exist. It's imaginary. Life changes." What you want now will be different tomorrow or next decade. You never begin "this process of **refinement and purification** because you can't seem to allow yourself to fail." "What you want out of life becomes clear when you realize what you don't want out of life and work in the other direction. Since you haven't made any mistakes on your own path, it's obvious why you don't know what you want" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). Refinement and purification, in this use, is the process of clarifying what you want through your own mistakes.

The positions contradict each other at the level of formulation, and Koe does not reconcile them. They can, however, be read as answers to different questions and different listeners. "You do know" refers to a general direction: everyone can tell a good life from a bad one, and everyone has observed what they don't want. "You can never know" refers to the detailed content of a future: it is imaginary and keeps changing. And "you may not know" is addressed to the beginner, who has not yet made the mistakes that would supply the data. Read this way, the harsh version is aimed at someone who already has experience and avoids acting on it, the lenient one at someone just starting, and the epistemic one at anyone who is waiting for certainty before moving. All three converge on the same practice: work away from what you don't want, toward a provisional aim, and let mistakes refine it.

The same variation runs through Koe's statements about how clear the vision must be. In 2024 "without one, by definition, you are lost"; in 2024 also, "hopeful delusion" and a life's work that lives "in the unknown"; in 2025 "a very vague idea of a good life"; in 2026, "a minimum viable vision," and in the same video, a vision that "is not a vague wish" but "as specific as it can possibly be." The two 2026 statements, made minutes apart, are compatible on a careful reading: the minimum viable vision is the first, imperfect version; the specificity is what that version should aim for as it is refined, because, as Koe explains, specificity is what allows decisions and tactics to be evaluated. A vague direction is enough to start moving; a specific vision is needed to decide well. Both versions agree that the vision is indispensable as a direction and that it is never finished.

#### The subtractive path

Underneath the three positions is a method that Koe states as a chain. "How to do whatever you want: do what you want, but don't ignore the consequences of your actions. You have to do what you think you want to realize what you don't want. You have to remove what you don't want to do what you really want" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). The chain is subtractive. Action on provisional wants reveals non-wants; removing non-wants reveals real wants. You do not find what you want by searching for it directly but by clearing away what you don't want until what remains is visible. The method parallels the sculptor of section 7.1 and Chapter 4: the form is found by removing material.

A January 2024 video gives the method its sharpest slogan. Step two of a seven-day reset is to get clear on what you want, and for that you have to realize what you don't want, "even if it is destructive." Observe: how do I not want to end up? Broke, in that job, ugly. "Negativity is everywhere." Use it as a reference point: what is the opposite? Then act, which sparks experimentation; this is "a **self-corrective compass**": "you need to do what you think you want in order to understand what you don't want," and you correct your decisions over time. His example: "I don't want to end up ugly," so you go to the gym; you dislike that training program and find it unsustainable; so you try running. The principle: "preventing yourself from making mistakes is the stupidest mistake of all." He repeats that it is impossible to know the future with absolute certainty, that the masses flock to secure jobs and belief systems as an illusion of certainty to avoid struggle, and that mistakes are "your light in the dark" (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07). The gym example shows the method at its smallest scale. The anti-vision (ugly) gives a direction (fitness); the first attempt (a program) reveals a non-want (that program); the correction (running) is still in the same direction. The compass does not tell you the destination; it tells you when you have turned the wrong way. The phrase "even if it is destructive" admits that some of the experiments will be mistakes in the full sense, which is exactly why they are informative.

The 2022 version is the most patient. "If you don't know, then you don't know." "There is literally only one thing you can do if you don't know: you have to try new things so you can know what you don't like." And the flip side: "once you know what you don't want, you understand what you want more." "You can't really directly experience exactly what you want the first time around": you think you want something, then try something else: "that's better, that's worse, that's even better, that's worse; it's a process." "You're never going to have the perfect answer" (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16). The sequence "better, worse, even better, worse" describes a search by comparison rather than by insight: no single experience reveals the answer; the pattern across experiences does.

In 2025 the same idea becomes a picture of progress itself. "The progress in life isn't just this linear path from 'I'm here, I want to get to this goal'; it's 'here's a weird cloud of what I think I want, potentially.'" You go toward it, up and down, all over the place, until you get to something you think you want. "You never get exactly what you want"; you try things, experiment and find some direction (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). The **weird cloud** replaces the point-target of conventional goal-setting with a region: the vision is a fuzzy area of what you think you want, approached non-linearly. It is the same image as the 2025 frame of reference in section 7.2, where the steps go "all over the place" but in the right direction.

#### "I think I want this": the soft goal

If the target is a cloud, what does commitment look like? Koe's answer, from 2022, is a formula: the meaningful goal will be "more so like, I think I want this, so I'm going to work towards this on a daily basis and pivot along the way if I have to." Try that new business model, learn about that new interest, buy a course, enroll in college if you want: "you don't have to listen to people that say certain things are bad if it's something you genuinely want to do." "Given time, you'll know when it's time to go all in; you'll feel that feeling of excitement. That's what you're looking out for: the dopamine that comes from visualizing a better future for yourself" (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16). The **meaningful goal** in this formulation is held provisionally ("I think I want this") but acted on daily. Two things are notable. First, Koe explicitly permits conventional options (a course, college) if they are genuinely wanted, which qualifies his critique of school in Chapter 2: the problem is the assigned path, not any particular institution. Second, the signal for going "all in" is emotional, the excitement that comes from visualizing a better future, which links the soft goal back to the energy of the vision.

A 2023 passage names the same stance. Once a self-generated goal or purpose comes into play, "you know at least what you think you want to do"; "it's not an absolute: I know this is exactly what I do and it's going to turn out this way." It is rather: "I have a **soft idea** and I'm going to act towards it, and I'm going to educate myself in alignment with it, and I'm going to acquire the skills necessary to make it work." If you have the vision to start a business, you obviously need the skills to start a business (**Source:** `If You’re Lost - Here’s How You Find Clarity For The Future.md`, 2023-06-04). The soft idea has three verbs attached: act toward it, educate yourself in alignment with it, acquire the skills it needs. The softness concerns the destination, not the effort. This is the stance that Chapter 14 builds on: learning is directed by a goal, even a provisional one.

The most practical form of the stance is a heuristic from a 2023 video on focus. Start by creating a hierarchy of goals: without conscious, meaningful goals you can't place focus on what matters (it won't be top of mind), and "your attention is going to be manhandled" by day-to-day life. It doesn't matter if you don't know what the goals should be yet: "just pick something that sounds meaningful." Only by pursuing something you think you want can you realize what you don't want ("oh, this wasn't it"); now, acting from better experience, you set the next goal and keep self-correcting toward a much better direction. (He suggests two of his own programs, The 2 Hour Writer or Digital Economics, as a starting direction for many people.) (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11.) The phrase "your attention is going to be manhandled" restates the thesis of Chapter 1 in the vocabulary of Chapter 6: without a chosen goal, attention is organized by whatever pulls hardest. Picking something "that sounds meaningful" is preferable to picking nothing, even if it is wrong, because a wrong goal pursued produces information while no goal produces only drift. (The suggestion of his own programs is a commercial aside; it does not affect the argument.)

The same year, Koe connects the heuristic to the structure of goals. Because goals are only visible at certain levels, he is "so big on just pick something and do it." Stop worrying about the little things that can go wrong: "they're gonna go wrong; good, accept it, overcome it." He thought he would be doing web design his whole life, but after accomplishing that goal and "burning through that karma, outward, inward," he unlocked the next level and understood what to do next. That is where people get stuck with advice like "only focus on one thing" or "find what you want to do and do it for life": "you don't know unless you start doing" (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). The claim that "goals are only visible at certain levels" is a strong one: the next goal is not hidden by lack of reflection but by lack of position, and the only way to see it is to reach the level from which it is visible. The phrase "burning through that karma" is metaphorical (Koe uses spiritual vocabulary loosely): it seems to mean exhausting a stage by fully living it, so that its lessons are learned and its desire spent. The rejection of "do one thing for life" is aimed at a common piece of career advice; it does not contradict the later emphasis on concentration of force (section 7.2), which concerns focus within a stage rather than permanence across stages.

#### Exposure as a chain

Why can't you think your way to what you want? Koe's answer is that wants are discovered through exposure, and exposure is sequential. In a March 2023 video: you don't automatically know what to go all in on. You need to expose yourself to experience, "not distraction," and break out of "your little bubble of comfort" to expose yourself "to the potential of your life." Obsession or passion "doesn't come from sitting around and waiting for it to come to you." "You haven't been exposed to the thing that allows you to become exposed to the next thing, and then the next and the next." If you're wasting away on video games, Netflix, porn and the like, that blocks it; you can enjoy life, but if you're not making forward progress, you shouldn't be enjoying life in excess (**Source:** `Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md`, 2023-03-30). **Exposure** and **forward progress** are Koe's terms for this mechanism: each exposure unlocks the next, and without progress there is no passion. The distinction between "experience" and "distraction" is important: both are inputs, but distraction loops back to the same place while experience moves you to a new one. The moral note about enjoying life "in excess" is conditional: enjoyment is not condemned, but it is ranked below progress when progress is absent.

In December 2023 he adds the scale argument. You can't sit around and think your way to what you want, nor follow what society tells you and remain trapped "in this narrow path of, like, 0.1% of the information that you can find in reality." You must explore curiosity to figure out what you want, and it takes time. You can't experience 100% of reality, nor even 50% in a lifetime, "so you better start exploring now." Most people won't pursue it at all, or won't give themselves enough time to find it (**Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31). The figure "0.1%" is rhetorical, like the percentages of section 7.1; its point is that the default path exposes you to a tiny slice of what exists, so whatever you think you want is selected from a very small sample.

In 2024 he describes the chain as it actually unfolds. "I don't know that starting a business is a possibility for me until I start learning a skill that I have the desire to learn to increase my value for a job." So "it's not that you just start exploring and you immediately find it": you find one thing interesting for your current use case, then the next, then you are exposed to a new potential, "and then you find the clarity and education that allows you to get to that potential" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). The example is the most important in this section, because it shows that the chain can start from a conventional goal. Someone who wants a better job learns a skill; the skill reveals that independent work is possible; that possibility becomes a new goal. The starting goal was not wrong; it was a door.

Two months later the chain becomes a principle about goals. Only by starting conversations in the direction of a goal "is how you're going to open up potentials aside from the goal that you just came up with; you're going to find new possibilities and new goals that you can pursue and that may make more sense to you." If you're focused on the problem, "you're never going to think of the goal that leads to the next goal that makes you aware of the next goal that makes you aware of an idea that leads to this goal that you never thought was possible, but now you're pursuing and make your life's work" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). The breathless sentence is its own illustration: **goals lead to goals**, and the life's work is usually several links away from the first goal. The contrast with "focusing on the problem" belongs to a debate within the corpus about whether motivation starts from the problem or from the goal; in this May 2024 passage Koe sides with the goal, while two months earlier he had said that "a problem creates a goal" and that a goal without a felt problem lacks gravity. Chapter 8 (section 8.6) examines the oscillation; for this chapter, the anti-vision supplies the problem and the provisional goal supplies the direction, so both are present.

The chain is driven by curiosity, and Koe's criterion for following curiosity is physical. In 2022 he describes how he decides whether to keep reading a new book: he can feel areas of his brain "lighting up." The words "dig into my soul," curiosity pulls him deeper into the pages, thoughts flood his mind and keep him up past his bedtime ("around 9 p.m. nowadays"). He feels he has to keep going down the rabbit hole, to understand and dissect every perspective on the specific thing that is lighting his brain up, "so it lights up more." "If a book doesn't do that for me, why would I read it?" (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). He describes the sensation as dopamine; the description is phenomenological (what curiosity feels like to him), not a measurement. Its function in the method is to identify which exposures deserve to become links in the chain: the ones that light up.

In the same video, Koe immediately limits the criterion. The real question behind the video is what the utility of pursuing your interest is, and whether you can do it full time. If there is no reason to pursue genuine curiosity, if it doesn't "pull in the money" or fulfill some need in daily life aside from entertainment (which he also considers good), then "there's nothing kind of holding you accountable to pursuing those curiosities and keeping that reward mechanism in your head in check." He admits he has been questioning whether intellectual development and the pursuit of knowledge are worth it if his job and relationships do not require them: "if there's no purpose behind my curiosity pursuits, then why would I continue doing that?" (**Source:** same video). The two passages together state the condition under which curiosity becomes direction: curiosity has to be attached to some real-world utility, or it either fades or becomes another form of consumption. This is the same principle as "learn and build in unison" (section 7.2) and the core of Chapter 14's "build to learn."

The same video offers an image of where curiosity can lead: the **iceberg of opportunity**. What you consume is the tip of the iceberg; everything under it is what you are trying to dissect. You can get sucked into that iceberg and "turn that iceberg into an entire career" (**Source:** same video). The image converts consumption into a starting point: what you already watch or read shows where an iceberg is, and the career lies in going below the surface of it. Chapter 16 develops the passage from consumer to researcher that the iceberg describes.

#### Going all in

Exposure and soft goals are the exploratory phase. At some point, Koe says, you must commit. The March 2023 video that introduced exposure is structured around three things that "will determine your future": (1) one big decision to go all in on one thing, which you must first find through exposure and problem solving; (2) repetitive focused action on the fundamentals that bring results, learned through self-education; and (3) the ability to persist and iterate, which he says is greater than consistency and repetition. Midway through the video he corrects himself: the second element should have been called "persistent focused action" (**Source:** `Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md`, 2023-03-30). The live correction is a small record of a larger change. Around 2023 Koe moved from praising repetition ("a system is a process of doing the same thing every day," 2022) to saying that "persistence and iteration are greater than consistency and repetition," and in 2024 to saying that consistency maintains progress but does not create it. The reason he gives is that persistence does not depend on motivation; Chapter 12 (section 12.7) and Chapter 15 follow the theme. For this chapter, the point is the sequence: exposure finds the thing, the decision commits to it, persistent action and iteration carry it.

What does genuine commitment look like? Koe's example, in a 2022 video on productizing your knowledge, is the bodybuilder Chris Bumstead (transcribed "Chris Bromstad"). Bumstead said he had to give up a normal life, staying home to cook healthy meals, not going out drinking, not being tempted by junk food, because he had a mission he was dead set on with his skill set. "His friends didn't get it. But he knew that it was going to lead to something great." Koe's question to the listener: if something like that exists in your life, something you are genuinely obsessed with, why not turn it into something to help people like you? (**Source:** `You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md`, 2022-12-04.) The example serves as a test of obsession: real commitment shows up as sacrifices that others don't understand. It also shows how Koe connects commitment to the one-person business: the obsession, once found, is also a product (Chapter 33).

**Complementary context:** Chris Bumstead is a Canadian professional bodybuilder, a multiple-time winner of the Classic Physique division of the Mr. Olympia competition, known for documenting his training and lifestyle online.

#### Pivot, or quit?

What if the thing you went all in on turns out to be wrong? In November 2024 Koe's answer is to adjust rather than abandon. "If you can't achieve a goal, or you just realize that the goal or vision for your life was wrong in the first place, then you iterate and you pivot rather than quitting. If you start a business and realize that you didn't want to be in that business in the first place, then change it" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). The passage extends the cybernetic correction of Chapter 6 from the steps to the target itself: the loop corrects not only how you pursue a goal but which goal you pursue.

In December 2025 he says something that sounds like the opposite. "I've quit 10 times as many goals than I've set." The book he had said he was writing, he is no longer writing. He thinks that should be the case for most people: "how else are you going to narrow in on the correct goals?" He adds that it remains true that people try to change and fail almost every time (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). Here quitting is not a failure to be avoided but a selection mechanism: goals are hypotheses, and most hypotheses should be discarded.

The two statements can be reconciled, and the reconciliation clarifies the whole section. What is abandoned in the 2025 statement is goals; what is preserved in the 2024 statement is the direction (the vision) and the process of change. "Pivot rather than quit" means: do not quit the pursuit of a better life because one goal failed. "Quit more goals than you set" means: do not cling to a particular goal once it has shown itself to be wrong. Both are forms of the same compass. (The detail of the abandoned book is also a small inconsistency in the corpus's account of the author's publications; he speaks elsewhere of two published books, and the statements about which books were planned differ.)

#### When you are simply lost: radical honesty

All of the above assumes some starting point: a dislike, a curiosity, a soft goal. What if there is none? Koe admits, in a January 2025 video, that the question was hard for him to think through: how do you know what problem to solve without a goal, or set an impactful goal if you are lost "in the middle of nowhere"? His answer is to be extremely honest about where you are. Ask: "are you content with life?" It is a trick question, because nobody is: "show me someone who is happy at every second of the day and I'll show you a liar." Life comes in waves, cycles, highs and lows; without lows there is no creativity and no life as we know it. Humans make sense of the world in stories, which mirror the structure of reality; nature cycles through birth and death (a forest has dead things everywhere; rubbing your arm kills skin cells). Eternal happiness doesn't exist, so you are not content with every aspect of your life, and "in lies the problem": the starting point of "the **infinite string of problems**" toward your vision. Find where you are not content and start there (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12).

The argument has an unusual structure. It moves from a cosmological claim (reality is cyclical, life includes death) to a psychological one (no one is always content) to a practical one (any discontent is a valid first problem). The cosmological premises are offered as images, not as evidence, and the conclusion does not depend on them: it is enough to observe that some area of one's life is unsatisfying. The point is that "lost" never means "without problems." It means without a chosen problem, and choosing one requires only honesty. The vision does not have to come first; the first problem in a chain that leads toward it does. Chapters 8 (section 8.6) and 39 develop the two halves of this idea: purpose as the most urgent problem, and the cyclical nature of happiness.

A 2023 video adds a test for the first problem. "When you identify this problem, are you actually able to work towards this problem right now, or is there another problem that begets that problem that you have to solve first?" With money, the goal might be starting a business, but "do you have time ... do you have the bandwidth to start a business? Possibly, possibly not." If not, experiment with other techniques first: "starting a budget, learning about financing, learning how to get a better job, studying a new skill so that you can eventually start a business or get a better job. You can't go from zero to five; you have to go from zero to one" (**Source:** `Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md`, 2023-02-26). The **problem that begets that problem** is the prior problem that must be solved first, and "zero to one" is used in its literal sense: the first step from nothing, as opposed to a jump to an advanced stage.

**Complementary context:** *Zero to One* is also the title of Peter Thiel's 2014 book, where the phrase means creating something genuinely new rather than copying what exists. Nothing in this passage refers to that book, and the two uses should not be confused.

The heuristic tempers the big goals of section 7.5: a large vision is compatible with a very small first problem, and often requires one.

The same video applies the test to relationships. If your problem is self-esteem or relationships and you want a boyfriend or girlfriend, "does that even make sense? You have to step back and think, be honest with yourself: are you in the financial or even spiritual position to be able to sustain a lifelong partner?" "If your goal is different and you just want to sleep around, go ahead, try it out; you learn from your mistakes, as I have in the past. But that does not take away from the fact that if you do that and you eventually want a lifelong partner ... you're putting a veil in front of your current problems, and you're still going to have to solve it." Once you identify the goal, "research and experiment with techniques that allow you to reach it" (**Source:** same video). The example shows radical honesty working in both directions: it can lower an ambition (you may not be ready for a lifelong partner) and it can expose an evasion (a substitute goal that veils the real problem). The tone is permissive about experimentation ("go ahead, try it out") and strict about self-deception.

In a 2025 video on reinvention, the advice for the dissonance phase (the first of the three phases of change in Chapter 4) is simpler. When you feel lost or stuck in a rut, "the best advice I can give is to try more things. Try new things." And to make it "more complex": "you search for excitement and enthusiasm and pursue those things without shame, because that shame signals a lower level of mind, and you can develop the skill of confidence during that" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19). Excitement is the signal (as in the 2022 "I think I want this" passage); shame is the obstacle. The claim that shame "signals a lower level of mind" uses the concept of level of mind from Chapter 4 (section 4.7): embarrassment about one's enthusiasms is treated as a sign of an earlier developmental stage that the pursuit itself helps outgrow.

The last element of Koe's advice for the lost concerns fear. In a 2025 video on multiple interests: "You are literally only afraid of survival. You're afraid of not making money." "You're thinking too generally, too abstractly, so you're thinking from the amygdala. You're not thinking specifically," not thinking through what you can do and how. The remedy is a plan: "a plan is a **surface area for luck**. It's something that evolves. It's something that brings order and clarity to the mind, and once you have a plan, then your anxiety tends to fizzle down and you start to see that you can do it." Then a caveat and a confession: "Obviously don't do anything stupid," though he personally would: "I quit my job, I move into places that I can't afford all the time because it forces me to stretch and grow," which most people fear "because they'll sink" (**Source:** `How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md`, 2025-02-23). The claim that abstract thinking happens "from the amygdala" is Koe's informal neuroscience; the amygdala is involved in threat and fear processing, but the contrast between "abstract" and "specific" thinking as amygdala versus non-amygdala is his framing, not an established finding. The practical point does not depend on it: vague fears are reduced by specific plans, because a plan converts an undefined threat into a sequence of manageable problems, and because it increases the number of situations in which favorable accidents can be recognized and used ("surface area for luck"). The confession illustrates the "tactical stress" of Chapter 10 (section 10.6), which Koe recommends with warnings that grew over the years; here the warning ("don't do anything stupid") and the personal practice sit side by side.

#### The author's own compass

Koe's career is his main illustration of the subtractive path, and he tells it the same way across several years. In a 2023 video, explaining why he wants to write for the rest of his life (because he realized his ideal future requires it), he says that "writing was not the first thing": "you have to try things and start to execute on fundamental tasks in order to understand what it is that you want." At first his desires were selfish ("they still kind of are"): he had to make money to build a future lifestyle. He did freelancing and other things, then social media; through trial and error: "I don't like client work. I don't really care for web design anymore. I did at the time, but I outgrew it." Then writing, which he loves: "I would have never stumbled on that unless I had already been working towards my ideal future." So start writing fifteen minutes every morning, or working on whatever brings you closer, so "you can figure out what you don't want and allow that to act as a self-corrective compass into figuring out what you actually do want," and then "execute, execute, execute" until it becomes a seamless part of your day (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; repeated almost word for word in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The story contains every element of this section. The starting motive is superficial (money for a lifestyle), which Chapter 9 defends as a legitimate beginning. The path is a sequence of exposures (freelancing, web design, social media), each of which revealed a non-want (client work, web design) and opened the next possibility. The discovery (writing) was not available at the start; it became visible only because he was already moving toward an ideal future. And once found, the instruction changes from exploring to executing.

A 2024 version adds what the early search was looking for. "I always believed that passion was found in a specific skill." He tried web design, digital art, programming "and a few others," "but every time I was left feeling empty." "It wasn't until I decided to take control of my life, get clear on what I wanted for my future and start pursuing that relentlessly that I figured out what a creator actually is" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). The correction is conceptual: passion was not in a skill but in a direction. The skills were tools; what made one of them (writing) satisfying was its relation to a chosen future. This is why section 7.2 quoted the same video's instruction to "choose a vision, not a skill," and why Chapter 8 (section 8.7) will argue that passion is created by investing energy in a goal.

The compass did not stop working once he found writing. In June 2023 Koe says he pushed too far into a business model he didn't like going after, which made him realize what he doesn't want, and therefore what he wants. He is actively trying to get back to being one person with a virtual assistant (a small team is fine). Above all he wants to write and take on creative challenges with his writing: "I want to write and I want to take on creative challenges with my writing; that is what gives me energy for the rest of the day," plus work on his health and maintain his lifestyle and routine. He knows that peeling back the business will decrease his income, but he wants to focus on writing books and content, flex his mind and advance his understanding of his topics (**Source:** `The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md`, 2023-06-25).

The episode is a case of the anti-vision working at a later stage: success itself produced a new non-want (a business model that drained him). It also shows that the compass can point in different directions at different times. In 2023 the correction was toward fewer people and lower income; from late 2024 onward Koe built a team and a software startup and described himself as the "CEO of a small scrappy startup" (Chapter 37). His stated reasons for the later move (wanting cycles, intensity and challenge; treating the business as a vessel for personal growth) are not presented as a refutation of the 2023 decision. Read through this section, both are applications of the same method: do what you think you want, notice what you don't want, adjust. The 2023 statement is evidence that he followed the compass; the later change is evidence that the compass keeps moving, which is exactly what "you will never know what you want" predicts.

### 7.5 Big Irrational Goals

Section 7.4 argued for provisional, soft, revisable goals. This section turns to their size. Koe's most quoted rule on the matter seems to push in the opposite direction: "big irrational goals followed by small rational steps." The two are compatible once the function of the big goal is understood: it is not mainly a target to be hit but an anchor thrown into the unknown, a lens that changes what the mind looks for and what it is willing to learn. The section reconstructs that argument, examines the calibration Koe adds ("just above your level") and the typology of easy, difficult and impossible goals, and then follows the argument into the domain where he applies it most concretely: business and money.

#### Big irrational goals, small rational steps

The formula appears in a 2023 video on becoming "multidimensionally jacked": "big irrational goals followed by small rational steps." Koe calls it "the cone shape that dictates truth": wide at the top (the goal), narrow at the bottom (the step). Concretely: one habit to build your mind, one for your body, one for your business; experiment until you find ones you can stick with for life; "in five years you won't be the same person." Why big goals rather than small ones? Because big goals give vision, motivation and long-term focus. He gets more excited about building a million-dollar business in a year than about sending ten networking emails a day, and more about looking shredded on the beach than about weekly meal prepping (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30).

The argument depends on a distinction between what motivates and what is done. The networking emails and the meal prep are the rational steps; they are necessary and boring. The million-dollar business and the shredded body are the irrational goals; they are improbable and exciting. The excitement of the goal is what makes the boring step bearable. Without the big goal, the small step has no meaning (the intention of section 7.2: nothing to stretch toward); without the small step, the big goal is fantasy (the "mental masturbation" of section 7.2). The "cone" is Koe's image for the relation: the steps are narrow because they must be executable today; the goal is wide because it must be compelling. Chapter 8 (section 8.1) develops the same principle as "big goals for direction, small goals for clarity."

#### Tossing an anchor into the unknown

A December 2023 video states what a big goal is for. "You must allow yourself the room to discover new goals by tossing an anchor into the unknown." Create "a massive goal that acts as a spotlight in the unknown." You don't set it for practicality or even achievement ("you're not trying to necessarily achieve this goal") but for "vision, direction and filtration of opportunities." Make it as desirable as possible, and write it in a notebook (or in the Focus planner he released with his book) (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17).

Two images are combined: the anchor (thrown ahead, something to pull toward) and the spotlight (it illuminates part of the unknown). The second image is the more informative. A massive goal does not mainly describe a future; it lights up the present, making visible the opportunities, skills and people relevant to it that would otherwise stay in the dark. That is the lens function of Chapter 6 at its largest scale. The explicit statement that the goal is "not necessarily" to be achieved is one side of a tension in the corpus: in some passages Koe says goals are not for achieving but for perspective ("a goal is a point of view, not something you achieve"), while in others he says "the destination is what determines the journey" and is "vastly more important than the system." He concludes elsewhere that path and destination are inseparable. The most consistent reading is that the destination determines the direction of the system, while each particular goal functions as a lens that can be adjusted or abandoned (section 7.4).

The same reasoning explains Koe's impatience with small ambitions. In a 2023 video on becoming a digital writer, he answers an imagined objection from a reader: "I don't want to learn those things, I just want to do design." His reply: "think bigger, like, zoom out and think what do you actually want in your future ... if you want that, then you want to do the things that are going to take you there; otherwise you're just thinking too small and lying to yourself." And the consequence of thinking small: "that's when your mind gravitates to the surface level pleasures and distractions that you succumb to, and then you get absolutely nowhere" (**Source:** `How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md`, 2023-10-08). The argument is that a small goal is not a modest goal but a dishonest one: if the designer actually wants a certain life, then the skills that life requires are part of what they want, and refusing them is self-deception. The second sentence repeats the logic of section 7.2: a goal too small to organize attention leaves attention to be organized by pleasures and distractions.

#### Goals that scare you: the cosmic pull and ten goals in ten years

In July 2025 Koe gives the big goal an energetic description. The first thing to do is to set goals that scare you. Pursuing goals your younger self wouldn't have conceived is "a source of energy that is so potent that even the world's strongest stimulant just can't compare." It feels very good, "but that's not sustainable. Don't try to sustain it." Pursuing a big, grand vision is a massive source of intrinsic motivation: "like a **cosmic pull** that is just calling you to become your higher self. It's your calling. It's a **self-imposed calling**" (**Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`, 2025-07-27).

The phrase "self-imposed calling" is a small philosophical move. A calling, in the traditional sense, comes from outside (from God, from a vocation that chooses you). Koe keeps the experience of being called (the pull, the sense that something larger is asking for you) while making its source the self: you set the goal, and then it calls you. The "cosmic pull" is the gravity of section 7.1 at its most intense, and "higher self" connects it with the identity tools of Chapter 4 (section 4.5). The warning not to sustain the high is the important qualification. It belongs to the 2025 refinement noted in section 7.1: the energy of the anti-vision and of frightening goals is real but episodic, and trying to live permanently at that intensity leads to what a 2024 video calls the "intensity trap": burnout and overwhelm that come from trying to stick out intensity too long instead of transitioning into consistency, when the wiser move, as in trading, is to "take profits" and "drop down to a new baseline and maintain the progress you've made." Chapter 10 discusses it.

The same video gives the exercise. Pull out a sheet of paper and write at the top "**10 goals 10 years**." Write ten goals that make you physically uncomfortable, one per year for ten years, and then focus on one massive goal per year. The reason: "your mind expands to fill the goals set for achievement. It's like Parkinson's law, but for goals" (**Source:** same video). The adapted law is named but not explained in the transcript: just as work expands to fill the time available, the mind expands to fill the size of the goal set. It justifies setting very big goals, because a small goal will be filled by a correspondingly small mind.

**Complementary context:** Parkinson's law ("work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion") comes from a 1955 satirical essay by the British historian C. Northcote Parkinson in *The Economist*, about the growth of bureaucracies. Koe uses it elsewhere in its original sense for deadlines (Chapter 12). The extension to goals is his own: it is a metaphor for the claim that ambition stretches capacity, not a corollary of Parkinson's observation.

The physical discomfort criterion ("goals that make you physically uncomfortable") is a calibration test, like the discomfort test of the anti-vision. A goal that produces no reaction in the body is not big enough to reorganize the mind. And the sequencing (one massive goal per year) combines the size of the irrational goal with concentration: ten goals spread over ten years, not ten goals at once.

#### Calibration: just above your level

Against these statements stands a 2023 heuristic from a video on tactical stress. "Of course I'm not going to be able to buy, like, a billion dollar house and expect myself to reach that point in business; it has to be just above your level. If you're a level two, then you need to aim for level four, not level 100." "When you do this, you're forced to create clarity from chaos; it's gonna feel a bit chaotic at first, you're going to feel that stress." "So you set a goal that is borderline impossible, force yourself to achieve it, and transmute the stress to study and act your ass off until it becomes reality" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). **Clarity from chaos** names the process: a goal beyond your current ability produces stress and confusion, and meeting it forces you to create order. The **borderline impossible goal** is calibrated just above your level, and the stress it produces is transmuted, the same verb used for the negative energy of the anti-vision, into study and action.

The tension is visible. "Irrational" goals, goals that scare you, ten goals that make you physically uncomfortable (2023, 2025) sit beside "level four, not level 100" (2023) and, elsewhere in the corpus, the "golden zone" of challenge at around 85% (2025, Chapter 10). The positions do not follow each other chronologically; they coexist. Koe's own partial resolution is the separation already mentioned: big goals are for direction, smaller goals for clarity and action. On that reading, the "level 100" goal is legitimate as a spotlight in the unknown but not as the goal you act on this year; the goal you act on should be at level four. The 2023 "billion dollar house" example supports this: the problem is not imagining the house, it is expecting to reach it from level two. The "10 goals 10 years" exercise is compatible too: the ten-year horizon is the irrational goal, the one massive goal per year is a nearer target. What the reconciliation cannot fully absorb is the emotional register: "scare you" and "just above your level" describe different experiences, and Koe recommends both.

#### Easy, difficult and impossible goals

A December 2025 video gives a typology that explains why big goals are not simply unrealistic. The third "tell" of high-agency people, Koe says, is that they "believe in the difficult." Goals come in three forms. **Easy** goals are things we do daily or can achieve with our current skills and resources. **Difficult** goals are things we can't do now but can eventually, with the right skills and resources. **Impossible** goals are outside reality's possibility, or things we can't do "until we complete the series of difficult goals that allow us to see impossible goals as possible." Low-agency people have a belief system, likely conditioned in childhood or later through an ideology (a political party, a religion, a friend group), that skews their perception so that they see difficult goals as impossible and don't even try, not understanding that "impossible goals are only impossible right now until you get to a point where they become possible" (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md`, 2025-12-21). In this passage Koe presents the typology without attribution; elsewhere in the corpus the same three "buckets" appear in connection with the writer Devon Eriksen, so it is best read as an adapted framework that Koe has made his own.

The typology does two things for this section. First, it redefines the impossible as a relation between a goal and a position: some goals are impossible from here but possible from further along a series of difficult goals. That is the logic of "goals lead to goals" (section 7.4) applied to ambition. Second, it locates the main error not in setting goals too high but in misclassifying difficult goals as impossible, an error of perception produced by conditioning. This connects the size of goals to the diagnosis of Part I: a closed belief system (Chapter 1, section 1.7) does not only limit what a person thinks but what they attempt. Chapter 35 develops the concept of agency to which "believe in the difficult" belongs.

A 2023 passage explains how impossible goals become possible. There is a way to achieve impossible ideas, Koe says: create a path that lets you actualize that reality. Most people fail "because they skipped building the ideas that must come before." You can't skip making mistakes on the way to having the companies, capital and path. Often people didn't know the later thing could exist until the earlier solutions made it possible. His examples: Elon Musk wanting to land on Mars, impossible a few years ago and becoming more possible every day; "he didn't start with SpaceX," and he made smart choices among many dumb ones. And from the history of technology: "the plow came before the tractor, the filing cabinet came before the second brain," and the horse-drawn carriage came before the car (**Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31). The examples make the argument about sequence concrete. An impossible goal is impossible mainly because its prerequisites do not yet exist; building them, one difficult goal at a time, is what moves it into the possible. The irrational goal is legitimate because it points to a sequence, and the rational steps are the sequence. (The Musk example is used as illustration, and its biographical simplification, that he "didn't start with SpaceX," refers to his earlier internet companies; Koe does not elaborate.)

#### Thinking big in business

Koe applies the big-goal principle most concretely to business, and the application shows how a large goal changes not only motivation but the design of a plan. In a 2024 video on minimalist productivity he gives a five-step "high-leverage" plan for a six-month goal. (1) Set a big six-month goal "so your mind biases education toward that goal"; without the goal, your mind lacks the perspective that can receive the information that would help achieve it. (2) Start a business, so you have full control over product, audience and revenue; in a job, the marketing, sales and operations departments do this for you, and you get a small fraction of the income while the company keeps the audience. (3) Because of the first two, you are forced to think creatively about how to achieve the goal. (4) Choose a business model that allows you to achieve the goal without sacrificing your interests. (5) Multiply your income (one, two or three times) year after year rather than settling for a 10% raise on a six-figure salary (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

The first step restates the goal-as-lens principle in its learning form: the goal determines what education the mind will accept. The second and third show why Koe connects big goals with entrepreneurship: a large goal pursued inside a job runs into the job's ceiling, while the same goal pursued with control over product and audience forces creative solutions. The fourth step protects the vision from the money goal: the business model must serve your interests, not replace them.

Later in the same video he gives the cognitive reason behind the plan. If your mind is limited to a small local shop, you hinder your ability to raise dopamine through novelty, discovery and pattern recognition, "the fuel that keeps drawing you back to the project." Eventually skill acquisition and growth slow down and you reach the same point you did when you started hating your nine-to-five. Therefore: "your mental capacity is limited by the potential of the business you are building." Think big and reverse-engineer how to get there: vision, ten-year goal, one-year goals, monthly goals, weekly goals, **daily levers**; and then forget about the goals (referring to an earlier video) (**Source:** same video). The claim that mental capacity is limited by the business's potential is the Parkinson's-law-for-goals idea applied to a vessel: a small project gives the mind a small space to grow in, and when the space is exhausted, the boredom of the old job returns. The reverse-engineering sequence (vision down to daily levers) is the hierarchy that Chapter 8 develops; "daily levers" are the daily actions derived from the vision by that breakdown.

Koe's earliest worked example of the breakdown is from June 2022, in the video that introduced the childhood notebooks (section 7.1). He calls the structure the "creation pyramid" and applies it to money. The vision: "creative work making six figures," with the experience it implies: feeling good, deep in flow, spending most of your time on creative work, positive emotions. The ten-year goal: a million dollars a year in ten years ("we don't have to make it super serious right now; we're just breaking it down for the sake of clarity"). The one-year goal: six figures; imagine how life would look and feel, what you could afford, what you could outsource, how you would free up time. Then he breaks the year down further: six figures is "four clients a month at 22.50, if I remember that number right" (that is, $2,250 per client, a figure he is not sure of), or "84 sales of a 99 [dollar] product ... a month" (he hesitates over the numbers: "something like that ... do the math for yourself"). This, he says, makes the goal tangible. The monthly goal: land my first client this month. The weekly goal: "I'm going to send five DMs, let's start small": cold outreach ("that's how you land clients at first"), or studying how others land clients. And if this makes you anxious, in terms of the flow model (the balance between challenge and skill that Koe adapts from Csikszentmihalyi and that Chapter 10 develops: a challenge too far above your skill produces anxiety, one too far below it produces boredom), the remedy is to raise the skill, so self-educate: buy a course or scour the internet (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

| Level of the creation pyramid | Koe's 2022 example |
|---|---|
| Vision | Creative work making six figures; feeling good, deep in flow |
| 10-year goal | $1 million a year |
| 1-year goal | Six figures (about four clients a month at roughly $2,250, or about 84 sales a month of a $99 product, by his rough arithmetic) |
| Monthly goal | Land the first client |
| Weekly goal | Send five DMs (cold outreach), or study how others land clients |
| If anxious | Self-educate: a course, research |

The example is the cone of "big irrational goals, small rational steps" drawn out in full. A million dollars a year is irrational for someone with no clients; five DMs a week is trivially rational. The pyramid connects them, and the connection is what makes the small step meaningful and the big goal credible. The arithmetic is approximate, and Koe says so; its purpose is tangibility, not accuracy. Chapter 8 (section 8.3) treats the creation pyramid as a framework in its own right.

#### Removing money limits

The most extended application of big goals concerns income, and Koe presents it through a thought experiment. Take a six-year goal of making $1 million. The low-leverage way to pursue it is familiar: create a budget and avoid buying five-dollar Starbucks coffees; try to get a promotion or raise so that income increases by roughly 10%; calculate how much to invest each month in the stock market; live below your means and hope nothing catastrophic happens to you. The alternative, Koe warns, will sound insane to people who are not successful in business, because they haven't broken out of the "labor theory of value frame" ("my time is worth this much"). The question becomes: "how in the world can you achieve that six-year goal in 6 months," if the listed ways are not the way? He frames the exercise as illustrating a point, not as a promise (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

**Complementary context:** The "labor theory of value" is the doctrine, associated with Adam Smith, David Ricardo and Karl Marx, that the value of a good derives from the labor required to produce it. Koe uses the phrase loosely, for the personal belief that one's income must be proportional to one's hours ("my time is worth this much"), which is the frame he criticizes in Chapter 34 ("leveraged, not hard-working"). He does not engage with the economic doctrine itself.

The thought experiment works by making the conventional method visibly inadequate. Compressing six years into six months cannot be done by saving on coffee, so the question forces a search for a different kind of method, one with leverage. It is the "big irrational goal" used as a cognitive tool: not a target to be met but a constraint that rules out incremental thinking.

Before any plan, Koe says, three realizations are necessary. Step one: realize that it is possible. If you can't believe it, you can't get to step two. His evidence: people make a million in six months all the time; many businesses make a million in a month, sometimes in a day, especially on Black Friday (he acknowledges the objection that revenue is not profit and dismisses it as missing the point). Step two: realize that you just don't have the knowledge and skill that make it possible; you haven't sought knowledge from an education that can't be found in schools, relevant to a financial goal you don't have in the first place. Step three: truly understand what it takes to learn something of this magnitude: "it's a metric ton of failures until you can afford to succeed." He adds again that this is not a promise; even $50,000, $100,000 or the first dollar is a win (**Source:** same video).

The three realizations correspond to three parts of this book. Believing it is possible is the "believe in the difficult" of the typology above; recognizing the missing knowledge is the self-education of Chapter 14; and the "metric ton of failures" is the error-driven learning of Chapters 6 and 15. The phrase "until you can afford to succeed" inverts the usual relation between failure and success: success is something you have to be able to afford, and failures are the payments. The dismissal of the revenue-versus-profit objection is a rhetorical move; the objection is real, but it does not affect the point he is making, which is about the existence of the possibility, not its ease.

A September 2024 video adds an argument from scale, borrowed in part from Alex Hormozi. "If you can make $1,000, you can make $100,000, and if you can make $100,000, you probably have the knowledge and skill to make a million." "Like Alex Hormozi says, it's a lot easier to make a million than it is $100,000." Most people aim too low and therefore don't acquire the necessary skill; pursuing a much larger goal requires you to learn much more, be more creative and understand what leads to the million. So focusing on $1,000 (the subject of the video) "may be shooting ourselves in the foot"; he does it for educational purposes and because $1,000 is believable as a hook. "Don't limit yourself to thinking in thousands: think in hundreds of thousands or millions, and shoot for the stars, you'll land on the moon." You don't need a large audience, only the basics of two high-income skills; "all of your actions compound, and nothing goes to waste if you stick with it" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md`, 2024-09-15).

**Complementary context:** Alex Hormozi is an American entrepreneur and author (*$100M Offers*, 2021; *$100M Leads*, 2023) whose content on offers and business growth Koe cites often. The line about a million being easier than $100,000 is a recurring theme of his. Koe adopts it in his own voice and does not elaborate on Hormozi's reasoning.

The argument is a version of Parkinson's law for goals: the size of the target determines the size of the learning. A $1,000 goal can be reached with tactics; a million-dollar goal requires understanding. Koe's admission that his own video's framing ($1,000) works against this principle, and that he chose it because it is "believable as a hook," is a candid instance of the persuasion principles of Chapter 32: the hook is calibrated to the audience's level of belief, and the content then pushes the belief upward.

The comparison between a job and a business is made explicit in a 2023 passage. "At a certain point you will have to start a business or become an entrepreneur to get to where you want to be in life, unless you settle for something less, which of course there's nothing wrong with if you're actually happy with that," though his audience wants more. He could repeat tactical stress over four years only because "I had a business that I had control over." In a corporate job, in four years "maybe you can get, like, a five to ten percent boost in salary, if that; I bet that's extremely rare": $100,000 becomes $110,000. If you focus that work on a business, "I don't see why in four years you couldn't go from a hundred thousand a year to a hundred thousand a month, if not more." "All that it takes is a proper strategy, the right skill set, a compelling offer and enough traffic to sell one hundred thousand dollars' worth of a product." "I'm aware that's an oversimplification, but that doesn't mean it's wrong" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

The passage contains its own two qualifications: the explicit validation of choosing less ("nothing wrong with that if you're actually happy") and the explicit admission of oversimplification. The comparison itself (a 10% raise over four years against a twelvefold increase) is a projection, not a typical outcome; Koe says "I don't see why you couldn't," which is a claim of possibility. The four ingredients he names (strategy, skill set, offer, traffic) are the subjects of Parts IX to XI, and the equation "audience plus product" that Chapter 28 presents is already visible here in the pair "offer and traffic."

A 2024 example makes the same comparison at the level of a single paycheck. "You being paid, let's say, $2,000 every paycheck for the certain amount of work you do in a day: how much is the boss getting paid?" Alternatively: "let's say I actually learn a skill and go and get a client for $2,000, and that takes a series of actions to reach. If I replicate that and I systemize that, so manually I can get like four clients a month..." (the transcript then gives a total of "$88,000," which does not match four clients at $2,000, that is $8,000 a month; the figure is ambiguous and probably a transcription error). "Let's say I systemize it, get better at it and make my process more efficient, because I have something called technology: I can land 10, 20, 30; if I hire employees, 40, 50, however much. If I create an actual business, I can make as much money as I want by doing what I talked about earlier: having a vision, goals, creating systems and then being able to identify a problem" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`, 2024-07-13). The example has two halves. The first is a question about the hidden ceiling of a wage: the paycheck reveals what you are paid, not what your work produces for someone else. The second is a model of scaling: one $2,000 client becomes a repeatable process, the process is systematized, technology and hires multiply it, and the ceiling disappears. The last sentence ties the money back to this chapter: what makes the scaling possible is "having a vision, goals, creating systems and then being able to identify a problem," the sequence that runs from this chapter through Chapter 12.

The principle behind all of these passages is stated in a 2023 video on the digital writing business. It won't be easy, Koe says, but "the principles are pretty straightforward": "use writing to build a readership that grows infinitely with the internet," and know how to improve "when you hit a stunt in growth." As an entrepreneur, "you have to be able to identify problems in both your life and your business and solve them": if the problem is "I'm not making the amount of money that I want," then "you have to change something; you have to create a solution." Stick it out "for multiple years, if not the rest of your life." "Entrepreneurship means you are in full control of how much you make. Your income is only limited by your perspective, beliefs, skill set and real world practice. That is it" (**Source:** `How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md`, 2023-10-08).

The four limits Koe names are worth listing because they are all internal and all changeable: perspective (the frame of reference of section 7.2), beliefs (the conditioning of Part I and the misclassification of difficult goals as impossible), skill set (Part VI) and real-world practice (the trial and error of Chapter 6). The claim that income is limited "only" by these is a strong one, and it ignores external factors such as markets, capital, timing and luck, which Koe acknowledges in other contexts (section 7.2 quoted his view, borrowed from Orange Book, that luck is aligned by choices). Read in context, the statement is a principle of attribution rather than an economic law: it tells the entrepreneur to look for the limit in themselves, where it can be changed, rather than outside, where it cannot. That is the same move the anti-vision makes at the start of this chapter. The life you don't want is the result of actions you can change; the income you don't have is the result of a perspective, beliefs and skills you can change. In both cases the frame comes first, and the plan follows from it.

That plan is the subject of the next chapter. The anti-vision and the vision set the frame; a big goal throws an anchor into the unknown; and the distance between the goal and today has to be broken down into a hierarchy of goals, a path and daily levers, under constraints, and anchored in the most urgent problem. Chapter 8 takes up that breakdown.

### Exercises

1. **Write and test your anti-vision.** Using the July 2023 five-step process (observe, reflect, list, project ten years, transmute) and the December 2025 "average Tuesday" questions, write an anti-vision of at most one page. Then apply Koe's own test: does reading it make you uncomfortable? If it does not, diagnose why, using the 2026 explanation that "the self doing the exercise is the self being evaluated." Which items did you soften, and which "anchors" (real people on the same trajectory) would make the projection harder to rationalize?

2. **The asymmetry of knowing.** Koe claims that it is easier to know what you don't want, from experience and observation, than what you want, from imagination. Construct one case from your own life in which this held, and one in which it failed (for example, a time you knew clearly what you wanted, or a time when rejecting something led you to its equally unsatisfying opposite). What does the failure case suggest about the limits of deriving a vision "by inversion"?

3. **Map the frame.** Write your frame of reference in Koe's 2025 terms: one sentence for your vision (commitment to excellence) and one for your anti-vision (moving away from mediocrity). Then take three decisions you made in the past week and classify each one: inside the frame, outside the frame and brought back, or outside the frame and not brought back (Koe's definition of distraction). Compare this classification with one based on his 2023 definition of distraction as "every single thing that does not align with your purpose or vision." Where do the two definitions disagree, and which is more useful to you?

4. **Adjudicate a contradiction.** Section 7.4 sets out three positions: you may not know what you want (2022–2023), you already know and are evading the work (2024), and you can never know because it lies in the future (2025). Choose the position you find most convincing and defend it against the other two. Then identify a person, or a moment in your own life, for whom the position you rejected would be the right advice.

5. **Design a chain of exposure.** Choose a soft goal ("I think I want this") in some domain of your life. Following Koe's examples (learning a skill for a job that reveals the possibility of a business; web design that leads to writing), sketch three possible links in a chain of exposure that pursuing this goal could open. For each link, name what you would have to do to be exposed to it, and what signal (excitement, emptiness, "brain lighting up") would tell you to go deeper or to pivot.

6. **Calibrate a big goal.** Write a "10 goals 10 years" list in Koe's sense (goals that make you physically uncomfortable). Then classify each goal with the easy/difficult/impossible typology, and for one goal you consider impossible, identify the "ideas that must come before" it (the plow before the tractor). Finally, choose this year's goal and test it against the "level two aims for level four, not level 100" heuristic. Does the big-goals principle conflict with the calibration principle in your case, or does the separation between direction and action resolve it?

7. **Question the income claim.** Koe states that in entrepreneurship "your income is only limited by your perspective, beliefs, skill set and real world practice." List the factors this formulation leaves out. Then argue the strongest case for reading the statement as useful even if it is not literally true, and identify the conditions under which adopting it could be harmful (for example, for someone without financial runway, in light of Koe's own warning "obviously don't do anything stupid").

8. **Compress and commit.** At the end of a day spent on exercises 1 and 3, answer the evening questions of Koe's December 2025 protocol: what feels most true about why you have been stuck, and what is the "actual enemy," an internal pattern or belief rather than circumstances or other people? Write one sentence for what you refuse to let your life become and one for what you are building toward. Then write a post-mortem in the 2026 sense: assume the anti-vision has come true, and list the mistakes that would explain it. Which mistake is already happening?
