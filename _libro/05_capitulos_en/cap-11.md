## Chapter 11 — Dopamine, Consumption and Environment

Part IV ended with a design: a vision broken into goals and projects, held together as a game whose rules you write and whose difficulty sits at the edge of your skill. Part V asks what it takes to play that game on an ordinary day, and the first obstacle is not laziness or lack of time. It is that the day is already full of other games, built by people who are very good at capturing attention, and the reward system that would normally pull you toward your own goals is being pulled toward theirs. This chapter explains how Dan Koe understands that reward system and what he proposes to do about it.

The chapter follows the order in which the argument depends on itself. Section 11.1 presents dopamine as Koe uses the term, borrowing from Daniel Z. Lieberman: not the molecule of pleasure but "the molecule of more," a signal of what matters for the goals your mind is running, which is why it can be read as a map of those goals and why the algorithm can hijack it. Section 11.2 draws the central distinction of the chapter, between *cheap* dopamine (novelty received without effort) and *meaningful* or *earned* dopamine (novelty created through effort), and extends it into a distinction between pleasure and enjoyment and a set of definitions of happiness. Section 11.3 turns from the reward to its inputs: if the mind is programmed by what it consumes, the choice of consumption is the choice of identity, and Koe offers methods for curating the information environment. Section 11.4 gathers the radical tools, the protocols for resetting a mind that has already been flooded: disappearing, monk mode and its opposite, war mode, the 30-day dopamine detox, the theory of "true boredom" that explains why detoxes work, and a seven-day creative reset. Section 11.5 closes on the smallest scale, the three seconds between an impulse and the hand reaching for the phone, and on the one substance (alcohol) on which Koe and his guests disagree openly. The chapter requires the goal-centered model of the mind from Parts III and IV, because the whole analysis rests on one claim stated in Chapter 1 and now given its mechanism: without a goal of your own, something else fills the place where the goal should be. It prepares Chapter 12, where the attention recovered here is organized into focused work.

### 11.1 The Molecule of More

#### Not the pleasure molecule

Koe's account of dopamine begins with a correction of the popular one. In a 2024 video on what he calls the "cheap dopamine epidemic," he quotes Daniel Z. Lieberman, co-author of *The Molecule of More*: "in a broad sense saying something is important is another way of saying it's linked to dopamine. Why? Because among the many things it does, dopamine is an early warning system for the appearance of anything that can help us survive." Then he states the thesis he takes from the book: "dopamine isn't the pleasure molecule, dopamine is the molecule of more" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24).

The correction matters because of what follows from it. If dopamine were the chemical of pleasure, the problem of distraction would be a problem of enjoying things too much, and the solution would be to enjoy less. If dopamine is the chemical of *more*, of wanting, anticipating and pursuing, then the problem is a misdirected pursuit: a mechanism designed to flag what helps you survive is flagging things that do not. Koe's own addition to Lieberman's line is diagnostic. Most people, he says, do not understand dopamine, and so "it tends to control their behavior," and with it their life. They cannot become aware of the thought processes that occur when "cheap dopamine is spurted into their brain," and they cannot reverse them because they cannot see another way. Ignorance of the mechanism is what gives the mechanism control. This is a specific case of a principle that runs through the whole book, from the Matrix of Chapter 1 to the awareness of Chapter 2: what operates outside awareness governs you; what is seen can be steered.

**Complementary context:** *The Molecule of More* (2018) is a popular-science book by the psychiatrist Daniel Z. Lieberman and the physicist Michael E. Long. Its central argument is that dopamine is associated less with consuming rewards than with anticipating and pursuing them, and that it governs our orientation toward things that are not yet ours. Koe names Lieberman in the 2024 video; in 2022 he cites the book without naming its authors.

#### The up world and the down world

The book gives Koe a second distinction, which he first presents in 2022 by reading from it. There is a "down world," managed by a handful of neurotransmitters that let you experience satisfaction and enjoy whatever you have "in the here and now." And there is the "world of up": when you turn your attention to it, the brain relies on a single molecule, dopamine, which motivates you to move beyond what is at your fingertips, to "pursue, to control and to possess the world beyond your immediate grasp." That world includes physical things, but also things you cannot see, such as knowledge, love and power (**Source:** `Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md`, 2022-10-09).

From this Koe draws a claim about value: "dopamine plays a huge role in how we perceive value, or at least what catches our attention first." The hedge is worth noticing. He does not say that dopamine *determines* value; he says it governs what catches attention first, which is a weaker and more defensible claim. Chapter 1 showed the other half of this argument, in which value is repeated into objects by conditioning rather than discovered in them; here the neurochemistry supplies the reason the repetition works: what is far away and desirable is what the "up" system flags.

Later in the same video he names some of the "down" chemicals that produce appreciation: oxytocin, serotonin and, as the transcript renders it, "neuropanephrine," adding that "there's a bunch of different ones that play a role in the down network." The transcribed word is ambiguous; it is most likely norepinephrine, but the transcript does not allow certainty. The list grows over the years: in 2023 he names serotonin, oxytocin, endorphins and endocannabinoids, and in 2024 oxytocin, noradrenaline, adrenaline, serotonin, endorphins and endocannabinoids. The framework does not change; it is filled in.

What Koe does with the down network is interpretive rather than chemical. He equates it with what spiritual teachers mean when they talk about the present moment and appreciation in the now, and he calls those neurotransmitters "much more sustainable" for developing appreciation for what you have rather than what you lack. The equation of a neurochemical system with a spiritual teaching is his own bridge, offered as an interpretation; it lets him say that the contemplative traditions and the neuroscience of reward are describing the same split between wanting and having. The bridge will matter in section 11.4, where boredom and deprivation are presented as ways of restoring the capacity to enjoy what is near.

**Complementary context:** Lieberman and Long call the "down" chemicals the "Here and Now" neurotransmitters and describe the up/down division spatially, through the distinction between peripersonal space (what is within reach) and extrapersonal space (what is beyond it). Koe's lists of chemicals are his own summaries, not quotations of the book's list, but the vocabulary is not foreign to him: in the 2024 video in which he names Lieberman, he himself speaks of the "down chemicals" or "here and nows," of "peripersonal space" (the things you can grab, possess and control) and of everything that lies in "your extrapersonal space," from shop windows to "this constant feed called social media" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24). The terms are the book's; their adoption and application to the feed are Koe's adaptation. Chapter 39 (section 39.4) develops that passage.

#### The psychology of desire: you, the object and the gap

In the 2022 video Koe turns the book's idea into a graphic of his own, which he calls the psychology of desire. The image is shown on screen and can be reconstructed only partly from the transcript. A black or outlined circle represents *you*. A white circle represents a valuable object, or something you perceive as valuable. A line between them shows how far away that object is from you (**Source:** `Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md`, 2022-10-09).

The mechanism is stated plainly: "the further something away is from you, the more of a dopamine response there is," and the more of that response you can tap into. When the object is close, and especially once you have taken control of it or possess it, "it loses its shine." The dopamine response drains quickly, and if you want to keep valuing what you now have, you have to switch to the "down" chemicals that produce appreciation.

| Element of the graphic | What it represents | What it does to dopamine |
|---|---|---|
| Outlined circle | You, where you are now | Reference point |
| White circle | An object perceived as valuable | Target of desire |
| Line between them (the gap) | Distance between you and the object | The longer the line, the stronger the dopamine response |
| Possession (the line closes) | The object becomes yours | Dopamine drains; appreciation now depends on the "down" chemicals |

Koe draws two consequences from the gap. The first is negative: a huge gap "is what causes overwhelm." His example is someone driving a Toyota Corolla now who desires a Ferrari, and the wider version is the social media image of things "way, way, way" beyond you. The second is positive: the same dopamine can be used "for fuel." The gap is double-edged, and the difference between the two edges, as the rest of the chapter will show, is whether there is a plan or path across it. He insists on the moral of the graphic: "dopamine is not bad, it's only abused in this technological world."

The term **the Gap** recurs across the corpus with related senses. In this passage it is the distance between you and a valued object; elsewhere it names the distance between where you are and where others are, which produces overwhelm when you lack clarity. The common thread is that a gap without a path is anxiety and a gap with a path is motivation.

#### The honeymoon phase

A 2023 version of the same idea describes what happens after the gap closes. Motivation "can be illustrated by the behavior molecule dopamine." When something is far away we desire it more; when we acquire it (a nice house, a car, a watch, an attractive person, an internet course, a new book) we feel good for a while, then it becomes normal and we no longer desire it. Koe calls this the **honeymoon phase**: "maybe a two week to four week period" in which you are not really in love with the thing but "infatuated" with it (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). The figure of two to four weeks is his estimate, not a measured constant; its function is to make the normalization concrete.

Two features of this passage keep it from becoming a lament about desire. The list of objects deliberately mixes material things with a person, a course and a book, so the mechanism is not about materialism but about anything acquired. And Koe insists that dopamine is "one of the most important neurotransmitters to understand," "the drive behind your dreams, and that should be utilized." The honeymoon phase is not an argument against wanting things; it is a description of what wanting is, and therefore a warning against expecting the acquisition itself to deliver lasting satisfaction.

The 2024 video applies the honeymoon phase to relationships, which shows the cost of not understanding it: "you possess a partner thanks to dopamine-fueled motivation, but you fail to reprogram your habits to appreciate and savor that relationship after the honeymoon phase" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24). The verb is "reprogram." Appreciation is not a feeling that arrives by itself after possession; it is a habit that has to be built, which is the practical meaning of switching to the "down" chemicals.

#### The berry bush: novelty, anticipation and normalization

To explain why dopamine works this way, Koe tells an evolutionary story that appears in several chapters of this book in different roles (in Chapter 3 it explains how ideas survive and reproduce). The version in the 2022 dopamine video is the most complete as an account of desire (**Source:** `Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md`, 2022-10-09).

An ancestor walks past a bush every day. They are conditioned to see it as ordinary and irrelevant to survival. One day it has berries. That registers in awareness; they get a hit of dopamine because it is new, and it produces excitement and the feeling, in Koe's words, "I need to actually know this, I need to understand this and I need to act on this." We evolved, he says, "to use dopamine for novelty, anticipation and other things." Then comes the step most popular accounts leave out. Once the survival-relevant information is understood and the bushes keep producing berries, it "would become normal to us," and we switch to a new mode. "It's not like we got addicted to seeing new berries grow on bushes."

The story establishes a normal cycle: novelty, excitement, learning, action, normalization. Dopamine flags the new thing so that you learn it, and once it is learned it stops being flagged. The problem of the present is that the cycle never reaches its last stage. Novelty crosses our screens every day: new perspectives, information to act on, money-making opportunities, people to meet, people flexing lifestyles we desire. Each is flagged, none is learned or acted on, and the next arrives before normalization can occur. The lifestyle content is the worst case, because it is so far away (the long line of the graphic) that it can send us into what Koe calls a "downward spiral of overwhelm and anxiety," since we have no plan or path to bridge the gap.

The story is an explanatory metaphor, not a piece of evolutionary research, and Koe offers it as such. Its value is that it locates the problem precisely: not in dopamine, and not in novelty, but in a volume and distance of novelty that the mechanism was never built to process.

#### Signal, noise and the salience network

A 2022 video gives the same diagnosis in the vocabulary of information. "We have a reward mechanism in our brains," dopamine, "to help us separate signal from noise." The unfortunate thing is that "modern society has set us up to drown in this dopamine and make it difficult to separate that signal from noise." Not everything is signal: not every piece of entertainment that crosses your screen is supposed to produce a dopamine spike. And even then, "we aren't built for that level of exposure," for that quantity of stimuli passing our eyes every day (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). The consequence he draws in that video concerns curiosity: dopamine is the natural guide toward genuine interest, and overstimulation blurs the guide. When every stimulus is flagged, nothing stands out, including the things you would actually care about.

In 2025 Koe compresses the point into a single sentence that he uses as the foundation of his detox protocol: "Your brain operates on a salience network. Meaning whatever provides the most dopamine becomes the most important" (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26). If importance is assigned by dopamine, an environment full of high-dopamine stimuli reorders your priorities without asking you. A feed does not merely distract you from what is important; it changes what *feels* important. That is why, for Koe, a detox works where willpower fails: removing the high-dopamine stimuli lets the ranking of importance return to its normal order.

**Complementary context:** in neuroscience, the "salience network" is a large-scale brain network (centered on the anterior insula and the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex) involved in detecting which internal and external stimuli are behaviorally relevant and in switching between other networks. Dopamine is one of several systems implicated in salience. Koe's gloss, "whatever provides the most dopamine becomes the most important," is a simplification he uses as a working model, not a technical definition.

#### Drowning in motivation

The flooding of the system has a name in a 2023 passage: most people **drown in motivation**. They see many things out of their reach every day, especially on social media, and over and over they waste "this precious resource that could be used to act on their dreams." And even when they do acquire those dreams (the fancy car, the watch), they do not know how to swap their focus to sustain the enjoyment of the thing (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25).

The phrase is a paradox on purpose. The common complaint is a *lack* of motivation, and the common remedy is to consume more of it: videos, quotes, images of the life you want. Koe's diagnosis is the reverse. Motivation, understood as dopamine directed at something out of reach, is plentiful to the point of excess; what is scarce is its investment in action. Each image of a distant lifestyle spends some of the resource without moving you an inch toward the object. And the double failure he describes (wasting dopamine on what you cannot reach, then failing to enjoy what you do reach) shows the two halves of the up/down model breaking at once.

The 2024 version names the cause at a societal level: "we are so dopamine overloaded by the abundance of information forged by game-theoretic principles that we've lost touch with reality" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24). **Dopamine overloaded** is his term for the state; "game-theoretic principles" refers to the competitive logic by which platforms and producers design information to win attention against each other, which Chapter 1 (section 1.5) traced through the history of processed food and processed content. "Lost touch with reality" is the strongest claim in the passage, and the example he attaches to it, the relationship that fails after the honeymoon phase, shows what he means: reality is what remains after the novelty wears off, and a mind trained only on novelty cannot live there.

#### Dopamine signals what matters to your goal

Up to this point dopamine has been described as a response to distance and novelty. The second half of this section adds the element that connects it to the rest of the book: dopamine responds to *relevance to a goal*. This is the bridge between the neurochemistry and the cybernetic mind of Chapter 6.

The 2023 formulation is the most systematic. "Your mind automatically accepts and rejects information that aids in the achievement of the goals that are programmed into your head." If your goal is to get a job, dopamine signals the importance of opportunities and information leading there: "what you highlight in books will reflect that goal," as will what you take from conversations and what you engage with on social media. The mind runs on "a plethora of goals," but take one for simplicity (**Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17).

Then Koe runs the same mechanism through three cases, and the comparison is the core of the argument.

| Your goal | What scrolling memes feeds back | Result |
|---|---|---|
| None beyond the current job | Nothing contradicts it | You stay "stuck scrolling memes all day" |
| A promotion or a business, with energy already invested | Lack of progress becomes visible | Scrolling "will feel painful"; negative feedback; you eventually stop |
| Staying in a comfortable job | Confirmation of the goal | Scrolling does not hurt; positive feedback reinforces the goal |

The table shows why the same behavior can be harmless, harmful or self-correcting depending on the goal it is measured against. Scrolling is not intrinsically negative feedback; it becomes negative feedback only when there is a goal it visibly damages and when you have invested enough energy in that goal for the damage to hurt. This is the mechanism behind a claim made in Chapter 6 (section 6.6): without a goal there is no problem, and a bad habit exists only relative to a goal it obstructs.

A 2024 version adds the hunter and the algorithm in a single sentence: "If you want to get a job, dopamine will signal the importance of information and opportunities that help you get that job; your book highlights will reflect that goal; how you approach conversations with anyone will reflect that goal; and the algorithm will help deepen the roots of that identity by showing you more of that information, for better or worse." And then: "Like hunters that notice new berries on a bush, dopamine signals information that is important for the survival of your **top-of-mind goal**, so you are more likely to remember and utilize that information" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). If you want to quit your job, dopamine does the same for that goal. The berry bush is no longer about food; it is about any goal at the top of the mind, whose "survival" the system protects in the same way it once protected the body.

#### The algorithm programs the identity your goal implies

The phrase "for better or worse" opens the darker side of the mechanism, which a 2024 passage develops. "One thing about the algorithm is it straight up programs your identity into whatever your goal is": you notice the things that help you achieve your goals, and the platform shows you more of what you notice. "If you don't have a goal, that means someone assigned you a goal." And "importance is a signal that that is useful for survival towards your goal" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30).

The argument links three ideas that appeared separately in earlier chapters. From Chapter 1 (section 1.3) comes the thesis that you always pursue a goal and that, if you do not choose it, it is assigned. From Chapter 6 comes the goal as a lens on perception. From this section comes dopamine as the signal of goal-relevance. Combined, they produce a feedback loop: an assigned goal you are not aware of decides what feels important; what feels important is what you dwell on; what you dwell on is what the algorithm shows you more of; and what you are shown deepens the identity that the assigned goal implies. Koe's example is deliberately harsh: "If your goal is to unconsciously troll on social media and just be a subhuman, then the content that you're exposed to is going to condition that identity, because you're going to spend more time on that content, the algorithm is going to show you more, and you're going to end up in this echo chamber of content with people that you probably don't want to end up like."

The point of the example is not the insult but the mechanism. The algorithm is neutral in the sense that it amplifies whatever goal is running; it is not neutral in effect, because most people are running goals they did not choose. The same loop that would deepen a chosen identity deepens an assigned one, and it does so "for better or worse."

#### Reading your hidden goals through excitement

If dopamine signals goal-relevance, the signal can be read backward. A 2025 passage turns the mechanism into a method of self-diagnosis. Your goals are a set of conscious and unconscious goals, many of them programmed in childhood (the model of Chapter 3, section 3.2). You can notice them by observing what sticks out or sparks dopamine, because "dopamine signals what's important for your survival in the future." A hint of excitement or a novel insight in a book or on social media "is an arrow pointing to the goal that your mind is operating on" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12).

The method is useful precisely because the goals in question are hidden. You cannot ask yourself directly what your unconscious goals are; you can, however, notice what excites you and ask what goal that excitement would serve. If the posts that light you up are about other people's failures, the goal the mind is running may be protection of a self-image; if they are about building something, it may be the goal you would consciously choose. The arrow is diagnostic, not prescriptive: it tells you what is running, not what should be.

An earlier passage, from 2022, adds the condition that makes the method difficult. If you pursue what other people want for you in what you consume, "you may get stuck in a trap of moving in a negative life direction." Pursuing what you truly want "takes practice," specifically the practice of noticing "the feeling of excitement," or "raised levels of dopamine" in your head, becoming conscious of it and of what causes it (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). Noticing excitement sounds trivial, but in a flooded environment it is a skill, because the feeling is constantly triggered by stimuli that have nothing to do with you. The connection with the signal-and-noise passage is direct: genuine curiosity is a signal, and the practice of noticing it is the practice of separating it from noise. This is also the first appearance in the chapter of a theme that will become central in sections 11.3 and 11.4: the solution begins with reducing the noise so the signal can be heard.

### 11.2 Cheap Dopamine and Meaningful Dopamine

#### Receiving novelty versus creating it

If dopamine is the molecule of more, the question is not whether to have it but where to get it. Koe's answer is the distinction that organizes this chapter, and its clearest formulation comes from a 2022 video on breaking out of the Matrix: "**Cheap dopamine** comes from receiving novelty without struggle; **good dopamine** comes from creating novelty through struggle" (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20).

Both terms are Koe's coinages, and the definition turns on two variables: whether the novelty is *received* or *created*, and whether it comes *without* or *through* struggle. The berry bush of section 11.1 helps to see why these are the relevant variables. For the ancestor, the berries were received, but acting on them required work, and the dopamine served to start that work. On a feed, novelty is received and nothing is required: the signal fires and the cycle ends there. Cheap dopamine is the mechanism of discovery detached from any action the discovery should provoke.

In the same video Koe describes a graphic, again only partly reconstructable from the transcript. On one end is "you" with **instant desire**: "bing, bing, bing," dopamine immediately. On the other is "you" with **intrinsic desire** and "the highs and lows that come with it." The highs and lows are not a defect of the second path; they are necessary, "because that's where good dopamine comes from." It is "not instant," not available "at the click of a phone." It comes from breaking free of the Matrix through exposure to new experiences on a consistent basis, and he gives the sequence in three steps: set your own goals, educate yourself on how to reach them, and "execute at the edge of your fear." He also states the condition under which good dopamine appears: "matching learning with execution is how you create good dopamine." Learning alone is another form of receiving; it is the match between learning and doing that creates something new.

The passage closes with a link to the theme of cycles from Chapter 1. Cheap dopamine "is part of your cycle": how often and how compulsively do you check your phone? Are you even conscious of pulling it out and scrolling? The question anticipates section 11.5. A cheap-dopamine habit is not a series of choices but a loop that runs below awareness, and the first sign of escaping it is noticing that the hand has already reached for the phone.

#### One concept, four names

Koe's name for the positive pole changes over the years, while the negative pole keeps its name. The sequence is worth setting out, because each new name adds a facet rather than replacing the old one.

| Period | Name of the positive pole | Facet it emphasizes | Source example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | Good dopamine | Novelty created through struggle; the unknown | `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md` |
| 2023 (April) | Meaningful dopamine | Progress, effort and visible results | `Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md` |
| 2023 (July) | Expensive dopamine | Cost in effort; a ratio to be improved | `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md` |
| 2024 | Earned dopamine | Long-term gratification; leads to centropy | `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md` |

The corpus records no change of position across these names, and Koe gives no reason for the renaming. They can be treated as one concept seen from four angles: it is *good* because it serves your growth, *meaningful* because it is attached to something you care about, *expensive* because it costs effort, and *earned* because the reward follows the work rather than preceding it.

The 2023 "meaningful" formulation uses the gym as its paradigm case. "The gym is literally the opposite of cheap dopamine; it is **meaningful dopamine** that you see through progress, effort, hard work, and you get results because of it" (**Source:** `Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md`, 2023-04-20). Scrolling, Netflix and video games come without effort; the gym's reward arrives through effort and is visible as progress. The example is well chosen because the gym makes the delay explicit: no single session produces visible change, and the reward comes from watching an accumulation.

The "expensive" formulation of July 2023 turns the distinction into a measurable aim. The goal is to "increase the ratio of expensive to cheap dopamine sources in our life," along with three parallel ratios: signal to noise, significance to insignificance, meaningful to meaningless (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11). The ratio framing has two consequences that keep the idea from becoming asceticism. First, cheap sources do not have to reach zero; what matters is their proportion. Second, the aim is not to reduce dopamine but to relocate it. Dopamine makes you feel good, "and that's not bad, it's just a matter of where you actually get it." In the same passage he calls dopamine "the molecule of human behavior," the reason we act at all, and draws the conclusion: not scrolling and not soaking in cheap dopamine "doesn't mean you shouldn't leverage this neurotransmitter to achieve success." The term **molecule of human behavior** (also "the behavior molecule") is his, and it stresses the same point as Lieberman's phrase: dopamine is about doing, and the question is what it gets you to do.

#### Entropy and centropy

The 2024 renaming connects the distinction to the model of Chapter 5. "Dopamine isn't a bad chemical; it is the driving force behind success and failure. The difference is long-term versus short-term gratification," a difference "not to be taken lightly." Then the coinage: "short-term gratification, or cheap dopamine, leads to entropy; long-term gratification, or **earned dopamine**, leads to **centropy**" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24). He gives "syntropy" as a synonym in parentheses.

Both poles are defined in psychological terms. Entropy, "for what we're concerned with here," is "the gradual decline into chaos, uncertainty and disorder": anxiety, overwhelm, "that inescapable bubble of negative thoughts," which worsens "because we can't make sense of the world anymore, usually due to dwelling on the surface for too long." Centropy, "reversing entropy," is "the upward battle we are all facing as humans": "maintaining, creating and improving order and certainty." His examples are how challenge leads to fulfillment, flow and a sense of purpose or control over your life.

The definition adds a causal claim to the old distinction. Cheap dopamine was earlier described by its source (received novelty); now it is described by its effect (disorder in consciousness). The phrase "dwelling on the surface" is the link between the two. Received novelty keeps the mind on surfaces: each item is flagged and abandoned before it is understood, so nothing is integrated, and a mind full of unintegrated fragments "can't make sense of the world." That is psychic entropy in the sense of Chapter 5 (section 5.2), now traced to a specific input. Earned dopamine, conversely, comes attached to a structure (a goal, a challenge, a skill), and structure is what order in consciousness means. The term **centropy** therefore names both the direction of earned dopamine and the state it produces.

**Complementary context:** "syntropy" has been used since the mid-twentieth century, by the mathematician Luigi Fantappiè and later by the biochemist Albert Szent-Györgyi among others, for a tendency toward order opposite to entropy. "Centropy" is a rarer variant. Koe does not cite these earlier uses; in his work both words name the psychological reversal of psychic entropy described in Chapter 5.

#### Meaning lives at the edge of the known

A 2023 video on employment adds a spatial picture to the distinction, and it connects this chapter to the flow model of Chapter 10. Koe describes an on-screen graphic whose details cannot be fully reconstructed. At the top is "the unknown, which is where you get good dopamine, which is uncomfortable." At the bottom is "the known, which is just a cycle of cheap dopamine and comfort." And "in the middle is where meaning and fulfillment is found; that's at the edge of the known," or the edge of the unknown (**Source:** `How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md`, 2023-08-01).

Two lines cross the graphic. A white line represents employment. "You get that initial good dopamine because you're making progress," but the work becomes repetitive, "you probably won't get promoted," and "you quickly go into the known and just a cycle of cheap dopamine; you become a monkey in a cubicle." A dotted line represents entrepreneurship: "you go up and down and up and down, and you have much more control over the balance in your life, the good dopamine to cheap dopamine, and the skill required to stay at the edge and find meaning and fulfillment."

| Region of the graphic | Kind of dopamine | Felt quality |
|---|---|---|
| The unknown (top) | Good dopamine | Uncomfortable |
| The edge (middle) | A balance of both | Meaning and fulfillment |
| The known (bottom) | Cheap dopamine | Comfort, repetition |

The graphic does three things at once. It identifies the source of cheap dopamine as the known, not as technology: the phone is merely the most efficient delivery mechanism for comfort. It places meaning not in the unknown itself but at the boundary, which is the same location Chapter 10 gave to flow, where challenge slightly exceeds skill. And it explains the author's critique of employment in Chapter 2 (sections 2.3 and 2.4) in dopaminergic terms: the job initially offers progress and then flattens into a known in which the only remaining novelty is cheap. The **monkey in a cubicle** is Koe's recurring image for a mind wired to hunt and placed in a box; here the box is the known, and scrolling is what the monkey does inside it. The graphic does not say entrepreneurship guarantees meaning; it says it gives "more control over the balance," and requires a skill (staying at the edge) that employment does not demand.

#### Living in accordance with nature

A 2022 passage frames the same contrast in the vocabulary of nature. Dive into the unknown, develop your mind, collect dots to connect, and you get the benefits of "living in accordance with nature": psychological benefits and "good dopamine." It is a deeper satisfaction than "pigging out on nachos" and watching Netflix all night. Then a nuance Koe repeats in many forms: "I'm not saying these things are inherently bad, but when it becomes your entire unconscious life, yeah, it's pretty bad" (**Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`, 2022-06-25).

The two qualifiers in that sentence, "entire" and "unconscious," carry the argument. Nachos and Netflix are not the problem; the problem is when they fill the whole life and when they are not chosen. The same structure reappears in Chapter 6 (section 6.6), where Koe accepts drinking, Netflix or video games as a conscious "forced low," and it will reappear in section 11.4 in his refusal to moralize about the detox. "Living in accordance with nature" is not developed into a theory here; the phrase seems to refer to the evolved drive described in section 11.1 (to explore, discover and act), which the unknown satisfies and the couch does not.

#### Why chasing highs produces lows

A 2022 argument explains why cheap dopamine does not merely fail to help but actively produces bad periods. It is a chain, and the steps matter (**Source:** `Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02).

1. The mind is "a complex set of systems."
2. Systems make decisions through positive and negative feedback, which can be conditioned over time; "that's what makes a good system."
3. If you experience a high high and register it as positive feedback, you will want to reach that high again.
4. But according to the harmonic oscillator, "or just the inevitable waves," there is a counter-force.
5. So if you pursue those high highs and "consistently want, let's say, that cheap dopamine or those distractions," and you wonder why you keep going into low phases of life, "that is why," especially when you are not mindful of it.

The argument borrows a physical image (the oscillator, which swings back by as much as it is pushed) to make a psychological claim: the height of the peak you chase determines the depth of the trough that follows. Koe adds a qualification that keeps the argument from being fatalistic. If these phases do not affect you, it is because you are "separating an entity, emotion, from your being," becoming aware of it and navigating it without negative impact. The waves are inevitable; their power over you is not. From here, he says, opens the topic of rewiring, reprogramming or reconditioning the mind "for more beneficial outcomes," especially to navigate what we perceive as negative aspects of life, "which are in fact inevitable aspects of life."

**Complementary context:** a harmonic oscillator is a physical system (a spring or a pendulum, for example) that, when displaced from equilibrium, experiences a restoring force proportional to the displacement and so oscillates around its resting point. Koe uses it as a metaphor for emotional rhythm, not as a quantitative model.

#### The plateau of mastery and the escape into cheap pleasure

A 2025 passage locates the same trap on the path of skill. "To achieve mastery, you live in a plateau for most of your life, and most people try to escape that plateau by numbing their mind with cheap pleasures or cheap stimulation or food or scrolling on their phone, and then they get trapped there." They never continue to make slow upward progress "because they want to achieve rapid progress really fast" (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16).

The plateau is where the dopamine of visible progress runs out. A skill improves quickly at the beginning, then slowly for long stretches in which practice produces no felt reward. This is exactly where earned dopamine is hardest to get and cheap dopamine most tempting, and Koe's claim is that the escape is self-sealing: cheap stimulation makes the slow progress of the plateau feel even slower by comparison. The idea is developed as a theory of skill in Chapter 15 (the path of mastery); here it shows that the cheap/earned distinction is not only about leisure but about the most ordinary stretches of real work.

#### Craving the meaningful event

The distinction would be bleak if cheap dopamine always won by default. A 2024 passage describes how the balance shifts with practice. When a meaningful event happens (you read, learn or build something and excitement hits), "you literally feel the dopamine kicking into your brain." Once you do this long enough, "you start to crave that more than you crave the instant pleasure." His practical advice: when it happens, write it down, so you can use it in the project you are building (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11).

The passage is a claim about conditioning, consistent with the feedback model of the oscillator argument: what you repeatedly register as positive feedback becomes what you want. Cheap dopamine wins at the start because its reward is immediate and the meaningful reward is not yet familiar; as meaningful events accumulate, they become the preferred source. The instruction to write the moment down ties the felt reward to the project (Chapter 9), so that the excitement is not merely enjoyed but used.

#### Deconstructing desire

Section 11.1 showed that the further away an object is, the stronger the dopamine. A 2023 argument shows what this does when the objects are other people's lives on social media, and it describes a double dose of cheap dopamine (**Source:** `How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md`, 2023-01-12).

1. On social media you see a mansion or a car that you think you want. But you don't really want them; you want "the thing that lies under that," which those things hint at. You cannot see beyond the surface.
2. They are readily available to look at, without work, and looking feels good: cheap dopamine.
3. It keeps you in surface-level living, where you do not think.
4. You then say "lucky for him, he has the mansion, the fancy car, the hot wife," when in fact it took most of those people their entire lives (with special cases). That is a **quick-fix mindset**.
5. The excuse ("they're just rich, lucky, born with money") is "just another form of dopamine that keeps you enslaved in this little mindless bubble of not doing what you want."

The fifth step is the original one. The excuse is not just a false belief; it is itself rewarding, because it relieves you of the gap. If the other person is lucky, there is nothing to learn and nothing to do, and the discomfort of distance dissolves into a small pleasure of explanation. The first dose is looking; the second is excusing. Both keep you on the surface.

A 2022 passage gives the corresponding exercise. Start being conscious of how you perceive certain things, and "take the time to try to deconstruct or dissect them." If you get pulled into the cycle of short dopamine hits (a fancy car, a mansion, a body with six-pack abs: "damn, I want that"), you have two options. Either you bridge the gap through self-education about what it actually takes to get there, which Koe calls "another form of chasing that dopamine and learning," or you stay in the narrow, closed-minded thinking of "I want this but I'll never have it" or "I want this but I'm not going to do anything because I don't know how to get there." The steps he gives are simple: see what you want, have a desired outcome, educate yourself on how to bridge the gap or gain clarity, then start acting toward it (**Source:** `Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md`, 2022-10-09).

The exercise does not ask you to stop desiring. It converts the dopamine of the image into the dopamine of learning, which is the conversion from cheap to earned described at the beginning of this section. Its first step, deconstructing what you actually want, also connects to a 2022 observation about the people who post such images. Those flexing "false lifestyles," Koe says, sell what you have been conditioned to want: "you don't want the fancy car, you don't want the mansion, you want the experience," because you have been made aware of the feelings that stem from it (feeling good and energetic, the lust of mansion parties, the adrenaline of driving the cars). His advice: don't follow those people; follow those putting out good information (**Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`, 2022-06-25). **False lifestyles** is his term for these displays. The deconstruction reveals that the desired thing is a feeling, and feelings can often be reached by other routes than the object, or can turn out to be different from what the image promised.

A later passage nuances this line. In late 2023 Koe calls "money doesn't buy happiness" a half-truth: material goods have utility when they are not superficial pursuits, and he uses the example of an expensive watch. The two positions are compatible. The 2023 critique targets the image consumed as a substitute for action; the later nuance rehabilitates the object pursued as a vehicle of experience and learning.

#### Enjoyment versus pleasure

Koe's longest-running development of the cheap/earned distinction is a parallel distinction between **pleasure** and **enjoyment**. He uses two common words with a specific sense, and the sense becomes sharper over three formulations.

The first, from 2022, is about depth. Pleasure is "that shallow, surface-level dopamine pleasure hit." Enjoyment comes from diving deep, understanding "the depth and the consciousness beyond" the thing and how it affects every aspect of your body; you are "expanding your consciousness." His example is food: the healthy person's enjoyment of an apple is of the deeper kind, while a cookie offers the pleasure hit (**Source:** `Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02). The apple is not simply less tasty and more virtuous; the claim is that attention to it, and to what it does in the body, makes its enjoyment richer than the cookie's quick reward.

The second, from 2023, is about attention as an economic resource, and it is the most systematic version (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25): "Enjoyment comes from investing attention; pleasure comes from spending attention. Investments are long-term, purchases are short-term. Enjoyment is process focused; pleasure is outcome focused. Enjoyment is progress toward a long-term goal; pleasure is achieving a goal without effort."

| | Enjoyment | Pleasure |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Invested | Spent |
| Time horizon | Long-term (an investment) | Short-term (a purchase) |
| Focus | Process | Outcome |
| Relation to goals | Progress toward a long-term goal | Achieving a goal without effort |
| Depth (2022) | Depth, expanded consciousness | Shallow, surface-level hit |
| Dopamine (this chapter) | Earned / meaningful | Cheap |

From this he derives a general statement of what people want: "Our goal is to maximize the enjoyment in our everyday life; that's all anyone is trying to do." Most people fail not because they want the wrong thing but because they do not zoom out to gain a perspective "that is **distraction proof**" while pursuing goals; they are narrowly focused on what brings pleasure here and now. The zoom is the one described in Chapter 6 (section 6.3): from close up, pleasure and enjoyment look alike, and only from a wider view does their difference in direction appear.

The third formulation answers an objection that Koe states in the voice of his audience, almost word for word in 2023 and 2024: "But Dan, I want to enjoy myself and do what I want." His reply has three steps (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19; `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). First, being distracted and engaging in what you think is leisure keeps entropy high, so you are anxious and overwhelmed all the time; this is not enjoyment by the definition just given. Second, "human psychology has been mapped over the course of evolution to show that humans have an innate drive to grow, expand, transcend and create." Therefore "**enjoyment is found in progress**." In the 2024 version he adds that flow points to more than neurochemicals: "it points toward the divine." Third, "doing what you want is often the ego's way of ending the train of thought that would lead to improving yourself," which is what your nature wants. The conclusion is not to stop doing what you want but to do it in alignment with your goals: pursue curiosity, educate yourself, build meaningful projects. In the 2023 version he adds: "You can truly do what you want when you peel back the layers of what you think you want."

The argument is a redefinition of "what you want." The objection assumes that wanting is transparent and that doing what you want means following the first impulse. Koe's reply distinguishes layers: the impulse is often the ego (the self that, in Chapter 3's terms, defends its current form) closing off a line of thought that would lead to change; beneath it is a drive to grow that is just as much yours. Peeling back the layers is the same operation as deconstructing desire. The claim about evolution is stated as a general fact about human psychology without a specific source, and the remark about the divine is an expression of his own interpretive stance, which Chapter 40 discusses.

#### "Everything is interesting if you look deep enough"

A 2026 passage applies the enjoyment/pleasure distinction to a complaint familiar to anyone who has been overstimulated: nothing interests me. Koe calls his own obsessiveness and love of learning "a superpower" that lets him "get very far ahead of other people very quickly," because many people "just can't become interested in things." His counterclaim: "If you look deep enough, everything is interesting. Absolutely everything. Just stare at your wall, and then dig deeper. How was it built? What is the process that went into making it? Why is the outlet structured in that way?" "You have an entire learning rabbit hole that you can go down with anything." And the diagnosis: "I think you're just distracted, and you've engaged in so much pleasure seeking that simple things just don't bring you joy" (**Source:** `If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md`, 2026-06-13).

This is the 2022 definition of enjoyment (depth) turned into a test. If the wall and the outlet are boring, the problem is not the wall; it is that the capacity for depth has been dulled by a diet of surface hits. The passage anticipates the mechanism of section 11.4, where Koe explains why abstinence restores the interest of simple things (the hedonic reversal), and Chapter 14, where curiosity is treated as the guide to learning.

#### What happiness is, and what it is not

The enjoyment/pleasure distinction leads Koe to statements about happiness, and here the corpus does not offer a single definition. It offers several, each tied to a context, and they should be presented side by side rather than collapsed into one.

The most recent, from 2025, is a definition by contrast with euphoria. Answering the objection "what about going out, enjoying life, being social," Koe criticizes "the **Western notion of happiness**, which is just these endless dopamine hits that create euphoria. That's not what happiness is. Happiness is being fine. It's being calm. It's being in a good mood. It's not euphoria" (**Source:** `What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md`, 2025-08-03). In the vocabulary of section 11.1, the Western notion locates happiness in the "up" world, in the chase itself; Koe's definition locates it closer to the "down" world, in an even, calm state.

The second, from 2023, is borrowed. Koe sums up an argument "with a quote from Nietzsche": "happiness is the feeling that power increases, that resistance is being overcome." His adaptation: "we can feel this on the personal level" when making progress on a personal project, a hobby or learning something new. Then he extends it: "Business is a vessel for making progress and distributing that to the collective; that's when the feeling of progress compounds; that's when it really gets spiritual: when you help people make progress from the progress that you are making" (**Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`, 2023-11-12). This definition is active, not calm: it is the felt side of earned dopamine, and it explains why, for Koe, enjoyment is found in progress. The idea of business as a **vessel** for progress connects to Chapter 9 (section 9.4) and Part X.

**Complementary context:** the line Koe quotes comes from Nietzsche's *The Antichrist* (section 2), where happiness is defined as "the feeling that power increases, that a resistance is overcome." Koe later integrates the formula into a framework of his own (see below) and, in 2026, recalls it as "a niche quote" without being sure of its source.

The third, also from 2023, is explicitly conditional and the most provocative. In a video on escaping wage slavery Koe concedes that this "doesn't mean that you can't be happy; everyone is a slave in one way or another, and we all have waves of happiness in our life." Then: "If happiness is the feeling we get when our attention is distracted from the vast unhappiness in the world, then slavery may make some people happier than non-slaves, because you can be happy at points in your life while being unconscious of the problems in your life that would make you unhappy, until you have to develop the skill to fix those problems to become happy again" (**Source:** `How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md`, 2023-08-01). The sentence does not assert that happiness *is* distraction; it explores what follows *if* it were. Its target is the comfortable employee of the previous graphic: under a definition of happiness as distraction, the person inside the known can be happy, and the cost appears only when unacknowledged problems force a reckoning.

Two earlier definitions, both from February 2023, precede these three and supply their frame. In the same video from which the "sense of mastery" discussed below is taken, Koe says: "There are two types of happiness: one is derived from achieving goals, the second is derived from achieving nothing, but this balance of **peace and progress** has to be upheld by a sense of mastery, otherwise it becomes a shallow pursuit that will leave you disappointed." And, a moment later, that happiness cannot be isolated: "When we try to isolate and sustain and make sense of this thing called happiness, it ceases to make sense, because it can't make sense without an unhappy reference point"; he could not set a goal for the future, he says, without an experience like breaking up with his girlfriend in Costa Rica, wasting a lot of money and having to fly back (**Source:** `Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md`, 2023-02-26). The first statement already names the two poles; the second makes unhappiness the contrast that gives happiness its meaning, an idea Chapter 39 (sections 39.3 and 39.4) develops as polarity.

The Nietzschean line also has a later life. In January and February 2025 Koe presents it as a formula of his own and adds a second term: "If happiness comes from overcoming resistance and a **connection to something greater than yourself**, creativity is the vessel for both" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12). Solving problems supplies the first term; passing on what you have learned or built, through any product or service, supplies the second, which he describes as contributing to humanity's cumulative progress. Chapter 39 (section 39.1) treats that formula in full.

The definitions do not contradict each other if one sees what each is answering. The calm definition answers the confusion of happiness with euphoria; the Nietzschean one, and its 2025 extension, describe the felt reward of progress and contribution; the conditional one shows what a common definition implies and why it is inadequate. One way of ordering them is by the two poles Koe had already named in February 2023, before he quoted Nietzsche (October and November 2023) and before the definition of calm (2025): the Nietzschean formula belongs to the pole of *progress* and the calm one to the pole of *peace*. The pair is therefore not a later synthesis of the two definitions; it is the frame in which, from the start, both kinds of happiness had a place.

Two further observations from 2022 complete the picture from the side of what is desired and what is reached. The first is the line already quoted from the false-lifestyles passage: you do not want the car, you want the experience, which locates the object of desire in a feeling rather than a thing. The second concerns arrival. Becoming a value creator, Koe says, is "a very viable and purposeful career path"; he feels he has been working toward it all his life without knowing it existed, and "I still don't know what I'm doing" most of the time. Having a vision for the future is important, but "it's not like you have a vision for your future and then you get there one day and you're like, I achieved it all." "You feel the same," with the same human body and the same senses and perceptions, only in a better place: calmer, more stable, "not acting out of survival as much." He attributes much of this to writing and creating value for himself and others, which he calls "a personal growth journey in itself" (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31). This is a report of his own experience, not a law. It agrees with the calm definition of 2025 and with the honeymoon phase of section 11.1: reaching a goal does not produce a permanent high, and the change it does produce is a reduction of survival pressure, not a peak.

#### A philosophical sense of mastery

The last element of this section explains how a pursuit driven by cheap motives can be turned into one driven by earned ones without changing the pursuit itself. Koe's term for what makes the difference is a **sense of mastery**, also "philosophical sense of mastery": a philosophy or intrinsic reason behind a material pursuit.

The 2023 formulation is compact: "Everyone wants money, muscles and a desirable partner. The act of achieving those goals feels incredible at the start. If you don't have a philosophy or sense of mastery behind your material pursuits, you're in for a rude awakening. Even if your actions don't change, the why behind them can" (**Source:** `Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md`, 2023-02-26). His example is a bodybuilder who started training after a tough breakup. That bodybuilder can shift his focus "to find meaning in the microcosm of life that training is," if he allows his mind to see beyond identification with the material world. Those who do not "often acquire their material desires, change on the outside but not on the inside." The image he uses for them is "physically 60 years old while still having the mind of a 12-year-old going through an **emotional puberty**."

A version from the same month, in a video about writing, gives the analogy in reverse, with money as the starting motive. Money is not the only reason to write content, but it is "a driving factor that gets people interested"; once they develop a "philosophical sense of mastery" behind the writing itself, it becomes much more meaningful. The gym analogy: if you get into the gym just to get jacked and do not back it with a solid why, an intrinsic reason, it is a superficial, shallow pursuit, and "you're gonna wake up one day with the body of a 60-year-old but with the mind of a 15-year-old" (**Source:** `How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md`, 2023-02-05). The ages differ between the two versions (12 and 15); the image is the same.

The point connects this chapter to Chapter 9 (section 9.1), where shallow goals are accepted as portals to deeper ones. There the emphasis was on starting with the superficial; here it is on what happens to the superficial as you continue. The extrinsic motive (money, muscles, a partner) supplies the initial dopamine, the honeymoon. When that fades, either a why has grown behind the action or the action loses its fuel and you are left with an outward change and an unchanged mind. The sense of mastery is the mechanism by which cheap motivation is converted into earned motivation within the same activity.

A 2023 principle generalizes the mechanism: "When you start developing reasons behind your actions, you start to cultivate a philosophy," not only for those actions; they "culminate into a larger **life philosophy** that you have for yourself" (**Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). In that video Koe uses his own walking habit as the example: the reasons he developed for walking became part of his philosophy. The principle prepares a move made much later in the book, where the philosophy developed through the internal game becomes the substance of what you write and sell (Parts VIII and XI). Here its role is narrower: the reasons behind an action are what make its reward earned rather than cheap.

### 11.3 Choice of Consumption

#### If you don't program your own mind, someone else will

Sections 11.1 and 11.2 described the reward system. This section turns to what feeds it. The principle that opens it comes from a 2023 video on focus: "If you don't program your own mind, it will be programmed by someone else" (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11).

The sentence is the practical form of the thesis of Chapter 1. There, the Matrix was an inherited network of beliefs installed by repetition, and goals were either self-generated or assigned. Here the same logic is applied to information. In the age of information, Koe says, "everyone's trying to program you," to condition you. He does not call this inherently bad: being programmed is unavoidable, since a mind is shaped by whatever it takes in. What is avoidable is being programmed *by default*. The alternative is to "choose and limit your inputs from every plane of existence," which is how you condition yourself: by curating the information you allow to condition your mind. He adds a condition that ties the principle to Part IV: if you do not hold your future in the back of your mind, it is much easier to get distracted. The vision of Chapter 7 is the criterion by which inputs are chosen; without it, there is nothing to curate *for*.

The phrase "every plane of existence" introduces a division into three planes (digital, mental and physical) that structures the rest of that video. This section develops the first two; the third is treated in Chapter 4. On the *digital* plane, the instruction is to immerse yourself in information that programs you for success: "unfollow accounts that don't serve your future"; buy books that increase awareness of your potential; subscribe to podcasts, newsletters and blogs that teach what schools won't. "Every single place you turn, you need to curate a digital environment that is conducive to the future that you want" (**Source:** same video). On the *mental* plane, the instruction is to treat your mind as a garden: thoughts can be well kept and beautiful or grow like weeds and lead to a chaotic downward spiral. Koe supports the image with what he calls an Arab saying: "the nature of the rain is the same, but it makes thorns grow in the marshes and flowers in the gardens" (**Source:** same video). The proverb carries the argument well: the input (rain) is the same for everyone; what it produces depends on the ground it falls on. Information that nourishes a cultivated mind feeds weeds in a neglected one. The *physical* plane, the third, is not about the body but about where and among whom you live. Koe plays on a word: in the past people formed settlements; today people "settle" for where they are, grouping into environments that justify each other's mediocre behavior until it seems normal or good, and he concludes that "the single most important decision you make is where you live," because it drives business opportunities, relationships, food and water supply, politics, activities and day-to-day quality of life (same video). Chapter 4 (section 4.4) develops this passage as part of immersion in the environment of the future self; here it completes the three planes on which inputs must be chosen.

#### You are a product of your environment

The same 2023 video gives the mechanism behind the principle, in a passage that links environment, habit and identity (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11). If your standards are low, Koe says, it is usually because you have not placed yourself in a digital, physical or mental environment that would program you to raise them, or to think that high standards are normal. Thought processes created by your environments become habitual and "become who you are." Then, in the terms of Chapter 3's law of conceptual survival, you work to survive that identity: by justifying your actions, reinforcing your beliefs, following people and putting yourself in environments that let you settle. The loop closes, and it can run for decades: "Most people wake up after 40 years of **robotic living** wondering where the time went. You don't have to be one of them."

The sequence explains why environment matters more than any single decision. Environment does not act on you once; it supplies the thought processes you rehearse daily, the rehearsal becomes habit, the habit becomes identity, and the identity then selects environments that confirm it. The point of intervention Koe recommends is the environment, precisely because it is upstream of everything else.

A 2026 metaphor makes the unguarded mind vivid. Letting information into your mind without guarding it is like leaving the door of your house unlocked and letting everyone come in, party, ruin the house and leave, or even stay: "are you going to let 100 people live in your small apartment with you, on your bed," eating all your food? "That's what's happening in your mind." But you can "flip the script": "not easily, but you can turn it around" (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). The two qualifications are both important. The situation is reversible, and the reversal is not easy, which is why section 11.4 proposes radical protocols rather than gentle tips.

The people around you are part of the environment, and a 2024 passage names one kind of influence specifically. "We live in a generation of **whiners and complainers**": online, in comment sections, friends, parents. "The more you've surrounded yourself with these people, the more you've unconsciously adopted that as your own behavior, and then as you whine and complain about your own situation you further narrow and close your mind off to finding a potential solution." He asks you to "just think rationally": whining or complaining "is not going to help you at all" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). The mechanism is the same as for information. Complaint is absorbed from the environment without being chosen, and once it is your own it narrows the mind exactly where openness would be needed, around your own problem. This is the closed mind of Chapter 1 (section 1.7) produced not by ideology but by a social habit.

A 2023 passage states the whole syndrome as a recipe, by inversion. The way to live a mediocre life is "doing what everyone else tells you to do, never zooming out, never questioning your beliefs, never changing who you are," and "letting your attention get stolen from you and being distracted all day scrolling on your phone." That is "exactly how you program yourself to do absolutely nothing with your life" (**Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`, 2023-09-24). The verb is again "program." Mediocrity, in this account, is not a lack of programming but a successful program installed by default. The implied remedy, in that video, is to build the mental body, the deliberate construction of identity described in Chapters 3 and 4.

#### The distracted and the obsessed

The section's title comes from a 2023 coinage: "What seems to be the only difference between the successful and mediocre in this age is **choice of consumption**" (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). Koe divides people into two camps. *The distracted* scroll without intention, with no goals or focused effort. *The obsessed* research their interests in alignment with future goals. In the same passage he mentions self-experimentation and "launching into a season of obsession," which he develops elsewhere (Chapter 15).

"Only difference" is an overstatement, and the hedge "what seems to be" signals it. The claim is better read as: among people with similar access to information, which in the information age is nearly everyone, the variable that most separates outcomes is what they do with that access. The example he gives shows what obsession looks like in practice. A health expert worth following "has a constant finger on the pulse of the health market": up to date on studies and on what people are talking about, immersed. The same is true for business or any domain, via social media, books and podcasts. The obsessed and the distracted may use the same platforms; what differs is the intention behind the consumption.

A 2022 formulation says the same about the internet as a whole. There are people who use the internet to distract themselves, and people who use it to build leverage, make an income, spread a positive message and do what they enjoy. "Yes, it takes time," but this is "the main differentiator between abundance and mediocrity," and "it's literally a flip of a switch of how you use the thing that's in your hand" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28). The "flip of a switch" and the admission that "it takes time" are not in conflict. The decision to use the phone differently is instantaneous; its consequences accumulate slowly. The tool is identical in both cases, which is why Koe's critique of social media is never a rejection of it: Chapter 1 (section 1.5) already noted that the same medium is a conditioning machine for the passive consumer and a source of leverage for the creator.

#### Inputs set your standards

Chapter 3 (section 3.6) presented standards as what defines, for an identity, what counts as a problem. This chapter adds where standards come from. A 2023 argument puts it directly: the information you consume highly influences your identity, and thus your standards; it conditions, programs and trains your mind, in the sense of the "mental bodybuilding" Koe discusses in another video (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22).

His examples run in both directions. If you are surrounded by "internet hustlers" saying you need to make a certain amount a month (with all the goods and bads that come with them), and you adopt that number as a standard, the likelihood that you reach it is, in his estimate, "a thousand times higher" than for someone without that standard, who does not receive, store and use the information crossing their mind and stays stuck scrolling. Conversely, if you surround yourself, physically or digitally, with people who make it seem acceptable to be a hundred pounds overweight, to have no money, to work a job you hate, to stay with a partner you despise, to get drunk every night, "how will your life end up?" The figure "a thousand times" is rhetorical, not measured; the mechanism is the point. Then a test for whether a standard is real: "If your standards require you to eat from Whole Foods, you are going to look at McDonald's in disgust"; "if you don't, then those aren't **hard standards**." A hard standard, in Koe's sense, is one strong enough to make the alternative repulsive rather than merely forbidden. It is the opposite of willpower: you do not resist McDonald's, you are not tempted by it.

A 2023 passage names the worst case. "Mental phone slaves," Koe says, often end up unsuccessful, "because all of the ideas that they're consuming create who they are." If they watch "degenerate information and memes that tell them they hate their lives and they can only afford certain foods" (his example: "it's so funny, I have 10 cents in my bank account, I can't afford chicken nuggets"), "they think that's the standard that they set for themselves in their mind for who they are, because they program themselves to be that way, rather than surrounding themselves with the digital people that they want to become like" (**Source:** `How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md`, 2023-08-01). **Mental phone slaves** is his coinage, and he uses the passage to explain why the creator economy has "bad parts": the same economy that lets some people build leverage feeds others a steady diet of self-deprecation. The joke is not harmless because it is a joke; repeated, it becomes a description of who you are.

A 2022 heuristic turns the point into an audit. To use imitation intelligently (Chapter 1, section 1.4, showed that imitation is unavoidable), ask: is your environment, "or at least your perception of it," conducive to your goals and your future? In which direction will the things you consume on social media lead you? What echo chamber are you immersing yourself in, and how will it affect your future? Negative opinions and self-deprecating memes consumed "for the sake of entertainment" are affecting you even if you think they are not, and unless you become conscious of it you will wonder "why is all of this happening to me." The decisive question: "are you allowing the message of growth and progress and abundance to go into your brain," and are you in control of it, or the message of fear, self-deprecation and limiting beliefs? (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28).

A 2023 principle draws the strongest conclusion: "If you are not a successful person, it usually is because you haven't fixed the foundation," which he defines as filtering the negative information that goes into your head, getting rid of it and replacing it with information conducive to a positive future, "pretty much making success inevitable" (**Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`, 2023-05-08). "Inevitable" is the language of encouragement rather than prediction; the defensible content of the claim is the order of operations. Inputs are the foundation because everything else (standards, identity, goals, action) is built from them. Fix the foundation first and the rest has something to stand on.

#### Talking to a social media feed

A 2024 heuristic shows the result of unfiltered inputs in another person, which is often easier to see than in yourself. "In the past, the unconscious Boomers, when you talk to them, you're just talking to a TV. Now Gen Z and Millennials, the unconscious ones, you're just talking to some polarizing social media feed." The test: "if you can predict exactly what they're going to say next and they aren't willing to budge on their beliefs, you're **talking to a social media feed**" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24).

The test is about predictability, not about the content of the opinions. A person whose responses can be predicted from the feed they consume is not thinking but replaying. The generational labels are rhetorical; what defines the type is "unconscious," and it applies to any age.

Koe then explains the damage in the language of section 11.2. If you are conditioned by the feed and the algorithm keeps showing you short-term gratifying content that keeps you in stress and survival, "the combination of fear and desire pull you away from the present moment and entropy increases." The next sentence corrects a common intuition about harm: "What you thought was a harmless post was actually a combination of 50 short posts you read in unison that silently induces chaos in your mind." You regress, or stay the same, and "lock yourself into a paradigm of political ideologies and static opinions that aren't conducive to achieving anything worthwhile." The unit of harm is not the post but the stream. No single item is the problem; the problem is the accumulation, which is why an audit of any one input always looks reassuring and why the remedy has to address the environment rather than the item. Fear and desire are named as the two drivers, which corresponds to the two pulls of the "up" world: toward what threatens and toward what attracts, both of which take attention out of the present.

#### Outsourced mental health and the flow of energy

A 2022 argument puts the habit in a sharper light. "I would argue that 95 percent of people have outsourced most of the things that they have control over." In particular, they have outsourced their mental health to social media, to get "pleasurable dopamine hits" so they can feel good, "because they lack effort in consuming better information, learning, self-educating and producing information." Then the principle: "Input, output: you have to have that energy flow. You need to take energy in and you need to put energy out." Given the outsourcing, "it's no wonder" anxiety plagues society more than ever and mental health problems are rising across the board (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16).

The figure of 95 percent is an argumentative estimate ("I would argue"), and the link between social media and the rise in anxiety is offered as his interpretation, not as a demonstrated causal claim. The useful idea is the image of **outsourced mental health**: the regulation of mood, which could be done through effort and production, is handed to a platform whose incentives are not yours. The input/output principle explains why better inputs alone are not enough. Chapter 9 (section 9.3) already cited Koe's nutritional version of it: even with good inputs, consumption without creation leaves you "mentally obese." Energy has to flow out as well as in, and the outlet is production: writing, building, teaching.

#### Ninety-nine percent is distraction

If inputs program the mind, how much should one take in? A 2023 heuristic gives a provocative answer. The modern world is "an infinite web of information, ideas, opportunities, business models, relationship advice, productivity doctrine, conspiracy theories, news and drama," in which you get lost if you do not train focus. "At the current level of your life, 99% of the information that you are exposed to does not matter; it is a distraction." The advice is to get used to registering 99 percent as distraction and to learn to focus on the important things (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11).

The qualifier "at the current level of your life" is the key to the heuristic and keeps it from being anti-intellectual. Information is not useless in itself; it is useless *relative to where you are*. Advice for a business with a hundred employees is noise to someone looking for a first client. This connects the heuristic to the goal-as-lens model of Chapter 6: what matters is what is relevant to the current goal, and most information, however good, is not. The number is again rhetorical; the habit it describes is a default of rejection, in which information has to earn its way in.

#### Garden or swamp

A 2023 metaphor gives the information environment its most memorable image. The internet lets you "tend a **digital garden** or be thrust into a **digital swamp**." With unprecedented access to information, Koe believes it is creating identities "for the worse" rapidly. On social media the default is to follow entertainment and meme accounts rather than value creators, and almost all of them subject your mind to "**unwanted tenants** that party like it's a frat house." When the parents come down, they are devastated: "your awareness is the parents coming down to see the swamp that you've created in your mind" (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22).

The image combines two metaphors already seen. The garden is the mental plane of the 2023 focus video (the rain that grows flowers or thorns); the tenants are the strangers of the unlocked apartment. What the frat-house version adds is the role of awareness: it is not what creates the mess but what sees it. The devastation of the parents is the moment of brutal awareness described in Chapter 7: the state was there all along, and seeing it is painful precisely because it is the first step toward changing it.

A 2023 method describes how to audit the tenants. Koe lists three ways (**Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`, 2023-09-24). First, be conscious of your social media consumption. Second, use the information in your head to create: put it on paper so you understand it, get it out and metabolize it, so that you can see "this isn't that good, I need to change this about myself," or "this is good, I'm going to leverage this and build something worth utilizing by other people." Third, question why you are doing anything you do: "if you don't know why you were going to that class, going to that job, marrying that person or doing anything that you do, you did not create yourself." He adds a sentence that summarizes the stakes: "Sustained long-term focus is a lost art in a distracted world, and it's wreaking havoc on our ability to achieve anything meaningful."

The second step deserves attention because it reverses the usual order. Writing is presented not as a way of expressing what you already think but as a way of finding out what has entered your mind, of digesting it (the metabolism image) and of sorting what to discard from what to use. This is the output half of the energy flow, and it anticipates Part VIII, where writing becomes the base skill of the whole system. The third step applies the logic of Chapter 1: an action whose reason you do not know was installed by someone else.

#### Curating the digital environment

The corpus's methods for changing the digital environment become progressively more demanding between 2023 and 2025, without contradiction. Before the methods, Koe states why curation, rather than abstinence, is the right response.

In 2024 he grants that social media "can turn toxic fast," but "it's difficult to ignore that it's an integral part of how we do and will work, how we communicate and how we stay on the same page as everyone else, so we can improve as a society." The last function, he says, "is heavily being taken advantage of": nobody is on the same page if they let the algorithm show them any information and "shut off." He refers to the scene in *WALL-E* where people sit in floating capsules drinking soda, watching screens all day, "drip fed this pleasurable stuff": "it's very dystopian if you just let social media control your life and you don't curate." And then, bluntly: "if you're not intentional about the information that you're consuming, your life's going to be freaking terrible" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24). The argument rules out the easy exit. Leaving social media altogether would also cut you off from work, communication and shared understanding; the remaining option is to stay and curate.

**Complementary context:** *WALL-E* (Pixar, 2008) depicts a future in which humans live aboard a spaceship, carried in hover chairs, fed through straws and entertained by screens, having lost the habit of walking and of looking at each other. Koe uses the scene as an image of an uncurated life of passive consumption.

In the same video curation becomes the first of three steps for "creating your own order": curate your digital environment, focus on a meaningful project, and earn your control and freedom (the second step was discussed in Chapter 9, section 9.3). Step one begins by naming "the first offender": short-form content. It is useful for quick insights but "can quickly devolve into a rabbit hole of memes and meaninglessness." We no longer challenge our beliefs with books and articles "whose incentives aren't to become a New York Times bestseller or to create the most inflammatory engagement-bait headline." Koe offers a criterion based on the author's intention. "Everyone is chasing status in one way or another"; if an author's main intention is status, the information is tailored to that and is "probably mostly useless." If an author does not intend to help "increase order in the reader's mind," to go long-form and in depth, to create understanding and to challenge, it is probably not worth reading. Then the rule: "Most of your time spent on the internet should be dedicated to long-form content that challenges, expands and nurtures your perspective." Read long, read difficult concepts, read things that do not confirm your beliefs. Reading "can easily be confused with mental masturbation when it is so much more." And the strongest claim of the passage: "I would argue that most of your future success is dependent on this single habit: read longer things." The practical steps: unfollow accounts that are not serving your growth; find long-form writing, videos and podcasts you enjoy (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24).

Why long-form? The passage implies two reasons. One is about incentives: long-form requires the author to build understanding, which is incompatible with pure engagement-bait. The other is about the reader's mind: long-form offers structure (an argument with a through-line), which is what order in consciousness requires, while a stream of short pieces produces the fragments that section 11.2 identified as entropy. "Mental masturbation" names the risk on the other side, reading as a pleasurable activity with no output, which is why the energy-flow principle remains in force: long-form reading is better input, not a substitute for output.

A heuristic from the same video qualifies the attack on short-form. "Follow those long-form creators on social media, because they write better short-form content that helps fill in the knowledge gaps of their long form." If someone is only on short-form (only TikTok, with no newsletter, no YouTube channel, no podcast; or on X or Instagram without writing threads or carousels), "a lot of their growth comes from being polarizing, being inflammatory, being kind of engagement-baity." Koe softens the judgment immediately: "which is not bad all around; they're probably not trying to do it," and such creators are probably teaching better than people just posting memes. The more you curate, "the more you promote centropy," reduce entropy, and "the less you fall into this chaos, anxiety and overwhelm" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24). The criterion is not the format but what stands behind it: short-form that points to a body of long-form work is a fragment of a structure; short-form that points to nothing is a fragment of nothing.

#### Resetting the feed

The method of unfollowing hardens over time. The 2023 version, quoted above, is selective: unfollow accounts that don't serve your future. A 2023 process from another video adds the slow rebuilding (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22):

1. Unfollow anyone who does not serve the conditioning of better standards, however you justify following them. The people you follow influence your actions, "slowly, then all at once you become someone you may hate."
2. Take your time following valuable accounts: ones that challenge your worldview, make you think outside the box and educate you on skills for a higher quality of life. This does not happen overnight. You may not find the information useful at first, because it takes time to bridge ideas into understanding; it will probably be boring "until it's one of the most interesting things in the world."
3. Follow one account, let the algorithm work, then follow the accounts that reply to the first account's posts. Over time you create an environment conducive to growth.

He closes with a variant of the choice-of-consumption thesis: "The only difference between you and the person more successful than you is the consistent intention behind the information they consume."

The 2024 version is total. "Use this as a time to literally unfollow everyone, myself included. Then, from a clear conscience and toward the goal that you're trying to pursue, start to follow people": follow one person, look in their comments to find others to follow, look at the people they follow and follow them, "and be absolutely rigorous on maintaining your feed on social media, because social media is such a powerful tool for learning and building and meeting new people and actually creating the life that you want to live, but it can quickly, very quickly destroy your mind" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30).

The difference between the versions is that the 2024 method no longer trusts the user's judgment about which existing follows to keep. Starting from zero removes the need to justify each removal, and including himself ("myself included") makes the point that no account is exempt, however useful it claims to be. The rebuilding method is the same in both: start from one valuable node and expand through the network around it, using the algorithm deliberately. It uses the same machinery that section 11.1 described as a trap (the algorithm deepens whatever goal is running), but this time with a chosen goal at the top of the mind. The remark that useful content will be boring at first applies the hedonic logic of this chapter: a mind accustomed to cheap stimulation finds valuable content dull until the capacity for depth returns.

#### Organic content and digital farmers markets

In 2025, with AI-generated content multiplying, Koe gives the criterion for what to follow a name taken from food. Chapter 1 (section 1.5) reconstructed his comparison between processed food and processed content (digital fat, sugar and salt). Its counterpart is that "on the flip side of processed food and content, there's organic food and content," and "**organic content** is syntropic." "Unlike entropy that leads to disorder and chaos, syntropy leads to order and clarity." You cannot expect the For You page to feed you this; you must actively search, curate and nurture your digital feed, especially as AI content ramps up: "shop at **digital farmers markets**" (**Source:** `The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md`, 2025-02-16).

He then gives three marks by which organic content can be recognized (same source):

| Mark | What it means | Caveat or exception |
|---|---|---|
| Useful | It helps you achieve your goals | You must have self-generated goals; "if you don't have a vision, most content, aside from that which helps generate vision, is useless. Go read a philosophy book." |
| Often long-form | Books, articles, newsletters, podcasts, YouTube, with room to deliver sense and value | Short-form creators can post insights that act as "a small puzzle piece for your mind," but if it doesn't connect to anything you want to do, it does you no good |
| Authentic | Created not because it has to be, but because the creator deems it valuable enough to share with an audience that may benefit | — |

The first mark makes the whole criterion depend on Part IV. Usefulness is relative to goals, and without self-generated goals almost nothing is useful, except the content that helps you generate them. The parenthetical "go read a philosophy book" is a concrete recommendation for the person without a vision: before you can filter information by your goals, you need material that helps you form goals.

Koe adds a nuance that prevents the criterion from being naive about form. Organic content, which he also calls **delayed gratification content**, can still use attention and marketing tactics to hook you: "that's the game you have to play," and any book that hooks you uses them. Attention-grabbing headlines are not the problem; the problem is the content within and whether it suits you and your goals. Otherwise you are "manhandled around and being programmed by your digital feed to want things that you don't actually want," which is what makes you feel lost. The final phrase links the section back to its beginning: the feeling of being lost is, in this account, the felt symptom of a mind programmed to want things it does not want, the condition that "program your own mind" was meant to prevent.

**Complementary context:** "syntropic" appears in the transcript in a garbled form ("Copic"); the most plausible reading is "syntropic," consistent with the term "syntropy" that Koe uses as a synonym of "centropy" in 2024 (section 11.2).

#### Long lectures on walks

The last element of this section is a practice from Koe's own life, presented as such. In 2022 he describes going on walks and listening to a long lecture (by Terence McKenna, by Alan Watts, or from actualize.org), using YouTube Premium so the phone can be off, "and just let things go." He encourages taking several days, if not weeks, to listen to these lectures and "munch on the ideas," rather than being bombarded by "500 ideas a day just by scrolling through social media," which overwhelms the mind and leaves no clarity about what to do with your future. The contrast he draws is with "some kind of **cheap dopamine cycle** on social media" (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20).

The practice illustrates every principle of the section at once. It is long-form; it is chosen rather than served; its pace allows digestion ("munch on the ideas") rather than accumulation; and it is combined with walking, which Koe elsewhere treats as a condition for the mind to process what it has taken in (section 11.4 develops the mechanism). The contrast between one lecture over several days and 500 ideas a day is the contrast between the two diets of this chapter.

**Complementary context:** Terence McKenna (1946–2000) was an American writer and speaker on psychedelics, consciousness and culture; Alan Watts (1915–1973) was a British writer known for popularizing Zen and Eastern philosophy in the West; Actualized.org is the channel of Leo Gura, a self-development and philosophy commentator whose ideas Koe adapts elsewhere in the corpus (the circular validation of Chapter 1).

### 11.4 Disappear, Monk Mode and Detox

Section 11.3 described how to curate inputs going forward. This section addresses a harder case: a mind that has already been flooded, in an environment that keeps reproducing the flood. For this case Koe offers a family of radical tools, all of which share one structure: a deliberate, bounded period in which the usual inputs and obligations are removed so that something else can emerge. The tools differ in scale (a week, thirty days, three to six months), in emphasis (removal, reset, creativity) and, in one case, in direction: war mode reverses the logic of the others. The corpus also records how the tools changed over time, and those changes are presented where they occur.

#### Successful people disappear

The family starts from an observation that Koe repeats in 2024 in almost identical words: "There is one pattern I've noticed in all successful individuals: they live in their own little world. They disappear from society for months at a time to force pure focus on themselves and their vision. They laser in on one meaningful goal and make it a reality" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21; also `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). It is an observation, not a study; "all" is a generalization from the people he has noticed. Its content is a mechanism: **own little world** names a protected environment in which one goal dominates attention, and disappearing is how such an environment is created when the existing one is full of competing demands.

A 2026 passage gives the smallest concrete version. Another difficult thing many successful people do is take a **sabbatical**: go away for a week, often to the woods, or rent a remote Airbnb, alone; "all they bring is their laptop." Many creatives do this to re-spark creativity and have better ideas for something important, such as writing a book. The reason Koe gives is environmental: it "immediately removes you from your environment," so you can form new habits and behaviors (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). The sabbatical applies the conclusion of section 11.3 in its simplest form. If environment programs behavior, and changing an environment is slow, leaving it is fast.

A 2025 framework gives the longest version, a plan for what to do with six months of disappearance: **one project, one book, one meditation, one workout**. These four commitments are the "offense" of what Koe calls the holistic monk mode, whose two halves are presented later in this section. Each element "checks the boxes" of "the good, the true and the beautiful" in a dimension of life; together they align you with all three and lead to a higher state of enjoyment through curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy and mastery. He closes the video with an exhortation: "Get sick of being sick. Give yourself permission to be extreme. Disappear and come back unrecognizable" (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29). The transcript makes the mapping explicit only in part: the project, as work, integrates all three values, and reading out of curiosity is "a prime representation of the true"; for the meditation and the workout it assigns no single value, and the mapping should not be guessed. What the framework makes clear is that the disappearance is not empty: it is defined by four commitments that cover mind, body, work and contemplation, each reduced to one, which is the opposite of the many-inputs condition the disappearance escapes. The five terms that close the description (curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy, mastery) are the intrinsic flow drivers discussed in Chapter 10.

#### "Disappear" is a metaphor

The word invites a literal reading, and Koe has had to correct it. In 2022, and again in a 2024 compilation that repeats the passage word for word, he says that when he tells people to "disappear for 6 months," they take it literally, but "much of what I say is metaphorical and exaggerated to get the essence of the message across." What he means is "disappear from the things that don't belong in your life; cut them out," and these "can be people, games, apps or even bad habits." The precondition is awareness: become aware of what is holding you back. There are multiple ways to do it, but what has worked best for him is to "rip the Band-Aid off" and get rid of them all at once. And you do not need to explain yourself to the people who will message you asking where you went ("hey bro, where'd you go"): "just disappear" (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

**Disappear** is therefore a common word used in a specific sense. It does not mean leaving society; it means selective absence from whatever drains you, done all at once and without negotiation. **Rip the Band-Aid off** is the method's name: removal in one move rather than gradual reduction. The admission that his language is "metaphorical and exaggerated" is also worth keeping as a reading instruction for this whole chapter. Koe uses extreme formulations ("unfollow everyone," "disappear for six months") to transmit a direction, and the literal instruction is usually milder than the phrase.

#### Breaking the addiction to feeling bad

Why remove things at all, if the problem could be addressed by adding good habits? A 2022 argument answers by identifying what the removed things are doing. It is a chain (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25):

1. "Break your addiction with feeling like [expletive]."
2. That feeling has multiple causes. The physical ones (nutrition, food) are set aside; the mental cause is mental energy spent on people and things that "don't give a [expletive] about you."
3. Redirect the freed mental energy into your vision.
4. You have then created a system that prevents entropy: the system that takes you from anti-vision to vision.
5. To maintain a system that prevents entropy you need energy, in this case mental energy, to "actualize or manifest that vision, if you want to call it that."

Chapter 7 presented the 2024 restatement of this chain as the reason the anti-vision needs a system. Here it explains what disappearing is for. Feeling bad is described as an *addiction*, which is a strong word: the claim is that the bad feeling is sustained by repeated investment of energy in relationships and activities that give nothing back, and that this investment is habitual and difficult to stop, like any addiction. The entropy model of Chapter 5 supplies the reason it must stop: order costs energy, and energy spent on what does not care about you is unavailable for the structure that would reverse your entropy. Disappearing is energy reallocation. The coinage **addiction to feeling like [expletive]** names the condition it treats.

A 2024 passage repeats the argument and adds a diagnostic. Break your addiction to feeling bad, which is usually due to giving time and energy to people, activities and things that do not care for your well-being. You do not need to explain yourself to anybody; you are doing this for you. Then the test: "the moment you find yourself having the desire to explain what you're doing is the moment that you should realize that you're not taking back control," because it means you are doing it for someone else, which is "an even bigger problem" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10). The urge to justify a change is evidence that the change is still oriented toward others' approval, the same need for approval that installed the original programming (Chapter 1, section 1.2).

The same 2024 video explains the all-at-once method by contrast with the alternative: "Disappear not from life but from distractions" (people, games, apps). "It's easier to remove everything at once and deal with the pain than it is to tell everyone your plans and listen to their **crab in a bucket** opinions." Koe does not elaborate the image; it implies that others, like crabs in a bucket that pull back any crab trying to climb out, will drag you down if you announce an attempt to leave. And he states the principle behind the whole approach: "success is less about being disciplined and more about removing the distractions that make discipline difficult" (same source).

**Complementary context:** "crab mentality" or "crabs in a bucket" is a common idiom for a group's tendency to pull down any member who tries to rise above it, by analogy with crabs that, in a bucket, prevent each other's escape.

The principle about discipline is part of a longer development across the corpus. Between 2023 and 2024 Koe repeatedly downplays discipline: it is about "compounding choices," it "comes from clarity, not force," and here it is less important than removing distractions. From 2025 he redefines it as "a feature of identity": you are already disciplined toward whatever is compatible with your current identity. The two positions share a mechanism, which Chapter 3 (section 3.6) presented: behavior follows from identity and environment rather than from force of will. Removing distractions is the environmental side of that mechanism. Discipline, in this account, is not the cause of a focused life but a by-product of an environment where the competing options have been removed.

#### Three months minimum

A 2024 process places disappearance inside a larger sequence of change and gives it a minimum duration (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15). After reaching "absolute awareness" through contemplation, "you disappear for three months minimum," cutting all the anchors that hold you in your situation. The next step explains why disappearance without direction fails. Without a vision you are lost; negative energy has nowhere to go, gets trapped and wreaks havoc, which Koe calls "psychic entropy." Without a meaningful path, system or goal in which to invest energy, you decay.

The answer is a vision, and Koe immediately lowers the bar for what counts as one. A vision does not start clear: "it starts as an educated guess" ("I think I want to try this business," "I think I want to read this book"), enough to begin reprogramming. Map it: write what you don't want and what you want, and break it into weekly goals and priority tasks (he offers a template for his Cortex app). Then "laser in": fix your physical diet, fix your mental diet, take more walks, learn a skill, go to the gym, talk to someone new. It is uncomfortable, he concedes, but "a mediocre life is the most uncomfortable thing in the world."

This passage adds two things to the earlier ones. The first is the duration. The 2022 phrase was "disappear for 6 months"; here, in 2024, it is "three months minimum." Across the corpus, the time frames Koe gives for change vary widely across videos (from weeks to years) depending on the goal, and he does not reconcile them; the most defensible reading is that a change of direction can be quick while the consolidation of a new baseline takes longer. The second addition is the warning that removal alone is dangerous: energy freed from old attachments and not invested in a goal becomes psychic entropy. This is why every disappearance in Koe's system is paired with something to disappear *into*: a vision, a project, the four elements of the six-month plan. The term **mental diet** in the list connects the process back to section 11.3: what you consume is the diet of the mind, and fixing it is part of the work of the disappearance.

#### The author's own disappearance

Koe's clearest example is his own, and he tells it in a 2025 video. In 2021 his business was starting to do well; after the pandemic he was 20 to 30 pounds overweight, despite a lifetime in the gym, and he disliked how he looked; he was drafting his first book, *The Art of Focus*. Every weekend there was a reason to go out (concerts, friends, drinking), "normal life for normal people." Since his progress was good, he figured he could take a break: he partied, drank, stayed up late and went off his diet. His progress went rapidly downhill. Each weekend took four or five days for his energy and mental capacity to recover, and by then another event came. He could not think straight for the book draft, his fitness fell off and his business slowed. It was painful, he says, but not excruciating, and so there was no reason to change yet (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29).

His friends had planned a birthday trip to Seattle for months. Tired of not progressing, he justified it as "one last time." After that weekend he was done, "so mad at myself," and he explains why: "when you have goals that are the most important thing in your world, they shine a light on everything you're doing wrong." The most painful thing, he says, is watching those goals fade, replaced by goals of shallow pleasure. On returning home he disappeared: he did not respond and became unavailable; every invitation got an **automatic no**, without thinking. Every action then felt seamless; he wanted to be disciplined, and it gave him joy. His mind, body and business returned: "my entire world changed in an instant."

The story illustrates almost every claim of this chapter. The weekend cycle is the oscillator of section 11.2: a high followed by a four-to-five-day low, repeated before recovery is complete. The fact that the pain was "not excruciating" explains why change waited: a cost that is real but tolerable produces no decision (Chapter 4, section 4.2, discusses the triggers of change). The sentence about goals shining a light on what you do wrong is the feedback mechanism of section 11.1: with a goal that matters, the same weekend that used to be harmless becomes visible negative feedback. And the automatic no is the all-at-once method, with its predicted consequence: once the options are removed, discipline stops feeling like discipline. "Changed in an instant" should be read as his description of the decision and of how it felt; the consolidation he describes (mind, body and business returning) necessarily took longer.

A 2024 story shows the same pattern applied to a single habit (**Source:** `The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md`, 2024-06-16). Koe started using nicotine when Juul became popular, conforming during college; he went through Juuls and disposables and then Zyn, tobacco-free nicotine pouches, which he thought healthier. On a random day in 2019 he realized that nicotine abuse was hurting the attention he could give others and making him tired, "like a corpse of a human being." In conversations he kept thinking "I need to put in a Zyn," and people noticed he was not listening. Around the same time he saw a post about walking versus cardio, claiming that 10,000 to 15,000 steps a day can burn calories comparable to cardio, though perhaps not with the same cardiovascular benefits. It was nearly summer and he wanted to get lean. "The stars aligned": he quit nicotine cold turkey and replaced it with walks, a complete reinvention in the two domains that occupied most of his attention. His strategy: whenever he felt the urge, he trained himself to get up immediately, go on a walk and put in chewing gum; it was relatively seamless. He calls this a **mental monk mode**: not disappearing from friends, but "disappearing from the habits, the bad habits in my life."

Two features of the story matter. The first is the method of substitution: the craving was not resisted but redirected into a specific action (walk, gum), which gave the urge somewhere to go. This is the same logic as the 2022 argument about energy: what is removed must be replaced. The second is a disclosure that complicates the story. Koe says he still uses nicotine, in much smaller amounts, for work and its cognitive effects, without endorsing it ("do your own research"). The corpus records the tension: the episode is presented as the origin of his walking habit and as a story of quitting, yet the quitting was not permanent. The honest reading is that the story demonstrates a method for breaking a pattern of abuse and a habit transition, not complete abstinence.

#### Monk mode: the popular version, the original, and a holistic one

"Disappear" is close to a term that the self-improvement culture already had, **monk mode**, and Koe's relation to it changes over time. In 2021 he used it casually and with a nuance. In a conversation with Justin Welsh, who rejected "going all in" in favor of many small bets, Koe agreed with small bets but said his position was "nuanced": "the majority of the things you do should be diversified, but then when there is that pull to say, like, go monk mode on one project or something similar, that's when you go all in, in my opinion. You need to focus in on that one thing" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20). He added that he had been making connections about those small risks and writing about them, "which helps a lot with understanding it." Monk mode here is a temporary total focus on one project, an exception within a diversified default.

**Complementary context:** Justin Welsh is a solo entrepreneur known for building a one-person business through LinkedIn and digital products; the preference for "small bets" over going all in is associated with Daniel Vassallo, whose approach Welsh endorses in that conversation. The corpus records a later tension: from 2025 Koe argues for "concentration of force" as the way to get rich, without resolving it against the small-bets position.

By 2025 Koe criticizes the term directly (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29). Monk mode, he says, has become "a bastardized version of its former self," reduced to "cut everyone off and make money." He will not follow that version, nor the original. He then gives a historical account of the original, which he presents as his own framing. In agrarian societies the horse-drawn plow created an abundance of food, giving men free time to pursue their interests; writing and mathematics were invented, and people had time for deep contemplation, discovering that the divine was not "out there or up there but in here." That led to the Axial period, when revolutionary spiritual and philosophical ideas emerged across civilizations: Socrates in Greece, Gautama Buddha in India, Lao Tzu in China. This "ascending" spirituality, or asceticism, treated the manifest world as evil: money, sex, food and pleasure were demonized. True monk mode, in that sense, was a period dedicated to the ascending path and to self-realization.

His objection to both versions is the same: reduction. In 2025, with access to many worldviews and to history, any non-dogmatic person sees spirituality as one piece, a very important one, of a larger picture. Reducing all solutions to contemplation is as bad as reducing them to atoms, money or physical things. Spiritual people who criticize scientific materialists for reducing the world to atoms are doing the same thing: "they're just reducing the world to spirit." The popular monk mode reduces life to money; the ascetic one reduces it to spirit; neither fits a person whose problem is their whole life.

**Complementary context:** the "Axial Age" is the philosopher Karl Jaspers's term for the period roughly between the eighth and third centuries BCE in which major religious and philosophical traditions emerged in parallel in China, India, Persia, Israel and Greece. The vocabulary of "ascending" and "descending" spirituality resembles Ken Wilber's, which describes ascending (otherworldly, transcendent) and descending (this-worldly, immanent) paths and criticizes each when taken alone; Koe does not attribute the terms in this passage. The dating and causal story he gives (the plow, free time, contemplation) are his compressed narrative.

In place of both versions Koe proposes a **holistic monk mode**: disappear for a period of intensity toward a better life, obsessing over a problem until it is solved; since the problem is changing the entirety of your life, "we need a holistic monk mode, if you will." It has two parts: *defense*, which eliminates distractions, and *offense*, which forms four **focus habits**. The two halves belong together: the defense clears the ground, and the offense decides what grows on it. Without the offense, the holistic monk mode would be one more version of the reduction Koe has just criticized, removal for its own sake.

The defense is described in detail (same source). Focus does not apply only to work, and distractions are not only notifications. The point of disappearing is to "create a **glitch in the matrix**," to change your identity overnight, and to rid yourself of "the crabs and parasites" that pull you down and drain the energy needed for choices that create a better future. For anyone willing to be extreme, "it's easier to change everything at once than it is to change one thing at a time": rip the Band-Aid off. Stop responding to people who only take and never give. Any invitation that pulls you away from your goals is an automatic, firm no. Every bad habit that doesn't serve you stops tomorrow, no exceptions. "It is an incredible feeling to **choose your own limitations**," to be free of what splits attention from the highest good you can do.

The phrase "choose your own limitations" is the conceptual center of the defense. Limitations are usually imposed (by a job, by others' expectations, by the algorithm); the holistic monk mode turns limitation into an act of authorship. It is the constraint logic of Chapter 8 (section 8.4) applied to the environment: by removing options you decide what your attention will be available for.

The offense answers the question the defense leaves open: what the freed attention is for. Koe grounds it in a triad: "a good life stems from the pursuit of the good, the true and the beautiful," which he glosses as the pursuit of truth as a whole and defines through three kinds of truth. **The good** is intersubjective truth (the transcript reads "inner subjective"): what is good for humanity or for a community, doing what is right. **The true** is objective truth, what scientific study finds: learning and understanding how things really are. **The beautiful** is subjective truth, the realm of aesthetics: noticing and creating beauty around you. Many habits could serve the triad; he picks four as "the most impactful and easiest" (same source; Chapter 39, section 39.1, develops the triad and its application to work). The four habits are the six-month plan described at the start of this section, now with their daily doses:

| Focus habit | What Koe prescribes | Where the book develops it |
|---|---|---|
| One project | One hour of deep work a day, on the calendar, on a project that is a building block for the life you want (a creative project leading to independent work, or a portfolio project leading to a career change); he recommends that everyone at least try entrepreneurship as a hedge against replacement by AI | Chapter 9 (section 9.3); its relation to the triad, Chapter 39 (section 39.1) |
| One book | Read one book 30 minutes a day; self-educate by immersing your mind in the thoughts of those who have lived the life you want; learning out of curiosity is "a prime representation of the true" | Chapter 16 (section 16.2) |
| One meditation | At least 10 minutes a day of noticing reality in greater detail, meditation as "a way of perceiving the world through a lens of wonder and gratitude" | Chapter 40 (section 40.4) |
| One workout | Commit to one form of training, whichever you are most pulled to (running, weightlifting, Pilates, yoga), because a lack of health "shoots upward" into the mind, the work and relationships | Chapter 9 (section 9.1) and Chapter 40 (section 40.2) |

Read as a whole, the holistic monk mode has the same structure as every tool in this section: a removal paired with something to occupy the space it frees. The defense is the "rip the Band-Aid off" of 2022 extended to people, invitations and habits; the offense is the six-month plan, reduced to four daily commitments, three of them with an explicit daily dose (an hour, thirty minutes, ten minutes).

The claim that changing everything at once is easier than changing one thing at a time stands in tension with another strand of the corpus, which Koe also maintains: start small, reduce friction, make gradual changes (identity as a city from which pieces are removed carefully, progressive overload, the walking habit begun with the smallest step). Koe moves between the two registers without ever explicitly correcting either. A reading consistent with both is that gradual change suits single habits and central pieces of identity, while all-at-once removal suits the environment and a deliberate, brief turn of identity. The defense of the holistic monk mode is aimed at the environment (people, invitations, habits that feed the old self), which is where the extreme register applies.

#### War mode: the opposite pole

In March 2025, a few months before the holistic monk mode, Koe introduced its opposite. He says he oscillates between "Warrior and Monk." Monk mode, the familiar self-improvement tactic, means disappearing from the world, removing distractions and practicing asceticism, rejecting worldly pleasures in pursuit of personal development. **War mode** is the opposite: "you attack your goals, you simulate a hunt, you say yes to everything," expose yourself to massive experience, overwhelm your mind with chaos and force yourself to expand into a new identity, "the person who can bear a massive load of responsibility," who does not crack under pressure "because that means death." He uses war mode, he says, to take his mind, body and business to another level when needed (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02).

In that video war mode is not a free-standing attitude but the last stage of a process. Koe first sets out three steps, **Vision** (an "attention anchor"), **Clarity** (a plan) and **Identity** (the alter ego, the answer to "who must you become?"), and then calls the execution phase war mode, which has four steps of its own: commit with a physical, irreversible act (his example is to shave your head) and remove your plan B's; learn by embracing chaos (30–60 minutes of learning and 30–60 minutes of building); build mind, body and business; and expose yourself to massive experience for a period of two to three months, then recover and repeat (same source). Chapter 4 (section 4.6) presents the whole sequence, and Chapter 10 (section 10.6) treats the first step, the irreversible commitment, as a case of tactical stress. What this section quotes is mostly the fourth step, the period of massive exposure, which is where war mode becomes the opposite of the monk's withdrawal.

Later in the same video he gives the boundaries of that period (same source). War mode is a period of intense growth and does not last forever: "maybe 2 to 3 months," because you can only give so much before burnout. During it, give everything: say yes to everything, talk to new people, accept the invitation, launch the business, ask for help, fill your mind with so much chaos that even if you crack under pressure you come out more developed. "Fly too close to the Sun and land on the moon." He promises that you will look back in twelve months amazed. After recovery, you do it all over again. And once practiced, you can set up the right variables to encourage this growth period deliberately.

The transcript contains an ambiguity in this passage. Koe says "growth is linear; it comes in short exponential bursts of progress." The second clause contradicts the first, and he probably meant "growth *isn't* linear"; the transcript does not allow certainty, and the corpus leaves it unresolved.

| | Disappear / monk mode | War mode |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Withdrawal | Attack |
| Response to invitations | Automatic no | Say yes to everything |
| Relation to stimulation | Remove distractions, reduce input | Overwhelm the mind with chaos and experience |
| Purpose | Clear noise, redirect energy, protect focus on one goal | Expand into an identity able to bear more responsibility |
| Duration in the corpus | 3 months minimum to 6 months; a week for a sabbatical | 2 to 3 months, then recovery and repetition |
| Risk named by the author | Psychic entropy if there is no vision to invest energy in | Burnout if prolonged |

The change is best described as a refinement: from a single mode (withdrawal) to an oscillation between two. The reason Koe implies is functional. Withdrawal clears noise; war expands identity. They are compatible as phases, and his own phrase, oscillating between Warrior and Monk, suggests a rhythm rather than a choice. War mode also connects to two earlier chapters: "simulate a hunt" is the hunting psyche of Chapter 2 (section 2.4), deliberately activated, and the decision to place yourself under all-or-nothing pressure is the tactical stress of Chapter 10 (section 10.6). The difference from cheap stimulation is the one drawn in section 11.2: war mode's chaos is novelty *created* through struggle, not received.

The extreme register of both poles has a counterweight later in 2025. In an August video on the pressure created by artificial intelligence, Koe advises using "this moment of urgency as a time to turn your life around," because that is when people change the most, and then adds that changing your life "doesn't mean packing up, moving across the country"; it requires thinking about your work, pursuing something you care about, taking risks, and creating and sharing a story worth telling (**Source:** `You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md`, 2025-08-17; Chapter 35, section 35.3). Set beside war mode's irreversible gestures (the shaved head, the plane across the country with a few dollars) and beside "disappear," the sentence pulls in the opposite direction: the change that matters is in the work and the thinking, not in a dramatic rupture. Koe does not reconcile the two formulations, and the alternation between the gradual and the extreme registers noted above remains open.

#### The 30-day dopamine detox

The most fully specified of the reset tools is a 2025 protocol, "A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days." Its place in the corpus has a history that should be stated before the protocol itself. In November 2024 Koe said that detoxes help because they force you to find appreciation in boredom, "but you don't have to do a dopamine detox; you simply have to change how you approach, use and leverage the technology." In October 2025 he published the full protocol and presented it as a necessary tool for anyone who will not live as a monk. In March 2026 he proposed a one-week commitment that "isn't a full dopamine detox." The change runs from optional tool to recommended protocol to reduced version, and Koe gives one reason for the middle position, discussed below. The three positions can apply to different situations: adjusting habits, a periodic reset, and a creative block.

The 2025 video opens with the signs that you need it (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26). It is "for those times where life starts to lose its wonder and zest": when you feel more tired than calm, when "you may not realize you're using coffee as a security blanket," when you notice dark circles under your eyes, when you look back to a time when "life felt seamless and almost magical." Then "the harsh reality": "By simply living, we take on risk, but not the good type of risk." He groups the bad risks into four categories, which later define what the detox removes.

| Category (Koe's name) | Examples he gives |
|---|---|
| Binge tech | Social media, streaming, gaming, news |
| Simulated (or unnatural) sexual stimulation | Pornography, dating apps |
| Consumables | Alcohol, drugs, junk food; in the protocol, "sweetness in general" |
| Thrill-seeking | Gambling, shopping sprees, extreme sports |

Before the protocol Koe states his stance toward morality, and it explains the tone of everything that follows. He is not "one to demonize your actions," he says, "not a saint by any means," "just a guy who studies weird stuff and shares what he learns on the internet. For money, sure, but also meaning. Because if I wanted to get filthy rich, I'd start a religion." Most people online "put on this guise of perfection" and are the first to tell you when you do something slightly bad, yet their lives are usually not in "the physical, mental, spiritual or financial situation that you would ever want to be in." "You've done what someone else thinks is bad at least five to 10 to 100 times this week. And so have the people that think what you're doing is bad. And that's fine." He sets morality aside explicitly, to discuss tools (same source). The disclaimer matters for how the detox should be read: the four categories are not sins but risks, and the protocol is not penance but maintenance.

The argument for the tool follows (same source). In "today's world of convenient quick pleasures," if you don't want to become a monk ("because if you did want that, it's out of ego, which I find funny"), "you need a tool to perform a **factory reset** on your life." The reasoning: "Humans create tools to solve problems. Technology creates problems, but it also allows humans to do what used to be impossible," so in a world of technology "you need some kind of tool to mitigate the problems that technology causes." If you will not live in the woods, implement something to mitigate the risk of your phone, the internet and cheap convenient foods. Doing the protocol "at least once a year for 30 days at a time" will "buffer the consequences of modern life," bringing back "childlike wonder," "that feeling of not caring," open-mindedness, not being stuck in your head, experiencing life on a deeper level.

The argument is the reason behind the change of position noted above. Koe does not frame the detox as a rejection of technology but as technology's own logic applied to itself: every tool creates problems that require other tools. The aside about monks wanting asceticism "out of ego" connects to his critique of the ascetic monk mode: complete renunciation is not presented as the higher option but as another identity to be attached to.

The protocol has seven steps, which Koe numbers explicitly in the video. Two of them, the motivational foundation and the removal itself, define the detox and are presented here in full; the other five are treated at length in the chapters where their subject belongs, and are summarized below with a reference to each.

The first step, which Koe calls the **pain and gain story**, builds the motivation, and he begins it with a prediction aimed at the viewer: you have watched this far, you are motivated, but "you will give up in two weeks like always." The reason is the principle that "all lasting behavior change is identity change," and identity does not change on a burst of motivation. So "you need to create a visceral, emotional, and specific story. Two stories to be exact. One for where your life will end up if you keep living in pain, and one for who you will become if you decide to change." This is "the foundation for having the motivation to actually change," and the reason the first step matters for surviving the second (same source). The two stories are the anti-vision and the vision of Chapter 7 in compressed, emotional form; Chapter 4 (section 4.2) develops the pain-and-gain story as a trigger of change, and Chapter 17 (section 17.3) the way Koe uses AI, fed with personal context, to dig it out.

The second step is the 30-day monk mode itself, and Koe opens it by restating why the first step matters: "If you've never done this before, it'll be difficult. And if you don't have a deep intrinsic reason to see it through, you will probably give up very quick" (same source). For thirty days you eliminate the four categories: binge tech (social media, streaming, gaming, news), unnatural sexual stimulation (porn and dating apps), consumables (alcohol, drugs, junk food, "sweetness in general") and thrill-seeking (gambling, shopping sprees, extreme sports). To the obvious question, "What will I do with my time then?", he replies: "That's the exact problem... You do what non-addicted people do. You move, you exercise, you talk to people, you prepare your food, you learn and build towards your goals. And when you aren't doing those things, you sit with the boredom until it's no longer boring."

The answer reframes the difficulty. The empty time that frightens people is not a side effect of the detox but its diagnosis: if removing four categories of stimulation leaves nothing, the stimulation was filling the space where a life should be. "What non-addicted people do" is an ordinary list on purpose; nothing in it is heroic. And the instruction to sit with boredom "until it's no longer boring" announces the theory of true boredom, which follows.

The remaining five steps fill the space the second one empties, and the list of "what non-addicted people do" is their outline: move, exercise, prepare your food, learn and build, and, at the end of the day, take stock (same source).

- *Step 3: walk.* Koe starts from the familiar target of 10,000 steps a day and corrects it: "10,000 steps a day was actually a marketing gimmick to help sell pedometers. The sweet spot for walking is actually 7,000 to 8,000 steps." For most untrained people walking is low-intensity cardio; it improves digestion, blood pressure and insulin sensitivity, brings sunlight for circadian health, and gives undistracted time to learn, reflect and generate ideas for deep work. His prescription is the **3 by 20 method**, "that's what I'm calling it": walk 20 minutes after each of the three major meals, which yields most of the benefits (probably 7,000–8,000 steps) and morning, afternoon and evening sunlight. Chapter 40 (section 40.2) develops it.
- *Step 4: go to the gym.* "When I look and feel terrible, I don't want to do anything with my life. I want to hide from the world," which leads to a downward spiral of letting go, binge eating and feeling worse. Everyone, he says, should do some form of resistance training, and 30-plus minutes of exercise "provides steady and sustainable dopamine release," which is why it belongs in a dopamine protocol. Chapter 40 (section 40.2) treats the case for lifting and Chapter 9 (section 9.1) the physiological claims.
- *Step 5: an elimination diet.* Everyone should do one at least once; it "nearly guarantees a drastic boost in energy," lowers inflammation, and after 30 days can rewire your eating habits. To keep it simple he recommends Stan Efferding's **vertical diet** (red meat, white rice, micronutrients from other sources), which is what he returns to "when I need to go back to a baseline." Chapter 40 (section 40.2) develops it.
- *Step 6: build a project.* "The best way to learn is to build a real-world project and only search for information when you need it"; "skip the tutorial phase, outline a project first." A project is "simply a structured way of achieving a goal," and it narrows your frame of reference so that "good dopamine" flags the information relevant to completing it: the earned dopamine of section 11.2, put to work. Chapter 9 (section 9.3) gives the definition and the steps to start a project, and Chapter 14 (section 14.3) the critique of tutorials.
- *Step 7: reflect for ten minutes every night*, "arguably the most important one that nobody does." Unprocessed experience hijacks attention at the worst moments, which Koe explains with the Zeigarnik effect; reflection closes those loops, turns mistakes "into data, rather than regrets," and after 30 days leaves "30 data points about what actually moves your life forward." The method: a non-negotiable alarm at 9:30 p.m. or 30 minutes before bed, the **3-2-1 framework** (three wins, two lessons, one intention for the morning), a physical notebook, and two minutes if ten feel like too much. Chapter 13 (section 13.4) develops it.

Koe then summarizes how the elements fit together in one sentence: "The dopamine detox clears the noise, the walking creates space, the gym builds confidence, the diet optimizes your biology, the project gives you purpose. Reflection is how you track mental and spiritual progress" (same source). The pain-and-gain story underlies all of them as the motivational foundation. | Step | Element | Function in the protocol | Developed in |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pain and gain story | Intrinsic reason to see the 30 days through | This section; Chapter 4 (4.2) |
| 2 | Dopamine detox (30-day monk mode) | Clears the noise | This section |

The structure follows the logic of this whole section: removal (the detox) is never alone. Each removal is paired with something that occupies the freed space, and the project, as in Chapter 9, supplies the goal without which freed energy becomes entropy.

Finally, Koe states what the detox is for, and the statement guards against the most likely misunderstanding (same source). "The ultimate goal isn't to become an ascetic or a monk or go off and live in the woods and never experience modern pleasures again. The goal is to develop **sovereignty and agency**": "the capacity to choose consciously rather than to react compulsively, the capacity to notice where your life is heading, generate new goals that align with the future you want, and act toward those goals without permission from anyone else." "A periodic detox is a **calibration tool** to keep you on track every 6 to 12 months." It creates the foundation for what Csikszentmihalyi ("our lord and savior," Koe jokes) considers "the highest human aspiration, a life composed of flow states where challenge and skill dance in perfect harmony, creating meaning through engaged attention and action."

The definition of sovereignty and agency gathers the book's earlier themes into one sentence: conscious choice instead of compulsive reaction (awareness, Chapter 2), noticing where life is heading (the anti-vision, Chapter 7), generating your own goals (Part IV), acting without permission (the theme of agency developed in Chapter 35). The final link to flow places the detox in service of Chapter 10's ideal, and it is the conclusion of the chain that opens the next subsection. A small inconsistency in frequency should be noted: earlier in the same video Koe says "at least once a year," and here "every 6 to 12 months." The two are compatible as a range.

#### Why the detox works: true boredom

In the 2025 video the explanation of why the detox works comes before the steps, and it is built as a chain (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26). The first link is the salience network of section 11.1: "whatever provides the most dopamine becomes the most important." The second link is borrowed from Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, whom Koe calls "the godfather of flow": flow requires "a specific skill to challenge ratio," with anxiety when the challenge is too high and boredom when it is too low ("you wouldn't fight a level 100 character when you are a level one"). Koe joins the two: "Overstimulation destroys the potential for flow states or enjoyment because it raises the bar for what you consider stimulating. Everything that is more boring than your phone, gambling, porn, partying, and the rest is automatically labeled as boring by your brain." That is why, he says, a dopamine detox "has become a well-marketed way of resetting your **baseline for stimulation**." The third link is the three reasons that follow, which explain how the reset happens. The last is the goal already quoted, sovereignty and a life "composed of flow states." The chain explains why the video ends on flow: the detox is a way of lowering the stimulation threshold until ordinary, appropriately difficult tasks can again produce flow. Chapter 10 (section 10.5) presents the Csikszentmihalyi link as the third variable of the flow model, the level of stimulation a person is accustomed to.

The 2025 detox video and a 2026 video on lost creativity share the explanation of the third link, why removing stimulation helps, and the 2026 version reuses many passages almost word for word. The explanation begins with a correction of how people use the word "bored."

"Boredom is how you really tap into creativity and a creative state. It's how you get ideas to just pop into your head. But then people say that they're bored all the time and they're not creative." Koe's answer: "Being chronically over-caffeinated and overstimulated is not boredom. You're so fried mentally that you've gone all the way off the deep end, and then you associate that with boredom because things that would previously give you euphoria or enjoyment don't even impact you anymore... you quite literally can't go any further away from true boredom. You have to go back." **True boredom** comes "after you go through your withdrawal period" (**Source:** `If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md`, 2026-03-28). The coinage distinguishes two states that feel alike from inside. The overstimulated mind feels bored because nothing registers; the truly bored mind feels bored because nothing is being supplied. The first is saturation, the second is emptiness, and only the second is creative. The phrase "you have to go back" makes withdrawal the path from one to the other.

Koe then gives three reasons why true boredom works (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26; `If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md`, 2026-03-28).

*First reason: "boredom is the gateway to novelty."* Koe cites Carl Jung, "OG psychologist," on the importance of "shadow work," which he describes as confronting the uncomfortable aspects of ourselves that we typically avoid; "sitting with boredom does just this." It activates "breakthrough insights when the rational mind stops trying to solve everything" and "reveals our authentic desires beneath external conditioning." It also sets the scene for three flow triggers that make it likely you will enter "a season of intense learning and building": deep embodiment (being present with discomfort), novelty (boredom forces you to seek new, healthier stimulation) and unpredictability ("not knowing what will emerge from the void"). The 2025 version ends with a chain: "You're bored because you don't have a quest. You don't have a quest because you're playing someone else's game. And counterintuitively, the solution is to stop playing any game and sit with your boredom until your own becomes clear." The 2026 version reuses the passage on Jung, but the transcript is cut by editing in the middle of the sentence on insights, so the exact claim of that version is uncertain; it adds nothing that the 2025 version lacks.

The chain about the quest connects boredom to the video-game model of Chapter 10. Overstimulation is the experience of playing a game designed by someone else (the feed, the job), and true boredom is what remains when you stop. The counterintuitive move is that the solution is not to find a better game immediately but to tolerate the absence of any game until your own appears, which is the subtractive path to vision described in Chapter 7 (section 7.4).

**Complementary context:** in Jung's psychology the "shadow" is the part of the personality containing traits and impulses the conscious ego does not acknowledge; "shadow work" is a later, popular term for the practice of bringing them to awareness. Koe's equation of sitting with boredom and shadow work is his adaptation. "Flow triggers" is a term associated with Steven Kotler's work on flow, which lists conditions such as novelty, unpredictability and embodiment; Koe does not name Kotler in this passage.

*Second reason: deprivation reverses the hedonic treadmill.* "The brain will upregulate dopamine receptors when deprived." The mechanism: "hedonic adaptation is your psychological thermostat. No matter how high or low the temperature goes, it always tries to return to the set point." This creates "what psychologists call the hedonic treadmill": you are always running toward the next source of pleasure, but the satisfaction never lasts; each experience becomes your new normal, requiring more intense stimulation to achieve the same high. When you deprive yourself, "the opposite happens. It's a **hedonic treadmill reversal**. Slowly, then rapidly, simple pleasures become enjoyable again." In 2025 Koe connects this to "what the Buddhists call beginner's mind": you notice the detail in the sky on a walk, "the hint of rosemary in a well-cooked meal." "Life becomes electric, as it should be." The 2026 version renames the effect **hedonic reversal**, drops the reference to beginner's mind, and adds a remark that makes it a matter of agency: you have noticed this before, "you just haven't registered it as something that you can actually do and control."

This reason closes the loop opened in section 11.1. The honeymoon phase described the normalization of a single acquisition; the hedonic treadmill describes the normalization of an entire level of stimulation. The thermostat image explains why turning up the stimulation never helps (the set point follows it up) and why turning it down does (the set point follows it down, and ordinary things exceed it again). The 2026 addition is the practical conclusion: the restoration of simple pleasure is not luck but something you can produce by deprivation. It also answers the 2026 complaint that "nothing is interesting" (section 11.2): the wall and the outlet become interesting again when the set point comes down.

**Complementary context:** "hedonic treadmill" was introduced by the psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell (1971) to describe the tendency to return to a relatively stable level of happiness after positive or negative changes; "hedonic adaptation" is the general term. "Beginner's mind" (*shoshin*) is a Zen concept popularized in the West by Shunryu Suzuki's *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind*. The specific claim that a month of abstinence upregulates dopamine receptors is stated without a source; "dopamine detox" is a popular practice, not a clinical protocol, and Koe's explanation should be read as his working model.

*Third reason: you don't need motivation, you need clarity.* Koe quotes a line he attributes to Naval: "All of humanity's problems stem from man's inability to sit quietly in a room alone." He asks when you last "created space for emergence," because "that's how reality works. Through chaos, or a change in perspective of chaos, order emerges" (he refers to an image on screen that the transcript does not describe). "A dopamine detox creates space for **sense-making**. That is processing and integrating experience." In the information age, modern technology "creates a **context collapse**." The brain processes "around 50 bits of information per second through our conscious attention" (the figure Chapter 6, section 6.1, discussed). Then the analogy that gives the coinage **mentally obese** its fullest meaning: binge food for a month and you gain fat; binge information from social media every day since installing the app and "you're walking around mentally obese. And you don't notice it because it's become normal. Homeostasis." Depriving yourself until you are bored forces you "to confront all of the problems that you suppressed over the years"; it is painful, "but if you sit with it long enough, you'll receive a **burst of clarity** that launches you into a new phase of life" (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26). The 2026 version repeats the quote and the core sentence ("boredom creates space for sense-making, that is, processing and integrating experience") and draws the simplest conclusion: "So if you're consuming too much information, you just need to stop" (**Source:** `If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md`, 2026-03-28).

This reason connects the detox to the theory of Chapter 5. "You don't need motivation, you need clarity" restates the claim of section 5.2 that the remedy for psychic entropy is order, not energy. Clarity is not produced by more input but by processing input already received, and processing requires unoccupied attention, which a mind receiving fifty bits per second of feed does not have. "Homeostasis" explains why the condition is invisible: like body fat accumulated gradually, mental overload becomes the new normal and stops registering as a problem. The "burst of clarity" is the promise; the suppressed problems are its price.

**Complementary context:** the line about sitting quietly in a room alone is generally traced to Blaise Pascal's *Pensées* (seventeenth century), where Pascal writes that all of humanity's unhappiness comes from not knowing how to stay quietly in a room. Naval Ravikant has quoted the idea, which may explain Koe's attribution; the corpus records the attribution as Koe's and gives no other source.

#### The three narrowers of the mind

The 2026 video that reuses the theory of boredom places it inside a broader diagnosis of lost creativity, and it introduces a coinage that Koe admits is awkward: "In my eyes, there are three narrowers of the mind. We'll call these the **three narrowers of the mind**. That one's difficult, but that's what we're calling it" (**Source:** `If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md`, 2026-03-28). They are conditioning, productivity as a priority, and infinite input with zero processing time. Each buries the mind's natural creative capacity by narrowing it.

The first, conditioning, is the subject of Chapter 1. The third, infinite input with zero processing, is the subject of this chapter so far: it is the context collapse and the mental obesity just described. The second, productivity as a priority, is new here, and Koe develops it through his own situation.

"The trap that I fall into, and everyone falls into, is that they just feel like they're falling behind." The occasion is artificial intelligence: Anthropic and its Claude models "just pushing out updates," "this **speed tunnel**," "you just can't keep up." His own software company, he says, "made the mistake of competing with AI companies in general" and constantly felt it had to keep up; "so we're taking a step back and looking at what's going to be here no matter what. What are the human things that we need to help facilitate, like creativity, so that we aren't just constantly stuck in this hype cycle." Then the conclusion: "If you're being real, you're never going to catch up. So what's the only way out? Creativity." And the mechanism: a deadline is "a productivity hack if you choose it, if you implement it yourself. But if there's just this **invisible deadline** in your life all the time, that just leads to a constantly stressed and narrowed mind" (same source).

The distinction between chosen and invisible deadlines is the most useful element of the passage, and it prepares Chapter 12, where deadlines appear as a tool of focused work. A deadline you set narrows attention toward a task for a bounded time; a deadline you merely feel, with no end and no task, narrows attention toward anxiety without limit. The AI "speed tunnel" is a case of the second kind: a race defined by others in which the finish line keeps moving. The admission that his company fell into it is an example of Koe applying the critique to himself.

The next sentence makes the claim that will most directly qualify Chapter 12: "If your life isn't centered around efficiency and optimization in today's world, everyone just thinks you're useless. In other words, if you aren't a robot, you're useless. But that's exactly what creativity demands. It demands **useless wandering**, true boredom, or creating space for the right idea to emerge that will take you much further than the productivity bros stuck in the same race as everyone else. People who schedule every hour of their day don't stumble onto anything" (same source).

The corpus records a tension here. Five months later, in a 2026 video on strategic thinking, Koe promotes Musashi's precept "do nothing useless." He does not reconcile the two. They can be read on different planes: what is useless from the standpoint of productivity (wandering, boredom) may be useful from the standpoint of creative strategy, and Musashi's "useless" refers to actions that do not serve one's aim. But the reconciliation is an interpretation, and the opposition of formulations is real.

#### The seven-day creative reset

For the person whose creative capacity has been narrowed, the 2026 video proposes a one-week protocol, explicitly smaller than the 30-day detox. Koe begins with a principle about how to address any problem: "How do you actually prioritize this thing? And it's the same way you get results in anything. We look at the problem, which is being mentally overweight or mentally bloated or overstimulated, and then we design a system that results in the alleviation of those things. And **true change is behavior change**. The only way to solve a problem for good is to change your own behavior." He adds why a video like his can help: "a lot of the times you don't notice your own problems," and a video "can shine a light of awareness on something you were blind to before. And sure, you can ask ChatGPT for this stuff, but you can't ask ChatGPT what you don't know to ask" (**Source:** `If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md`, 2026-03-28). The remark about ChatGPT restates the awareness principle of Chapter 2 in the context of AI: a tool that answers questions cannot supply the question you have not thought to ask, and the first step of any change is noticing the problem. "All change is behavior change" is a principle Koe states in several forms between 2023 and 2026, with the corollary that systems are organized behavior changes.

The protocol is divided into four stages (same source).

| Days | Stage (Koe's framing) | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Reduce input fast ("intermittent fasting for your mind") | Strict work blocks with an alarm that ends work; cut your primary input source and replace it with nothing; walk without headphones or phone |
| 3–4 | Digest | Read one chapter "way too slowly"; sit with nothing for 10 minutes; walk and actually see things |
| 5–6 | Become interested in life again | Romanticize things; don't take notes; have one real conversation; extend the walk |
| 7 | Create from abundance | Make something with no plan; don't share it for at least 24 hours |

*Days 1–2: reduce input fast.* The first action is strict time blocks. "If you can, you want to limit work to 4 hours a day for this next week. And if you can't, just set an alarm that marks the end of your work. And when it goes off, you're done. You're not doing one last task. Your job is to not think about work or productivity when you're not working. You're practicing the skill of letting something feel unfinished without anxiety." The skill named in the last sentence is the antidote to the invisible deadline: the ability to leave things open without the openness consuming attention (the open loops of Chapter 6, section 6.1). The second action targets the **primary input source**: "like the junk food in the cabinet at night, you want to pick the one source you reach for the most mindlessly and then eliminate that" (the commute podcast, the scroll before bed or in the morning, the morning news) "and just replace it with nothing. Sit in silence. Listen for an idea." The third action is a walk, "because ideas are caught in motion. So don't use any headphones, and even leave your phone at home... just trust the process." The mechanism he gives: "removing constant input allows your brain's default mode network, which is the brain's wandering system, to fire. This is the network responsible for random insight, self-reflection and imagining the future. It cannot be active while you're consuming." The choice of a single source rather than all sources is a deliberate contrast with the 30-day detox: the week-long reset removes the most mindless input, not every input.

**Complementary context:** the default mode network is a set of brain regions that tends to be more active during rest, mind-wandering, self-referential thought and imagining the future, and less active during externally focused tasks. The claim that it "cannot be active while you're consuming" is a simplification; Koe uses the network as a mechanism for why unoccupied time produces ideas, a theme Chapter 13 develops in relation to rest.

*Days 3–4: digest.* With space created, "it may feel like a flood of ideas, kind of chaotic, because your mind is opening up": unfinished thoughts, memories, suppressed feelings, random connections, forgotten ideas. The goal now is to digest and "notice the deeper layers of reality." First, read one chapter slowly, "like way too slowly. You're not even trying to learn something. You're just trying to simply notice when a sentence makes you stop and think. When it does, you just put the book down. You sit with why that line hit you." Koe generalizes the practice: "In my eyes, this is the best way to read in general. You don't have to finish books. Just get what you need and put the book down. That one idea will impact you more than the entire book if you actually think about it." This happens once a day, in the morning. Second, stacked right after: sit with nothing for ten minutes. "Call it meditation, sure, but I don't want you to use a meditation app or any guided breathing technique. Just sit, let your mind do whatever it wants. It will be chaotic at first, but that's the digestion happening." Third, keep walking with the same rules, "but this time try to actually see things": detail in the sidewalk, the structure of tree branches, the vastness of the sky; "I bet you didn't even look up the last walk." The purpose of the stage is to "release the grip that you have on your subconscious," which is responsible for processing background tasks and surfacing "aha moments," something best done by "stepping away from task-oriented work." And Koe draws a conclusion about productivity: "If those moments of insight allow you to do less but higher-leveraged work, aren't you being more productive by not trying to be productive?"

The reading instruction anticipates Chapter 16, where reading is treated in full; its principle here is digestion over accumulation, the same contrast as one lecture over several days versus 500 ideas a day. The refusal of meditation apps follows the logic of the stage: a guided technique is another input, and the point is to sit without one.

*Days 5–6: become interested in life again.* Koe recalls that as a child he would say he was not really interested in anything; now his reaction is "haven't you looked around?" His example is the camera recording him: "this piece of technology is just incredible. I don't know anything about it. I don't know how to recreate one. It's a work of art, genius, mass-produced at scale, and it allows me to talk to you asynchronously." If he lets his mind go down that rabbit hole without letting beliefs ("this is just an immature line of thinking") stop him, he reaches ideas: how it was done, how to replicate it, how to apply something in it to his business, "or even that's going down the productive line of thinking, but just let yourself go crazy." "All of life is interesting." By this stage, he says, the fog lifts, colors are more vivid, "small things feel meaningful again. When was the last time you enjoyed a breath of fresh air?" Three actions follow. First, "trust that ideas will come back": "resist the urge to take notes on everything. If it's important, it will find its way back to you." He explains with his own practice: with his co-founders he is building idea-capture software, and he notes that he does not go back to ideas older than seven days; "if it's important, it will find its way back to the top of my notes." "Practice not writing something down, stay in the stream of consciousness, learn to trust your own mind." Second, "have one real conversation. No catching up for 5 minutes before your next meeting. No networking that feels fake. We're trying to remove the performative act." Listen and be present; "your brain may just light up." Third, extend the walk: "you might find that you don't want to stop."

The camera passage is the 2026 version of "everything is interesting if you look deep enough" (section 11.2), now placed at the point of the protocol where the hedonic reversal should have begun. Romanticizing, in Koe's sense, is not idealizing but attending to the depth of ordinary things.

*Day 7: create from abundance.* "Why we waited for 7 days to actually create something is because most people try to create from a depleted state, or just an overstimulated state, and then everything just feels forced." Koe offers his own case. He has created content consistently for five years (long-form, and short-form for longer), and in the month before the video "it just felt forced"; many times in his career he tried "to force it out anyways, because I wasn't in this creative way of living." After six days of "creating space, processing information and letting connections form, we can actually create from a **place of abundance**." Two actions. First, "make something with no plan. Write, draw, record a 20-minute voice note, or cook without a recipe. The only rule is no rules. No strategic thinking or trying to find the perfect angle. Just start a thread and follow it." Second, "don't share it. I know this is antithetical to everything that I preach, but we need to remember what it's like to not have **silent opinions** influencing your direction. Notice how it feels to have made something that's yours." After at least 24 hours you may share it.

He explains the principle behind day seven: "What we're doing on that seventh day is we're separating **generative thinking** from **evaluative thinking**. Normally this happens in tandem. When you're writing an article or a newsletter intentionally, you're trying to produce novel ideas and criticize them and edit them at the same time. But when you do that, you suppress the generative thinking's potential. The level of novel and creative ideas you can have is kind of suppressed by the part of your mind that is critiquing them and judging them and telling you that they're worthless" (same source).

**Complementary context:** the separation of idea generation from evaluation is a long-standing principle in creativity research and practice; it appears, for example, in Alex Osborn's rule of deferring judgment in brainstorming and in J. P. Guilford's distinction between divergent and convergent thinking. Koe does not cite these sources.

Two instructions in the protocol contradict Koe's usual advice, and he acknowledges one of them. Not taking notes runs against his repeated recommendation, elsewhere in the corpus, to capture ideas so they are not lost; not sharing runs against his recommendation to publish in order to get feedback, which he himself calls "antithetical to everything that I preach." Both exceptions are declared and limited to the protocol. They make sense within its logic. Capture and publication are evaluative and social acts; during a week designed to free generation from evaluation and from "silent opinions," they would reintroduce exactly what the reset removes. The everyday practice and the protocol serve different states of the mind.

#### Some ideas must be contemplated, not practiced

Two older passages complete the picture of what unoccupied time is for, and both qualify the action-centered advice that dominates Koe's work. The first, from 2024, is presented as an exception to his usual emphasis on applying knowledge. In value creation, he says, you need to make knowledge valuable, and "there's also a trap there": only so many things can be practiced tangibly. Some ideas you must "munch on and contemplate": go on walks, and "let your default mode network, let your subconscious mind bring ideas to your conscious mind, so you have firepower to write with." Sit with a topic, contemplate, introspect, self-reflect: what is the true value of this message, and what has it helped me with in my life? (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). The corpus records that this passage immediately nuances a warning in the same video against accumulating knowledge without applying it; the two belong together. The rule is application, and the exception is that some knowledge is applied by being thought through until it yields something to write. "Firepower" is the material that the default mode network surfaces and the writer then uses, which connects this chapter's practices of empty time to the writing process of Part VIII.

The second passage, also from 2024, uses a borrowed idea to justify Koe's own way of working. To people who ask how he gets work done while walking hours a day, he points to his videos on the 4-Hour Workday, to his book *The Art of Focus*, or to chapter 7 of Nassim Nicholas Taleb's *Antifragile*, which gave him a new perspective on his philosophy: procrastination as a natural filter, a way to separate signal from noise. He works on consistent things every morning but otherwise "doesn't really work unless he has to," because "if I don't have to work on something, then it's usually just noise" or distraction, and he could be doing better things: walking, sunlight, health, happiness (**Source:** `The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md`, 2024-06-16).

The Taleb idea is adapted rather than reproduced. In Koe's version, the reluctance to do a task is information: if a task can wait indefinitely without consequence, it was probably noise. It is the same signal-and-noise problem that opened this chapter, now applied to tasks instead of stimuli. The filter only works with a structure around it (his "consistent things every morning"), which is why the passage points to the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12: procrastination is a filter inside a protected core of work, not a substitute for it.

**Complementary context:** in *Antifragile* (2012), chapter 7 ("Naive Intervention"), Taleb argues that procrastination can act as a natural defense against unnecessary intervention, letting events filter out what does not need doing. Koe applies the argument to the organization of his own workday.

### 11.5 Interrupting Autopilot

#### The three seconds when the hand reaches for the phone

The tools of sections 11.3 and 11.4 all act on conditions: the feed, the environment, the month, the week. A 2026 passage identifies their limit with unusual precision. Finding a reason to change, or changing your environment, happens *outside* the moment of the urge to remain your past self. These things create better conditions, "but they don't help you in the 3 seconds when your hand is reaching for your phone," and it is not only the phone; "it's everything" (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02).

Koe's answer for those three seconds is paradoxical: "The way that you detach from old patterns is to do nothing. You increase the gap, because doing nothing is the only way that you can interrupt the survival mechanism in real time." He frames the idea with a line from a social media audio, "break the pattern today or you'll repeat the loop tomorrow," and then states the task: "Your job is to **starve the old self** that is trying to survive of the normal feedback it receives." The practice is observation throughout the day. Are you repeating yesterday on autopilot? When you read an opinion, do you solidify your stance by arguing with it in your head? And the mechanism of change: "When you sit with this uncomfortable feeling without making a decision, you train your nervous system to tolerate the gap between impulse and action. From that neutral state, it's much easier to make a conscious choice in the right direction" (same source).

The **gap between impulse and response** is the coinage, and it connects this section to the model of identity built in Chapters 3 and 4. The "survival mechanism" is the law of conceptual survival: the old self survives by receiving its normal feedback (the phone checked, the stance reinforced, yesterday repeated). Each automatic response feeds it. Doing nothing in the gap withholds the feedback, and an identity that does not receive its feedback weakens, as an unused habit does. The example of arguing with an opinion in your head is important because it shows that the mechanism is not limited to consumption: defending a belief is also a way of feeding the old self. The "neutral state" is what the gap produces. It is not the right choice itself but the condition in which a choice can be made consciously rather than automatically.

The corpus records a nuance between this passage and one from 2025, which Chapter 6 (section 6.6) presented: "Go to the party, get drunk... because denying those desires is only going to bind you to them," with the condition that you must be able to realize when those things are a mistake. Starving the old self in 2026 sounds like the denial the 2025 passage rejected. The source does not reconcile them. The compatible reading, which Chapter 6 already proposed, distinguishes repression imposed from outside (a rule you obey while the desire becomes the focus of attention) from the interruption of an automatism from inside (awareness holding the gap open until a choice can be made). The first binds; the second frees. Whether the distinction holds in practice is a fair question, and the exercises at the end of this chapter return to it.

#### Tools for the gap, and the religion of doing the thing

The same 2026 video names the popular practices that train the gap and warns against what has happened to them. Meditation, cold showers or cold exposure, and fasting "become popularized and kind of bastardized from what they're supposed to be... those things have become religions. That's the exact opposite of what we're trying to accomplish." His rule: "anything that helps you delay gratification is useful, but just don't join the **religion of doing the thing**." You do not need an opinion on whether cold showers are good or bad; "they're just a tool for increasing the gap." And he adds a personal judgment: aside from meditation, he does not think you need any of them (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02).

The warning applies the theory of identity to the tools of self-improvement themselves. A practice that becomes a religion becomes an identity ("I am a cold-shower person"), and an identity, by the law of conceptual survival, defends itself, producing opinions, arguments and tribal loyalty: exactly the automatic, stance-reinforcing behavior the gap was meant to interrupt. The same pattern appeared in section 11.4, in Koe's critique of monk mode: a tool for a period of intensity became a lifestyle and then a caricature. The criterion he keeps is functional: does it help you delay gratification, that is, widen the gap? If so, use it; if it has become something to believe in, it has stopped doing its job. The exception for meditation is stated without argument in this passage; it is consistent with the rest of the chapter, where sitting with nothing is the core of both the detox and the seven-day reset.

#### Reminders through the day

A 2025 protocol gives a concrete way to interrupt autopilot at intervals rather than waiting for the moment of the urge. It is the second part of a one-day protocol whose first part consists of reflective questions about the life you have and the life you want (the anti-vision and vision exercises of Chapter 7). Koe introduces it bluntly: "Those journaling exercises are cute, but we want real change," and real change requires breaking current unconscious patterns. Throughout the day you contemplate everything from part one, letting questions trigger questions and thoughts trigger thoughts, "like a meditation on a mantra," without getting distracted by tasks or by the phone. To make the contemplation recur, you create reminders or calendar events with pointed questions (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28):

| Time | Question |
|---|---|
| 11:00 a.m. | What am I avoiding right now by doing what I'm doing? |
| 1:30 p.m. | If someone filmed the last 2 hours, what would they conclude I want from my life? |
| 3:00–3:15 p.m. | Am I moving toward the life I hate or the life I want? |
| 5:00 p.m. | What's the most important thing I'm pretending isn't important? |
| 7:30 p.m. | What did I do today out of identity protection rather than genuine desire? (His hint: most things.) |
| 9:00 p.m. | When did I feel most alive today, and when most dead? |

**Interrupt autopilot** is Koe's name for the practice. Each question is designed to catch the automatic self in the act. The 1:30 question is the most ingenious: it replaces your account of what you want with the evidence of your behavior, as an outside observer would read it, which is the same move as reading hidden goals through excitement in section 11.1 (behavior as an arrow pointing to the goal actually running). The 7:30 question names the mechanism of Chapters 3 and 4 directly ("identity protection"), and the hint that the answer is "most things" is a reminder of how much of a day runs on conceptual survival. The 3:00 question uses the anti-vision and vision as a compass. The protocol works by placing the gap on a schedule: the reminder interrupts whatever is happening and forces a moment of the neutral observation described above.

#### Breaking a cycle on purpose

An older and more extreme method, from 2022, interrupts not a single impulse but a whole bodily cycle, in order to see the mind's reaction. Koe proposes, calling it "a bad and extreme example, but doable, you're gonna live," breaking the sleep cycle: "don't sleep for a night, break the sleep cycle, wake up the next day and notice how your mind reacts, notice how it tries to revert back." He acknowledges that much of what happens is biological, but says you will make interesting discoveries about yourself and the world. For personal cycles he offers a milder alternative: intermittent fasting, or a full fast of one day. The aim is to break the cycles you control in order to notice what you had not noticed (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20).

The method belongs to the same 2022 video in which Koe described cheap dopamine as "part of your cycle" (section 11.2), and its logic is the logic of the Matrix in Chapter 1: what runs continuously cannot be seen; a break makes it visible. Breaking a cycle on purpose is a small "glitch in the matrix," the phrase Koe later uses for the holistic monk mode. The safety of the specific example is his responsibility, and he flags it himself as extreme; the principle (deliberately disrupt a routine and watch how the mind tries to restore it) does not require it, and the one-day fast is offered as the gentler version.

#### Alcohol: moderation or abstinence

One substance receives sustained attention in the corpus, and it is the only topic in this chapter on which Koe and his guests openly disagree. The discussion spans four years and three conversations, and the disagreement is left open.

The earliest passage is a guest's protocol. In a 2021 conversation, Koe asks Justin Welsh whether he did anything to regain control before leaving his job. Welsh says he went alcohol-free for 90 days "to kind of reset my balance." He clarifies the reason: not because he liked to "chug alcohol," but because "it was a coping mechanism for the stress that I felt at my job." Having already decided to leave, he wanted to "clear my head." He describes a chain: no alcohol, then "I eat better when I'm not drinking alcohol," then better food choices, then better sleep ("not in the beginning, but it starts to come... much better, much cleaner") (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20). The chain makes removing the coping mechanism the first domino: one subtraction improves several systems at once. This is Welsh's experience, not Koe's.

Koe responds with his own. As a young man who partied in college, he caught himself "trying to justify my alcohol consumption," which was "not a lot by any means," perhaps once every two weeks on the weekend. His realization: stopping "alone, it does give you more of that sense of control, because the brain fog and other things can last up to a week, and you're just not making the progress that you could" (same source). He links this to Welsh's framing of burnout as a loss of control. The observation is the same as in the Seattle story of section 11.4: the cost of a single night is not the night but the days of reduced capacity after it.

By 2023 Koe had taken a different route. In a long conversation with Dickie Bush he says he stopped drinking for a while and then reintroduced alcohol to "tackle it": he did not see himself "winning the alcohol battle by getting rid of it completely"; "it was more so, okay, how do I moderate this so I can have that drink at the table." Previously, once a week he would go out intending eight to ten drinks, planned into his calories; now he drinks on occasion, and roughly every three months there is "a hard reset," which he sometimes did not like, so he stops for a while ("not a very conducive reminder to my health"). He describes how he questioned his own habits: hearing that Alan Watts died of alcoholism made him ask "is he fake," which led him down rabbit holes, reading books on the philosophy of drinking and exposing himself to the other side. Without recommending anything, he thinks he won, and the evidence he offers is a single ordinary scene: a dinner out and a first martini: "we went out to dinner, I had a drink, it was awesome" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

Bush answers from a different experience. Earlier in the same conversation he mentions that he has gone fifteen months without alcohol, after a decade, since early high school, in which he had probably not gone more than a week without it, a habit he calls "extremely normalized." From that experience he states the opposite position, with a metaphor: "complete abstinence from anything is easier than slight moderation." In an ice bath, "it's easier to stay still in the ice bath than it is to move around, but it feels like it's harder"; flailing your arms the whole time is much harder. So cutting alcohol to zero is "actually easier to do what I'm doing than it would be to do what you've done." He does not expect to stay at zero for life; zero is meant to "reset the slate and then break the frame." He explains his reason in terms of his stage: he is in a season where "every day that I lose is costing my future self in a big way," with the goal of taking his company, Ship 30 for 30, to eight figures by the end of the year, a goal he describes as "a literal number of hours away" of execution. Koe concedes the key point: cutting to zero is better when building, because you can allocate all your energy elsewhere, and it is a conscious decision (same source).

**Complementary context:** Dickie Bush is a writer and entrepreneur, co-founder of Ship 30 for 30, a writing program for online creators. The ice-bath image and the claim that abstinence is easier than moderation are his. The idea resembles a distinction sometimes drawn between "abstainers" and "moderators" (popularized by Gretchen Rubin), according to which some people find total abstention easier than limited use and others the reverse; neither speaker cites it.

The ice-bath metaphor is precise about what makes moderation hard: every occasion requires a decision, and each decision is a small movement in cold water. Abstinence removes the decisions. This is the same argument as "it's easier to change everything at once" in the holistic monk mode, applied to a single substance, and it explains why Koe accepts zero during a building phase even while defending his own moderation.

In 2025 Koe gives a more candid account of his own difficulty. Speaking with Sahil Bloom, he says he drank a lot in his first two years of college, then "battled back and forth" while outgrowing much of what his friend group did; "there's nothing wrong with either path. It's just we split ways." Online he heard polarizing views: someone he respected said "zero alcohol, it has no place in your life," others said "you can manage two drinks." He clarifies that he was not an alcoholic; "it was just like I couldn't find that balance. When I did drink, even if it was every two weeks, every month, every three months, it would either be **all or nothing**, because I couldn't find that happiness or that enjoyment of just having one drink with a friend." He suggests this may be a particular battle for people who began by drinking heavily (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30).

The 2025 account nuances both earlier ones. The 2021 version presented stopping as a gain in control; the 2023 version presented moderation as a victory ("I won the alcohol battle"); the 2025 version describes an inability to find balance. Read together, the three passages describe a person for whom moderation was the aim and remained difficult, which is close to Bush's prediction. The corpus records that in the same conversation Bloom questions the "zero alcohol" movement for the social connection it costs and proposes a happy medium, and that later in 2025 Koe says getting drunk is not a mistake if you have no meaningful responsibilities the next morning (Chapter 6, section 6.6). The tension remains open between the author and his guests, and within the author between abstinence and permission.

| Position | Who holds it | Reason given |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary abstinence as a reset | Justin Welsh (2021) | Alcohol was a coping mechanism; removing it improved eating and sleep |
| Stopping gives a sense of control | Koe (2021) | Brain fog lasting up to a week slows progress |
| Moderation as the goal | Koe (2023) | He did not see winning as elimination; wanted "that drink at the table," like the martini at dinner |
| Abstinence is easier than moderation | Dickie Bush (2023) | The ice bath: staying still is easier than moving; zero during a building season; fifteen months without alcohol |
| Zero is better while building | Koe, conceding (2023) | All energy can be allocated elsewhere |
| Difficulty finding balance | Koe (2025) | "All or nothing" for someone who began by drinking heavily |

The most defensible resolution is that the answer depends on the stage. A radical reset suits a period of abuse or intense building; a middle path suits someone already stable. That resolution is consistent with the rest of this chapter. Every tool in it (the detox, the disappearance, war mode, the seven-day reset) is bounded in time and tied to a phase, and none of them is presented as a permanent rule for life. Alcohol is simply the case where the bounded and the permanent are hardest to tell apart, which is why it remains the most argued example.

#### From recovered attention to focused work

The chapter has moved from a molecule to a moment. Dopamine, as Koe uses the term, is the signal of what matters to the goals your mind is running; cheap dopamine is that signal fired by received novelty with no action attached, and earned dopamine is the same signal attached to progress you create. What you consume programs which goals run, through the algorithm and through the standards your environment makes normal; curation, disappearance, the detox and the reset are ways of changing the inputs and clearing the noise; and the gap between impulse and response is where the old programming is starved in real time. Every one of these tools frees attention. None of them says what to do with it. Section 11.4 warned that freed energy without a goal becomes psychic entropy, and the detox, the six-month plan and the creative reset all paired removal with a project. Chapter 12 takes up that pairing directly: how the attention recovered here is organized into a few hours of focused, high-leverage work a day.

### Exercises

1. **Map your gaps.** List five things you currently want that you encountered through a screen (a possession, a body, a lifestyle, an income, a relationship). For each, apply Koe's psychology-of-desire graphic: estimate the length of the "line" between you and the object, and say whether you have a plan or path across it. Then use the deconstruction exercise of section 11.2 to state what lies "under" each object (the experience or feeling you actually want). Which of the five, once deconstructed, turns out to be reachable by a different route, and which turns out not to be wanted at all?

2. **Read your hidden goals backward.** For three days, note every moment of genuine excitement or "novel insight" you feel while consuming (a book, a post, a conversation), and what triggered it. At the end, apply Koe's claim that excitement "is an arrow pointing to the goal that your mind is operating on": infer, from the pattern, which goals your mind is actually running. Compare them with the goals you would state if asked. Where they diverge, decide which list better predicts how you spent your time, and what that implies for the 1:30 p.m. question of section 11.5.

3. **Test the three goal scenarios.** Koe argues that the same scrolling produces no pain, painful negative feedback or reinforcing positive feedback depending on the goal it is measured against. Choose one habit you consider bad and describe it under each of the three scenarios in his table. Then identify the conditions he names for the habit to become self-correcting (a goal it visibly damages, energy already invested). Which condition is missing in your case, and what would supply it?

4. **Audit and rebuild a feed.** Take one platform you use daily. Classify the last fifty items you saw using Koe's three marks of organic content (useful toward a self-generated goal, long-form or a puzzle piece of long-form, authentic) and the "talking to a social media feed" test. Then design a rebuild using either the 2023 method (selective unfollowing and slow refollowing) or the 2024 method (unfollow everyone, then expand from one node through comments and follows). Defend your choice of method for your situation, and state in advance what result after one month would show it worked.

5. **Choose between monk mode and war mode.** Describe your current situation (work, obligations, energy, the main goal you are pursuing) and decide whether the next three months call for withdrawal (disappear, automatic no, the holistic monk mode's defense), for attack (war mode's "say yes to everything"), or for neither. Justify the decision using the risks Koe names for each (psychic entropy without a vision; burnout if prolonged) and the corpus's unresolved tension between changing "everything at once" and changing gradually. What would make you switch modes partway through?

6. **Examine the detox's claims.** Koe gives three reasons a dopamine detox works: boredom as a gateway to novelty, the reversal of the hedonic treadmill, and space for sense-making. For each, separate what he presents as a mechanism, what he presents as a metaphor and what he borrows from others (Jung, the psychology of hedonic adaptation, the quotation he attributes to Naval). Which reason would remain persuasive if its neuroscientific language turned out to be oversimplified, and why? Design a small personal test (a week, not a month) that could give you evidence for or against one of them.

7. **Reconcile "starve the old self" with "denying desires binds you to them."** Write a short argument that either reconciles Koe's 2025 and 2026 positions or shows that they genuinely conflict. Use a concrete case from your own life (a desire you have tried to deny, or one you have watched without acting on). What, in your case, distinguished repression imposed from outside from the interruption of an automatism from inside, if anything did?

8. **Decide on a substance or a stimulus.** Choose one consumable or stimulus you use regularly (alcohol, caffeine, nicotine, a game, a type of content). Using the positions in the alcohol table of section 11.5, decide whether moderation or abstinence fits you at your current stage, and defend the decision with the arguments of the person whose position you adopt (the ice bath, the sense of control, the drink at the table, "all or nothing"). Then state the conditions under which you would reverse the decision.
