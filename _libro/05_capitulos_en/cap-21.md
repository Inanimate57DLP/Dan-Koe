## Chapter 21 — Writing as the Base Skill

Part VII settled where a creator speaks from: the niche is the person, the ideal customer is the past self, and the generalist who follows a goal across fields is the profile the period rewards. That leaves the question of *how* a person's perspective reaches anyone else. Koe's answer, repeated across five years of videos with remarkably little variation, is a single skill: writing. This chapter presents writing as he understands it, which is much broader than the school subject or the literary craft. For Koe, writing is the foundation of all online media, the most accessible form of leverage available to an individual, the apparatus with which a mind thinks, and the daily practice that turns a life into a public body of work.

The chapter builds that case in five steps. Section 21.1 explains why Koe calls writing the base skill or "meta skill" of media: the argument from "code or media," his definition of digital writing, writing as the leverage that unlocks other leverage, the evergreen skills, the claim that everyone is already a writer, the reasons to start with writing rather than video, and Koe's own business as a case of a company resting on one to two hours of morning writing. Section 21.2 turns inward: writing as thinking, through his late metaphors of "forced linearity" and "Legos with ideas," the read–write–build rule, the guest testimony of Sahil Bloom, and his 2026 argument for essays as "slow content" in an environment of "fast content." Section 21.3 explains what writing does in the market: content as a magnet that attracts people to a product, the "idea warfare on mental real estate," the coined measure "time under attention," and the thesis that "ideas are the new oil." Section 21.4 describes the creator as a way of life rather than a job: documenting one's mind, the public journal, "brand is what you do," and positioning oneself as a student, experimenter or leader on the path. Section 21.5 closes with a guest's widening of the argument: Devon Eriksen's claim that "stories are the DNA of societies."

The chapter depends on Part VI. Chapter 14 argued that building and teaching consolidate learning; Chapter 16 described reading, notes and the second brain; Chapter 17 described thinking; Chapter 18 described creativity, synthesis and articulation. Writing is the activity in which all of these meet. It also depends on Chapter 5: Koe describes writing as a way to "order consciousness," one's own and other people's. The chapter prepares Chapter 22 (what to write), Chapter 23 (structure and attention) and Chapter 24 (process and content ecosystem), and, beyond Part VIII, the treatment of audience in Part IX and of business in Part X.

### 21.1 Writing as the Foundation of Media

#### The starting argument: code or media

The oldest form of Koe's case for writing is an argument about the structure of the internet. It appears in a 2023 video whose title states the claim ("I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day") and again, at greater length, in the 2024 compilation "The One-Person Business Model (Full Guide)." It runs as a chain.

First, the internet offers almost unlimited leverage: the possibility of reaching anyone. To use it, Koe says, one must learn one of two broad pathways. "The back end of the internet is code, the internet runs on code; the front end, what you see, is media." The future, in his 2023 formulation, "belongs to those that master the internet; they master media or code."

Second, code has largely become a solved problem for most people. No-code website builders, organizational tools such as Notion and the social platforms themselves mean that anyone "smart and able to network" can build a site and reach anyone. What once required a team of web developers (a membership platform for a course or a community, for example) can now be assembled in ten to sixty minutes. Koe adds that AI is "coming in" as well, and that most people should "leave that to the passionate coders." He says this as a former web developer who chose not to follow his own original path.

Third, media is therefore where most people should invest. Media is "the front end of the internet where people see what catches attention, what leads into products or services... that's what drives sales, that's what puts money in your pockets." The logic connects three things: attention, a product and money. To make money outside a job one needs a product or a service (a course, a physical product such as a bag of coffee, freelance writing or another media or marketing skill); to sell it one needs people to see it; and what makes people see is media.

Fourth, "the foundation of media is writing." Tweets and threads are writing. Newsletters and blogs are writing. YouTube videos come from a written script, and they come out better that way, because writing first organizes the message. Online advertisements are usually built on text. Even TikToks and Reels are often writing performed aloud: Koe's own short videos are frequently him reading a tweet from his phone, a choice he explains in an August 2022 version of the argument: he reads the tweet "because the message is more impactful." Everything visible online, in this view, is "writing transformed." Hence his conclusion that writing is "the best skill you can learn."

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The 2024 compilation also repeats two personal reasons that were already present in that August 2022 passage ("Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years"); they are not a later addition. He claims that high-impact writing will outperform "any dude with six-pack abs and a poor message," and offers his own Instagram as evidence: he grew it (to 230,000 followers in the 2022 passage and in one segment of the compilation, 240,000 in another segment, recorded at a different date) by posting screenshots of tweets, without a single physique photo. And he prefers writing because it is "more private": it lets him dedicate himself to a craft and a message instead of joining what he calls "the sausage fest online," the competition for attention through looks.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

#### Everything online starts with writing

The same claim reappears in many videos, each time with a longer list. The earliest version, from August 2022, already contains the full structure: media is "what people see on the internet and what catches attention," and "the foundation of media is writing" because nearly every format either is writing or "do[es] the best from a written script because you can organize the message." By 2024 the list has grown to include cold emails, social captions, landing pages, product descriptions, course modules, client communications, customer resources, ads of every kind and Instagram images, which "usually come from a well-written quote." Koe's summary: "Everything that your audience, customers and network sees starts with writing."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

Two consequences follow from the list. The first is that "every single person in business is a writer at their core; they just don't label themselves as that." In an October 2023 video that opened what Koe called his "digital writer series," he puts the same point as an equivalence: "if you're interested in the one-person business, then you're interested in writing, because... writing is the foundation of everything." Whatever one does in business, "you're going to have to write and you should get good at it... almost every single day." The second consequence is a description of what working online actually consists of. Koe estimates that about 95 percent of what he does on his computer to build his business is writing: "if you're not typing on the keyboard you're not doing anything online." In a 2024 video on the future of work he generalizes the point: when one builds on a laptop, "the only thing that you're doing the entirety of your day is typing on a keyboard, that's writing," whether one is texting friends or writing landing pages, content, video scripts, tweets or emails. "I argue that that is the base foundational skill that you need to learn." The phrase "base foundational skill" gives this chapter its title.

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md; The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The 2024 passage also contains an autobiographical admission that explains why Koe insists on the point. He failed at almost every other model he tried before his one-person business (digital art, SEO, Facebook ads, drop shipping, e-commerce) because he concentrated on learning a skill and building a website and "forgot I actually had to get customers." How does one get customers? By writing. Content, in this passage, is how one captures, holds and converts attention "in a sea of self-deprecating memes and valueless content," and content rests on writing. The emphasis on writing is therefore not an aesthetic preference; it is Koe's diagnosis of his own early failures.

By December 2025 the formulation had become a definition that ties writing directly to survival: "The foundation of media, which is how you or your employer gets your work in front of other people and persuades them to care about your work so you can survive and get paid, is writing." The second half of the passage adds a principle about direction: "If you want to succeed in any venture, you must go where the attention is." Most attention now sits on social media, YouTube, podcasts and paid advertising, and all of it requires the ability "to articulate persuasively in the form of video scripts, posts, sales copywriting, post captions... which is nearly everything." Koe then explains his own routine through the same logic. He writes one to two hours every morning because "that's the foundation of literally my survival... If I don't write... how am I going to ever get a customer for my own work?"

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

#### What "writing" means: digital writing

When Koe says "writing," he means something specific, and the specificity matters because it removes most of the objections people raise against the advice. In an October 2023 video he defines the term for his series: "By writing I mean anything that is written on social media: captions, Instagram posts with text on the image, tweets, LinkedIn posts, reel scripts, YouTube scripts, etc." The point of the definition is practical: it describes "how you can start writing on social media and growing an audience without having to post pictures of your body or images or designs."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don't Tell You).md

The most important part of the definition is negative. Koe calls what he does **digital writing**, "as opposed to professional writing or academic writing." In the 2022 passage where he introduces the term, he says he is "not a writer" in the credentialed sense and has no English degree: "you don't need an English degree; I don't think the world wants English degrees writing online." What the online world wants are "good, authentic and impactful writers," and "authentic and impactful" here means high energy: writing about the things one actually wants to talk about, in one's own voice, "like you would be texting a friend; that's what social media is." He throws "many rules out the window," writing how he speaks, whether or not it follows "the perfect little writing rules," in whatever way he thinks will deliver the most impact and value. The passage was repeated almost word for word in the 2024 compilation, which shows that the position held.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In 2025 Koe restated the position in more personal terms and added a new reason for it. "I call myself a writer, but I wouldn't actually consider myself one. I don't care about grammar. I don't care about how clever I sound most of the time. And when I start paying too much attention to being more clever, my articulation tends to suffer a lot." He does not care if his sentences run on or if he fails to say something in the best possible way: "This actually helps me stand out against AI. My writing isn't that polished." Millions follow him anyway, he says, because "they found some form of value." The observation is a piece of self-description, not a rule: Koe is reporting that cleverness damages *his* articulation. But it connects with a theme that will return in Section 21.2 and in Chapter 24: in an environment where machines produce polished prose at no cost, the marks of a person thinking are a differentiator.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The broad definition also dissolves the apparent opposition between writing and video. In a 2024 video Koe says that writing was the key to his success because on social media "you write, even if you don't think so": one outlines YouTube scripts, writes on Twitter without showing a face, cross-posts to Instagram in image format, writes reel and TikTok scripts. Someone who goes into a video "blindly," wondering why they are not a good speaker, has misdiagnosed the problem: "it's because you didn't write it out first. You're a writer." His Writer's Bootcamp program, in that passage, teaches the ecosystem he describes in detail in Chapter 24: posts become threads, threads become newsletters; posts go to every platform and become reel or short scripts; newsletters and threads become YouTube or solo podcast scripts, all from one to two hours of writing a day. In 2023 he described his YouTube channel the same way: apart from occasional animations, his videos are "just a written script, it's all writing"; he stands and talks through the script's points. He adds why he works this way: he finds deep fulfillment in using the internet to "curate, organize and share good thoughts," doing what he enjoys for a living, under his own control. The goal, he says, is not fame or a mass following (though both are possible) but to distribute one's value "somewhere that can reach anyone."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md; If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md

A January 2023 passage gives this a conceptual form: **the medium comes before the platform**. "The medium for distributing your message comes before the social media platform; the social media platform is just a vessel, a modality." Writing and speaking are the mediums, the means for distributing, or at least articulating and packaging, a message. All of Koe's growth rests on writing: however polished an Instagram design or an animated reel looks, there is a written script underneath. The video in which he says this was itself a newsletter first; on camera he goes "off the cuff" but follows the written draft for its persuasive structure. Koe ends the passage with an admission: "This skill is so important and it's hard to articulate why." The word "vessel" here is one of his recurring terms (the business, the brand, the platform as containers for something that matters more); in this context it means that the platform is replaceable and the skill underneath it is not.

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

#### Writing as leverage

From the definition of media, Koe derives a claim about leverage. In an October 2025 video on reinventing one's life, he advises choosing opportunities by asking "what the highest lever moving skills are." His own business "is built off of two hours of writing in the morning, that's my entire routine," because "writing is media and digital media is leverage, because one person can write it and a million people can see it." Writing, he adds, is also a way to clarify thoughts, journal, learn and research, and it underlies landing pages, content, captions, ads and video scripts. The comparison with other skills is explicit: "If you choose a skill that isn't as holistic, then it's going to take a lot longer to see results." He cites his course Two-Hour Writer (the corpus also spells it "2 Hour Writer"; it is the same product), which he says almost 30,000 people have bought, as the systematization of this routine.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md

**Complementary context:** "Leverage" in Koe's vocabulary comes largely from the investor Naval Ravikant, who distinguishes labor, capital, code and media as forms of leverage and calls code and media "permissionless" because they can be multiplied without anyone's consent. Chapter 25 discusses how Koe adapts this framework.

In July 2026 Koe gave the idea its most compact form: **writing is the leverage that unlocks other forms of leverage**. For most people, he argues, writing is the most accessible and practical form of leverage. One can learn to code, but "if you can't attract people to care about the product, nobody will use or pay for it." Code and products are leverage only once there are people to use them; writing is what brings the people. Everything is "essentially writing" (captions, DMs, ads, posts, emails, web copy, video scripts, articles), and that includes the path into video. Someone who wants to make videos but lacks editing time or confidence on camera should start by writing, for two reasons: they will accumulate a backlog of scripts, and they will be better at grabbing attention, so that when they do start making videos they will be "miles ahead" of those who never practiced organizing their thoughts on paper. The same holds for audiences. Building a following on a writing platform (X, Substack, Threads, even Instagram, where all of Koe's posts are written posts rendered as images) means that when one starts a YouTube channel or a podcast there is an existing audience to send there. This is what Koe did, "and I'm just an introvert." He says he enjoys his one to two hours of morning writing "knowing it fuels his entire business and life's work."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

A 2024 video on his daily routine extends the argument from creators to everyone who wants autonomy. "Writing as a habit is something that every single person needs to do if they want to build a life of their own," because otherwise there is no way to attract people who will pay. "That's through media." The argument is historical: attention has moved away from radio, newspapers and television (some remains there) toward social media, so one must be where it now is. People treat this as optional, "a new internet job," but Koe offers two signs that it is not: corporations are asking employees to post on LinkedIn, and the political commentator Tucker Carlson (Koe adds "I'm not big into politics") moved from mainstream television to full-time work on his own YouTube channel "because he doesn't need them to survive anymore." The claim that follows connects writing to every other medium: "Writing is the one thing that you do on a daily basis that builds an audience": posts, video scripts, emails, newsletters, texts, messages to clients and prospects, outreach. And "marketing, sales, persuasion, all of the things that make writing impactful are only going to benefit the videos that you create. If you're not getting results in your videos or anything that you do, it's usually because you lack persuasion and therefore you have lacked practicing persuasion with writing."

**Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md

The last sentence is a diagnostic rule, and it shows the logic of the whole section. For Koe, writing is not one channel among many; it is the place where persuasion is practiced, and every other channel inherits the result.

#### The meta skill

In January 2024 Koe gave the idea a name he would reuse: "Writing is the skill that breathes life into any other skill you learn. It is the **meta skill** that people avoid learning until they are forced to because they need to survive." People underestimate it, he says, "because it's just plain old writing, who needs to learn that." In the video he shows a graphic reading "writing is everything" and suggests using it as a wallpaper.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

"Meta skill" is a term Koe uses for several things across the corpus (at different dates he applies it to code and content, to entrepreneurship, to agency and to an understanding of human nature). In March 2024 he gave the definition that applies to writing: "Writing is just a meta skill. By meta skill I mean that you can start without experience and build skills underneath it to make it more powerful." Two features define it. Writing has almost no barrier to entry ("you don't need to show your body, you don't need to have an English degree, and you already write every single day with texts and messages; this is just doing it online"), and every other skill one acquires (persuasion, research, design, knowledge of a field) can be built "underneath" it and makes it more effective. Grammatical perfection is not required: "you just need to write something impactful and share it." Koe offers a test for anyone who believes online writing must be professional: "go on social media and look at what posts you've liked. Is that professional writing?"

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

The same video calls writing "the greatest skill of an Irreplaceable individual," Koe's term in that period for the self-reliant person who cannot be replaced by an employer or a machine (Chapter 35 develops the idea). Writing "knocks out three birds with one stone":

| Function | Koe's formulation | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Articulation | "It teaches you to articulate your value from reader feedback" | Readers' reactions show which ideas land and which are unclear |
| Distribution | "It is the foundation of media: how to build distribution by getting your writing shared" | Shared writing reaches people the author could never reach directly |
| Practice | "It is the morning habit that builds any project you build" | Posts, threads, emails, outlines, modules, ads, DMs, books and video scripts "all start with writing" |

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

A March 2024 passage adds two more attributes and makes the causal chain explicit. Writing "forces you to articulate your value; it is the foundation of all media; it can be repurposed into any other medium, and any other skill you acquire enhances it; it brings immense mental clarity." "It takes some time," Koe says, "to realize that writing is the highest leverage work." The task he describes is one of synthesis: "You must synthesize your value into impactful ideas that live in the heads of others rent free and influence their thoughts and behaviors. You do this by writing emails, content, guides and products with persistent and iterative effort." Then the chain:

> writing is the foundation of communication → communication is how you distribute value → distributing value is how you attract people → if you don't attract people you will never have someone else in the value exchange process.

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

The chain matters because it links writing to Koe's definition of business as value exchange (Chapter 28): there is no exchange without a second party, and writing is what produces the second party. It also anticipates Section 21.3, where "ideas that live in the heads of others rent free" become the concept of mental real estate.

Koe also tells this as a business decision. In February 2024, recommending his writing course, he says that "writing is just the greatest skill that transfers over into any other skills." He had sold several products in the past, web design among them, "but it all came back to writing," which is why he changed what he sells: to offer something useful that teaches "an invaluable skill that can create career opportunities" and lets people build their own careers.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md

#### The evergreen skills and the results-oriented skills

Writing does not stand alone in Koe's framework. In 2023 he groups it with three other skills under the name **evergreen skills**: writing, speaking, marketing and sales. In the second version of his one-person business model (June 2023) he states that "for any path to work" one must learn these four, and later in the same video he gives a more specific starting point for someone with no idea what to learn: marketing, sales, copywriting and direct response marketing, "because that alone will change your life."

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

In September 2023 he turned the grouping into a two-level framework. The first level, the evergreen skills, is about "how to create a valuable message and distribute it," whether in writing or in speech. The instruction attached to it is about sequence: "You must start building, not learning, and when you encounter a problem, study writing, speaking, marketing and sales courses and content." As a first book he recommends *Influence* by Robert Cialdini. The second level he calls **results-oriented skills**: practical, modern and changing with time, such as AI, graphic design, web design and development, SEO, email marketing, social media marketing agencies, "any business people tell you to start." The relationship between the levels is the core of the framework: "All of these are made effective by the evergreen skills." Someone who learns one results-oriented skill alone, graphic design for example, "gets primed for replacement and can usually only earn an income from a job." Without marketing one cannot land clients; without a holistic study one cannot scale, and one gets "stuck as a freelancer in the **starving artist phase**, doing manual outreach all day and hoping that you get referrals."

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

**Complementary context:** Robert Cialdini's *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (1984) describes principles such as reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity. Koe recommends it here as an entry point; elsewhere he adapts reciprocity to networking (Chapter 26).

| Level | Skills | Character | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evergreen skills | Writing, speaking, marketing, sales | Stable; create and distribute a valuable message | Determine the results of every other skill |
| Results-oriented skills | AI, graphic design, web design, SEO, email marketing, agencies | Practical; change with the technology | Effective only through the evergreen skills; alone, "primed for replacement" |

In his January 2024 summary of his book *The Art of Focus* Koe states the relationship as a principle: "Writing, speaking, marketing and sales are the foundational skills that will determine the results of any other skill you learn." The same passage gives the order of learning: start by learning to write, harness psychology through marketing and sales, and write "a valuable message for the world to discover online." It adds two observations: consistent writing improves speaking, and human psychology applies to every form of writing, wherever it appears (emails, posts, ads, scripts, captions, DMs).

**Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md

The justification for writing changed between 2023 and 2026, and the change is worth stating precisely. In 2023 and 2024 the argument was structural: master media or code, and since code has become no-code, master media, whose foundation is writing. In July 2026 the argument has become cognitive and is framed by AI: "If you don't know what skill to learn, learn to write." Writing teaches one how to think, how to learn any skill fast and how to attract an audience that supports one's work, which is needed "for your own business or any independent, creative or meaningful work." In the age of AI, Koe argues, three skills are timeless: thinking, learning and distribution ("getting your value in front of other people so you actually have the chance of being paid"). Writing lets one practice all three at once while "building digital leverage as a byproduct." The business argument remains, unchanged: a business owner writes marketing, ads, captions, posts and emails; any communication with customers or partners comes down to writing, "what the entirety of your business hangs on," so it should be a daily habit and practice. The corpus does not present this as a correction of the earlier argument; the earlier argument is kept and a new justification is placed beside it. The shift parallels the one recorded for long-form content generally: by 2026 Koe defends long-form writing as "a moat" because AI cannot replicate it.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

#### You are already a writer

A large part of Koe's case is aimed at an identity obstacle: people who do not think of themselves as writers. His response has two levels, one practical and one anthropological.

The practical level is an observation about daily communication. In a September 2024 video he concedes that persuasion can happen through other means, speaking or design, but says that writing "usually comes before speaking or enhances it," so he focuses on it. And "you are a writer: you text people probably more than you talk to them on the phone," you write emails, and unless your job is manual labor, much of it probably consists of writing. A December 2025 passage makes the same point about intention: "You should start writing intentionally because you already write every single day": texting family, emailing prospects, clients and coworkers, writing project outlines, feedback and proposals.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md; How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The anthropological level appears in two 2024 videos and rests on the idea (developed in Chapter 18) that the human being is by nature a creator. "You don't need to identify as a writer to start writing; you as a human are a creator": unlike animals, humans build tools and solutions from acquired knowledge in order to survive and evolve. "You already write every day; it's inherent to the human experience; writing is how we as a species survive." Humans carved into rock and wrote on papyrus to "immortalize information" that would let others progress faster and avoid dangers, and no other species has done this. Hence a short formula: "**The ability to write is your edge.**" In May 2024 Koe said the same thing as a contrast: "Most people think that writing is just a type of skill or a hobby; they don't see it as the one thing that makes us human. We are the only species that can pass down information by documenting knowledge with writing to help those who come after us avoid danger and evolve." Without writing and documented communication there would be none of the technology around us. "Writing started as carvings in caves and have now become ideas on the internet"; the difference is reach: cave carvings were barely seen, while the internet allows one to share ideas with thousands. Koe's conclusion gives writing a double role: "**Writing is the intersection of purpose and profit.** Don't underestimate that power."

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md; It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md

These are interpretive claims, not research findings. Koe presents them as a way of seeing writing ("the one thing that makes us human"), and the evolutionary story is offered as framing, not as an argument from anthropology. Their function in his teaching is to remove the sense that writing is a specialist's activity.

The August 2024 passage also contains a condition that keeps the anthropological praise from becoming naive. With a goal and focus, going online can change one's life, because any information is available. Without a goal, or something to build, one cannot distinguish signal from noise and drowns in it. Ordinary writing (emails, texts, notes) is partly a defense against this abundance: we write "to keep your mind from going insane with the abundance of information." This connects writing to the model of Chapter 6, in which the goal is the lens that decides what information counts. Thanks to the internet, Koe concludes, anyone can now write at scale, build an audience and earn a living from skills or interests their audience benefits from.

#### Start with writing

If writing is the foundation, the practical recommendation follows: start there. Koe has said so since 2022 ("I always recommend starting with writing": Twitter, a newsletter, a blog, even YouTube, as in the video where he is reading his newsletter as the script). The first reason he gives is that one does not need to show one's face, and the rest of the passage frames this as a matter of constraints: stop letting limiting beliefs stop you, and build the solution that fits your constraint.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md

Across the following years he gives several reasons, which together form the argument for writing as the entry point.

*It is the most accessible form of distribution.* In 2024: "The distribution of value has been around since the beginning of time but it has taken many shapes; the most accessible form of distribution at this point in time is writing on the internet." Koe stresses the word "accessible." Video and public speaking are widespread, but writing needs no camera, microphone, lights or "$44,000 computers in the background... you need a keyboard, you just type and you post it on Twitter or Medium... it's low friction, accessible, and then those turn into video." The same passage gives the basic channel structure: write on social media "because that's where the attention is," transfer readers to email "because it can't be taken away from you," "keep that flow going, and then you have an audience."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md

*Speaking demands more.* "With something like speaking or video you need editing skills and the confidence to not stumble over your words, and you also need to be able to think on the fly." Writing is therefore "the best place to start in my opinion": it allows one to practice "articulating your thoughts, clearing your mind and testing ideas before you get into speaking." Koe marks this as opinion.

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

*It does several jobs at once.* In July 2023 Koe listed the properties that make writing the first choice: it "clarifies your thoughts, it makes you more articulate, it can be repurposed into any other medium (speaking, video or visuals), it is also a seamless non-demanding **lever moving task** that you can schedule in the first block of each morning." The practical order follows: grow first on writing platforms (Threads, Twitter, an email newsletter) and then repurpose: read a tweet as the script of a reel or short, build a YouTube video from newsletters and blog posts. "Lever moving task" is Koe's term (see Chapter 12) for the few daily tasks that actually move a project forward.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

*It is a principle, not a tactic.* In August 2023 Koe framed the recommendation within his preference for principles (Chapter 15). He is "not a fan of methods and tactics." Writing, speaking, marketing and sales are principles one will need whatever the business model: email marketing is writing, advertising is writing, a YouTube script is writing. Writing is the most valuable skill, especially paired with marketing and sales, and it is the best option for someone who lacks confidence. (In the same passage Koe notes that Twitter has been renamed X and that everything he says about Twitter transfers to any platform.) The passage ends with his most quoted line on the subject: writing "allows you to **flex your intelligence while everyone else competes for looks**."

**Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md

*Every ambition requires ideas and paying people.* An August 2024 passage starts from a different observation. Many people want to write a book or escape society's conventional path, but do nothing that signals they are pursuing it; they seem to believe it will happen the moment they decide. They do not see that to write a book "you need a plethora of prior ideas; you need to have iterated on so many freaking ideas," and that to "do your own thing" you need "people who are willing to pay you for it." Both require the same activity: sitting at a keyboard "typing words in a specific sequence." The most affordable, accessible route to both, possible only "at this point in evolution," is social media, where most content is writing (in a design, a video script, a tweet, Instagram text) and where one builds an audience by attracting people to that writing. And because "anything ever that you create is composed of ideas," the rational first move is to write short pieces that test ideas and let them be "validated by the market, AKA engagement." This anticipates the logic of validated content and of "a tweet is the new MVP" (Chapters 24 and 26).

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

*Writing is how one reflects on learning.* The 2025 version adds a third reason to the two usual ones. Writing is "the foundation of media"; it is "accessible, and anyone can start writing right now, no video editing skills required"; and it "holds much more power than just building an audience of supporters: writing is how you systematically reflect on what you learn. When you teach what you learn you expose more knowledge gaps, you struggle more to understand, you have more specific knowledge to research." This reason links writing to Chapter 14's principle that teaching consolidates learning, and it leads directly into Section 21.2.

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md

#### The author's case: writing as the foundation of his business

Koe's arguments for writing are inseparable from his description of his own working day, and the description is detailed enough to reconstruct.

In January 2023, explaining the "4-Hour Workday" (Chapter 12), he listed his three priority tasks at that stage of his business, in order. The passage was later reused in a March 2024 compilation, split into three segments.

1. **Writing content, always first.** "My first priority task is always writing content. I wake up and I write my newsletter and tweets and threads for 45-minute blocks each, so about two 45-minute blocks, 90 minutes total, 45 being my newsletter and then 45 being like tweets, threads, posting to other platforms." The reason he gives is the thesis of this section: "writing forms the foundation of literally everything I do."
2. **Generating traffic.** Content attracts people who may follow and buy, but "with any social media platform that you start on, you're not going to have much traffic at the start; nobody's going to see your content, so you need to focus most of your effort on just generating traffic." His methods: networking in direct messages, making friends with people who may share his posts, replying to large accounts. One example: setting notification bells for every high-subscriber YouTuber and commenting on their videos every morning in blocked focus time, a lever because it brings traffic to his profile over time. At that point he was doing this mostly on LinkedIn (and sometimes Instagram), "the platform that is lagging the most," putting effort into growth there rather than into "monotonous content creation."
3. **Promoting products.** "I have to make an income if I want to do this full-time." With consistent traffic from content and growth, he leads people either into free products, "to build authority more and just give out free value," or into paid ones (at the time, 2 Hour Writer, Digital Economics and Modern Mastery). "My products are good and I put a lot of time into them, so it only makes sense that I promote them so more people get results." Auxiliary tasks exist, but they are done "towards the end, when I have like an hour to spare before my 4 hours of focused work are up."

**Source:** The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md

The order is the argument. Writing comes first because the other two depend on it: traffic is attention directed to something written, and promotion is writing aimed at a product.

In June 2023 Koe described the result of this arrangement after it had matured. He realized that writing is the foundation of everything because it is reused everywhere: tweets are repurposed to every platform (Instagram reels, YouTube Shorts), and the newsletter is repurposed into YouTube videos. With the momentum he had built, he could write for one to two hours every morning on content alone, which is "traffic generation to fuel the products that sell when I sleep," and "that's literally all I have to do." Additional projects (writing a book, building software, both of which he was doing) add time, but remain manageable because he keeps his attention on levers and does not let "unnecessary **time suckers**" take his time and attention.

**Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md

In another June 2023 video he called this his **maintenance system**. "That's literally my process... the things that keep my business going is writing two hours in the morning, focusing on my brand and my content, and then filling that or sending that to products or services." On most days, when he is not working on additional projects, "I work two to three hours, really; that's my lever moving task to maintain my current level of income." Once the task is prioritized and placed in a routine (his is in the morning), "it just becomes systemized, habitual and almost effortless, because you're not expending so much mental energy on something new"; it becomes "second nature." The term "maintenance" connects to Chapter 5's account of entropy: what is not maintained decays, and here the maintenance of an entire income consists of a writing habit.

**Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md

#### The daily practice

The recommendation for beginners comes in two forms. A September 2023 passage gives a routine in four steps: "Start with 30 minutes of writing each morning; write a long-form newsletter; use the ideas you write about as short-form posts; eventually let your engagement data act as a compass for what you write more about." The last step, **engagement data as a compass**, is the seed of the validation logic developed in Chapter 26: one does not guess what to write more of; the audience's response indicates it. (He adds that the full process is what 2 Hour Writer teaches.)

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

A September 2024 passage gives the same recommendation as a standard, which Koe introduces as "brutally honest": "If you are not writing content daily, if that isn't a 30 to 60 minute practice every single morning for you, you're not really building a business." Without money to invest in building the business another way, it will not grow. One can design a website and a logo all day, but "the audience is really the only thing that matters here," apart from the offer and the product; writing content and prioritizing followers will do most of the selling. Koe then reframes what a week of posts is: "Think of your 10 pieces of content that you post over this week as a landing page in and of itself, it's just in real time." Purchasing behavior on social media has changed, he says, and growing an audience is not a matter of luck with the algorithm. The **real-time landing page** is a useful image for what the next sections develop: content does not merely precede selling; it is a continuous, distributed form of the same persuasive work a sales page does.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The reasons assembled in this section are mostly external: writing reaches people, creates leverage and sustains a business. Koe's later work puts at least as much weight on what writing does inside the writer. That is the subject of the next section.

### 21.2 Writing Is Thinking

#### Writing clarifies thought

The inward argument appears early, in October 2022, in a passage about why beginners struggle to make their experience useful to others. More of a problem than failing to see one's own experience as valuable, Koe says, is "ineffective communication." What people on social media are doing is "effective and persuasive communication to make their own interests valuable and interesting, or at least perceived as valuable and interesting, to other people." So the skill to master has two faces: writing as **effective communication**, and **effective thinking**, "because writing is how you clarify your thoughts." The passage was reused verbatim in the 2024 compilation. Its significance is that from the start Koe does not separate the outward and inward functions of writing. Communicating clearly and thinking clearly are the same skill seen from two sides.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In *The Art of Focus* (as quoted in a January 2024 video) Koe gives a definition that holds both sides together: "Writing is the vehicle for articulated thought and communication. It is the medium for putting your message in front of those that can adopt the perspective it presents and operate within that reality." The first sentence is about the writer: writing is how thought becomes articulated. The second is about the reader: a piece of writing offers a perspective that readers can adopt and then "operate within," which in Koe's model of identity (Chapter 3) means that it changes what they perceive and how they act. Section 21.3 develops this second half.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

A December 2025 passage repeats the claim as the answer to a familiar question. "If you don't know what to learn, start writing. Not because writing is some shortcut you can't stop looking for, but because writing teaches you how to think, how to learn, and how to inspire people to care about what you do. Writing is so much more than writing. It's not only just putting letters on a page." The three verbs (think, learn, inspire) are the same triad Koe later calls thinking, learning and distribution.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

Koe connects this explicitly to a third party. In a 2022 video on solitude he lists writing to clarify one's thoughts as one of the activities to do alone, and reads part of a thread by the psychologist Jordan Peterson: nobody tells students why they write; it is not for the grade but to learn to think, because thinking lets one act effectively in the world; if one can think, speak and write, one is "absolutely deadly," and nothing can get in one's way. Koe adds his own testimony: he has experienced this firsthand, and one does not experience it until one starts writing, which is why he advocates writing online and building a one-person business through it. The idea is Peterson's; the claim of having verified it in his own life is Koe's.

**Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md

**Complementary context:** Jordan Peterson, a Canadian clinical psychologist, has long argued in lectures and in the materials of his online writing program that essay writing is training in thinking. Koe cites him in several places as a model of articulation.

#### Reading without writing; the read–write–build rule

In August 2024 Koe compressed the relation between reading and writing into a metaphor drawn from the gym: "Reading without writing is like eating without training. The excess gets stored as **mental fat** and you begin to feel sluggish, tired and overwhelmed." Consumption without processing is not neutral; it accumulates as a burden. He then states the chain that justifies writing: "writing equals **organized thinking**, organized thinking equals effective communication, communication equals how to get what you want out of life." Reading alone only takes one so far: "we've all met that person who has all the ideas in the world but they haven't built anything worth following or paying for."

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

The metaphor of mental fat belongs to a family of bodily metaphors that run through the corpus (Chapter 15's "mental bodybuilding," Chapter 13's "fill, empty, use"). In a later version of the image, consuming fattens the mind, emptying it lowers its weight, and creating builds mental muscle.

Two years earlier, in November 2022, Koe had given a decision rule that places writing between reading and building: "If you don't have ideas, read; if you have ideas but can't articulate them, write; if you have ideas and have the clarity to execute, build." The transcription of the middle step is uncertain (the transcript has "right," which the context makes almost certainly "write"), but the structure is clear: each activity answers a specific lack. Reading supplies raw material; writing converts raw material into articulated ideas; building converts articulated ideas into things. The rule matches the sequence "read, write, build" that Chapter 13 presents within the Fill–Empty–Use framework.

**Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md

#### Forced linearity: the forest and the road

The most developed account of how writing changes thinking appears in a July 2026 video, "The Writing System That Saved My Brain." Koe opens with a general claim: writing goes deeper than putting pen to paper; it "shifts your mind to see the world more meaningfully." He then enumerates benefits. He announces "four key benefits," but the enumeration in the transcript actually yields more (writing rewires thinking, increases pattern recognition, improves articulation and accelerates learning, plus a final point introduced with "lastly" about understanding), so the count is ambiguous. What follows reconstructs the benefits whose content is clear.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

The first benefit is the core mechanism, for which Koe coins the term **forced linearity**. One can feel that one understands something in one's head; writing forces that feeling to collapse into a single stream. He explains the mechanism with a metaphor: "thinking without writing is like walking through a forest where you can see every direction; writing is like driving on a road through the disorienting mess." On the road one must decide where one is going, which turns to take, whether to take the scenic route and how to navigate back if lost. The forest offers the illusion of seeing everything; the road obliges one to choose an order, and the order is the thinking.

From this mechanism Koe draws his strongest formulation: "**Writing is the apparatus for thinking. It's the gym where your mind is built.**" People who go to the gym for their body but never write for their mind, he says, need to make the connection. And he extends the claim to the contents of identity: one may feel that one understands one's feelings, counterarguments, values and beliefs, but until one writes them, "I can almost guarantee that you don't." What one has not examined in writing has often been planted by someone else: beliefs and ideologies adopted without question that "now dominate your mind and control your life." This links writing directly to the diagnosis of Part I. If the Matrix is an inherited network of beliefs, writing is one of the instruments for seeing which of them one actually holds.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

#### Legos with ideas

A 2025 passage gives the same mechanism a more playful image and a personal origin. As a child Koe admired articulate, knowledgeable people; when he read a good book and tried to explain it in his own words, "it's just not coming to mind; I didn't actually learn the thing." Once he started writing, things changed: "**writing is Legos with ideas**." Piecing things together and playing with thoughts on a screen made learning much easier, "because it's not just like I'm trying to do it on the spot as I'm speaking." Speech demands that ideas be produced in real time and in order; writing allows them to be laid out, moved, recombined and tested. "The more that I wrote, the more good things happened internally and externally."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md

"Lego" is a metaphor Koe applies to several things (developed notes as building blocks for content, elements of a lifestyle that can be swapped, blocks of work time); in the version about ideas he later adds that ideas come in predictable shapes (pain points, examples, stories, statistics, metaphors, quotes, reframes, questions), which is the bridge to the structural techniques of Chapter 23.

Set side by side, the two metaphors describe complementary aspects of the same process. Forced linearity emphasizes constraint: writing removes the option of holding everything vaguely at once. Legos emphasizes freedom: writing gives one time and space to manipulate ideas that speech would demand instantly. Both locate the value of writing in externalization: the thought is put outside the head, where it can be looked at.

#### What writing does to perception

The July 2026 video continues with effects on perception. Writing makes one form more connections, and those connections deliver what Koe calls "good dopamine" (the earned, meaningful kind distinguished from cheap dopamine in Chapter 11). Conversations become inspiration; thoughts fill with ideas one is wrestling to understand; one notices more detail in life "rather than living on the stressful surface."

Koe divides the increase in **pattern recognition** into three layers.

1. *An external record of thinking.* Writing regularly builds a record of one's thinking. One can draw on it to articulate current thoughts better, not by literally looking things up but because once one has written the newsletter, article or journal entry, "that is now more of a part of your mind," and one says it more clearly.
2. *The photographer's eye.* After years of serious practice, a photographer sees frames and compositions in everyday life; on a hike through a mountain range, they compose the scene as if taking a picture and appreciate it more. Writing produces the same effect in conversations, in other people's writing, in books: "It opens up a deeper appreciation for the art in your life."
3. *Noticing ideas.* One begins to notice ideas one normally would not. Koe connects this to goal-directed perception, the principle (Chapter 6) that a goal acts as a lens determining what the mind registers as relevant; writing is, in effect, a standing goal that makes the mind collect material.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

These are reports of experience framed in the vocabulary of attention and dopamine that Koe uses throughout the corpus; he does not cite studies for them. The "photographer's eye" is offered as an analogy, not as a finding.

#### Writing exposes the gap

The same video adds a benefit that is also a source of fear. Most people believe they understand things they do not, "not because they're dumb but because they've never been forced to externalize the thought and look at it." Writing improves communication, and with it most areas of life, and it exposes whether one actually knows what one is talking about. That exposure, Koe argues, is what people avoid. It is part of "the struggle that comes from doing something meaningful." Many people want to coast, to enjoy life, to make money without much effort, and they end up in a mediocre life on autopilot: "They don't choose their struggle. Again, they're assigned their struggle." They want creative work to be easy and ideas to arrive immediately; they do not want to sit before a blank page and be forced to research, learn and discover what they actually believe. The vocabulary ("assigned" versus chosen) is taken directly from the thesis of Chapter 1 that a person always pursues goals, chosen or assigned, and from Chapter 39's idea of choosing one's struggle.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

The final point in the video turns the exposure into a method. One reads something, feels that one gets it, tries to write about it and finds one cannot. Most people interpret this as proof that they are not qualified to write about the subject, which Koe calls nonsense: anyone can write about an idea, and "if you can tell your friend about an idea you heard, you can write about it." The belief that one needs qualification stops many would-be creators: "You're just creating **invisible gatekeepers** in your head out of fear of actually doing the thing and publishing your thoughts." The failed attempt is in fact useful: it shows that one does not yet understand, exposes **the gap**, and identifies the next thing to learn, which one would never have found without trying to write what one had learned. The idea has two halves that must be kept together: no permission is needed to write about an idea, and the difficulty of writing about it is diagnostic, not disqualifying. Koe's 2026 position on AI, recorded in the evolution of his views, rests on the same reasoning: struggling to articulate one's own thinking "creates a gap that you can then go learn and fill," which is why he does not want AI to articulate opinions for him (Chapter 24).

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

#### The daily craft: Sahil Bloom's testimony

In a January 2025 conversation, the writer Sahil Bloom (author of *The 5 Types of Wealth*) gave a guest's version of the same thesis, from the experience of a weekly newsletter writer. The ideas in this subsection are Bloom's; Koe hosts and agrees.

Bloom's principle: "My quality of thinking tracks very directly with the quality of my writing, and I cannot write clearly if I'm not thinking clearly on a topic. And so I use writing and the teaching that comes through with my writing as my way of more deeply thinking." His newsletter is "my favorite piece of writing that I do every single week," and it is "all about me refining and deepening and **sharpening my sword** on a specific topic." He describes a preparatory process. During the week before he writes, he talks about the topic with people who would not understand it, to see "what are the questions they have, where they want more, where are they understanding it? Where are they not?" The purpose is to deepen his own thinking before writing.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

**Complementary context:** Bloom's procedure resembles the so-called Feynman technique, named after the physicist Richard Feynman, in which one tests one's understanding by explaining a subject in simple terms to a non-expert and returns to the sources wherever the explanation breaks down. Chapter 14 discusses the technique and the related "protégé effect."

Bloom then tells a story. Five years earlier he had watched the author Ryan Holiday on podcasts and wondered "how can one person have so much knowledge stored in their brain... the way that he pulls from different quotes, different stories, different frameworks, different models?" Now he finds himself doing the same thing, pulling out the Feynman technique or "energy calendars" as needed in conversation. This, he says, is "not built through any magic. I'm not smarter... It is built through a **daily craft**, executed over and over and over again for several years, and that daily craft is writing... forcing yourself to sit down and put words to paper to clarify your thought." He frames it as "empowering," because anyone can do it, and he lists benefits beyond content: deeper thought "helps you in every area of life," including relationships and "my ability to connect with smarter people. I'm much more interesting to smart, exciting... successful people now than I was when I was on a safe, stable track. Because I'm doing something different."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

The "Ryan Holiday effect," then, is the outward appearance of the external record of thinking described above: a mind that has written about many things has those things available.

#### Articles and essays

The most elaborated part of Koe's account of writing as thinking appears in 2026, in a pair of videos arguing for the essay. It begins with a distinction. In a March 2026 video: "An essay doesn't start with a conclusion. You figure out the conclusion as you write. It's a way of figuring out what you think about things. An article, on the other hand... while these things can overlap in many ways, it starts with the topic. It starts with the conclusion. You map out the structure and you fill it in, and it's informative in a sense."

**Source:** If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md

In the April 2026 video "I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing)," the distinction becomes a set of contrasts, built around the formula "**Articles are answers, while essays are arguments.**"

| Article | Essay |
|---|---|
| An answer | An argument |
| Packages existing knowledge | Changes the author's beliefs |
| Starts with the conclusion | Figures out the conclusion |
| Informs or educates | Is an act of thinking |
| Communicates what is already there | Discovers what isn't |

Koe adds a caveat that prevents the table from becoming a rule: this does not mean one should never write articles, and the two forms overlap. The point is to write "with the intention of refining what you believe and actually impacting the person on the other side."

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

The distinction sits in tension with Koe's own earlier teaching. In 2024 he recommended the pyramid principle as a starting point for persuasive writing ("start with your answer, conclusion or big idea"; "state, argue, back up your point"), and in 2024–2025 he presented it as complementary to problem-first structures. The 2026 table assigns exactly that answer-first approach to the article, not to the essay. The corpus does not present this as a retraction. In December 2025 Koe classified PAS (problem–agitate–solution) as a beginner's framework and the pyramid as an intermediate one; in 2026 the answer-first method remains the method for persuading, and the essay becomes a separate practice whose purpose is to think. Both can apply, to different goals: the pyramid to a piece meant to convince, the essay to a piece meant to discover. Chapter 23 presents the structures themselves.

**Complementary context:** The pyramid principle was formulated by Barbara Minto, a consultant at McKinsey, in *The Minto Pyramid Principle* (1987): start with the main conclusion, then group supporting arguments beneath it. PAS is a classic copywriting formula.

#### Fast content and slow content

The essay video places the distinction within a diagnosis of the information environment, introduced as a third layer of the argument. Koe quotes the philosopher Daniel Schmachtenberger: "Wisdom is not algorithmic and cannot be made algorithmic." For decades, he argues, the content that has dominated is content "that delivers conclusions without requiring thought. In other words, it's **fast food for the mind**," which he names **fast content**. The comparison with food is meant literally as a mechanism: social media companies use the same psychological triggers as fast food companies, which realized that fat, sugar and salt were scarce in our evolutionary past, that our brains are not wired to handle them in abundance, and that finding them spikes dopamine because it once favored survival. Fast content exploits the same wiring with information.

At the fast end of the spectrum Koe places BuzzFeed listicles, rage tweets, AI-generated summaries, hot takes, engagement-optimized threads and TikTok explainers that give "the feeling of understanding in 30 seconds" ("it makes sense why everyone thinks they're an expert these days"). At the slow end, **slow content**, which requires thinking and potentially a change of behavior: essays, long-form conversations that are not optimized, certain books, certain lectures, and even tweets "crafted to make you think" in order to receive the insight. The last example matters: the distinction is not between short and long formats but between content that hands over a conclusion and content that requires the reader to do part of the work. Essays, Koe suggests, may be "**the last bastion of real thinking**."

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

**Complementary context:** Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher known for work on the "metacrisis" and on the degradation of the "epistemic commons," the shared capacity of a society to make sense of reality. Koe uses his framework in several places (Chapters 2 and 20).

This critique represents a change of emphasis in Koe's views on content, which the corpus records without a clean break. In 2022–2024 he argued that structured content orders the consciousness of readers, that clickbait hooks "aren't necessarily bad" if the whole narrative expands the mind ("one stock can be down while the index fund is up"), and that shallow content has its place for meeting people at their stage. From 2025 he begins to describe fast, short content as entropic, with exceptions for "syntropic" short-form creators such as James Clear, Naval Ravikant and Andrew Huberman. In 2026 he criticizes creators who optimize for engagement rather than transformation, and lists "engagement-optimized threads" among fast content, although he had taught thread-writing himself. He does not retract his defense of hooks. The two positions can coexist if they are applied to different objects: a hook or a superficial entry point as the door to a deep piece, versus a whole piece that only gives the feeling of thought. Chapter 23 returns to the question.

#### Why essays

The video's opening states what Koe means by "essay" (not school essays) and why he cares. Essays are "one of the greatest tools to learn faster, think deeper, improve the articulation of your ideas and beliefs, and avoid being replaced by AI." Those are what he calls the "selfish personal benefits" of putting words on paper. The deeper reason concerns the environment: the modern information environment is breaking our ability to think; most people do not notice, and even more do not care. Essays may be "one of the last forms of content that actually develops your capacity to make sense of reality." We are, he says, "living through the largest scale production of **fake thinking** in history," and this "fake thinking epidemic," if it continues, will make one's life worse and could contribute to the collapse of civilization. The video promises to help the viewer write a first essay to repair "one of the most precious resources that you have, which is your mind," adding that a career or side income from it "doesn't hurt either." The civilizational claim is a forecast offered as a warning, not an argued prediction.

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

Later in the same video Koe explains why essays in particular, giving three reasons.

1. *They can be produced alone* and leveraged to take advantage of "the meaning economy we're heading into," Koe's term (Chapter 36) for an economy in which people pay for meaning and perspective rather than for information.
2. *They are the most scalable and durable form of thinking-content.* "A meaningful conversation can shift one person, but it lives and dies in the memory of the participants." An essay develops the thinking capacity of readers and writer for thousands of people across, potentially, thousands of years.
3. *They force one to think through one's beliefs.* Most people, Koe included, walk around with opinions they have never thought through; they have never written out exactly what they believe, "especially not in a way that would survive a smart person's criticism."

As evidence that essays form minds he lists people whose thinking was forged by writing them: Paul Graham ("for the tech bros"), Isaac Newton, Jordan Peterson "in his prime," Friedrich Nietzsche, Ralph Waldo Emerson. And he closes with a definitional claim: "**The defining factor of an essay is that AI cannot write one.**"

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

That last claim stands in tension with Koe's evolving position on AI, which Chapter 24 treats in full. In 2024–2025 he was skeptical of AI for writing; by 2026 he accepted it as a drafting tool "as long as [writers are] in control of the ideas," and used it to generate iterations of good posts. The essay claim survives that change only if "write" is understood as "think through and decide what one believes": AI can produce text in the form of an essay, but in Koe's sense an essay is the record of a person changing their mind, which is what a machine cannot do on that person's behalf. The corpus leaves this reconciliation implicit; the reading offered here is an interpretation.

#### How to write an essay: six steps

Koe ends the video with what he calls six "rapid-fire" steps. He warns first that the process is supposed to be difficult, especially for non-writers, and that all one needs is paper or an app.

1. **Write to discover, not to perform.** Most social engagement comes from packaging anyway, which can be learned later. Start from a concept, a viewpoint, a question, an experience, a thought, something that bothers you, or a topic. "An essay begins with uncertainty and an open mind."
2. **Write about what genuinely interests you.** Focus on a single main idea. Use it to research and learn: go down rabbit holes, challenge everyone's point of view, do not accept a single source as law.
3. **Resist the template.** One will discover how one likes to structure writing as one improves, because structure is a skill, "but for now, just write." Debate yourself; ask questions to keep the writing going; worry about structure afterwards, asking AI for help if desired. "Do the thinking first."
4. **Ask "Do I actually believe this?"** It is easy to write what one already believes; the point is to change it. Koe calls this the most difficult part and adds: resist acting as if you are absolutely right.
5. **Read essays and consume "syntropic" content.** One's sense-making capacity is shaped by one's inputs, and the For You page will not supply nourishing ones; one must actively search, curate and cultivate one's feed. "Syntropic" is Koe's word for the opposite of entropic: inputs that build order in the mind (Chapter 5).
6. **Build a body of work, not a content calendar.** People follow a body of coherent work; each essay compounds on the previous one; and "AI can't replicate a coherent philosophy built through years of genuine thinking."

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

Two of these steps contradict earlier advice, and the corpus records both tensions.

The first concerns templates. From 2022 to 2025 Koe taught templates and frameworks as "training wheels": in September 2024, "organize your writing into a framework until you understand what makes writing impactful"; in February 2025, "templates and frameworks don't make you inauthentic." Step 3 says to resist the template. The difference lies in what each piece is for. The essay puts thinking first and leaves structure for later; the distribution post puts packaging first, because engagement comes mostly from packaging. Both pieces of advice remain valid for their own object. Similarly, "build a body of work, not a content calendar" qualifies, without cancelling, his earlier promotion calendars and structured outlines (Chapters 23 and 24).

The second concerns conviction. In November 2023 Koe told creators that "you are right about everything at this very point in time" and that "everyone is on social media to be told what to do in a confident way," accepting the risk of being wrong in exchange for certainty, with the safeguard "do not abuse these for the sake of engagement," and later he left "the burden of nuance" to the viewer. In the essay, the hardest step is asking "Do I actually believe this?" and resisting the posture of being absolutely right. Koe does not present this as a correction, and gives no reason for the change. The two attitudes apply to different phases: conviction to the published piece, doubt to the process of writing in order to discover. Both share a willingness to change one's mind when feedback is convincing. Chapter 22 discusses conviction in more detail.

#### The essay that changed its author's mind

Koe illustrates the process with the video itself. Among its points is that **thinking in public** is the most valuable skill. The finished video is a polished package with cuts, but the outline (published first as a newsletter on his Substack) was "incredibly difficult to write and just think through." He had written three other drafts on why one should write essays, "that I scrapped entirely" because they did not feel right. Staying with the challenge and trying to synthesize a point of view that he aligned with and felt to be true and impactful changed his mind and helped him "make something coherent in my mind." If the video changes the viewer's mind or behavior, he adds, that brings more meaning into his life and theirs.

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

The story closes the argument of this section in a small, verifiable case: the author applying his own six steps and reporting the predicted result. Writing was not the transcription of a conclusion he already had; it was the process that produced the conclusion. That inward function, though, is never separated in Koe's teaching from its outward counterpart. A thought clarified on paper is also a thought that can enter someone else's mind, and the next section is about what happens when it does.

### 21.3 Content as a Magnet and the Mental Real Estate

#### Content is a magnet

In December 2023 Koe stated the function of content in a sentence designed to correct a misunderstanding: "**Content creation is not a job or a business, it is a magnet.**" The misunderstanding is that posting is itself the business, that a creator earns by creating content. Koe "brings it down to earth" by reducing business to two necessary elements.

The first is people. "Business is value exchange": if one wants money, someone must give it in exchange for something of equal value. People forget that money always comes from a person: "you can invest all day long, but how did you get the money to invest? From a person." The second is a product, services included: "a valuable creation, physical or digital, that preferably solves a real problem in someone's life." There is more nuance (marketing, selling, technical skills), "but you absolutely need people and a product... it's not optional." Content is the link between the two: "Content is how you attract people to your product as a creator," by "deconstructing your mind and sharing its best parts on the internet."

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

An August 2023 passage defines the division of labor among the skills involved: "Content is how you distribute your product; marketing and sales are how you create products that are actually valued by the market." Content, whether written or spoken, is "a vessel, either visual or written, to distribute the value that you have to offer, that is crafted by marketing and sales or psychological principles." This is the evergreen-skills framework of Section 21.1 seen from the business side: writing and speaking are the vessel, marketing and sales shape what the vessel carries.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md

A 2026 example shows the distinction between content and product from outside Koe's own field. If he were an electronic dance music producer, he says, he would do what he does now to get on stage: share thoughts and opinions. Musicians tend to think they should just post their music and hope it goes viral. "No, I'm talking about the quality of ideas": choose a problem and a mission, create content around it, and attract people. "Your music is the product that you're selling. It's not the content." A loyal fan base would then come to shows and the work would spread. He compares this to his own experience at the Futureproof Summit, where he spoke on stage for the first time (public speaking "isn't his thing"; writing is, and he says he needs to write another book): that event was his "live show," and his content plays the role of a musician's content. The general lesson: once one has learned media and content, one can sell anything, including a book without a publisher or music.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

#### Content is king: why it compounds

In a 2024 video on the future of work, Koe ranks content above the other elements of an online presence: "Beginners really need to understand that **content is king**. Content matters more than your bio... your profile... your landing page or your ability to do well in a sales call. Writing is the most important skill because it gives you so much more slack on other things." "Content is king" is a common saying; Koe gives it a specific meaning through a comparison of two ways of acquiring customers. If people follow you, read your content and trust you before getting on a sales call, "you don't have to be an expert at sales." If you reach them through cold email, "you have to be extremely good at sales calls and your closing rate matters a lot," because people are much more skeptical. After three months of following someone, sales calls become easy (Koe immediately corrects his own exaggeration: "not that simple").

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The mechanism is the important part: "Content compounds because your message compounds in people's minds; it clicks." Koe explains it with an analogy. When one reads a book, one adopts the author's worldview, and sometimes one idea changes one's life. "When people follow you over time, it's like you writing a book to them over that time period, and it has a very similar effect." A November 2023 passage gives the time scale: "Good content occurs when you attempt to inject your mindset and skill set into your readers over the course of 6 to 12 months, not in one post." Content, in this view, is not a series of independent pieces judged one at a time but a long book delivered in installments, whose effect is cumulative.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md; Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The same November 2023 video presents content as a necessity that marketers discover late. "Content is your mindset and skill set." Marketers already know the power of traffic, of a list of interested customers and of long-form writing or video, but many (Koe includes himself) come to the "content game" late, without realizing that "the long webinar that made you millions can just be a video on YouTube that gets hundreds of thousands of views once you get the hang of the social media game." A webinar funnel is more strategic and more specifically "funneled," he concedes, but that does not exclude the other benefits of content, which he lists as a four-part framework.

1. **It enhances your thinking.** Ideas become clearer over time, and this "ripples into the rest of your life." "You can't have a great idea if you don't write a good idea first"; the first pieces will not be good, and that is acceptable.
2. **It is free most of the time.** One can grow organically, or move money from ads into paid growth by connecting with large accounts that share one's posts.
3. **It creates customers.** Unlike an ad, content leaves one with an audience one keeps and can remarket to at any time; and as one creates educational content, "people become customers because your information shapes who they are."
4. **It validates and tests ideas fast.** A tweet that performs better than usual can become a sales-page headline, a YouTube title or an entire product "that I know will sell," which removes the need to split-test newsletter headlines or video titles.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The first benefit repeats the inward argument of Section 21.2; the third anticipates the claim, developed below, that information creates identity; the fourth is the validation logic that Chapter 26 develops.

#### Where content sits in the business

Koe's insistence on content is easier to understand against the frameworks in which he places it. Over the years he has described the components of a one-person business several times, with changing numbers and names. Several of those versions belong to this chapter because they define content's role; Chapter 28 presents the pillars as a whole.

The earliest, from October 2022 (repeated literally in the 2024 compilation), names three things a one-person business needs.

- **Brand**: the audience, or the "storefront"; how one is perceived. A one-person business is branded on oneself (a personal brand, a personal business), and the brand revolves around one's vision for the future and one's goals, which is what one leads people toward. "Followers follow leaders, right? So if you are not a leader, then don't try to start a business."
- **Content**: how one attracts people to the brand. It is created from one's interests (self-development or skills) by educating people while adding one's own spin, personal thoughts, beliefs, mindset and what one does daily. One entertains, educates and inspires, and learns to structure content so that it catches attention, delivers value on that attention, and converts attention into a follow, a purchase or an email subscriber, "slowly building leverage over time."
- **Monetization**: the part people get wrong. Advertising revenue such as YouTube AdSense is fine but not under one's control, since a platform can remove one at any time. One needs an email list or one's own product or service, with control over price and traffic; that determines income. One must become valuable enough to sell something to a person "one to two steps behind you," or something with a use in other people's lives (his examples include a coffee brand, an L-theanine coffee).

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

By July 2023 the list had four items, stated as universal: "you need a brand to display yourself, you need content to show your authority, you need an offer or value (and that can be a digital product, service, or just working for someone else; that's a service), and marketing or promotion. You need all of that no matter what you're doing." In October 2023, in his "digital writer series," Koe gave a version specific to writing, the four principles or levers of monetizing writing: **distribution** (choosing a platform; "just having traffic"), **offer** ("something to send the traffic to; that's how you monetize an audience"), **leverage and scalability** (without which one cannot reach $1M a year "or at least without hating your life") and **promotions** (telling people about the product and sending them to a payment page). Distribution plus offer is "how you make money"; leverage makes it scale; promotion makes it convert. (One phrase in the transcript, "distribution is how you [build a] product and people," is garbled; the sense is that distribution supplies the traffic.)

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md; How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

In October 2024 the four became "the four pillars of creative work": brand, content, product and promotion. They describe how one distributes one's value and serve as the constraints within which one gets creative, and they solve two problems: most people focus on the skill with no idea how to monetize it, becoming a "starving artist," and most people do not know how to stand out. In that video Koe develops brand ("brand is an environment"), content ("people follow perspectives"), marketing ("education is the new marketing") and product ("systems are the new product"); promotion is not developed separately beyond the section on marketing, so its mapping is unclear.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

| Date | Components | Name of the fourth | Content's role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 2022 | Brand, content, monetization | (three items) | Attract people to the brand |
| Jul 2023 | Brand, content, offer, promotion | Promotion | Show authority |
| Oct 2023 | Distribution, offer, leverage & scalability, promotions | Promotions | Distribution = traffic |
| Oct 2024 | Brand, content, product, promotion | Promotion | "People follow perspectives" |
| 2026 | Brand, content, product (three pillars, "which used to be four") | Dropped | Content = knowledge, pieces of your mind, or map |

The fourth component is called monetization, offer, marketing or promotion at different times and disappears in 2026; content is described as "school" in one version and as "map" in another. Koe gives no reason for the changes, except to call one version an update. All the versions describe the same architecture (attract, nurture, monetize) through different lenses; only the metaphors collide.

Two further frameworks place writing within the larger picture. In a July 2024 thought experiment, Koe asks the viewer to imagine being an entrepreneur rather than an employee, with several products to build in a lifetime, "either before retirement or before death, because you found that retirement is an illusion and rest loses meaning without work." (He adds: "If we never worked we'd be miserable, because we'd eventually crave to work on something; the problem with that now is that we just work on the wrong things so we hate work, or we don't take enough rest so we don't crave work enough.") What would such a person need? A brand with a mission, vision and philosophy for an ideal customer; writing, speaking, ads, video, designs and emails to attract and nurture customers; a persuasive marketing message that stands out; a product that keeps iterating on feedback; "and more, repeated across all products you plan to build." The exercise was posed to reframe the question of what AI will replace: these are the components a person must orchestrate.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

And in March 2024 Koe drew a map of one's "domain of mastery," which locates writing precisely:

|---|---|---|
| Entrepreneurship | Vessel | Full control over one's future |
| Marketing and sales | Message | Inspire people to care |
| Writing and thinking | Medium | Distribute the message in public |
| Technical know-how | How | Succeed in the current market |

**Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md

The map's pairing of writing *and thinking* as the medium is the conclusion of Sections 21.1 and 21.2 in a single cell.

#### Idea warfare on mental real estate

If content is a magnet, what makes it attract? Koe's first answer, from December 2022 (repeated in the 2024 compilation), corrects what he sees as the beginner's misreading. Most people starting a one-person business "don't see internet content for what it is." It is not about catching attention (important, he concedes, "but not the point"), nor about having the most followers or engagement: "it's about valuable ideas." He defines the term: "**valuable ideas** equal relevant, understandable and actionable to the point of being spread without personal effort." And he names the arena: "**Internet content is idea warfare on mental real estate.**" The more perspective-shifting ideas one puts out, the more "mental real estate in the collective psyche" one occupies. His example is Eckhart Tolle's *The Power of Now*, which put mindfulness "on the map" for a large part of the collective psyche.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

**Complementary context:** Eckhart Tolle's *The Power of Now* (1997) is a spiritual book on presence and the observation of thought. It plays a role in Koe's biography (Chapter 2) and recurs as his standard example of an idea associated with a single author.

The three criteria of a valuable idea each exclude a common failure. An idea that is not relevant does not reach anyone it matters to; one that is not understandable cannot be adopted; one that is not actionable may be admired but changes nothing. The final clause ("to the point of being spread without personal effort") is the test: a valuable idea is one that others carry for you.

In April 2023 Koe told how such an idea had spread through him. When he is in an emotional rut, he thinks back to *The Power of Now* and one of its central ideas and how it helps him. He has recommended the book to others with the same problems because its ideas helped him so much and changed his behavior. Tolle, he says, "replicated a part of himself in my mind through education, that I attribute my progress to him." Koe then estimates how much money he has indirectly made for Tolle: through the people he recommended it to and the people they recommended it to, the network "layers out" to probably thousands; "three thousand times ten bucks, thirty thousand, is probably more than that honestly." The figure is a rough illustration, not a calculation. The lesson: ideas that stick spread through recommendation networks and earn their authors money indirectly.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

In December 2023 Koe pushed the idea to its most ambitious form. His chain begins with the biosphere, the biological substrate that must remain intact for what the transcript renders as the "new sphere" (apparently the sphere of mind) to expand and transcend. The self is a system or web of ideas (Chapter 3), and those ideas come from authors, thought leaders, parents and teachers. Authors therefore "become immortal" by "impregnating the minds of other people" through books, articles and videos. "This entire human existence is just a game of selling better ideas that lead to collective progress and transcendence and evolution," and it feels good to "reproduce on the conceptual level" by writing and improving people's lives, which he describes as "contributing to good versus evil," a moral frame that echoes the "spiritual war" over mental real estate he had described three months earlier (see the next subsection). His formula: "Authors and creators have had more children than Genghis Khan."

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md

**Complementary context:** "Noosphere" (the sphere of human thought), a term associated with Vladimir Vernadsky and Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, is the most likely referent of the transcribed "new sphere," though the transcript does not confirm it. The Genghis Khan comparison alludes to a widely reported 2003 genetic study suggesting that a large number of men in Asia descend from a single lineage often attributed to him. Koe uses both as rhetorical images, not as arguments.

This is a direct application of the law of conceptual survival (Chapter 3): if identities reproduce on the conceptual plane as bodies do on the biological one, writing is the reproductive act of that plane.

A 2023 passage on newsletter quality gives the practical consequence. The first step to a high-quality newsletter is to hunt for high-performing ideas, because "it's not the animations." It is not the captions on clips "like Alex Hormozi does," not tweet structures, specific YouTube structures, the perfect bio or the most colorful thumbnail: "**it's the ideas people collect from someone they like.**" In the same breath, however, Koe credits the animation agency he now owns with helping his top-of-funnel Instagram growth, and says it is how he went from 250,000 to 1.4 million followers. He states both things without reconciling them.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

That small inconsistency reflects a larger one that runs through the corpus: Koe alternates between "attention first" and "ideas first" depending on which error he wants to correct. In October 2022 he said that "the value inside does not matter until you catch attention"; in December 2022, that content is "not about catching attention... it's about valuable ideas"; in 2025, that those who abandon hooks lose the attention. There is no chronological turn; the corpus records it as an unresolved tension. Koe's own image elsewhere offers a way to hold both: the hook is the wrapping (packaging), the idea is the gift inside, and both are needed. Chapter 23 develops the wrapping; this chapter is about the gift.

#### The future belongs to whoever holds the most mental real estate

In April 2023 Koe gave "mental real estate" its programmatic formulation: "**The future belongs to those that hold the most mental real estate**," in other words those who capture and hold the most attention. TikTok "ain't gonna cut it," he says, because there one holds maybe ten seconds of attention in which to communicate a message, spread one's sense of self and influence actions "in an ethical, mutually beneficial and conducive manner to a good future." A creator and educator must focus on having ideas "stick in the back of people's minds," and one holds more attention with a newsletter, a YouTube video, a Twitter thread or an Instagram carousel.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

In September 2023 Koe gave the concept a collective dimension, quoting from his own book: "Whether they be positive or negative, the ideas that occupy the collective mental real estate will influence the progress we make as a species. The future belongs to those that can capture and hold attention with valuable information for the most amount of time possible." He then asks the question that connects the concept to Part I: "**Who wrote the code in your head?** That is to say, who fed you the information, ideas, beliefs, perspectives and worldviews that create your sense of self?" Mental real estate is "the most valuable resource," and "those who occupy the most mental real estate hold the most power in the modern world." "We are in a spiritual war." The phrase is meant as a description of a competition among ideas for minds, not as a religious claim; it echoes "idea warfare."

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

The same video illustrates the concept with an example Koe returns to often. James Clear wrote *Atomic Habits*. If he posted the single word "habits" on X, he would get thousands of likes; anyone else posting the same word would get "less engagement than they normally do." People forming new habits have Clear's ideas occupying their minds throughout the day, shaping their identity: "they become a half child of James Clear." From the example Koe draws three lessons.

1. "The longer you hold attention, the more power your ideas hold. Books will always reign supreme, podcasts and long videos come second, short content comes last, but all have their use cases."
2. "Your ideas must spark positive behavior change. If you change someone's life they will credit you over and over for your success, meaning your ideas spread without people consuming your work."
3. "When your ideas change lives, you hold attention longer": someone forming habits has Clear's ideas in mind all day.

Applied to writing: short-form writing on Twitter "is good and necessary for attracting initial attention, but long-form writing is for building trust and nurturing the mind and creating other selves." Short and long form go together, and neither should be neglected over time. Koe notes that he did not write a book until three years into creating content, but that the goal over time is to hold attention longer.

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

**Complementary context:** James Clear's *Atomic Habits* (2018) is a book on habit formation that became one of the best-selling nonfiction books of its decade; Clear built its audience through a long-running blog and email newsletter. Koe uses him as an example of authority, and elsewhere criticizes his "systems over goals" (Chapter 6).

An October 2023 passage states the same principle with a quotation from *The Art of Focus*, from the chapter titled "The Vessel" (on the one-person business, the shift in education and skill acquisition): "**Modern power belongs to those who hold the most attention in their lifetime.**" "A short post that holds 10 seconds of attention is nothing compared to a video, podcast or newsletter that holds 15 to 60 minutes." The step Koe recommends is to "create depth with your best ideas, depth and span": to turn the best short ideas into longer content. One can do well on a single platform, "but eventually you either choose to become a slave to that platform or you diversify." Long form is "how you get your name spread without you trying," how one builds "the roots of your brand deep into the collective psyche" and builds "trust and authority orders of magnitude higher." His evidence is the attribution effect: "you will almost always attribute the results in your life to a book or an author who taught you how, rather than someone you saw post about the same idea on Twitter." "I bet 90% of you that have read *Atomic Habits*... will think of James Clear" when someone posts about habits: "you're giving the authority to someone else because they chose to write long form." The "90%" is Koe's rhetorical wager, not a measurement.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don't Tell You).md

#### Attention as currency, and information as identity

A November 2023 video gives the theoretical basis for these observations. "**Attention is the currency of the third millennium**; the more people pay attention to you, the more power you have." Authors, podcasters and YouTubers often have the most loyal audiences "because **information creates identity**." The more attention one holds, the more information one delivers, and the more one "program[s] the minds of your audience" in a way that can change their lives, "and people attribute results to the people that change their life." One can write short posts all day, but "someone that has held more attention than you is always going to outperform you."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

"Information creates identity" is the market-side version of Part II's thesis that identity is built from absorbed ideas, and the creator-side version of Chapter 11's "choice of consumption." The creator who holds attention is, in the vocabulary of Chapter 1, writing part of other people's programming. Koe's qualifiers ("ethical, mutually beneficial," "conducive to changing their life") mark the responsibility that comes with this; Chapter 32 returns to the ethics of persuasion.

Koe applies the same reasoning to products. In September 2023 he argued that "people don't log on to social media to solve their problems; it isn't a learning platform, and most people can't focus, research and figure out a path for themselves." That is why products are valuable: "people know they are investing in a solution, then their attention and behavior follow suit." Koe's term for this is **invested attention**. A product holds a different kind of attention from content: attention a person has committed to, which therefore leads to action.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

He also cites a third party in support of long form. In August 2023, saying that "content is not optional anymore; it was a few years ago, but markets evolve," he quoted a tweet by Daniel Fazio, known online as the Cold Email Wizard: "sales cycles are getting longer, prospects are investigating you more, whoever has the most and best content will win." Koe makes the claim more specific: long-form content in particular, YouTube videos and podcasts, lets one prove the value one offers and show that one is experienced, can get results, is an expert and makes sense. The idea is Fazio's; the specification toward long form is Koe's.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md

#### The attention math

In the November 2023 video Koe puts numbers on the difference between formats. If he publishes 365 short posts a year on every platform, and each holds 30 seconds of attention, that amounts to about three hours of attention per reader per platform per year. If instead he publishes one long-form piece a week for his newsletter, YouTube and podcast (the same piece in three forms), each holding 20 minutes, he gets, in his words, "52 hours of attention held per reader for all platforms compared to 3 hours per reader for short form."

| Format | Pieces per year | Attention per piece | Total per reader |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short posts | 365 | 30 seconds | ≈ 3 hours (per platform) |
| Long-form weekly piece | 52 | 20 minutes | "52 hours" (as stated; see note) |

The arithmetic needs a note: 52 pieces of 20 minutes equal about 17 hours per platform; the figure of 52 hours appears to sum the three platforms. The comparison is as Koe stated it; the order-of-magnitude difference survives the correction. His conclusion is not to abandon short form but to treat short-form platforms "for what they are": a way to build a broad audience faster, validate ideas, and funnel people into products and long-form content. Koe names the unit of measure **attention held per reader**: the hours of attention one holds per reader per year, depending on the format.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

#### Time under attention

In the same video Koe gave the concept its definitive name, with evident satisfaction: he "finally labeled this concept, I created this concept, I'm so proud of this." **Time under attention**:

> the amount of attention you hold with each piece of content × the quantity of content × the quality of content × how often your ideas live in their head × how much they spread your ideas without effort = the authority, profitability and power your brand holds.

The name comes from weight training. "Time under tension" is the length of time a muscle remains under load, which some argue is a main driver of muscle growth along with progressive overload; Koe argues that its equivalent is a main driver of business growth. "This is arguably the most important thing that you should pay attention to when you're building your brand or writing your content."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The formula is multiplicative in form, though Koe does not define units for its terms; it is best read as a list of factors that compound rather than as an equation to compute. Its five factors summarize the section: attention per piece (long form beats short), quantity (consistency), quality (valuable ideas), recurrence in the reader's mind (ideas that change behavior are recalled), and effortless spread (ideas others carry). The September 2023 discussion of "mental real estate" already contained the substance; the November video supplied the name.

**Complementary context:** "Time under tension" is a strength-training variable referring to the total duration of a set during which the muscle is loaded. Its importance relative to load and volume is debated in exercise science; Koe's use is analogical.

Koe again uses James Clear as the case. Clear gets more engagement and reach because people have adopted his worldview; because they quote him in their own content, newsletters and podcasts; and because whenever someone else posts a related idea, "the authority is given to him because he comes to mind while reading your content." Having read *Atomic Habits*, readers of another post on habits have "James Clear sitting in the back of their mind; they're internally giving James Clear more attention": "Clear is still holding their attention while they read others' content." A tweet holds 30 seconds of attention; a book that "lives in their head rent free" holds far more than the five hours it takes to read: "it could consume days, weeks, months, years of attention over their lifetime," depending on how well it resonated. "This is why I'm writing a book even when books are like the least profitable thing you can make directly."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

Koe then adds the qualification that keeps the concept from becoming dogma. "This does not mean that short form posts on X, TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Instagram or LinkedIn are useless. It's the complete opposite; they have their use cases. This isn't black and white; you have to think holistically." The analogy continues: time under tension is not the only driver of muscle growth. One can hold a weight for a long time, "but if you're just sitting there starving, not eating anything, are you going to build muscle, are you going to grow your brand? No." Short-form platforms attract a broad audience faster and feed long-form platforms, which are "arguably more difficult to grow." Koe "almost always" recommends starting with short form to build an audience and a network and to validate high-performing ideas that can become long-form videos, newsletters or a book.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

That recommendation conflicts with other passages of the corpus, and the conflict is recorded rather than resolved. In other 2023 videos Koe recommends starting with the newsletter (in one, short and long form together); his writing course teaches starting from long form or even a book; in 2024 he says "short form is your base" and recommends that everyone start a newsletter; in 2026 he says to master tweets first while calling long form "a moat... because AI can't really replicate it." There is no clean chronological change in the entry point. What changes is the weight of long form and its justification, which moves from technical leverage toward cognition and differentiation from AI. The positions can be applied to different functions: short form to grow and test, long form or the newsletter for depth, ownership of the audience and sales. Chapter 24 develops the relationship in its treatment of the content ecosystem.

#### Ideas are the new oil

The last layer of the argument places writing in an economic history. In a June 2024 video Koe states: "What made the last generation rich won't make you rich." New wealth is generated in the digital space, which he calls **the idea space**. "The new rich aren't extracting finite physical resources, they're synthesizing the infinite resource called ideas." The physical world has limits: it is costly and slow (houses must be built, products shipped, communication spread). Ideas are "the new oil that anyone can tap." The formula is Koe's own; in a June 2025 passage (Chapter 22) he credits the underlying ideas, "ideas are the new oil" and getting rich "in idea space, not physical space," to Naval Ravikant, so the phrase is best read as Koe's reformulation of Naval.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

In the same video he argues against a piece of startup wisdom: that execution matters more than the idea, because anyone can have an idea but only execution creates wealth. Koe answers that execution is now readily accessible. Ideas are too, but "it's not about the quantity of ideas, it's about the quality of ideas," and about the ability to expand one's mind, develop oneself and solve an actual problem with one's execution. "The quality of ideas, distribution of ideas and iteration on ideas lead to a rich life." The digital world has no such limits, especially as specialized tasks are outsourced to AI; with focused study online one can become top 10 percent in any skill or interest and build a one-person business as a creator, which was impossible a few years earlier. Those who cannot, he says in internet slang, have a "skill issue."

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

A November 2024 passage generalizes the claim and turns it into a sequence of actions. "All new wealth is being generated in the digital space, the idea space, the creator economy, a place where people aren't limited by their connections or physical location because they can reach anyone with an internet connection." With the right skills ("writing, marketing and building"), "all you have to do is get what you build in front of a **micro-fraction** of people that perceive what you built as valuable." The closing sequence: create the content you want to see in the world; build the product you want to see; create content that stretches your mind and encourages you to grow as a writer; attract like-minded people to your interests by building an audience ("these are the people you can help the most"); build the product that contributes to the development and self-actualization of others; promote it to your audience "because you don't have a boss to do it for you anymore"; and earn your freedom.

**Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md

An earlier formulation, from June 2023, explains why ideas sit at the center: "**ideas are the new currency**." To make money one needs attention; a product or service to direct it to; and an understanding of how to guide attention through a journey (brand → newsletter → lead magnet → product → other platforms) and how to hold it within a long piece such as a YouTube video. "It all comes from ideas": one must "capture, hold and nurture their attention with ideas." Without creativity and ideas, everything about the brand suffers, and one falls back by default on direct response marketing, on frameworks and templates "for manipulating people's attention and holding it with something that isn't by your own design," which are not creative and do not advance understanding in the niche; "it becomes so shallow so fast." Koe says he has been in that position himself. The criticism is notable because direct response marketing is also something he recommends learning (Section 21.1). One reading, which is this book's interpretation rather than Koe's statement, is that his target is not the discipline but its use as a substitute for ideas.

The position did not stay where the 2023 passage leaves it, and the later step should be stated in Koe's own terms. In the earlier period he set value creators against attention tactics: they educate "instead of memes, drama or commentary" (October 2022), direct response becomes "so shallow so fast" (June 2023), the synthesizer stands apart from "general influencers who attract the masses with looks" (December 2023), and in May 2024 he calls himself a "creator, not an influencer." In parallel, as early as July 2023, he accepted memes as legitimate provided they are one pillar of a brand among others: "you're supposed to entertain, you're supposed to be yourself," but one cannot be only a meme account and expect to be valuable and paid. In September 2025 the relation is reformulated. His "level 3" value creators use social media as a tool for their life's work, with a through-line, a frame and a mission, and they understand that "if they don't capture attention, that meaning won't be transmitted"; capturing it does not require quick edits and rage bait, since books absorb readers "simply by telling a great story." His proposed solution is "merging level one with level two," that is, integrating the mechanics of attention with the pursuit of meaning instead of opposing them. The reason he gives is that without attention meaning is not transmitted. What is rejected in the later version is attention without meaning, not attention as such. Chapter 38 presents the three levels of creators and this proposal in full.

**Source:** The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md

**Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md

These formulations belong to a sequence the corpus tracks as a change of emphasis about what the "currency" of the new economy is. In 2022 Koe said that "attention isn't the new currency, information is"; in 2023, that ideas are the new currency and that mental real estate is "the most valuable resource"; in 2024, that ideas are the new oil. In 2025 and 2026 the emphasis moves again: "intelligence is no longer a differentiating resource," "taste is the new intelligence," and "meaning is the scarcest commodity in civilization right now." The reason Koe gives is that each preceding resource becomes cheap: when information is abundant, ideas matter; when ideas can be generated, taste and meaning matter. The layers are compatible, and each remains valid in its context. Writing is the constant across them, because it is the means by which information, ideas, taste and meaning are all made visible to others. Chapters 18 and 36 develop taste and the meaning economy.

### 21.4 The Creator as a Way of Life

#### Everything pointed to writing

The previous sections treated writing as a skill and content as a mechanism. Koe also describes something larger: a way of living in which writing is the daily activity that connects learning, work and income. He tells how he arrived at it in the October 2023 video that opened his "digital writer series," as the third of several realizations.

Writing on Twitter showed him that he was not just writing tweets: "I was writing emails, newsletters, landing pages, promotions, video scripts, direct messages, course curriculums, product material, everything." Explaining what he did had become complicated ("I kind of sell web design services, but I'm also on social media"), so he simplified: "I'm just a writer." From this he arrived at a definition of the content creator "not as a job but as a way of life: of capturing ideas and distributing the valuable ones to people like you, and then solving your own problems and selling the solution to the people like you that have the same problems. It's just like the most natural business model there is. It's much deeper than 'oh, I just want to be a YouTuber.'" He wrote every single morning even when he had client work to fulfill: "that was the driver of my business." This, he says, is how his course 2 Hour Writer, which teaches his writing ecosystem, was born.

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

The definition has four verbs (capture, distribute, solve, sell) and a single recurring object, "people like you." It condenses Part VII ("you are the niche," "sell to your past self") and Chapter 28's principle "solve your own problems and sell the solution" into a description of a daily life. Koe later uses "content creator" in a different, critical sense: in 2024–2025 he rejects the term because it focuses on output rather than on building an audience. The 2023 definition, as a way of life, is the one that belongs here.

#### Document your mind

In August 2024 Koe reduced the creator's job to one sentence: "**Your job is to document your mind and experience on the internet. That's it.**" The mechanism of attraction follows from the model of identity: one attracts a specific group "by writing ideas in a way that makes sense to you from your worldview," and "the specific group of people are those with your same personality. That's how you set yourself up to create a product for yourself that sells without market research." The practical instruction: "When you notice an idea, post it, but from your own point of view. That's what makes you unique. Take the ideas that already work and post them under your brand."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

The last sentence touches a question the corpus tracks over several years: how much a creator may borrow. Koe's position moves between "people need to be reminded more than they need something new" (which allows repeating validated ideas) and an increasing demand for one's own perspective. The 2024 sentence holds both: the idea may already work elsewhere, but it is posted "from your own point of view." Chapter 22 presents the full evolution.

An earlier version, from June 2023, calls content "**documenting your mind**" and treats social media as a public note-taking system: one notes what one is learning and how it applies to one's life, one's thoughts and opinions on one's skills and interests, and the lessons drawn from the story of one's life. Koe adds a collective observation: "Without knowing it, we are actively building out the collective consciousness online in an explorable fashion." The same passage, though, sets a limit on documentation alone. One cannot "write and pray" that it grows. One must study the evergreen skills (marketing, sales, copywriting) while building one's brand toward a goal, so as to have something to apply them to. If one does not know what to learn, his answer is the same as in Section 21.1: marketing, sales, copywriting and direct response marketing. He recommends the book *Digital Millionaire Secrets*, admitting the "cringe" title and its pushy-salesman connotations, as a short, insightful read for understanding what happens online through a marketing lens; one should take its lessons "and use them ethically." (He also mentions the free portion of his own course, Digital Economics.)

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

In January 2026 Koe gave the creator's work a guiding principle that adds curation to documentation: "The guiding light for your content should be to curate the best possible ideas in one place. **Your brand is a collection of all the ideas you care about in your own words under one account on the internet.**" The definition is useful because it removes the need for a single topic: what unifies a brand is the person whose words and whose caring connect the ideas, which is the argument of Chapter 20 on multiple interests.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

#### The holistic synthesizer and the trail up the mountain

Koe has given the documenting creator several names, each emphasizing a different aspect. In March 2023 he called the ideal the **holistic synthesizer**: "someone who pursues their own vision and forges their path with the unique skills and interests they acquire." Such a person does not treat skills and interests as separate parts or as temporary pieces "for a quick cash grab" but as an interconnected whole, necessary aspects of a life. Their life's work "is to distill, educate and distribute their personal experience on the path." In short, "a holistic synthesizer is someone who documents their journey to the good life in an educational and persuasive manner." Koe explains both words: "holistic" because the skills form an interconnected whole rather than compartments; "synthesizer" because the person combines them. He speaks of "the emergence and demand for the holistic synthesizer." The term connects to the generalist of Chapter 20 and to the synthesizer of Chapter 18.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md

In September 2023 he described the same practice as **building for yourself**, the step that comes after learning with intention: pursue your goals and document the process, keeping running notes, logs, successes and failures somewhere safe, physical or digital. "From your journey you gain a **knowledge of 100**; from that you can create a **path of one**." The formula describes a distillation: a person who has tried many things and read many sources can extract one specific route that worked, and that route is teachable. This, Koe says, is how one passes down "a unique system that markets itself and gets better results than those looking to make a quick buck." Along the way one posts the advice, lessons and authentic successes and failures one has experienced: "This is called building in public. Don't do it for others; do it as a digital journal. It will pay off."

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

The earliest version is a metaphor, from November 2022. Holistic development is "like climbing a mountain but carving out a trail along the way that other people can follow, or documenting your journey so others can achieve it faster." If enough people do this, Koe adds, people will reach higher levels of consciousness and of personal and professional growth much faster. The image contains the whole model: the climb is one's own development, the trail is the content, and those who follow are the audience, who move faster because someone went first.

**Source:** Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md

#### Energy transfer: where the best content comes from

A September 2023 passage, from the video "Life Is A Video Game," defines content in the vocabulary of Chapter 10: "Your content is the ideas, thoughts, beliefs, opinions, lessons and advice you acquire as you pursue your challenging hierarchy of goals" (elsewhere he uses the related phrase "lessons learned completing quests"). Koe says that writing changed his life by giving him self-awareness, self-understanding and the ability to organize his thoughts, which he describes as the ability to "order consciousness." This is why his course teaches "high impact writing," which, he says, will not go out of style "even with the emergence of AI."

**Source:** Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md

Content frameworks and templates help, Koe concedes, "but **the best content comes from energy transfer**." As one acquires knowledge and skills, some things register more strongly than others: what produces excitement or dopamine, what is novel, what is useful through the lens of one's vision. For that filter to work one needs a vision or goal and an active problem, so that one can tell good information from bad; when one finds good information, one feels it, notes it and shares it. Because it excited the writer, it will excite others; if it spreads, that is how a "like-minded tribe" forms. The concept is a direct application of the goal-as-lens model (Chapter 6) and of the distinction between cheap and meaningful dopamine (Chapter 11): the creator's excitement is a signal of relevance, and content transmits it. (In a May 2023 passage, Koe used "energy transfer" in a related sense, as what good writing is: transferring energy rather than being professional or grammatically perfect.)

**Source:** Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md

An April 2024 video on his daily routine adds a distinction that places the creator within a broader category. The routine is meant for anyone who is "an entrepreneur of sorts," trying "to gain full control over your income so you can be in full control of your life." For many people this falls under **creative work** (design, writing, art) or **creator work** (self-improvement, productivity or fitness content). "They're all relatively the same thing, and you're going to have to do creative work in order to do creator work." Without a daily habit that can be executed "day after day after day" and that leads to long-term results, "you can't expect much results." The claim is that the creator's public output depends on a private craft practiced daily, which in Koe's case is writing.

**Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md

#### The public journal

Koe's most persistent image for the creator's practice is the **public journal**. He uses it from 2022 to 2025 with almost identical wording. In October 2022: "In essence you treat Twitter, or even just social media in general, as your public journal... your public note-taking software... and... your public school for other people." In December 2022, he adds a practical use: "Think of social media as a public note-taking software, a public journal and a public school." When he writes a newsletter, he runs an advanced search on his own tweets (for a word such as "valuable") and finds material ready to work from. Social media thus serves as a note-taking system, as a journal for relatable thoughts, worries and ideas, and as writing practice.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md; You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md

The three terms describe three functions of the same act. As a journal, posting serves the writer's reflection. As note-taking software, it stores ideas in a searchable form that later work can draw on (this is the "external record of thinking" of Section 21.2, made public). As a school, it teaches whoever reads it. The metaphor removes the pressure of authority: one does not need to be an expert to keep a journal.

A July 2024 passage states that consequence directly. "I like to think of my Twitter, or just social media in general, as my notes. I like to share the ideas that I found helpful myself. I like to give myself advice; it's like a public journal." "I don't need to promise certain things like making $5 billion; I can just talk about my own experience and the ideas that I found impactful," and "I don't think you need any experience to share an idea that you found in a book in your own words."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

#### Writing to yourself: past, present and future

The public journal has a specific addressee: oneself. In the 2024 compilation (in a passage on packaging one's knowledge), Koe presents the step of treating oneself as one's own ideal reader. While one is one's brand and content, one is also the reader: one creates value for oneself. The method distinguishes three selves.

- **The past self.** Self-reflect (through solitude, thinking, free thinking on walks, listening to what one wants), remember a time when one was in a low place, and write to that person. The writing can be encouragement ("hey man, I know you're in a hard place... just keep doing what you're doing," which Koe admits is cheesy) or advice ("I remember when I didn't understand this thing in marketing" becomes "here are the five marketing principles you need to understand: catching attention, creating value...").
- **The current self.** If one is going through a hard time, give oneself advice for overcoming it; others will benefit. If things are going well, tell oneself why, reinforcing good behavior and trying to fix bad behavior.
- **The future self.** Give oneself encouragement to keep going.

In essence, Koe says, one treats social media as one's "public journal," "public note-taking software" and "public school for other people." He connects this to the role that business, writing and content creation play in his life: they are his method of self-actualization, in place of meditation. In the same passage he mentions that he is looking for a term for "productizing myself," with candidates such as "mental monetization" and "the business of self," since "one-person business" is too broad.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

An August 2024 passage restates the method and adds both a mechanism and a warning. "The best way to think of this is writing to yourself. All of my posts and newsletters are to me. My profile is my public journal." The mechanism is reinforcement: by writing, Koe convinces himself to make better decisions (reading more, for example), so that the ideas become part of "a better, more holistic identity." This is Part II's model of identity applied to oneself: what one repeatedly articulates becomes part of who one is. The warning follows from the same mechanism. Writing only about shallow, myopic topics, "getting laid or making a quick buck," without thinking beyond them makes one shallow and myopic. Koe does not say one should never write about such topics, but that one should "cultivate a philosophy of depth and meaning behind them through education and experience." The practice in its 2024 form: "Write to your past self and encourage them to improve; show them how. Write to your present self and program them to do better. Write as your future self and give your current self advice."

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

The same video gives the method a simple input. When reading, one notes ideas that stick out, "aha moments" and actual advice one can experiment with and make one's own. One is not only sharing ideas from books; often one uses them to achieve goals and then shares "the mindset and skill set" that helped. "Writing doesn't have to be complicated": one does not need his course or his software, Cortex, which are tools and systems for experimenting, getting more results and moving through trial and error faster. Passing down teachings, he adds, is how humanity evolves: calculators replaced manual arithmetic, and website builders replaced the ten-person teams once needed when coding languages were less efficient. If one simply enjoys writing, that is fine; if writing is tied to future income and a life's work, he argues that $150 for a course is "really not that much of an investment" (adding, "I'm terrible at promotions"). The passage ends: "**Writing can just be writing. You already do it; just do it consciously in a way that benefits your future and others.**"

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

The "write to yourself" principle has a counterweight in the corpus, recorded as a tension between sources rather than a correction by Koe. In 2023 he advised talking about oneself in replies, because it produces the most profile clicks, and described his own writing as addressed to himself. In a 2025 conversation, the founders of the creator platform Stan offered partly different advice: one proposed writing to one's younger self and moving beyond the "me, me, me" of the survival stage; another proposed sharing one's life story while warning that "me, me, me" content fails. In 2026 Koe clarified that filtering ideas through one's story does not mean talking about oneself all the time but translating ideas. The positions are compatible if the self is understood as the *source* and the bridge of identification, and the audience as the *focus*. Chapter 19 discusses writing to one's younger self.

#### Writing in public and confidence

In May 2024 Koe offered public writing as "an unconventional path to becoming self-confident," explicitly not the only one. "When I started writing it changed my life, and not just writing in a journal, which is extremely helpful, but writing in a public journal." Writing helped him "become confident in my thoughts and life direction" in four ways:

1. by exposing him to public criticism, which forced him to audit his beliefs;
2. by helping him find friends and connections with shared goals, "so I stopped feeling like an outcast";
3. by letting him practice a skill and see progress every day;
4. by "failing often to keep me humble and give me new direction that I wouldn't have normally found."

**Source:** It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md

These are reported from experience; Koe frames them as one path among others. They map onto themes from earlier chapters: criticism as the public form of the self-examination that Section 21.2 attributes to writing; connection as the like-minded tribe; daily progress as the small wins that drive identity change (Chapter 4); failure as the compass of Chapter 15.

Later in the same video the advice becomes a step in a process for building confidence. If one is not a fan of writing ("which I would heavily question"), one should notice that "everyone writes: you text your friends, you send emails, you're a writer whether you think you are or not," or think of it as communicating in public. "Writing is a superpower": it lets one achieve goals faster, attract like-minded people, start with zero experience and become comfortable with people seeing one's ideas. The justification repeats Section 21.1: "Writing is the foundation of media; everything you see online, from posts to videos to advertisements, starts with writing. Media is where the attention is," and to do anything worthwhile "you're either going to need money or attention." One can no longer rely on a boss to generate attention, attract customers and assign "measly work": "you must do all of the above." Hence: "**Writing in public is the first step for beginners who don't have capital or experience to start a business**," a "**zero barrier of entry skill**." The method has two steps. First, choose two or three topics to write about: ones one wants to learn about, build a name in, and turn into a meaningful career. Second, learn through observation: watch what others in those topics write, how they write it and why they say things the way they do. "If you think 'I could have written this,' then write about it; you don't need permission on the internet."

**Source:** It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md

A 2022 passage gives the deepest version of "you don't need permission." Before the internet, only the most profound people (Socrates, Plato and their like) had works that survived them, and now they are seen as almost "mythological." But in their own time their way of speaking was normal, as Koe's vernacular is normal now; it would be strange for him to speak like an ancient Stoic today. Their words are profound, with "insanely good lessons," but to think that one is not capable of the same "just by speaking your truth or trying to actually create something of value to put out to the world and impact people's lives" is a mistake. There were probably more profound people than the ones we know, he adds; the difference now is that the internet lets anyone spread ideas widely. The argument does not claim that every writer is Plato; it claims that the distance between the classics and ourselves is partly an effect of time and survival, and that this distance should not be mistaken for a prohibition.

**Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md

#### Brand is what you do: writing without being an expert

The public journal answers one objection (the pressure to be an authority); a set of heuristics answers the related objection, which beginners raise constantly: "I'm not an expert, so what can I write?"

The first answer dates from August 2022. Someone objects: "I can't get online and say I'm making $40–60k a month writing, so what can I do?" Koe's response is to talk about it from a different perspective. One can post something as simple as "seven things that I learned about impactful writing," a framing that builds authority without faking it or trying to be "this guy that makes a ton of money." "You just be the guy that is learning. **Brand is what you do.**" Whatever one is doing right now is what one writes about: if one is learning something, one writes about how one is learning it and teaches what one is learning.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md

In November 2022 he added a second position. Experience is always the struggle, but "you don't have to be a content creator from the start; you can be a **content curator**." It is a matter of positioning and perspective. Someone who wants to write about starting a one-person business but has not started one can research everything about the subject, write the post, and make clear that the advice is not how they did it but how other people did it and how they intend to do it.

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md

The most recent formulation, from June 2026, begins with a diagnosis. People who want to start writing have problems that "revolve around the same things. They don't know what to write, and they don't know how to write it. They don't know what makes their writing different from everyone else's. They don't know why people would listen to them over someone else. They don't see themselves as experts, but don't understand that **experts aren't the only ones who have an audience**." The solution follows in the same video: "What if I don't consider myself an expert? That's why you position yourself as a student and a researcher rather than an expert. You don't have to claim results. You don't have to claim that your videography skills are going to 10x their views overnight. Learning the skill in and of itself, if the tip is useful, is useful." One can build a "**student-style personal brand**": "You just learn, research, and share what you found without claiming any results until you actually have results."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

The four questions in the diagnosis (what to write, how, what makes it different, why anyone would listen) are, in effect, the agenda of the next three chapters: Chapter 22 answers the first and third, Chapter 23 the second, and the whole of Part VII the fourth.

#### The beginner market

A related heuristic concerns not the writer's level but the reader's. In October 2022 Koe stated that "95% of the market are beginners." Everyone is ahead of others in some area and has information to pass down at a very beginner level as actionable advice: "that's what every single person does to grow at the start." Vloggers did not start with random vlogs ("I bought a new car"); they taught something from a beginner's level. A health creator posts foundations and fundamentals (maintaining health, building muscle, losing fat); the same holds for writing (how to start, its benefits, foundational principles, persuasive communication). "Shut your ego off," Koe says, and provide value to those who need it, even if one feels above that level.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

In January 2023 he turned the observation into a diagnostic for messages that do not land. If one's words do not resonate, one is doing one of three things wrong: speaking from an advanced level "when 95% of the market are beginners"; not understanding persuasion, which requires answering, or implying an answer to, "**what's in it for me**"; or failing to capture attention and deliver a valuable message to that attention.

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The figure varies across the corpus (90 percent in a 2023 passage, 95 percent in others), and it is a heuristic estimate, not a measurement. Its formulation also evolved. In 2024 it was qualified by the instruction to "prioritize depth and clarity"; in 2025 Koe spoke of starting at the surface ("vanity") before depth ("therapy"), saying that "shallow content has a place" and that one should "dumb down your ideas as a creative challenge"; in July 2026 he restated it in terms of developmental stages: 90 to 95 percent of people are in the stages of survival and identity, and the creator's task is to bring them "up the ladder." The same heuristic thus moves from a market statistic to a developmental map (Chapter 38). In practice the positions combine: basics in short form, depth in long form and in the product.

#### Public experimentation, breadcrumbs and the portfolio of failures

Three further heuristics turn the beginner's lack of results into an asset.

The first comes from Dickie Bush, a writer and co-founder of the writing program Ship 30 for 30, in a March 2023 conversation with Koe; the idea is Bush's. Looking back at Koe's early fitness content, Bush named the principle behind it: "**public experimentation**," figuring things out as one went, even though "it wasn't framed as that." Once the basics are covered and one starts talking about newer, less fundamental (more esoteric) things, the stance tends to shift from "I'm experimenting" to feeling that one must justify what one is doing. Bush's principle is to keep doing the experimentation and to share it, because that journey is what people are interested in: "they want to see that you don't have it all figured out." Meeting the people they had looked up to showed both men that those people, too, were figuring things out along the way. This "takes a lot of pressure off." Instead of becoming an expert and telling everyone so, "completely flip it and say 'I actually have no clue what I'm doing most of the time'; it'll work so much better." The stance is a beginner's mindset: "here's what I'm trying, here's what I'm thinking, anyone else interested in this?", which attracts people running similar experiments. Bush says most of his own content takes this form ("through the lens of 'I'm trying to learn this'... a reminder to myself, or 'here's what I'm currently learning, I'm gonna try to distill that and then hear what other people have to think'"). His example: at 280 pounds, after his last college football snap, he felt he "was either going to 380 or 180," and he wrote publicly about what he was trying "as a way of crystallizing that experimentation."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md

The second is Koe's, from October 2022 (repeated in the 2024 compilation): position yourself from where you actually are. A beginner does not need to write posts or build products promising "how to make a million dollars in 3 days." One pays attention to where one is now (perhaps at $0), where one is going and how one will get there, and positions one's content, product and self-presentation accordingly. "You don't write a post on how to make a million dollars in 3 days; you write 'here's how I'm going to make a million dollars in 5 years,'" and one means it and publishes the plan. People follow because one is positioned as a leader ("people follow leaders") helping others reach the goals one is pursuing oneself, "leaving **breadcrumbs** along the way." The term has a second sense in the corpus: the breadcrumbs left as content and media can later be aggregated into a step-by-step system sold as a product (Chapter 33).

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The third, from March 2024, treats failure as the creator's story. "Be a failure. Launch the project to a crowd of crickets, publish your thoughts and get called a fool, start the venture and watch people criticize your first moves. Everyone else did it; you aren't a special case. **Invest in your portfolio of failures until you can afford to succeed.**" Koe then explains why failure attracts: "Your portfolio of failures is your story, and like a movie, people are interested more in the lows than they are the highs; the lows set up the scene for the highs." People are drawn to negativity and drama, "but you can present that in a way that allows them to actually change and improve and receive some form of value from it." And: "You attract people and profit to you when you have the solution to a problem in your life" (the transcript reads "people in profit," most likely "people and profit").

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

Taken together, the three heuristics describe the same position from three angles: the experimenter shares the process, the leader shares the plan, and the failure becomes the narrative. None requires results to begin; each produces results as it goes. They also connect back to Section 21.2: a writer who presents himself as figuring things out in public is practicing the stance of the essay ("Do I actually believe this?") rather than that of the authority.

#### Of course the first post will flop

The last piece of advice in this section is the simplest, and Koe delivers it with deliberate impatience. In an August 2026 video he addresses the beginner's fear that the first post will flop: "You don't think that the first post is going to do amazing... go viral and get famous all of a sudden? You don't think that it takes years? ... Of course, it's going to suck. Of course, it's going to be terrible. **Of course, you're going to be embarrassed. That's the entire point.**" He exaggerates his frustration for effect ("I just kind of want to... give you a little slap and be like, 'Hey, dude, just post'").

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

"That's the entire point" closes a line of reasoning that runs through the whole chapter. If writing is how one thinks (Section 21.2), then early writing is bad because early thinking is unclear, and publishing it is how the gap becomes visible and can be closed. If writing is a meta skill that one can start without experience (Section 21.1), then the first posts are the "without experience" part. If good content injects a mindset over six to twelve months (Section 21.3), then no single early post carries much weight. And if one is documenting one's journey (this section), the embarrassing beginning is part of the trail that others will later follow.

### 21.5 Stories as the DNA of Societies

#### A guest's widening of the argument

Everything so far in this chapter concerns nonfiction: posts, newsletters, essays, scripts, the documentation of a mind. The corpus contains one extended passage that widens the value of writing to fiction and to culture, and it comes from a guest rather than from Koe. In a December 2024 conversation, Koe interviewed the novelist Devon Eriksen, author of the science fiction novel *Theft of Fire*. Koe admits in the same conversation that *Theft of Fire* was the first fiction book he had read in almost ten years, which gives the exchange its tension: the guest is arguing, in part, against the host's habits. All the ideas in this section are Eriksen's. Koe does not adopt them as his own in the corpus, and several are stated polemically; they are presented here as a guest's opinions, with the evidence the transcript contains, which for the causal claims is none beyond assertion and example.

#### "Stories are the DNA of societies"

Koe asked Eriksen why he chose fiction over, for example, building a software tool as an independent developer. Eriksen answered that through self-examination he had realized that "stories meant more to me." Many people, he said, think genre fiction is frivolous; he sees this attitude in what he calls "sigma hacker grindset bros" who say "I haven't read a fiction book in 10 years, I only read tech manuals." "Well, that's how you lose the culture war, because **stories are the DNA of societies**." His example is Elon Musk: "he did not grow up reading *Little House on the Prairie*... I bet he grew up reading Heinlein and Niven and Pournelle, and then he went, 'I'm going to make that real.'" Later in the conversation he returns to the image: founders who build reusable rockets and catch the first stage "with a giant pair of Mecha Chopsticks" do not say they were influenced by Marcus Aurelius or Nietzsche; "he's like, 'I read *Ringworld*.'"

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

**Complementary context:** Robert A. Heinlein, Larry Niven and Jerry Pournelle are major American science fiction writers of the mid-to-late twentieth century; Niven's *Ringworld* (1970) is a classic of "hard" science fiction. The "chopsticks" refer to the mechanical arms SpaceX began using in 2024 to catch the Super Heavy booster on return to the launch tower. Eriksen's claim about what Musk read is stated as a guess ("I bet"), not as documented fact. Heinlein appears elsewhere in Koe's corpus as the source of "specialization is for insects" (Chapter 20).

The metaphor of DNA carries the argument: just as genetic information determines what an organism will build, stories determine what a society aspires to and therefore what it builds. The idea has an obvious kinship with Koe's own concept of mental real estate (Section 21.3), the claim that "the ideas that occupy the collective mental real estate will influence the progress we make as a species." Eriksen applies the same logic to a kind of writing Koe rarely discusses.

#### Science fiction as a technology for thinking

Eriksen develops the claim with a challenge: think of all the inventions of the twentieth century and "name four that didn't happen in science fiction before they were real." He presents this as a hard task, and concludes that science fiction is "in some sense very important to our future, because it is this technology that allows us to think about new technologies before they're real." It lets a society decide how it feels about true artificial intelligence before it exists, or about "genetically engineered posthumans and what we want to do about that possibility." It lays the groundwork for dealing with new technology "and it fires our imaginations to create it." Science fiction is entertaining, "but it's also important in ways that people don't realize."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The challenge is rhetorical rather than a demonstrated historical claim, but the function Eriksen describes is precise: fiction as a place to rehearse futures, both ethically (how should we feel about this?) and motivationally (should we build this?). In the vocabulary of this chapter, it is a form of thinking done through writing, at the scale of a culture rather than of an individual.

Eriksen also makes a claim about the present market for stories. "There's a lot of unfulfilled hunger for these kinds of stories right now, because we're sick of post-apocalyptic despair... we're sick of fiction which is a thinly veiled excuse for discussions about pronouns." "Science fiction used to be about how we were going to go out and conquer the galaxy, and I think we're ready to do that again; we're ready to work toward it." There is much we do not know how to do and will need to learn, "but we're ready to start doing that instead of sitting around and whining about how everything is unfair, so we need stories that have that attitude." This is an opinion about cultural mood, and its political edge is Eriksen's.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

#### The coupled artistic aesthetic

The most polemical part of the conversation explains Koe's own reading habits. Eriksen says he understands why Koe has not read fiction: Koe is "an alpha male type," and in a bookstore one "ain't going to find much" fiction appealing to that. His explanation is historical. Conservatives and people who favor Western civilization "somehow decided at some point that art was for sissies," because many artists were "long-haired, noodle-armed hippies," and so they ceded "that entire battlefield." "But see, children read stories, children read fiction, and if they're not reading your culture's fiction, whose fiction are they reading?" His example is the commentator Steven Crowder, who said proudly on a show, "I don't read fiction," calling it childish and mocking "tell me a story, daddy." Eriksen's reply: one cannot complain about losing the culture war and then rant that culture is for sissies; "if your children say 'tell me a story, daddy' and you're not telling them a story, then who is, and what values will your children absorb from that story?"

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

From this he draws a general principle: "Any political philosophy, any cultural philosophy that doesn't have art and stories associated with it, that doesn't have a **coupled artistic aesthetic**, is doomed, because it will not be able to propagate itself." He then applies it, again polemically, to masculinity: without fiction for "alpha male types," there will not be a next generation of such men but "a next generation of guys who are very high anxiety and low agency, and nothing feels right, and they don't feel comfortable in their own skin." (He follows this with a contested claim about gender identity that the transcript does not support with evidence.) He assigns responsibility by generation: "Generation X, we stood by and let this happen; the Boomers made it happen; but we as a society have not created or maintained the cultural and artistic scaffolding for a healthy, strong, robust masculine identity."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The general principle and its application should be kept apart. The principle (a philosophy without art cannot propagate) is a claim about how ideas spread, and it fits the model of ideas, identity and reproduction that runs through this chapter: if "information creates identity," then whoever supplies a society's stories supplies part of its identities. The application to masculinity and to a culture war is Eriksen's political interpretation, asserted without evidence in the conversation; Koe neither endorses nor disputes it in the passages available.

#### The bucket and the subconscious

The last of Eriksen's ideas returns to the act of writing itself. Asked whether he felt responsible for "baking" his values into *Theft of Fire*, he answered with a metaphor: "When you write, you're essentially tying a rope to a bucket, and you're throwing it into your subconscious, and you're dredging it across the bottom, and you take up that bucket and you pour it out on the table." The consequence is emotional: "it's a very psychologically vulnerable act in some ways, because **the fiction you write is who you are**." When one shows others who one is inside, one wants their approval, "but also it has to make sense, it has to resonate."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The bucket metaphor describes, from a novelist's side, what Koe describes from an essayist's side in Section 21.2. For Koe, writing collapses the forest of thought into a road and reveals what one actually believes; for Eriksen, it dredges up what was at the bottom of the mind and lays it out to be seen. Both make writing an act of exposure, and both add the same external condition: what is exposed must also make sense to someone else. Eriksen's "it has to resonate" is, in this respect, the fiction writer's version of Koe's "valuable ideas" and "what's in it for me." The chapter that follows takes up that condition directly: given that writing is the base skill, what should one write about?

### Exercises

1. **Audit your writing week.** For seven days, record everything you write: texts, emails, messages, notes, proposals, posts, comments. Estimate what share of your working communication is written. Then evaluate Koe's claims that "you are already a writer" and that "if you're not typing on the keyboard you're not doing anything online" against your own record. Where do they hold, and where does your work genuinely depend on something else (manual work, speech, presence)? What would it mean, concretely, for you to "do it consciously in a way that benefits your future and others"?

2. **Test forced linearity.** Choose a belief you hold firmly about your work, your values or a contested topic, one you feel you understand but have never written out. Write it as a single argument of 500 to 800 words, without a template, following Koe's six essay steps. Afterwards, answer: Where did the "forest" refuse to become a "road"? Which part did you find you could not articulate, and what does that gap tell you to learn next? Did you change your mind about anything, and if not, did you honestly ask "Do I actually believe this?"

3. **Classify your inputs and outputs.** Using the fast content / slow content distinction, sort the last twenty pieces of content you consumed and, if you publish, the last ten you produced. Which delivered conclusions without requiring thought, and which required you (or your reader) to think? Then assess Koe's position critically: he taught thread-writing and hooks, defended clickbait as "one stock down while the index fund is up," and later listed "engagement-optimized threads" as fast content. Is this a contradiction, a refinement, or two rules for two kinds of piece? Defend your answer with criteria you could apply to a specific post.

4. **Apply time under attention.** Take a creator or author whose ideas you think about often. Analyze their presence using the five factors of time under attention (attention per piece, quantity, quality, recurrence in your mind, effortless spread). Which factor explains most of their hold on you? Then apply the same analysis to your own output, or to an output you plan. Identify the weakest factor and design one change that would strengthen it. Finally, examine one limit of the formula: what could make a creator score high on every factor while still being bad for their audience, and what does Koe's qualifier "ethical, mutually beneficial and conducive to a good future" add or fail to add?

5. **Reconcile the entry point.** Koe recommends, in different passages, starting with short form, starting with a newsletter, starting with long form or even a book, and "mastering tweets first." Lay out the strongest case for each, using the attention math, the weightlifter "starving" analogy, the validation logic and the 2026 argument about AI. Then decide what you would recommend to (a) a beginner with one hour a day and no audience, and (b) an expert with a small, loyal list. Explain which factors in their situation determined your decision.

6. **Write to three selves.** Following the public-journal method, write three short pieces: one to your past self at a low point (encouragement or advice), one to your present self about a current difficulty or success, and one as your future self giving advice to the present. Then examine them with Koe's warning about depth: are any of them about a "shallow, myopic" topic, and if so, what "philosophy of depth and meaning behind them" could you develop? Finally, consider the tension recorded in Section 21.4: would these pieces serve a reader, or only you? What would have to change for the self to be the source and the reader to be the focus?

7. **Position without results.** Choose a field you are learning but in which you have no results yet. Draft the opening of three posts, each from a different positioning described in Section 21.4: the content curator ("how others did it and how I'm going to do it"), the public experimenter ("here's what I'm trying"), and the leader leaving breadcrumbs ("here's how I'm going to do X in five years"). Which feels most honest to you, and which would most likely attract the people one or two steps behind you? Identify at least one risk in each positioning (for example, the plan that is never carried out, or curation that never becomes experience).

8. **Evaluate a guest's argument.** Reconstruct Devon Eriksen's argument that "stories are the DNA of societies" as a chain of premises and a conclusion. Mark which premises are supported in the conversation by examples, which by guesses ("I bet"), and which by assertion alone. Then separate the general principle (a philosophy without a "coupled artistic aesthetic" cannot propagate itself) from its political application. Is the principle true of a domain you know, such as a profession, a movement or a company culture? What would count as evidence against it?
