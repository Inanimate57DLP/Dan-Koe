## Chapter 33 — Building the Product

Chapter 31 established what value is: the reversal of entropy, perceived through the identity and awareness of the buyer, and tested by behavior change. Chapter 32 showed how that value is communicated. This chapter turns to the object that carries value from one person to another: the product. Part X already argued that a business is, at minimum, an audience plus a product (Section 28.5), that the first money usually comes from a service (Section 29.3), and that service work eventually becomes a "new 9-to-5" that has to be productized (Section 30.1). What those chapters left open is the construction itself. What should you sell? Where does the product come from? Which form should it take, how is it delivered, and what should it cost? And why does Koe insist that every creator needs a product of their own instead of living off sponsorships and platform payouts?

The chapter answers these questions in seven steps. Section 33.1 presents Koe's founding claim that everyone carries a sellable product "in their head", and the seven-step process he gave in 2022 for extracting it. Section 33.2 gathers his methods for deciding what to sell, from the 2024 brainstorming questions to the 2025 research process and the 2026 chain of AI prompts. Section 33.3 shows how to derive the product from your own path ("productize yourself") and tests the method against Koe's own products. Section 33.4 covers the mechanics: offer types, the elements of an offer, delivery mechanisms, product ladders and pricing. Section 33.5 presents the idea that dominates the later corpus, that "systems are the new product", and follows its evolution up to Koe's 2026 claim that static info products must be transcended. Section 33.6 explains why you need a product of your own, with Koe's revenue figures as evidence. Section 33.7 closes with the author's own product line as an integrated case.

### 33.1 $100,000 in Your Head

#### The claim: information you already carry

The starting point of Koe's thinking about products is a claim about the reader, not about the market. In his long 2024 guide to the one-person business model, he proposes "a system" for packaging the information in your head so that it helps others and creates an opportunity to profit. "You have $100,000 worth of information sitting in your head," he says, and some people have that much.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

He goes further and calls this the best business model there is, though he concedes it is new. His justification has two layers. The first is the thesis, repeated across the corpus, that "everything is information." The second is a claim about the purpose of business. In his account, the only real goal is a higher quality human experience, and that does not require a mega-corporation. If everyone were at peace and living perfect lives, nobody would want more. Getting more people to the self-actualized and self-transcended stages of Maslow's hierarchy of needs would put humanity "in a better spot."

**Complementary context:** Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs places physiological and safety needs at the base, followed by belonging and esteem, with self-actualization near the top; Maslow later added self-transcendence above it. Koe adapts the model loosely. He does not discuss its stages in detail here; he uses the top of the pyramid as the direction in which information businesses move people.

The mechanism that links information to that goal is simple. Information lets people do more. With the right information, Koe says, he could build anything. But someone has to build it first and teach him, or he has to figure it out himself. If he is busy with his own projects, he will go to someone else for that information. This is the market for information products in one sentence: people pay to skip the time it would take them to discover what someone else already knows. Koe adds a realistic qualification. Not everyone will do this, and only a small fraction of people are interested in doing it, "but you can."

The same claim reached Koe's audience through other voices. In a 2023 conversation, Dickie Bush attributes it to him ("you've said this a bunch"): "everyone has a hundred thousand dollars worth of product ideas sitting in their head." Bush mentions that someone named Will says it too, though the transcript is unclear on who that is. Bush's addition is what he calls "a very frame breaking thing." Earning $10,000 in a year from his own ideas once seemed crazy to him, "until that first ten thousand dollar month," which "wasn't any more effort than I thought it would be," and after which he could "see a path to scaling." People who are dissatisfied with their job may not believe they have this ability. The internet has made it possible to take the ideas in your head, share them and be compensated, and "it takes remarkably few people to accomplish that amount."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

"Frame breaking" is Bush's term, not Koe's. It names the experience that dissolves a belief by contradicting it. The first $10,000 month does not teach a new skill; it removes the frame in which such a month was impossible. This connects the product to the identity model of Part II. A belief about what you are capable of is part of the identity that defends itself (Chapter 3), and the first sale works as evidence that forces it to update.

#### Content is pieces of the map; the product is the map

In 2022 Koe gives the clearest image of how a product differs from the content that precedes it. He had been using the metaphor of a map: each person's understanding of how to reach a goal is a map of the territory. You raise the collective consciousness by giving out "pieces of your map" through writing and posting online. That, he says, is what social media is for: it attracts a following. "Your products themselves are the map." When you build one, you are distilling the whole map and enclosing it "into a little zip file to upload in other people's brains."

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md (2022-10-02)

The metaphor carries a theory of the relationship between content and product that later sections develop. Content is partial and scattered, and it is free because it is incomplete. A product is complete, ordered and compressed. That is why, as Section 33.5 shows, Koe can answer the question "if I give everything away in my content, what is left to sell?" Content gives pieces; the product gives the route.

#### From $100,000 to $1 million, and the warning about beginner hell

The figure in the claim changes over time. In December 2022 the title of a video is "You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head." In February 2025 Koe opens his full guide to a first profitable product with a larger number: "Believe it or not, you have $1 million trapped in your head, or it's lying around in your Google Drive, or your Notion, or your Cortex." You have skills, interests and some expertise that can be packaged into a product "that sells while you sleep," and what you learn about this "will determine most of your success as a creative."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

Koe gives no reason for raising the figure. The idea is the same, stated at a stage when his own products operated at a much larger scale. Two things change along with the number, however.

The first is a warning. Building a product is not enough. It also involves marketing, launching and promotion, which creatives and creators tend to skip. They do not learn actual marketing or monetization and then wonder "why am I stuck in beginner hell?" It is not as simple, Koe says, as building a product and hoping your audience likes it. *Beginner hell* is Koe's term for a prolonged phase in which a creator posts into the void without growth and without understanding what they are doing; in this 2025 usage, it specifically names the stagnation of creators who never learned to market and monetize. The warning is the reason Chapter 32 (persuasion and marketing) precedes this one.

The second is a reframing meant to lower the reader's sense of difficulty. "Here's a mind fuck for you," Koe says. "You've already purchased a $1 million product. You've purchased hundreds, in fact, if not more." The inference is that "you're probably overthinking it": most ideas can be taken to $1 million if you understand what the rest of the guide explains. The argument works by pointing at the reader's own consumption. The products you have bought were made by people who were not fundamentally different from you, so a product of that kind is within reach. This is an encouragement, not a demonstration. Koe offers no data on how many products reach $1 million, and the claim should be read as a frame for action rather than as a forecast.

#### The seven steps for an information product

In December 2022, and again in the 2024 compilation, Koe lays out "seven steps on how to create a profitable information product with the knowledge in your head." An *information product*, in his usage, is a product created from what you know, as opposed to a physical good or a service delivered with your time.

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md (2022-12-04); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

| Step | What it asks | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Become above average | Develop above-average intellect, skills or interests in a select area | Something worth teaching |
| 2. Build every day | Build something with those skills every single day | Practice and results |
| 3. Document | Document your learnings, findings or results | Raw material for content and curriculum |
| 4. Experiment | Experiment for holistic understanding | A grasp of the whole domain, not one tactic |
| 5. Iterate on your process | Turn what works into SOPs and refine them for efficiency and results | A replicable process worth a curriculum |
| 6. Steal what works | Steal marketing and structure that already works; sell what is already selling | A proven market and offer structure |
| 7. Distribute | Build distribution and systemize promotions | Traffic to the product |

The order of the steps mirrors the order of the book. Steps 1 to 4 are the learning process of Part VI: build to learn (Chapter 14), experiment on yourself (Section 15.4), document and learn in public (Section 14.5). Step 5 is where learning becomes a product. Step 6 is the market logic of "sell what's already selling," discussed in Section 33.3. Step 7 is the audience work of Part IX. Koe closes the 2024 version with a caveat: you have to start writing, building and promoting, because the unknown only becomes known with time. The steps are not prerequisites to finish before beginning. They are a description of a process that runs while you are already doing the work.

The material assigned to this chapter only details step 5. The other steps are elaborated in separate units of the same videos, and several of them are treated elsewhere in the book.

#### Step 5: from SOPs to a curriculum

Step 5 is the core of the method, because it describes the moment when personal experience becomes something transferable. The reasoning runs as follows. When you pursue goals, you encounter problems. To overcome them you learn skills: you learn marketing to make money, you start freelancing, an agency or a personal brand. You get results. At that point, Koe says, you usually have the information to create your own process for replicating them.

In business, such a process is called an SOP, a *standard operating procedure*. Koe borrows the term from ordinary business language and gives it a specific role. "You're going to write down steps of how you get to a specific result and then you're going to follow that." Following the steps, you encounter problems; you change and improve the steps, and you keep doing so "until it is valuable enough to offer in a product and create a curriculum around." The SOP is therefore a draft product. It starts as a private procedure and becomes public only after repeated use has refined it.

His personal example is deliberately small. An SOP for being productive each morning might be: write down three priority tasks, remind yourself of your vision, close all browser tabs. The business examples show the same pattern at a larger scale:

- He had an SOP for creating websites. He turned it into a course and filmed videos going over the steps and the nuances.
- For 2 Hour Writer, he created a system for writing newsletters that he considers "arguably more deep and thought provoking" than many in the creative space, packaged it in Notion with a curriculum, and it gets people similar results.
- His free products show the same move. The Power Planner is a promise plus a system turned into a planner; Koe suggests deconstructing its landing page to see the two. Seven Days to Genius Ideas is his note-taking system for creative ideas.
- He used the book *Smart Notes*, together with something the transcript renders as "central casting" (the reference is unclear), as inspiration to create what he calls "value notes," and in 2022 he was building software around them.

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md (2022-12-04); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

**Complementary context:** *Smart Notes* is most likely Sönke Ahrens's *How to Take Smart Notes* (2017), which popularized the Zettelkasten note-taking method of the sociologist Niklas Luhmann. Koe adapts it rather than teaching it: "value notes" is his own name for his own version.

The last example shows the whole lineage in miniature. A third-party method (Smart Notes) is tested and adapted until it becomes a personal system (value notes), which becomes a free product (Seven Days to Genius Ideas), which becomes the seed of software (Cortex, later Eden). Section 33.5 shows that Koe spent the following four years renaming this same core: the SOP of 2022 becomes the "unique system," then the "personal system," then "systems are the new product."

### 33.2 What to Sell

#### The most common problem: not knowing what to sell

In March 2023 Koe states the problem this section addresses as a finding from his own work. He says he has worked with more than 5,000 creators, adding that the figure is "under exaggerating," and that "the biggest problem that they face is that they don't know what to sell or they don't know how to make what they sell unique."

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12)

The statement is an observation from his experience as a consultant and course seller, not a survey. It is nevertheless useful, because it divides the problem into two parts that the rest of the chapter treats separately: choosing the subject of the product (this section and Section 33.3) and making it unique (Section 33.5). Koe's methods for the first part change over the years, from introspective questions to market research and, finally, to AI-assisted chains of prompts. They are presented here in that order.

#### Brainstorming questions (2024)

The 2024 guide offers three questions and a bonus for finding a profitable product idea. Each one points at a different source of evidence about what you could teach.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

The first question looks at what is effortless for you. What is the highlight of your day: writing, yoga, the gym? What is seamless for you to do and to learn about? Alternatively, what do you do for work that you are very good at and could teach? Koe adds a subtle point. The thing to look for may be something you have done that you do not think is impressive, either because you are the only one who does not see it as impressive, or because you are so immersed in a community where it is normal that you think everyone has done it. The skill you take for granted is often the one others would pay to learn. *Highlight of your day* is Koe's phrase for this question.

The second question looks at comparison. What do you do better than your peers, or than most people? Koe suggests testing the answer by how you justify doing it. His example is the bodybuilder Chris Bumstead, a Mr. Olympia competitor, who said he had to give up a normal life: he stayed home cooking healthy meals, did not go out drinking and avoided the temptation of junk food, because he was dead set on a mission his friends did not understand. The way he justified those choices showed an obsession most people do not have. If you are obsessed like that with something, Koe says, turn it into helping people like you.

The third question looks at the market you already belong to. Who do you follow, and what do they sell? If you go on social media to be educated, the people who educate you sell something. Deconstruct their landing pages and their emails, reverse engineer them and emulate them. Koe offers himself as an example for readers interested in self-improvement and online business, and fitness creators as an example for those who want to see how people in that field work full time.

The bonus is for those who have not started at all: "If you haven't started you have to be decisive, pick a path that slightly interests you and just start." Deciding gets you to knowing much faster than procrastinating, and you continue to learn and build according to what you are learning. The bonus restates, in product terms, the argument of Section 7.4: you will never know exactly what you want before acting, because exposure is what reveals it.

A shorter list from September 2024 asks four questions about what to build a product around: "A problem you've solved in your life." "What is a domain of life you've mastered?" "What do you love to study and learn about?" "What skills have you acquired that help you live a better life or get a better career?" Then: "Build a product around that; teach people everything they need to know to reach the same goals you have."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The two lists overlap but are not identical. The first is organized around ease, superiority and market evidence; the second around problems solved, mastery, curiosity and life improvement. Both rest on the principle of Section 28.2, "solve your own problems and sell the solution": the product teaches others to reach goals you have already reached.

#### Ideas by interest and by personal meaning

Two 2023 passages illustrate what such products might look like. In September, Koe gives one example per interest. If you like productive activity, create a workflow with education around it. If you like spirituality, create meditations and education around them. If you like fitness, create a program with education around it. The common pattern is a practical artifact (workflow, meditations, program) wrapped in teaching, a pattern Section 33.5 will call a system plus a curriculum. He adds a sentence that sums up his attitude toward first versions: "You can't improve what doesn't exist, so don't stress the first iteration; this is a long game of improvement."

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md (2023-09-17)

In April he extends the idea beyond education to products with personal meaning. If journaling gave him peace, he could create his own journal with his own prompts, or a physical planner. Even clothing qualifies if there is meaning behind it: cotton shirts without microplastics, and a cotton brand built around a philosophy that is meaningful to him. The general point is that "self-improvers are creating their own career paths simply, it's just baked into it." Improving yourself means solving your own problems; "human behavior revolves around problems; people pay to have their problems solved," and therefore "by improving yourself you set yourself up for massive financial success."

**Source:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md (2023-04-16)

The chain of reasoning deserves to be made explicit, because it is the economic core of the whole book. Self-improvement is problem-solving applied to oneself. Every solved problem is a potential product, because other people have the same problem. So the self-improver is, without intending it, accumulating an inventory of products. The claim of "massive financial success" is Koe's enthusiasm, not a guarantee; Section 33.1 already showed that he considers marketing and promotion indispensable for turning that inventory into income.

#### The 2025 process: topic, research, persona

The February 2025 guide replaces the introspective questions with a more structured process. It begins from a principle about what makes an education product good, presented in Section 33.4, and then moves through three steps.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

*Step 1: pick a topic.* Think of the area in which you have the most experience, something you have taken time to study or implement. You do not need the personal system yet (Section 33.5), only the topic. Koe gives a list of possibilities: writing, video editing, speaking, social dynamics, relationships and dating, productivity, web design or development, artificial intelligence, mental health, weightlifting, running, nutrition, learning faster, knowledge management, managing finances, landing job interviews, "and really anything that you can find on social media or the topic of a book." The instruction is immediate: "simply think of one thing that you're above average at and write it down now." The criterion is relative, not absolute. You do not need to be the best, only above average, which matches the first of the seven steps of 2022.

*Step 2: research ten to twenty product and marketing ideas.* The goal is to "find the intersection of what works and what you can do better." Koe lists the sources:

- course sites such as MasterClass, Udemy and Skillshare, searched by topic;
- Reddit, searched for course reviews and "best training programs";
- the accounts you follow: join their email lists and identify the products on their site and in their funnel;
- products you bought in the past;
- X accounts in the topic, read for their top-performing tweets, and keyword searches whose results you can save as product or marketing ideas;
- YouTube accounts in the topic, filtered by most popular, whose titles you write down as potential products.

When you find courses, buy them: "Don't cheap out." If you do not know how to structure a product, a few of them will serve as references.

Koe illustrates the YouTube source with a third-party example. For a business around social dynamics, go to the Charisma on Command channel and filter by most popular. These videos get "an insane amount of views," and each title could probably be a course. Looking at the top videos, "you can instantly see that people want to be interesting and confident and less insecure," and "that's all you need to know in order to start creating a product that will sell." He adds an important limit: "We don't want to take what's free and make it paid. We want to create something new." The research tells you what people desire and how to position your marketing; it does not tell you what to put in the product. The result of step 2 is a list of product and marketing ideas that you can combine, add to, simplify or make unique.

The research also has an organizational side, tied to the software Koe was building at the time. In Cortex he suggests creating a document titled "Product." While researching (on YouTube, for instance), you press Option+C to open the Capture feature and connect the idea to the document by typing @Product. As you build the product, you add sub-documents under the main one. Later, when you refine the marketing by reading books and watching videos on the topic, you open the product document again, capture and connect the new ideas, so that everything is in one place when you build. The specific tool matters less than the principle, which repeats the second-brain logic of Section 16.3: research for a product is captured toward a project, not accumulated in general.

*Step 3: choose a target persona.* The first step of any marketing, Koe says, is choosing a target persona, and people get stuck here too much. The persona exists so that the marketing is "specific and tangible. That's it." A much larger market will also resonate, especially on social media: "you don't want to be too specific, or else your content won't get spread." Koe offers two options.

1. Target yourself or your past self. You know the pain points, the experience, the story and the results. "You are a customer avatar that you can research and interview at any time with self-reflection."
2. Target people with a lot of money: founders, executives, people with high-paying jobs. The problem is that you often do not understand them, and on social media they are few and far between and harder to reach. For agencies or direct sales, "that Alex Hormozi can help you with," targeting them can build a very good business.

Koe chooses the first option for himself, and his reason is not financial. He wants fulfillment, "the soul in my work": to help people like him and to build a community he enjoys. "You just need a vague idea of who you're targeting."

This passage marks a shift in Koe's position on whom to sell to. In 2023 he presented selling to yourself as an almost universal solution that freed you from worrying about competition. In 2025 it becomes one of two legitimate routes, chosen consciously according to whether you prioritize creative fulfillment or income; in January 2026 he describes "sell to the rich" as "just an alternative route" that "has to be somewhat of a conscious choice." The two positions apply to different goals, and Koe does not declare one of them wrong. Section 19.3 develops the "sell to yourself" side in detail, including its comparison with the starving market and with selling to the rich.

There is a second tension in the same process. Step 2 tells you to study what already sells, while the Charisma on Command example forbids turning free things into paid ones and asks for "something new." Koe's corpus contains both pieces of advice in parallel: "sell what's already selling" and "don't try to be clever, at least for the first product" on one side, and "you can't take someone else's planner, put your name on it and sell it" on the other. His own reconciliation works by layers. You replicate the market, the headlines and the structure of the offer, and "the why behind why it works"; you create the system at the center. Section 33.3 and Section 33.5 follow the two layers in turn.

#### The 2026 offer chain: three prompts

In March 2026 the same flow (persona, offer, landing page) reappears as a chain of three AI prompts, run as linked chats on the canvas of Eden, the software Koe co-founded.

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md (2026-03-15)

1. "Create a detailed customer avatar. This is something you'll keep safe and reference often whenever you're creating marketing materials." You save it in your workspace.
2. "Create your first offer: using your customer avatar and offer creation principles, you create a product or service that they can't resist."
3. "Turn both into a compelling landing page draft," "because most people create a boring product and then just illustrate what the product does on their landing page and wonder why nobody wants it."

The prompts "interview you extensively" and produce "a customer avatar, an irresistible offer blueprint, and then landing page copy that act as first drafts that you can iterate on." The structure is the point. The context of the avatar chat feeds the offer chat ("you need your customer avatar information when you're creating an irresistible offer"), and both feed the landing page prompt ("how else are you going to write copy to a specific customer avatar about your offer?"). Each output becomes the input of the next step, so the dependency between persona, offer and copy is built into the tool. The AI does not decide what to sell; it interviews you and drafts from your answers. This is the same position Koe takes elsewhere on AI and writing (Section 24.6): the tool removes friction, but the material is yours.

#### Selling to creators: lean toward education

A special case of "what to sell" arises when your customers are other creators. In October 2022, and again in the 2024 compilation, Koe gives a heuristic for it. He describes himself as a proponent of the one-person business model and calls selling to creators and influencers "the future" and an opportunity ("please note this down"). But "if you want to target other creators and become a creator yourself you're going to have to go more towards the side of education."

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md (2022-10-31); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

His reasoning uses himself as the customer. He does not want a website built for him. He would rather learn how to build it, or buy software. In many cases he would not spend time learning something that does not interest him, so he would rather pay $1,000 for a call that tells him exactly how to do it. (He adds, with some humor, that nobody should pitch him websites: he was a web designer.) What would actually work on him is different again: "if you find a problem in my business that I'm unaware of and you make me aware of it and you persuade me" why paying $1,000 for a step-by-step system will save time, "then I may buy from you."

The sentence contains the structure of Chapter 32 in compressed form. A problem the buyer is unaware of must first be made visible (raising the level of awareness), then the buyer must be persuaded of its importance, and only then does a solution become sellable. The product itself is a system, not labor. Creators, in Koe's reading, are people who prefer to learn and to keep control of their own work; done-for-you services sell poorly to them.

There is an exception, given in the same 2022 video. A coach who has trouble generating leads needs a landing page (web design), copywriting for that page (persuasion, marketing, copywriting) and an understanding of the business as a whole: the people who opt in should go into an email sequence with follow-ups. Koe calls this a *well-rounded service*, a "well-rounded service that fits someone's specific business in a way that will get results," and he argues that it is "impossible to just outsource to one specific software." The exception follows from the same logic as the rule. What a creator will not pay for is a single commodity skill that they could learn or automate. What they will pay for is integration across several skills, fitted to their specific business. This anticipates the argument of Chapter 20 that the generalist's value lies in combining skills, and the argument of Section 33.5 that a hyper-specific system is harder to replace than a generic one.

### 33.3 Productize Yourself

*Productize yourself* is one of Koe's recurring formulas, and it names the program of this section: turning your own skills, interests and path into something that can be delivered without your time. Section 30.1 explained why the move is necessary. Here the question is how the product is found and built when its source is your own experience. The answers come from three directions: what the audience tells you, what you yourself would want, and what you already know how to do.

#### The audience tells you what to sell

The simplest answer comes from John Hugh, co-founder of the creator platform Stan, in a 2025 conversation with Koe. Once you have an audience, "your audience is your customer base," and finding the first digital product, course or community is "just giving them more value." The audience "will tell you what they want to buy because they would be commenting on your stuff and DMing you with specific questions." Literally, Hugh says, "they will tell you what they want to buy." The task then becomes: "how do I productize or monetize that specific question."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28)

The idea belongs to Hugh, but it fits Koe's own model of the audience as an accumulated asset of trust (Section 25.4). Questions in comments and messages are demand made visible. Each repeated question is a problem that people have already admitted to having, addressed to someone they already trust to solve it.

#### The data-driven product: four signals

Koe gives his own version of the same idea in September 2023, with a time frame and a method. After about six months to a year of creating, "create a data driven product that you then iterate on let's say every six months it needs to evolve" as you gain experience. A *data-driven product*, a term that appears only in this video, is one whose subject and design come from evidence collected in public. Koe names four signals.

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md (2023-09-24)

1. *How your best ideas perform.* "If the idea performs well it probably means a product or service will also do well based on that idea." Content works as a free test of product demand. Section 24.5 develops the same logic under the slogan "a tweet is the new MVP."
2. *How other people's products and ideas perform.* Here Koe asks you to "become an observer over a consumer": to view content, landing pages, funnels, products, services and promotions "as inspirational Firepower." The instruction that follows is one of his most quoted: "sell what's already selling but under your own name and world view."
3. *What people tell you they want.* As you grow, readers become more vocal. Double down on what they ask for.
4. *The questions people ask.* Patterns in questions reveal problems, and "the more questions you get the bigger the problem and the more likely people are to pay you for help."

Signals 3 and 4 are Hugh's point, stated as a method. Signals 1 and 2 add something his version lacked: evidence that exists before anyone asks, in the performance of ideas and in the market's existing products. The phrase "under your own name and world view" is what keeps signal 2 from becoming plain copying. What is copied is the demonstrated demand; what is added is the perspective of the seller, which Chapter 31 showed to be part of the value itself.

The six-to-twelve-month timeline sits in tension with other parts of the corpus. Section 29.4 presented Koe's advice to launch immediately, to build before having an audience and to sell before building, and Koe himself launched with very few followers (see below). The book's reading of the evolution file is that the tension is never resolved but that both pieces of advice can apply at once. A first product launched immediately teaches you how products work; a later, data-driven product is designed from what the first period revealed. Authority is built while something small is being sold. The reason Koe gives for both positions is the same: data and feedback only exist in public.

#### Build the product you want to see in the world

The second direction starts from you rather than from the audience. In June 2024 Koe's advice is to "build the product you want to see in the world": "solve a problem in your life and sell the solution." He proposes an inventory. List the products that changed your life, shaped who you are and influenced your behavior. These probably align with what you already write about to build an audience. Then make those problems better by creating your own system, and sell it "under the most unique brand you can find in yourself." Start with information products to build cash flow; then, if you want, turn successful information products into another business. "That's what he did," turning his writing course into Cortex, his writing app.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

In March 2026 he applies the same reasoning to sponsorships. "If you're thinking of accepting sponsors or brand deals from someone, why wouldn't you just create your own version of it and then take all of the profits, and you'd probably create it better in a way that better suited you." That, he says, "is how you start a good business nowadays, especially with a personal brand: you take something that you use and you love and you make it better and tailor it to yourself, and since you are the niche, then you sell it to other people like you and you can find that tribe."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md (2026-03-15)

The argument links this chapter to Chapter 19. "You are the niche" means that your tastes and problems are representative of a group of people like you. If that is true, then the products you use and love, improved to fit you, are products that group would also buy. The sponsorship becomes evidence: a brand wants to sponsor you because your audience buys what you use. Koe's conclusion is that you should capture that value yourself.

#### Write systems for your skills, then slap your name on them

The third direction starts from what you know how to do. In August 2024 Koe gives an instruction he frames as a practice for the reader to do immediately: "Create step-by-step systems and practices that your readers can use to get better at your skills or interests. Do this now. Write out step-by-step plans for your audience to learn the skills or interests under your brand. Then write content about them. Turn the best ones into products or services. As a bonus, slap your own name on these."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md (2024-08-25)

The naming part of the advice is illustrated with examples of how popular concepts are reformulated. Take something like "1% better every day" or the Eisenhower matrix and "spin it and make it your own. Put a new name on it." If you see "solopreneurship," spin it into "the one person business." If you see "the 4-hour work week," turn it into "the 4-hour workday" or "2-hour writer." The examples are Koe's own: "one-person business," "4-Hour Workday" (Chapter 12) and "2 Hour Writer" are his reformulations of existing ideas. Elsewhere he summarizes the point by saying that concepts "are just made up": named concepts become contagious, and naming your own version is part of making it yours.

**Complementary context:** The Eisenhower matrix sorts tasks by urgency and importance into four quadrants and is attributed to a remark by U.S. president Dwight D. Eisenhower. *The 4-Hour Workweek* (2007) is Tim Ferriss's book on lifestyle design and outsourcing. "1% better every day" is a slogan popularized by writing on habits, notably James Clear's.

The instruction is not a license to relabel other people's work. The systems you name are the ones you wrote from your own practice, and the existing concepts serve as models of how a name can carry an idea. The distinction is the same one the 2025 process draws between researching the market and creating the system (Section 33.2).

#### Building it: premeditation and the sprint

How long does it take to build the product once you know what it is? In May 2025 Koe answers from his own practice. In the past, building a digital product meant two or three days of "going hard all day." That was possible only because "all of the work was premeditated beforehand": he had already generated the ideas in his leisure time, so when he sat down, he only had to flesh them out. Now it needs even less time because of AI. He mentions a video he made on creating a first $10,000 digital product with AI, which is not about having AI do it for you but about guiding you to flesh out your own ideas into a product while the AI handles the "busy work." If you cannot sprint for two or three days, space it out over a month at one or two hours a day: the total time is the same. Once built, the product scales infinitely.

**Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md (2025-05-04)

The passage connects the product to the theory of work of Part V. The visible sprint is short because the invisible work happened earlier, during rest and leisure, in the diffuse mode that Section 13.1 describes as half of the work. This is why Koe can claim both that a product takes a few days and that it comes from years of experience: the days are the execution of what the years prepared. The same passage closes with the stack built so far: traffic (social media) and a product, to which you now add a landing page. That requires learning copywriting and a no-code tool to host the product, and AI helps there too.

#### "How I do it": the author's products as proof

Koe repeatedly uses his own products as evidence for the method. The cases are worth reconstructing in detail, because they show each of the three directions above at work, and because they contain a tension Koe himself admits.

*The origin, done "backwards."* In 2022 and again in 2024, Koe tells his origin briefly. He is "just a regular dude." As a young, broke college student, he identified a problem: he did not want a job that would limit his future earning potential. He learned every skill he could. Once he saw results with freelancing, he started posting about web design and his freelancing results, teaching web design, his self-development journey, mindset, skill acquisition and money. He grew an audience and eventually created a digital product on how he started his web design business, which iterated over time. He admits that "I did it kind of backwards of what I'm recommending": he got results first and posted afterward, while his advice to others is to post content first. In the 2024 version, he also asks viewers to notice the gap between how they perceive him (authoritative, with "cool lighting") and who he actually is.

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md (2022-10-02); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

Koe gives no explanation for the inversion. He presents it with humility rather than as a correction of his advice, and the corpus leaves the contradiction open. One plausible reading, which is the book's and not Koe's, is that the advice to post first is meant for people who do not yet have results, while his own path started from results he already had; but he never states this.

*The first product and "buyers buy again."* Koe's first product was an ebook on how to freelance with web development. He knew it would sell for a personal reason. He had bought a freelancing product himself, and as a developer he "could not stop buying courses," even on the same topic. "That is how I learned that buyers buy again." The principle is that people who buy a type of product keep buying more of that type. Putting a good freelancing product in front of web developers would generate at least some income, and he could iterate from there. He also knew that most web developers bought courses on platforms like Udemy. For sustainable income, he would need a personal brand and to sell to his own audience, avoiding that competition: a "personal monopoly."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md (2022-12-04); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

*Buyers buy again* links two sides of the product question. On the demand side, your own buying history is evidence of what sells, which is why Koe could predict the ebook's sales. On the competition side, the same buyers can be reached on marketplaces where they compare you with everyone, or through a personal brand where they encounter only you. *Personal monopoly* is a term Koe adapts from Naval Ravikant; here it has the narrow sense of selling to your own audience to escape the price competition of course platforms. Section 27.5 develops its broader sense.

In December 2023 he adds detail. When he started on social media, he created and sold a web design and freelancing product because he was a freelance web designer with some success, and he had gotten results from courses: he had purchased five to ten courses on freelancing. "That's who I was... that's all of the knowledge I had in my head." What made his product unique was not invention: "it's not like I'm just copy and pasting from others; I have that entire experience to pull from; I have a way to make it better." The goal, he says, is "to soak in all of this knowledge, get results in your own way... push through the trial and error... and then create a better product that advances that industry." He knew those products sold because he had bought five to ten of them.

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md (2023-12-03)

*From freelancer to creator.* A 2022 account of his failed businesses gives the commercial sequence. He saw someone on Twitter selling a freelancing product and talking about their interests, and thought: why not me? In "monk mode," a period of focused seclusion, he created the freelancing ebook on the basics of freelancing, then a complementary web design product. The first ebook brought in about $3,000 a month on top of his client work. He networked and built products that built off one another. At around 10,000 followers he still landed clients on Twitter, because creators and consultants are service businesses, so he positioned his offer toward them. He describes the result as a "self-sufficient audience and content ecosystem" that brought in both clients and sales. He pivoted to consulting, reached 100,000 followers (on Twitter, by implication), branched out to Instagram and had recently reached 200,000, after two to three years of iteration. His offers at that time were Modern Mastery (a community), Digital Economics (a cohort) and some consulting for one-person service businesses: freelancers, coaches, creators and consultants.

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md (2022-06-18)

*Launching with a small following.* In a 2023 video Koe explains why he launched a product immediately as a new creator, against the usual advice to wait. He loves to test and to "go against the grain," and he had experience and clarity. He made good money with a low following because he did not depend on his following for income. He freelanced, so his clients did not come from his audience, and he used other people's audiences, through affiliates and networking, to sell his product. His reasoning is arithmetic: trying to sell a $50 product directly in DMs "is just not sustainable," but with a freelance service at $1,000 to $3,000 you need only two or three clients a month "and you're good to go."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md (2023-02-05)

The case clarifies the relation between Sections 29.3 and this chapter. The service pays the bills at low volume; the product is launched early, not to replace the service but to start learning how products work, and borrowed audiences make up for the small one.

*Three products, three "hows."* In 2022 and 2024 Koe describes three of his products as examples of "how I do it."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md (2022-12-04); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

1. He sold a product on how to freelance as a web designer because he had made six figures freelancing in web design and had specific processes for landing clients, onboarding them and managing projects.
2. He built Modern Mastery as something he would have used at the start of his journey: daily reminders to knock out priority tasks, a strategy library, habit challenges.
3. He sells a writing product because he writes every morning to clear his mind, build a better future, unlock career opportunities, grow on social media, network and journal. He built an audience of more than 500,000 across platforms with a two-hour daily writing habit, and he created his own templates for newsletters and tweets to systemize it. Therefore, "the how I do it is different from other people's."

The last sentence is the thesis. Many people sell freelancing, community and writing products. Koe's claim is that his are differentiated not by the topic but by the "how": the specific processes and templates that came from doing the thing himself.

*The Power Planner.* Koe tells the planner's origin in April 2023 as a case of "solve your own problems and sell the solution." His problem was productivity: he could not focus. He watched many YouTube videos to find strategies, ordered two or three planners (the Panda Planner and others from Barnes & Noble), used them, noted what worked for him and created his own system "based on the common patterns." He made his own planner, used it, gave it to friends, watched them use it, improved it and eventually launched a free digital planner, which he later turned into a physical planner. At the time of the video it was free in his video descriptions, because it no longer aligned with the brand. With few followers, he had written content about what he knew about productivity, because he had been learning and practicing, and "that's qualification enough to talk about it and give advice." He grew a following, promoted the digital planner as a free download with added educational resources, and people loved it. In the December 2023 account, he adds that he created it because he used planners all the time and saw their benefit, put into his own the things that actually worked for him, and enjoys talking about productivity in his content.

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md (2023-04-30); How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md (2023-12-03)

The planner case reproduces step by step the experimental method of Section 15.4: collect existing solutions, test them on yourself, extract the common patterns, build your own version, test it on others, refine it, release it. It also contains a minimal standard of authority. Koe considered learning and practicing to be "qualification enough" to give advice, which is his answer to impostor syndrome (Section 26.2). Section 28.2 already noted the related principle that you cannot take someone else's planner, put your name on it and sell it.

*Modern Mastery and the Ralph Lauren move.* In the same April 2023 video Koe calls the design of Modern Mastery "the Ralph Lauren move," an allusion to the story, which reaches Koe through Ryan Ayala, of Ralph Lauren making the clothes he could not find, on the assumption that others would want them too. Koe asked himself what kind of online community he would have wanted to be in. How would he maintain his habits? What would he want to learn: which business material, skill acquisition, lifestyle and self-improvement content? What kind of tight-knit community would form the friendships he makes over his life? He adds that from an education business you can evolve into selling whatever you want, such as a physical e-commerce brand, or you can "chill out and make your wooden cutting board and sell that."

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md (2023-04-30)

*Pivoting and positioning for beginners.* A 2024 account shows that Koe's products did not stay in one topic. He started in web design, but he talked about self-improvement, spirituality, emotional management and productivity. He built a physical planner and a self-help ebook called *The Path to Power*, which he describes with a word the transcript records as "complete" (possibly "crap"; the reading is uncertain). Making it let him improve and eventually publish a full book. As his audience grew by attracting people with big goals, he pivoted from web design into writing, marketing, consulting, productivity and branding. He says these were not even his interests, just things he did, positioned so that almost anyone with the big goal could benefit. Web design, for example, was taught not only as freelancing but as "hey you work a job do you want a freelance here it is." "You can frame and position almost anything... towards a beginner level audience" to show its importance.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md (2024-05-19)

The pivot shows what "productize yourself" means when the self changes. The products follow the person, not a fixed niche, and what holds them together is the audience's big goal, not the topic. This is the "broad catch, specific sale" logic of Section 19.5 seen from the product side, and it anticipates the beginner-level positioning of Section 33.4.

### 33.4 Offer Types, Delivery and Price

Once the subject of the product is chosen, a series of practical decisions follows: what kind of offer it is, what elements it contains, how it is delivered, how products are arranged in a ladder, and what they cost. Koe's material on these decisions is concrete and often numerical. It is also the part of the corpus in which he relies most explicitly on other people's experience, especially Justin Welsh's on pricing.

#### Three offer types: done for you, done with you, do it yourself

In May 2022, in a passage he labels beginner-level advice, Koe divides products and services into three types. In December 2024 he restates the division in the language of products and services.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md (2022-05-21); Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024-12-01)

| Type | What happens | Examples | Product or service |
|---|---|---|---|
| Done for you | You do the work for the client | Freelancing (building someone's website), agency services | Service |
| Done with you | You help the client solve a problem with your expertise | Consulting, coaching, online mentoring on calls or in DMs, tutoring, a bootcamp | Service |
| Do it yourself | You package your information and sell or distribute it | A course | Product |

The 2024 version adds a definition of *product* that the 2022 version lacked. A product is "a consumable," something you grab, hold or consume, as opposed to someone helping you. That is the do-it-yourself category. Freelancing, coaching and consulting are services, either done for you or done with you. The three terms are ordinary business vocabulary, but Koe uses them as a fixed taxonomy and returns to it when he describes his own history: he says he has made six figures with almost every type (Section 33.7).

#### Moving between the types

The taxonomy matters less as a classification than as a map of conversions, because Koe's central practical claim is that each type can be turned into the others with little effort.

In March 2023 he calls one direction of the conversion a "hack" people do not register: courses you take are usually a higher-ticket service in disguise. Take a Twitter course (Modern Mastery contains about three, he says) and apply it to someone else's business as well as your own: that is a ghostwriting service. If you learn website design, you do it for other businesses; you do not even need a course, because you can string together specific YouTube videos, build something and then do it for someone else. Anyone can pick up a skill quickly and start talking about and teaching what they learn. "You're not an imposter, just fucking teach what you know." That, together with writing content fairly well and pitching your offer, is how you attract clients.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md (2023-03-19)

In September 2024 he describes the other direction. "Did you know that people's coaching services are actually just their books broken down and distributed over 8 to 12 calls weekly, and they charge substantially more for the coaching service since it's one on one." If you have blog articles, can create a course curriculum or can map out the steps to help someone achieve a goal, "you don't need a landing page, you don't need that curriculum... you simply need to say here's my price, I'm going to help you do this over four calls." Only as you write and sell will you have the ideas and the feedback to make the offer truly unique, and how you treat and iterate on that information over time determines the success of the business.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md (2024-09-15)

Read together, the two heuristics show that the three types share one core: a body of knowledge about how to get a result. The difference between them is who executes it (you, both of you, or the buyer) and how much of your time is involved. A course becomes a service when you execute it for someone; a book becomes coaching when you deliver it in weekly calls. This is why the "micro offer" and "micro service" of Section 29.2 can be offered without a finished product: the curriculum is already in your head, and the calls deliver it.

#### The priority: a beginner-level product

If the types are convertible, which conversion should come first? In October 2023 Koe answers: "The obvious and priority thing to do is to turn your service like freelancing into a digital product like a course teaching the same thing to a more beginner level audience." His example: you sell copywriting services to clients at around $10,000 (the transcript reads "$110,000"), and you attract a broad audience of people learning copywriting. "Don't turn the freelance product into something that is targeted at something super niche, just create a basic copywriting course, teach people the basics... 90% of the market are beginners." The result is a two-part business: "take on fewer clients charging more and building the base of your income with a lower price digital product that allows you to scale."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md (2023-10-08)

The figure for beginners varies in the corpus: 90% in this passage, 95% in others. The heuristic is the same, and from 2025 Koe restates it in terms of developmental stages: most people are at the stages of survival and identity, and the creator's job is to take them "up the ladder." The two halves of the business follow from the market's shape. The minority of advanced buyers pay for high-priced services; the majority of beginners buy the basic product, which, being digital, scales.

#### The anatomy of an offer

In February 2025 Koe builds the offer element by element. The process opens with a principle about what the best education products have in common: "The best education products are the ones that solve the specific pain point. Give people a system to get results. Provide clarity on everything involved with performing the system." First you decide the topic of the product (the process of Section 33.2), then you market it.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

The three clauses of the principle correspond to three parts of the offer: the pain point is the problem, the system is the personal system, and the clarity is the education that accompanies the system. The guide's elements, reconstructed from the units available, can be arranged as follows. The material assigned to this chapter details elements 3 to 6; elements 1 and 2 are known through the worked example, and their numbering is inferred from it.

| Element | Content | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Big problem and negative outcome | The burning problem (point A) and what happens if it persists | Creates the urgency the marketing will address |
| 2. Desired outcome | Where the buyer wants to be (point B) | Defines the transformation |
| 3. Believable time frame | How long it takes to get from problem to outcome | Supercharges the marketing, makes the product tangible, compresses its contents |
| 4. Personal system (and 4.5, education) | The steps that take people from A to B, plus the curriculum needed to perform them | The core of the product (Section 33.5) |
| 5. Features and benefits | What is included, each item tied to what it does for the buyer | The persuasive presentation on the landing page |
| 6. Delivery mechanism | E-book, email course, cohort, course, community, software | The format, each with trade-offs |

*The believable time frame.* How long will it take to get from the big problem to the desired outcome? "Time frames supercharge your marketing": 30 days, three to six months, six weeks, two hours. They can be used in content too, "because your videos, your posts... they're an offer." A time frame makes the product tangible, and it is "a reference point that spark[s] desire." It also has an internal function: it helps compress what goes into the product. Many people throw everything they know into one product, instead of only what is valuable for solving the problem and reaching the outcome. A deadline forces selection. Koe adds that you may not know the time frame yet, and that you can come back to it after creating your personal system. The term *believable* matters: the time frame must be credible to the buyer, not merely attractive, which links it to the "believable goal" Koe uses elsewhere for goals that are clear rather than stretched.

*Features and benefits.* With point A, point B, the steps and the education outlined, you turn them into features and benefits. Most people list only features, "but that's not persuasive. People don't care about the product. They care about how the product changes their life. This is just marketing 101," and even advanced marketers forget the fundamentals. The technique is to pair each feature with a compelling benefit, or to exchange the feature for the benefit. Koe's examples:

- "a five week program so you know exactly what to do when you step in the gym. No more feeling lost";
- "a cookbook with 20 filling and low calorie foods so you can feel stuffed without the guilt";
- "the top five exercises for chest growth so you don't waste years without developing shirt splitting pecs."

The common theme is the bridge: "so you can" or "without." The list goes on the landing page, where you introduce the offer under a heading like "what's included." This is the "value is perception" principle of Section 31.2 applied at the smallest scale. A feature describes the product; a benefit describes the change in the buyer's life, which is what is actually being perceived and paid for.

#### A worked example: The Attention Marathon

Koe closes the offer-building part of the guide with a complete example, built for his own past self.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

- *Topic:* productivity.
- *Target persona:* Dan's past self, an 18-year-old balancing study and building a business.
- *Big problem and negative outcome:* "I don't want a traditional 9 to 5 job, but I'm falling behind in classes and my business has taken a backseat." Without long periods of study and work, "I'll get stuck in a comfortable job and it will be extremely difficult to get out." Forty years later, "I would wish that I took the time."
- *Desired outcome:* deep focus for twelve or more hours a day without Adderall.
- *Believable time frame:* 14 days.
- *Personal system:* "the Attention Marathon," described as "a training program for your mind to run a marathon."
- *Education:* flow state, dopamine, workspace setup, avoiding distractions and related topics.
- *Validation:* a YouTube video with 14 million views titled "how I studied for 12 hours a day over a year," which Koe treats as "validation enough that this can work, if I iterate and test enough."

The example applies every principle of the chapter at once. The persona is the past self (Section 33.2). The validation comes from the performance of someone else's idea, which is signal 2 of the data-driven product (Section 33.3). The negative outcome is written in the buyer's voice and projected forty years into the future, which is the anti-vision technique of Chapter 7 used as marketing. The name of the system is a metaphor (training for a marathon) that makes an abstract capacity, sustained attention, feel trainable. The education draws on topics Koe has developed throughout the book: flow (Section 10.4), dopamine (Chapter 11), environment design (Section 11.3). Note also that the example is hypothetical. Koe builds it to demonstrate the method; the corpus does not say that he launched it.

#### What a product is: physical and digital

Before the delivery mechanisms, a 2024 definition clarifies what kind of object is being chosen. "A product is what stands between the consumer and the creator": an item, good or creation that people can consume. Physical products have limits: shipping costs, manufacturing costs and what Koe calls "entropy costs," the deterioration of perishable goods and of books that get beat up. A digital product fits Naval Ravikant's definition of something with no marginal cost of replication, and it is, in Jack Butcher's phrase, "the epitome of build once sell twice." Digital products include code, movies, articles, podcasts, content, newsletters, courses and ebooks: "in a nutshell it's media and code."

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

**Complementary context:** "Marginal cost of replication" is the cost of producing one additional unit. For software and digital media it is close to zero, which is the basis of Naval Ravikant's argument that code and media are forms of leverage that do not require anyone's permission. Jack Butcher is a designer and creator known for the "Visualize Value" brand and for the slogan "build once, sell twice."

*Entropy costs* is Koe's coinage, and it connects the product to the entropy framework of Chapters 5 and 31. A physical product is subject to decay and must be maintained, shipped and replaced; a digital product is not. All digital products can be monetized (a paid newsletter, a paid podcast, a book), but not all of them profitably. Some are better free, like social media posts; some are better paid, where there is more depth, like a course or an ebook. Some people have had luck monetizing posts, Koe says, but he does not recommend it. The division reproduces, in economic terms, the map metaphor of Section 33.1: the pieces of the map are free, the whole map is paid.

#### Three things to sell from a skill, and the SKUs that scale

In February 2024 Koe gives the simplest possible version of the product decision. "Stop over complicating it." From any skill or interest you can sell three things:

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md (2024-02-25)

1. *A tutorial or tutoring:* a beginner-level tutorial of a specific skill, practice or interest, such as web design, building cars, Photoshop, guitar or music.
2. *A program or coaching:* an action plan or program that people adopt as a daily routine, as in fitness, productivity or self-improvement.
3. *A system, through consulting or freelancing:* a system people implement in their work or business, such as lead generation, content creation or Koe's writing frameworks.

The triad cuts across the done-for-you / done-with-you / do-it-yourself taxonomy. Each of the three can be delivered as a product or as a service. What distinguishes them is the kind of change the buyer seeks: learning a skill, adopting a routine, or installing a system in a business.

John Hugh of Stan adds the view from the platform side in January 2025. After the first clients, the options for selling at scale are "almost infinite." The common ones on Stan, which he calls "SKUs" (stock-keeping units, the retail term for distinct products), are a short mini course or video course; a community, and if you enjoyed person-to-person interaction, group coaching cohorts or a hosted community; full courses; ebooks; and templates, which Hugh "really personally loves." A template is "your process in template," something you "swear by" and that has proven credibility. Hugh describes Koe's Cortex as "your process in template for how you've created stuff," and gives his own example: the resume template he used to get a job at Goldman Sachs ("I'm not saying it's the best one"). Which SKU to choose depends on "where you want to take your business."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28)

Hugh's phrase "process in template" is close to Koe's SOP: a procedure proven in use, packaged so that someone else can follow it. The template is the most compressed form a personal system can take.

#### Delivery mechanisms and their trade-offs

The sixth element of the 2025 offer is the *delivery mechanism*, Koe's term for the format in which an education product reaches the buyer. He lists six, each with an advantage and a cost. "All of them work... some are harder, some are more easy."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

| Mechanism | Advantage | Cost or risk |
|---|---|---|
| E-book | Quick to make | Hard to charge a lot for |
| Email course | Structured learning | Most people fall off |
| Cohort (4–8 week curriculum with calls and a community, opened about four times a year) | Scarcity and perceived value | Often expensive |
| Course (evergreen) | Can be promoted at all times | Needs a lot of traffic |
| Community with monthly recurring revenue | Huge payoff if you commit | Harder to sell; "that equals monthly recurring work" |
| Software | Can pay off big "if you dedicate 5 to 10 years to it or land the viral jackpot fast" | Takes longer to build; higher chance of failure |

Two additions qualify the table. The first concerns supplements. Adding templates, worksheets or trackers, in Notion or Cortex, can by itself make an education product worth paying for, more valuable than a free video or a book. In September 2024 Koe names his favorite format in exactly those terms: "My favorite type of education product is something like a course curriculum paired with worksheets or resources or projects that you can build inside of Notion or now Cortex," since Cortex had just added public page sharing (he offered that video's notes as a page viewers could duplicate).

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The preference should be read with its date. In 2026, as Section 33.5 shows, Koe concludes that "static courses don't cut it" and that the course should become the knowledge base of an interactive tool. The curriculum-plus-worksheets format is already a step in that direction: the worksheet is where the buyer starts implementing.

The second addition is a warning about communities. Many people start communities on platforms like Skool, based on monthly recurring revenue. Koe has done them multiple times, and he finds them "very similar to client work": if you do not enjoy it day in and day out, it is a lot of work. "It's not monthly passive income, it's monthly 'I'm working hard in order to get more people into this'." People are skeptical of buying recurring subscriptions, especially high-priced ones, and you probably do not have a traffic source strong enough to give the community velocity. Koe's coinage *monthly recurring work* is the counterpart of monthly recurring revenue: the income recurs only because the effort does.

#### The product ladder and the launch cadence

The guide then arranges delivery mechanisms into a progression for beginners. Start with a $27 e-book on a specific topic, such as focusing twelve hours a day. Turn it into one module of a larger $150 course that includes a Focus Hub template for planning study and work blocks. Expand it into a more comprehensive $399 cohort "on Living in Flow." Promote the e-book anywhere at the top of the funnel; promote the course on the back end, in emails and in YouTube descriptions. The cohort runs only four times a year, is easier to launch and "usually leads to a big spike in revenue."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

The ladder follows the Attention Marathon example: the same topic, focus, is expanded across three formats and three prices. Each step contains the previous one, so the buyer of the cheap product has an obvious next purchase. This is "buyers buy again" (Section 33.3) organized deliberately.

A 2024 heuristic adds a rhythm. Launch products around topics that do well and that become stable parts of your brand. Monetize with courses, cohorts, templates, systems and tutorials, whatever helps people achieve "a smaller goal that helps them get a few steps closer to the big goal." "Every 3 to 6 months launch at least one free product and one paid product" until you have a brand and business you are satisfied with. Once you have cash flow, consider a product that requires capital and a large audience.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md (2024-05-19)

The phrase "a smaller goal... closer to the big goal" places each product inside the goal hierarchy of Chapter 8. The audience shares a big goal (Section 33.3 showed how Koe's pivots followed his audience's big goals), and each product solves one of the smaller goals beneath it. The rule about capital is the cash-flow principle that Section 33.6 develops: capital-intensive products come after, and are financed by, the cheap digital ones.

#### Pricing: start low, raise, never lower

Koe's pricing advice is consistent between 2023 and 2024. "You don't start by selling a $1,000 product." You start by giving the product to some people for free and collecting testimonials, then you put a lower price on it, then you increase the price. The mechanism has two parts. Starting high is worse because "you can't decrease the price you can only increase it." And "you make the most sales right before a price increase," because people want the best price. Koe frames this as scarcity, in a narrow sense specific to pricing: the announced increase creates urgency.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

The same asymmetry governs services. In March 2023, answering the common question of what to charge for a tutoring offer, Koe first says to just put a price on it and test it, warning that the quick-fix mindset will hurt you. You could put $5,000 on it, say it to people and see: you will get a lot of no's, learn by trial and error and refine until you sell in accordance with the value you provide. But he recommends starting lower, "because it's harder to decrease": $500 to $1,000 for a pack of four calls, raised as you get results and confidence.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md (2023-03-19)

The two passages show the same logic from two angles. Price is an experiment, but the experiment runs in only one direction. Each increase is justified by evidence (testimonials, results, confidence), and each announced increase is itself a sales event.

#### Justin Welsh: impulse-buy pricing and quarterly communities

Two pricing ideas in the corpus come from Justin Welsh, in a 2021 interview with Koe, and they should be attributed to him.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md (2021-12-20)

The first is what Welsh calls "impulse buy pricing." "I'm not out there selling $1,200 master classes. The amount of trust that it requires to build in order to get someone to spend 1,200 bucks is pretty massive." Pricing products low lowers the threshold of trust a purchase requires. Welsh applies it everywhere: his first product at $50, his second at $150 (often with a 25–30% discount), his community at $99 per quarter, his early coaching at $100 an hour.

The second concerns the billing period of communities. Welsh started with yearly pricing and found that "yearly really really screws with your optionality. So like 3 months in, if you don't like running a community, you got 9 more months." He moved to quarterly billing "mostly because I don't want it to be a hotel. Like I want people who want to come in and stay for a while." His argument against monthly billing, which he admits he has not tested, is that someone who does not like the community in the first 14 days will not stay for the last 14, so churn is higher, whereas "if you give the community 90 days, you're probably going to be really, really pleased with it." Quarterly billing "gives me the right amount of sort of umph to get the right people in, but it also gives me optionality, where I'm always 3 months away from if I don't like it anymore, closing up shop."

The two ideas complement Koe's. Impulse-buy pricing explains why the bottom of the ladder is cheap: the first purchase must not require a level of trust the audience has not yet built. The quarterly community is a structural answer to Koe's warning about monthly recurring work. It protects the seller's freedom to stop and the buyer's chance to experience the community long enough to value it. Welsh's "hotel" (a community people check into and out of quickly) is the opposite of what both of them want: buyers committed to a change.
