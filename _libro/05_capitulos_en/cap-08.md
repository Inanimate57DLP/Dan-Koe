## Chapter 8 — Goals, Plan and Purpose

Chapter 7 gave the reader two instruments of direction: an anti-vision (the life you refuse to drift into) and a vision (the life you would choose, written down as a first, imperfect iteration). Both are large and distant. A vision describes a destination years away; an anti-vision describes a fate one wants to escape. Neither says what to do on a Tuesday morning. This chapter is about the distance between those large pictures and the day, and about the structures Dan Koe has built to cross it. It moves in three steps. First comes the **hierarchy of goals**: the decomposition of a vision into goals at shrinking time scales, down to the one to three "levers" that should be pulled each day, together with the frameworks in which Koe has packaged that decomposition over the years (the Focus Formula, the creation pyramid, the 12 rules, the six-component plan). Second comes the **plan** and its boundaries: why, in Koe's account, someone who does not create a plan is assigned one; how constraints, deadlines and a performance-based framing make a plan workable; and how the ideal future can be practised now at a small scale. Third comes **purpose**, which Koe redefines in a way that turns the question "what is my purpose?" from a search into a diagnosis: your purpose is the most pressing problem in your life right now, and life is an endless chain of such problems. The chapter closes with his account of passion as something created by invested energy rather than found, and with the time horizons he asks people to commit to before judging whether a path is theirs.

The chapter relies on two ideas from Part III. From Chapter 5 it takes the claim that the mind tends toward chaos and that *clarity* (a visible path from where you are to where you want to be) is what orders it. From Chapter 6 it takes the claim that a goal works as a lens: it decides what information registers, what counts as a problem and what system one ends up building. If both claims hold, the structure of one's goals is not an administrative detail but the shape of one's perception and one's days. That is the reason Koe returns to it, by his own account, "in every single video."

### 8.1 The Hierarchy of Goals and the Daily Levers

#### Everything you do revolves around a hierarchy of goals

The starting point is descriptive, not prescriptive. In 2022, announcing a future video on the subject, Koe stated that "every single thing you do revolves around a hierarchy of goals whether you are unconscious or conscious of it." Culture has such a hierarchy: if it tells you to get a job, that is a goal, and the way to reach it ("go to school, learn") is a chain of further goals, whether one calls them goals, checkpoints, objectives or quests. Games are built the same way: here is how you win, here are the quests and checkpoints that get you there, "and it makes sense, that's why we play these games." The corporate world has its own version, a "corporate culture game" with rules such as showing up at a set time and finishing a set amount of work (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28).

A **hierarchy of goals**, in Koe's usage, is a nested structure: a final goal at the top, sub-goals beneath it, and smaller checkpoints or quests beneath those, each level serving the one above. The term has no single author in the corpus; Koe uses it as his own while drawing heavily on Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's psychology of flow, in which goals that structure attention are the precondition of order in consciousness (Chapter 5). The descriptive claim matters because it removes an option people often believe they have: living without such a hierarchy. On Koe's account there is no goal-free life, only a life whose hierarchy was chosen consciously and a life whose hierarchy was installed by someone else.

In a 2023 video he explained why installed hierarchies are so attractive. Hierarchical structures "also present a hierarchy of goals that help frame our attention... or order our mind." In a world of overwhelm, uncertainty and insecurity, "the masses flock to the thing that is going to be most likely to heal that uncertainty or order their mind", which is usually an external hierarchy such as climbing the corporate ladder. The reason is not stupidity but a lack of alternatives: people have no clarity on anything else, have never been exposed to another path that makes sense, and have never been shown how to reach a different goal and act on it, so they conclude it is impossible or "not a possibility for them" and return to what they have been taught their whole lives to attend to (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). The argument links the hierarchy of goals to the mechanics of Part III. A ready-made ladder sells *order*; it is an answer to psychic entropy, which is why people accept it even when the goals at its top are not theirs.

The same mechanism appears, in sharper language, in Koe's critique of schooling. "Many people don't want to hear this but schools were created to keep you narrow-minded and dumb. You are given a hierarchy of goals to pursue and this orders your mind, it makes you feel secure, but often those goals don't evolve, so you get locked into this ideological structure and then you lose all challenge or growth." Within one niche you learn only what leads to "the fabricated prestige that niche offers": you want the degree even when you suspect you could succeed without it, so you learn only what aligns with it, and "by the time you get it you aren't educated at all, you just spent 4 years learning your way into limited opportunities and potential replacement" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`, 2024-07-13). The analytic core of this passage is separable from its rhetoric. The analytic claim is that an assigned hierarchy orders the mind but does not *evolve*, and a hierarchy that does not evolve eventually removes challenge and growth. The rhetorical claim is about the intentions of those who created schools. On that second point Koe is not consistent. Here, in July 2024, he asserts it flatly; a few weeks later, telling a similar story, he calls it "just a metaphor... whether it's a conspiracy theory or not," and elsewhere he insists that he does not believe in a "puppet master" and that the outcome is "a property of the system." The tension runs through his whole corpus without a clean chronological resolution: the strong formulation works as a hook, the systemic one as the explanation, and when pressed he declares the metaphorical reading (Chapter 2 treats the full history). For this chapter, only the analytic claim is needed: a hierarchy of goals that is not your own and does not change with you will order your mind into a narrow shape.

#### Intrinsic and extrinsic hierarchies

From the descriptive claim follows the central distinction. Speaking from his experience of a nine-to-five job, Koe contrasts pursuing an **intrinsic hierarchy of goals** (your own) with pursuing an **extrinsic hierarchy of goals** (someone else's). The difference, he says, reaches the level of neurobiology: "it's not good dopamine versus bad but it is pursuing someone else's goals as opposed to pursuing your own goals." He supports it with a historical sketch, explicitly hedged: in the past there were no corporations, only communities, and communities are composed of individuals who have their own intrinsic hierarchy of goals within the community's intrinsic hierarchy of goals; they could hunt, gather and "do their own thing." "I may be wrong there but I'm speaking from experience." What he does affirm without hedging is the felt difference: building something you want to build "floods your brain with dopamine and other neurochemicals"; it feels good because it is progress (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21).

Two features of this passage deserve attention. The first is the status of the claims. The neurochemical statement is presented as a reading of his own experience, and the anthropological one as a guess; neither is offered as research. The second is that the distinction is not between "good" and "bad" goals in content. A corporate goal and a personal goal might involve the same tasks; what changes is whose hierarchy they belong to. A week later he specified where an intrinsic hierarchy comes from: it is "you creating your own reality by creating an intrinsic hierarchy of goals from the things that you're curious about and how they will lead to a better life for yourself," as opposed to the "x, y and z" of culture and corporate rules, which "you may not want to do" and which "may not provide fulfillment" (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). Curiosity is the raw material; the hierarchy is curiosity organized toward a better life.

In the same video Koe reads a passage from Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow*, a book he "highly recommends," to support the point: to overcome the anxieties and depressions of contemporary life, individuals must become independent of the social environment to the degree that they no longer respond exclusively in terms of its rewards and punishments, and to achieve such autonomy "a person has to learn to provide rewards to herself, she has to develop the ability to find enjoyment and purpose regardless of external circumstances" (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). The quotation is Csikszentmihalyi's; the use is Koe's. He reads autonomy from social rewards as the *function* of an intrinsic hierarchy: if your goals are your own, the rewards they generate (progress, the dopamine of building) do not depend on anyone's approval.

**Complementary context:** Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934–2021) was a Hungarian-American psychologist whose *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (1990) described the state of complete absorption that arises when clear goals, immediate feedback and a balance between challenge and skill coincide. The passage Koe reads comes from the book's argument that control over consciousness requires freeing oneself from dependence on social rewards and punishments.

From these premises Koe draws the practical principle he says he "harps on in every single video": create a vision for your future and a hierarchy of goals to get there, "because it brings clarity to your daily actions," with "bonus points" if the hierarchy takes the intrinsic drivers into account. Without it, disorder of the mind brings anxiety, overthinking and uncertainty into daily actions; you procrastinate, or you act without feeling or reaping the benefits of daily progress. With it, your actions lead toward what he calls a "positive potential reality." In that period he offered a free "Power Planner" as the tool for writing out visions, goals and priority tasks (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28). A few months later he gave the principle its most compressed and most quoted form: you need a vision or purpose and "a structure or hierarchy of goals to frame your attention" toward it and to eliminate distractions, because "that is how you create an endless source of good dopamine: by endlessly solving the right problems" (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22).

The phrase **good dopamine** is one of Koe's coinages of this period. In this passage it designates the reward that comes from solving problems inside a vision and a hierarchy of goals, as opposed to the "cheap" dopamine of consumption treated in Chapter 11. The phrase "endlessly solving the right problems" is worth marking now, because it anticipates section 8.6: the hierarchy of goals is ultimately a hierarchy of *problems*, and a life organized around one is a life of endless, well-chosen problem solving.

#### Aiming the mind: the monkey and the quarterback

Why should a hierarchy have levels at all? Why not simply hold the vision and act? Koe answers with an image he first used in December 2022 and repeated in a 2024 compilation. Games present the big goal of winning, and also clarity on how to reach it through sub-goals such as quests; a game that told you only "win," with no idea how to get there, "wouldn't be fun." Humans, he continues, "have the ability to aim with our minds into the future." Monkeys can throw their poop, but they usually hurl it straight at the ground and do not hit what is in front of them. A human with skill, practice and time can throw a football fifty yards and hit a bullseye. The mind works similarly: you create a vision and slowly level up until you are able to create it. "We don't want to be poop hurled at the ground like how monkeys do, we want to have a vision that we can build towards." And to avoid being overwhelmed by a grand vision, you create sub-goals (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21, where the passage opens the "micro games of life" and "set a hierarchy of goals" is named the first element).

The metaphor is crude by design, and it carries three claims. Aiming is a capacity, not a given: the quarterback's accuracy is the product of skill, practice and time, so the ability to direct one's life toward a distant target is something one levels up. The target must be ahead, not at one's feet: without a vision, effort lands wherever it falls. And the throw needs a trajectory: the sub-goals are what turn a distant target into a reachable arc. The last point is the one the rest of this section develops.

#### Building the hierarchy on paper

In the same 2022 video Koe gave the procedure, which he repeated almost word for word in 2024. Sit with pen and paper (or the free Power Planner) and:

1. Create "the first iteration of your vision" ("understand it's an iteration, it doesn't have to be perfect").
2. Create ten-year, one-year and monthly goals ("again these don't have to be perfect"; they are first iterations you can revisit).
3. Have "a place for weekly direction and reflection."
4. "Align your daily priority tasks with those."

(**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21.)

The 2022 version adds the reasons, and they are the most interesting part. Writing matters, and "most people don't understand because they've never done it": the paper is "like an anchor." He links this to his method for remembering what he learns (Chapter 14): learning and building should happen in unison, because you need something to build in order to apply what you learn, and once you have it your awareness starts registering more opportunities. The deeper reason invokes a concept from Part II. Humans, he says, survive on a conceptual level: we try to survive our beliefs and identifications. "It only makes sense that we would try to survive a project or we would try to survive our vision for the future." With the vision top of mind, we notice things that apply to it, clarify it and keep striving (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11). This is an application of the idea of *conceptual survival* (Chapter 3): the same mechanism that makes people defend an identity can be pointed at a written vision, so that the mind protects and feeds it as it would protect a belief.

The procedure was not frozen. In 2023 he presented a version in five steps of time ("a 10-year goal; a one-year goal; this month's goal; this week's goal; what you can do today"), written in the same notebook as the vision, whose purpose is to "bridge the clarity gap." The **clarity gap** is the distance between a vision and the next action, which, without intermediate goals, the mind cannot cross. He added an instruction about the attitude: "Don't attach to these goals, this is just a practice to generate clarity." Goals evolve: "I can have a different goal tomorrow if I wanted to, as long as I have a goal and I'm making decisions towards a better life" (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11). Later that year he extended the hierarchy across life domains: break the vision into goals for each of Mind, Body, Spirit and Business (relationships, in this version, are synonymous with Spirit), and for all four create ten-year, one-year, monthly and weekly goals. It is time-consuming, he warns, so "take your time, it's your future." Then every day write three to five priority tasks that move the needle "from the ground up," and do them first thing in the morning "before responsibilities and distractions have time to wake up." The premise is stated as a principle: "we need clarity more than we need motivation; an ordered mind is a happy one and a hierarchy of self-generated goals makes it hard for depressing distractions to penetrate your awareness" (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). (The names of the life domains shift across the corpus between "mind, body, spirit, business," "health, wealth, relationships, happiness" and other cuts, without explanation; Chapter 40 discusses them.)

In April 2024, before presenting his 12 rules (section 8.3), Koe offered the shortest version as a warm-up exercise: grab a notebook; write out exactly what you want in your future, "don't miss a detail"; break it down into goals by decades, years, months, weeks and days; and "every day write down the three levers you can move to create your vision." He closed it with a line that summarizes the whole section: "A dream without clarity is a nightmare" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14).

Three months later, the same steps came with a more specific statement of what goals are *for*. "Set a 10-year goal, set a one-year goal, set monthly goals, set weekly goals, set daily goals, then forget about them all." Goals exist, first, "to reorient your mind toward the habits you must form that create the future you choose"; second, "to form the system that would achieve those goals"; and third, "so your pattern recognition machine of a mind can notice and store information that aids in the achievement of those goals." And he states their limits frankly: "you probably won't hit all of those goals, they probably won't keep you motivated; use them to reverse engineer what you need to do to reach your vision, then forget about them" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`, 2024-07-13). The three functions are a good summary of Part III applied: goals reorient habits (identity), shape the system (the goal determines the system, Chapter 6) and tune perception (the goal as lens). None of the three requires the goal to be achieved.

Finally, Koe insists that the method is scale-independent. To get clarity "you need a vision for your future, you need a hierarchy of goals to get there, and you need prioritized, simplified, low friction tasks that just make it easy to actualize that vision," and this applies on "any plane of reality": a project you are working on, a social situation you are uncomfortable going through, or your life as a whole (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). The phrase "low friction" adds something the other versions only imply: the bottom of the hierarchy should be designed so that starting is easy.

#### Big goals for direction, small goals for clarity

The most quoted line of this part of Koe's thought is a division of labour between the levels of the hierarchy. In its 2024 form: "Big goals are for direction, small goals are for clarity, neither are for action. You don't need motivation when the task in front of you is so stupidly simple that you can't help but complete it" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The same sentence appears as Rule 5 of his 12 rules, followed by "they are your guide not your master. Be stubborn with vision and loose with details. Your goals will change as you do; be okay with that," and by a reminder: "if you aren't writing anything down right now, what are you doing?" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). In October 2024 he gave a variant under the name **act small**: "Big goals are for motivation, perspective and potential; small goals are for clarity, action and sanity." The method is to reverse-engineer a "minimum viable vision" (a starting point that does not have to be perfect) into ten-year goals, one-year goals, monthly goals, weekly goals and daily priority tasks, and then to focus on the priority task: "everything else is for clarity." He answered critics who told him that "you don't need all those goals, just act": the goals are not there to distract but to provide clarity, so that you can determine what to act toward (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13).

The two formulations differ in a revealing detail.

| Level | "Direction / clarity" version (2024-04, 2024-07) | "Act small" version (2024-10) |
|---|---|---|
| Big goals | Direction | Motivation, perspective and potential |
| Small goals | Clarity | Clarity, action and sanity |
| Action | Neither level; it comes from a task "so stupidly simple" that you complete it | The daily priority task; "everything else is for clarity" |

In the first version, neither big nor small goals produce action: action comes from the simplicity of the task. In the second, small goals are credited with "action," and big goals with "motivation." The difference is less a contradiction than a shift in what he wants to emphasize: in the first, that motivation is unnecessary; in the second, that a large goal is what makes the effort feel worth it. Both agree on the structural point: the levels have different jobs, and the mistake is to ask a big goal to tell you what to do today, or a daily task to tell you why.

The 2024 "direction" passage includes an instruction that the "act small" version omits: remind yourself of your anti-vision, vision and goals daily, because "these are your own little world. When your world is all you think about, your mind conspires in your favor." The effects he lists are perceptual and motivational: you notice small details others do not (an edge), the mind releases dopamine as a motivator, the scene is set for flow, and you stop worrying about problems you cannot control (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). "Your mind conspires in your favor" is a figure of speech for the lens effect of Chapter 6, not a claim about external forces.

In January 2024 Koe confronted the objection directly. People ask him, "why are you setting all these goals... I'm not even going to use them." His reply: "that's not what they're for." The same people later come back saying "Dan, I'm lost, I don't know what to do," because they have no goal giving them direction. Goals "are not for achieving the goals, they're for clarity, so you know what to learn, you know what to study, you know what skills to acquire," a direction that runs from macro to micro (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07). This is a central claim of the chapter and a distinctive one. In ordinary usage a goal is something to be achieved and judged by whether it is achieved. In Koe's usage, a goal is primarily an instrument for deciding what to learn and do next; achievement is secondary.

#### Clarity, not motivation: the canyon and the bridge

Behind the whole hierarchy lies a diagnosis that Chapter 5 introduced: most people lack clarity, not motivation. In 2023 Koe gave it the image that best explains why the hierarchy exists. "Most people don't need motivation, they need clarity." Motivation is like standing on one side of a canyon: the other side looks like a utopia (beautiful people, music, cars, houses, all calling you to take the leap), but you have no way to get over. "Clarity is the first plank of the bridge to the other side" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25).

The image clarifies the logic of the previous pages. Motivation, here, is the view of the other side: it makes the destination desirable but does nothing about the gap. More motivation only makes the canyon more painful. What changes the situation is a plank, a first step that is solid enough to stand on. Each level of the hierarchy is a plank further out, and the daily task is the plank you are standing on. The image also explains the instruction to make tasks "stupidly simple": a plank does not need to reach the other side; it needs to hold your weight.

Koe gives two procedures for building the bridge. The first, from 2023, is the **string of goals**. Any goal that is not immediate ("unlike saying this next word") needs clarity, and you get it by "attaching a string of goals to it like a quest in a video game," which narrows attention, blocks distractions and gets you obsessed with solving it; it gives clarity and orders the mind. His example is the purpose "get fit in the gym": goal one, learn how to go to the gym; goal two, understand what you actually need to do, what food to eat, what equipment to buy. With a specific goal such as gaining twenty pounds of muscle in the first year, you learn what is possible, align all your actions with it and execute, repeating the boring fundamentals. And you "study the principles" that actualize goals (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). The string of goals is the hierarchy turned sideways: instead of levels of time (decade, year, month), it lays out a sequence of stages, each one unlocking the next, as quests do.

The second, from 2025, is a four-step plan built around money. (1) Break the vision into **palatable milestones**: for a goal of a million dollars a year, the milestones could be the first dollar, then $10,000, then $100,000. You will not leap to a million without making the first ten thousand, and what gets you to $10K differs from what gets you to $1M; a million causes anxiety and is unclear, while one dollar is specific and makes sense. (2) Write exactly what you must do to reach the first milestone: for one dollar, start a business aligned with your vision and interests (in his case, writing), and as a beginner research, learn and do what it takes to earn a dollar as a writer. (3) Write what you must learn: for a writing business, marketing, sales, content creation and how to create a product or service. For the first dollar he could write an ebook, charge $10 and promote it to the small audience built with his content. (4) List the daily tasks that actually get results rather than "busy work" (for a writing business, posting high-quality content), and get "as granular and detailed as possible" (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). The second and third steps separate two lists that people usually mix: what to *do* and what to *learn*. The hierarchy produces both, and the daily levers draw on the first while the education draws on the second.

The same sequence appears, in its most general form, as a description of how to "use our mind to make progress in reality": "we adapt to the modern landscape by creating a vision for your future, deconstructing it into goals, acquiring knowledge and skills, and making small daily improvements," with attention on health, wealth and happiness (**Source:** `Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md`, 2023-04-20; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). Vision, goals, knowledge and skills, daily improvements: the four terms compress this entire section.

#### The path: an evolving hierarchy

In the summary of his book *The Art of Focus* (2024), Koe gives the hierarchy of goals a second name, the **path**: "Your path is a hierarchy of goals that evolves with time in alignment with your evolving purpose." There are skills, education and knowledge you must acquire to reach a new "level of mind," and "this is a never-ending path, the path of mastery." Each domain (health, wealth, relationships) is to be deconstructed into a path that uncovers the small priority actions that create a better life. You need not focus on all of them at once, but you will need to improve them all eventually, because "wealth and relationships can only improve as much as your health and vice versa." Finally: "Your priority will involve learning or building for a dedicated time block every day" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). This is the vocabulary of section 8.2 (purpose, path, priority) appearing in the hierarchy of goals: the path is the hierarchy viewed as something that grows with you, and the priority is the daily block in which you walk it. The phrase "path of mastery" echoes George Leonard's *Mastery*, which Koe discusses elsewhere (Chapter 15); here it designates the never-ending character of the hierarchy rather than Leonard's specific model.

#### How to hold goals: guide, not master

If goals are instruments of clarity rather than targets, how tightly should one hold them? Koe's answers differ, and the differences are worth laying out because he never reconciles them explicitly.

On one side are formulations that loosen the grip. "Don't attach to these goals, this is just a practice to generate clarity" (2023-07). In September 2023: to create a game you need a hierarchy of goals that frames your attention (a ten-year goal "that lets your vision blossom," and yearly, monthly and weekly goals), "all of which are loosely held in the back of your mind; they should not be your master but your guide." Only then can you align your daily education and "lever moving actions" with them (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05). And in July 2024 the most radical: set them all, "then forget about them all."

On the other side are formulations that ask for daily contact: "remind yourself of anti-vision, vision and goals daily" (2024-07, in the same month as "forget about them all"); in 2025, "focus on one massive goal per year"; in 2026, look at the vision each morning.

| Formulation | Date | Attitude |
|---|---|---|
| "Don't attach to these goals; this is just a practice to generate clarity" | 2023-07 | Loose |
| "Loosely held in the back of your mind... not your master but your guide" | 2023-09 | Loose |
| "Set... goals, then forget about them all" | 2024-07 | Loose |
| "Remind yourself of anti-vision, vision and goals daily" | 2024-07 | Close |
| Look at the vision each morning | 2026 | Close |

The tension is real, but a reading that fits both sides is available, and Koe's own maxim points to it: "be stubborn with vision and loose with details." What is held daily is the *vision* (and anti-vision), the frame of the "own little world." What is loosened or forgotten are the *specific goals*, which are revisable instruments. This reading is an interpretation; Koe does not state it as a resolution.

The September 2023 passage adds two observations that explain why holding goals matters at all. "You're overly focused on the negative parts of life because you haven't built positive responsibilities that demand your focus." And "the more attention you invest in your goals, the stronger their gravity becomes" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05). **Gravity** is one of Koe's recurring coinages: the pull a goal exerts on attention, which grows with the energy and attention invested in it. A goal that has been set but never fed has little gravity, and distractions easily outweigh it. Section 8.7 develops the concept, which turns out to be the hinge between goals and passion.

#### Goals as lenses at three scales

In his 2024 video on life as a video game, Koe connected the hierarchy of goals to flow (Chapter 10). The goals must be ones you choose, not ones assigned to you, and they operate at three scales: "a big life goal to frame the information that you see as important so you can actually notice that information"; "a series of reverse engineered goals from that to bring clarity to your actions"; and "a small goal in the present to rivet your attention in this very moment and kick you into flow." He adds that the deep work "hacks" (instrumental music, a hat, a timer, deadlines) "don't inherently put you into flow, but they help you focus your attention so you can get into it easier"; they build an environment that lets you focus, and a hat literally narrows your field of vision (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

| Scale | Function |
|---|---|
| Big chosen life goal | Frames which information is seen as important, so it can be noticed |
| Reverse-engineered intermediate goals | Bring clarity to actions |
| Small present goal | Rivets attention in the moment and triggers flow |

The model gives each level a perceptual role, not just a planning role. The large goal selects information; the middle goals select actions; the smallest goal selects the present moment.

In December 2025 the same idea became explicit in the language of Chapter 6. In the third part of a protocol for "fixing your life in one day," Koe asks for a one-year goal, a one-month goal and a daily plan, and clarifies that these goals "aren't for achievement": "These are lenses, macro and micro lenses," to be put on at a certain time so as to experience that point of view, notice the right information and take the right actions. When you are narrowed on a daily task you think of nothing else; when you zoom out to the vision you have "unbounded creativity." The questions he gives are precise:

- **One-year lens:** What would have to be true in one year for you to know you have broken the old pattern? One concrete thing.
- **One-month lens:** What would have to be true in one month for the one-year lens to remain possible?
- **Daily lens:** What are two to three actions you can time-block tomorrow that the person you are becoming would simply do?

(**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28.)

The questions encode the theory. The one-year question asks for a *proof* of change rather than an achievement ("the old pattern is broken"), which ties the hierarchy to identity (Chapter 4). The one-month question defines the month by what keeps the year *possible*, not by a fraction of the year's output. And the daily question defines actions by identity ("the person you are becoming would simply do"), not by willpower. The **macro and micro lenses** are therefore the latest form of the claim that goals are not for achieving: they are points of view one adopts deliberately at different moments.

There is a counter-current in the corpus that should be acknowledged. In late 2024 Koe also said that "it's not about the journey, it's about the destination" and that the destination "is what determines the journey," vastly more important than the system. The two positions coexist: the destination determines the direction of the system, while each concrete goal functions as a lens that can be adjusted or abandoned. Koe himself concludes elsewhere that journey and destination are inseparable, but he does not use that conclusion to reconcile these specific statements.

#### Goals as boundaries for creativity

A last function of goals anticipates section 8.4. "To create you need boundaries for your creativity or else things get too chaotic and overwhelming." Goals turned into projects give you something tangible to work on, and goals and projects provide boundaries for learning and creativity. So break the vision into yearly, monthly, weekly and daily goals and turn them into milestones for a project, for instance a personal brand ("the fuel for anything you want to build") or a one-person business, which he calls "the starting point that I believe 99% of people should start with before trying to go after any other business model," because it is holistic, evolves with you and is not a static model that sounds too good to be true (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). Here the hierarchy of goals hands over to the *project*, which Chapter 9 develops as the practical vehicle of change. The claim that creativity needs boundaries recurs in section 8.4 under the name "constraints."

#### The six-component plan

In December 2025 Koe consolidated the elements of this section, together with the anti-vision and vision of Chapter 7, into a single sheet of paper. On a new sheet, he says, write six components:

1. **Anti-vision:** the bane of my existence, or the life I never want to experience again.
2. **Vision:** the ideal life I think I want, which I can improve as I work toward it.
3. **One-year goal:** what my life will look like in one year, and is that closer to the life I want?
4. **One-month project:** what I need to learn, which skills to acquire, what I can build to move closer to the one-year goal.
5. **Daily levers:** the priority, needle-moving tasks that bring the project closer to completion.
6. **Constraints:** what I am not willing to sacrifice to achieve my vision.

Why is this powerful? Because these components "literally create your own world," and if you are meant to pursue that hierarchy of goals at this stage of your life, "you will have no other option but to become obsessed" (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28).

Two features distinguish this version from the earlier ones. The middle level is no longer a "monthly goal" but a **one-month project**, defined by what you must learn, which skills to acquire and what to build; the hierarchy now hands over to the project at the monthly scale. And the plan now contains a negative component, the constraints, which earlier versions treated separately (section 8.4). The conditional clause about obsession is also significant: Koe does not promise that any hierarchy will produce obsession, only that the right one at the right stage will. Obsession is used here as evidence of fit.

He then maps the six components onto a video game. Games, he says, are "the poster child for obsession, enjoyment, and flow states": they contain all the components that lead to focus and clarity, and reverse-engineering them yields deeper enjoyment, fewer distractions and more success.

| Plan component | Game element | Koe's gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Vision | How you win | "At least until the game evolves" |
| Anti-vision | What's at stake | What happens if you lose or give up |
| One-year goal | The mission | "Your sole priority in life" |
| One-month project | The boss fight | How you gain XP and acquire loot |
| Daily levers | The quests | The daily process that unlocks new opportunities |
| Constraints | The rules | "The limitations that encourage creativity" |

Together the components form "this concentric set of circles, like a force field" that guards the mind from distractions and shiny objects. The **force field** is weak the first time one draws it; the more energy one invests in building and adopting the frame, the stronger it becomes, "until it becomes who you are" (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). The final clause links the plan to identity change (Chapter 4): the frame begins as an exercise and ends as a self. The metaphor of the game is developed for its own sake in Chapter 10; what matters here is that each component of the plan has a job that a game designer would recognize, and that the protective effect (the force field) depends on invested energy, the same variable as "gravity."

The six-component sheet has precursors in the 12 rules of April 2024 (section 8.3), where two of its pieces appeared as separate rules. Rule 3, the **mission**, defined the role of what the 2025 sheet calls the one-year goal: "Your mission is the most important thing in your life. It is the bridge between what you do and what you don't want, your vision and your anti-vision. Anything that does not align with your mission is to be treated as a distraction. Your mission evolves with awareness of new beliefs, opportunities and knowledge. Your mission requires faith: you can't see the next step unless you take the first, and once you do, the second may be completely different from anything you could have imagined" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). *Mission* is a common word that Koe uses with a defined sense: the bridge between vision and anti-vision, the criterion of what counts as a distraction, and a commitment that requires acting before the path is visible. The clause on faith matters, because it qualifies everything said about clarity: clarity reaches only to the next plank, and the planks beyond it may not be where one expected.

Rule 8, **limitations**, anticipated the constraints: "A fool becomes rich at the expense of everything good in life. A creative becomes rich at the expense of his choice." "Limitations on your goals force creativity." The guiding question is "what are you not willing to sacrifice to achieve your goals?", and the creative challenge appears when you try to achieve your goals without betraying your vision: become rich without sacrificing your family, healthy without sacrificing your work, valuable without sacrificing what makes life worth living (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). Section 8.4 develops the idea under its other names.

#### Daily levers: the only productivity advice you need

At the bottom of every version of the hierarchy is the same object: a small number of daily tasks that actually move things forward. Koe calls them **priority tasks**, **lever-moving tasks**, **levers** or **daily levers**, and in 2025 **most important tasks**; the corpus alternates among these names almost without distinction. The image of the lever is precise: a small force applied at the right point moves a large weight. The levers are the points at which a small daily effort moves the project, and the project moves the vision.

Their earliest systematic presentation is Commandment 3 of "The 4-Hour Workday" (January 2023, reissued in a 2024 compilation): outline three priority, or lever-moving, tasks. "This is situation dependent, I can't tell you what your lever moving tasks are going to be." To find them you must understand what you are trying to achieve "from a very big picture, not technical details" (for a creator or one-person business, he suggests watching his big-picture rundowns), and then start testing. Levers depend on "what stage you're in, stage of development... in business." Other tasks exist, which he calls **auxiliary tasks**, but they are done toward the end, when there is an hour to spare before the four hours are up. His own three levers at that stage were writing content, generating traffic and promoting products (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). Chapter 12 places the levers inside the structure of the four-hour workday; here the point is their logic. Levers are not a universal list but a function of two variables: the goal, understood at the level of the big picture, and the stage of development. The same person will have different levers at $0 and at $100,000 a year.

In April 2024 the levers became Rule 9 of the 12 rules: "Every day you need priority tasks that move the needle toward your projects, goals and vision from the ground up." These are often seen as boring fundamentals "unless you cultivate a sense of mastery behind what you do": see every day as a practice and a process rather than an outcome, "or else it becomes boring." The rule closes with an instruction and a warning: "Do what needs to be done, but grip your vision as the anchor into the unknown" (the transcript reads "known," which makes the phrase ambiguous; "unknown" fits his recurring image of the vision as an anchor into uncertainty, but the reading is not certain), and "if you aren't making progress it's because you aren't moving levers, even if you think you are" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). The reference to mastery explains how a lever survives repetition. The task itself rarely changes (write, publish, promote); what keeps it alive is treating it as practice, which connects the daily lever to the path of mastery of Chapter 15.

In the 2025 version of the 12 rules, the levers moved to Rule 5 and acquired a test. "Every single day, complete at least one to three priority tasks that move the needle toward completing your project. That is the only piece of productivity advice you will ever need in your life." The rule of thumb: "After two weeks, if you haven't made any noticeable progress toward your goals, you are not moving the right levers. You are doing something wrong." Most people will not admit this, or they intentionally do busy work to avoid progress "because secretly they want to fail." The explanation draws on Chapter 6: the mind notices opportunities to achieve its goals, and most people have "this deep and unconscious programmed goal of staying the same," so that is what they work toward without realizing it (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The two-week rule is a heuristic, offered without data, but it gives the levers something they lacked: a falsification criterion. A lever that moves nothing in two weeks is not a lever. The explanation of why people choose non-levers is an application of the cybernetic model: if the operative goal is unconscious stability, then busy work is the system working perfectly toward that goal.

#### How many levers?

The number of daily levers changes across the corpus, and the changes are worth a table because Koe gives a reason for at least one of them.

| Version | Date | Number |
|---|---|---|
| Commandment 3 of "The 4-Hour Workday" | 2023-01 (reissued 2024-03) | Three priority tasks |
| Hierarchy across four life domains | 2023-12 | Three to five daily priority tasks |
| "The Millionaire Productivity Routine" | 2024-02 | "Two to three, if not more if you can handle more" |
| Warm-up to the 12 rules | 2024-04 | Three levers each day |
| His own "Lego day" | 2024-10 | Five to ten priority tasks in one- to two-hour blocks |
| Gamifying deep work | 2025-05 | Two to three most important tasks |
| 12 rules, 2025 version | 2025-09 | "At least one to three" |

The reason he gives comes from the February 2024 video: "Your work should consist of two to three, if not more if you can handle more, but start with two to three lever moving tasks that move the needle towards your project, and the project moving the needle towards your vision for the future. Some people can handle more tasks, some people can handle less." His own planner (its name is garbled in the transcript) had nine slots for priority tasks; that morning he had written his tasks, with his notes for the day at the top (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The movement is from a fixed number to an adjustable range: one to three for someone who starts with an hour a day, more for someone with more hours and better systems. The constant is the chain stated in the same sentence: the task moves the project, the project moves the vision.

#### Why people miss the levers

If the levers are so simple, why do people fail to pull them? Koe offers a diagnosis from his experience with students. Courses are extremely valuable, he says, because schools do not teach what they contain ("knowledge not found in schools equals money not found in employment"). But people take courses or read internet content and completely miss the lever-moving tasks, even when these are made very clear; they go through the course, forget it, do what they were going to do anyway, use the course as motivation, and then wonder why they have no results. His diagnostic: when someone says "Dan, I'm not getting results," he asks what exactly they do every day, for instance in the first morning block when they work on the business: what are the exact steps? They either list one thing or nothing tangible. "They're procrastinating for three hours while working on things that don't matter for the other eight" (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). (Koe uses "procrastination" in opposite senses across the corpus: here as unconscious avoidance, elsewhere as a deliberate "mindful procrastination" that saves energy for a sprint; Chapter 12 treats the difference.)

The diagnosis implies a practice that anyone can apply to themselves: describe, in concrete steps, what happens in the first work block of the day. If the description is vague, the levers are missing, whatever one's knowledge or motivation.

A month later Koe connected the levers to a quotation he attributes to Harrington Emerson: "As to methods there may be a million and then some, but principles are few. The man who grasps principles can successfully select his own methods. The man who tries methods, ignoring principles, is sure to have trouble." His adaptation: lever-moving actions are the *principles* behind the methods and tactics people promote ("start this social media marketing agency to make a million dollars a year," "use this AI business model"); those are tactics that operate from principles their creators understand. You absorb principles by studying multiple perspectives and noting the patterns that let all of them succeed, which he names as his own "one million dollar skill stack": writing, speaking, marketing and sales, paired with personal interests, plus the tactics learned over time. This is why he refuses to give prescriptions and how-tos that "pigeonhole everyone's perspective" so that only 0.01% get results: "you have to go and figure it out for yourself." The practical instruction that opens the passage is to make persistent action seamless by scheduling lever-moving tasks into high-energy time blocks (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11).

**Complementary context:** Harrington Emerson (1853–1931) was an American efficiency engineer and management consultant of the scientific-management era, author of *The Twelve Principles of Efficiency* (1912). The quotation about methods and principles is widely attributed to him; elsewhere in the corpus Koe cites it without attribution.

The adaptation solves a puzzle created by his earlier statement that he cannot tell anyone what their levers are. If levers depend on situation and stage, how can they be taught at all? Emerson's distinction answers: what can be taught is the principle (create content, generate traffic, promote products; or, at a more general level, do the things that move the project); the method by which one does it in a particular situation must be discovered by testing.

#### Busy work and "do nothing useless"

The negative side of the levers is **busy work**: work that does not move the needle toward the vision. In 2024 Koe made its elimination one of the steps in a video on leverage: "If the work doesn't move the needle toward your vision, stop doing it." He stresses it because viewers want "the steps to make a million," but "you don't understand what making money is and the skills, mindset, beliefs, habits associated with that." His estimate, offered as an argument rather than a measurement: "I would argue that 90% of your work right now isn't moving the needle, or else you would be successful; therefore stop almost everything that you're doing, because busy work is yet another distraction that people love to fall for." "Work can be a distraction, a very massive distraction" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). The inversion is the point: distraction is usually imagined as the opposite of work, but work that does not serve the vision is distraction wearing work's clothes, and it is harder to detect because it feels virtuous.

In August 2026 Koe found an older formulation of the same idea in Miyamoto Musashi's ninth precept, "do nothing useless." "The most actionable point is the ninth precept, which is to do nothing useless. Make that your operating principle for the next year and you will do incredible things. If you were to simply audit your thoughts and behavior for usefulness, you would rapidly turn your life around." Scrolling social media and dogmatic black-and-white thinking are useless; "the mindless way of life is a useless way of life." The precept raises open questions he leaves to the viewer: what do you master, what is your form of training, how do you understand gain and loss, how do you embody the principles, "how do you do something useful?" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

**Complementary context:** Miyamoto Musashi (c. 1584–1645) was a Japanese swordsman and strategist, author of *The Book of Five Rings*. Its opening book closes with nine precepts for those who would learn his strategy, the last of which is usually translated "do nothing which is of no use."

Koe adapts the precept from a rule of martial training into an audit of thought and behavior. The adaptation stands in an unacknowledged tension with a video he published five months earlier, in which he argued that creativity requires "useless wandering" and genuine boredom, against the culture of efficiency. He does not reconcile the two. A possible reading is that they apply at different levels: what looks useless for productivity (wandering, boredom) may be useful for creative strategy, so the audit for usefulness should ask "useful for what?" rather than "efficient?" That reading is an interpretation; the corpus leaves the two statements side by side.

#### Most important tasks: quantified and few

In 2025 Koe returned to the daily levers under the name **most important tasks**, as the first step in "gamifying" deep work. "The first thing is to quantify your two to three highest priority tasks or most important tasks." This does not mean you do only two or three things a day: "you choose two or three things that demand the most mental energy from you for deep work. Deep work is for deep tasks. Shallow work is for shallow tasks." Answering email, administrative work and "other things that don't move the needle toward the life you want to live" are not among them (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). The criterion has shifted slightly from the 2023 version. There, levers were defined by their effect (they move the project); here, most important tasks are defined also by their cost (they demand the most mental energy), which matches them to the scarce resource of Chapter 6.

He then gives a definition by consequence: "These are the tasks that if not completed will cause you to stagnate or move backward." Examples: "building a meaningful project before your 9 to 5 in the morning that will give you more time and financial freedom," or, for a creator, "building a new product, writing promotions, or pulling the levers that lead to distribution and sales." And a diagnosis that repeats the 2023 point about discernment: "You aren't where you want to be because you aren't focused on your two to three most important tasks first thing in the morning, or you don't know what your most important tasks are because you haven't actually thought them through and you're listening to someone else as to what the tasks you should be doing are, when that's up to your discernment and personal experimentation" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

Why limit them to two or three? Koe opens the section with what he calls a "cheeky little quote" from Cal Newport: "Who you are, what you think, feel, and do, what you love is the sum of what you focus on." Then his own argument: "Our brains have a limited capacity for deep focused work every day. So by limiting your top priorities to two to three tasks, it forces you to be ruthless about what truly matters and aligns you with your bigger goals. So that's level one of narrowing your focus to keep distractions at bay: just two to three tasks" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). The quotation is Newport's (whose *Deep Work* Koe says elsewhere he has not read); the argument from limited capacity and the term "level one of narrowing your focus" are Koe's. The limit is not arbitrary minimalism: it is a forcing function that makes prioritization unavoidable, the same logic that section 8.4 will apply to constraints in general.

**Complementary context:** Cal Newport is a computer scientist and author of *Deep Work* (2016), which popularized the distinction between "deep work" (cognitively demanding, distraction-free work that creates new value) and "shallow work" (logistical tasks that can be done while distracted).

Finally, the tasks should be quantified. "If you can, you want to quantify these tasks. So instead of putting my task as 'I want to write some of my newsletter,' I'll say 'I want to write a thousand words of my newsletter.' What this does is it creates the feedback loop. Like when you're completing quests in a game, you can see the progress that you're making, and that acts as a barrier for distractions. It prevents your mind from going elsewhere because it's consistently focusing back on itself" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). The mechanism is the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6 at the scale of a work session: a quantified target lets you compare where you are with where you are going, and the visible gap keeps attention returning to the task. A vague target ("some of my newsletter") gives no gap to perceive, so attention has nothing to come back to. Quantification also prepares the distinction between performance and vanity in section 8.4: a good quantity is one you control (a thousand words), not one you do not (fifty followers).

With the levers, the hierarchy of goals is complete from top to bottom: vision, goals at shrinking scales, project, daily levers. The next section steps back to show the single principle Koe believes underlies this structure and every other one he has built.

### 8.2 Goal, Path, Problem

#### "The foundational principle of everything"

Under the many versions of the hierarchy of goals, Koe sees one principle, and in March 2023 he stated it in its barest form. "It's all goals": human behavior is goals, marketing is goals, and the structure is always the same: a **desired goal**, a **path** to get there, and the **problem** that is preventing you from doing so. "That's literally the foundational principle of everything." It is offer creation (Chapter 33), it is how you plan your days on the micro scale, it is how you set a vision for your future and act on it, it is how you organize your movement through the world. And the path, he adds, must be made clear (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05).

The three terms are defined by their relations. A goal without a problem is a wish: nothing stands in the way, so nothing needs to be done. A problem without a goal is an irritation: something is wrong, but there is no direction away from it. A goal and a problem without a path is the canyon of section 8.1: you see the other side and the gap, but no bridge. Only the three together produce directed action.

Koe gave the relation between goal and problem a spatial definition: the goal is "the polar end of the problem that you're going to solve." Once you have found the problem, the goal is its opposite pole. And then, he adds, you must first learn how to solve it; you cannot go straight into solving it (**Source:** `Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md`, 2023-03-30). The phrase **polar end** recurs in the corpus and matters for section 8.6, where the relation is reversed ("problems are the polar end of purpose"). The added clause about learning first places education between problem and goal: the path is partly made of what one has to learn.

In 2025 he described the space between the poles. "A problem implies a goal": realizing a problem gives you a goal, which you should make conscious. Between the problem (now) and the goal (the polar end) lies the opportunity for "a plan, a strategy, a hierarchy of goals" that lets you enter flow and make cumulative progress, keeping chaos at bay as you trek into the unknown. The process is painful, he warns, because you are creating not just a new life but a new identity: "expanding your mind to house more complexity that allows you to solve more valuable problems" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12). This passage joins the three parts of Part IV that this chapter connects. The hierarchy of goals (8.1) is what fills the space between problem and goal; flow (Chapter 10) is the experience of moving through that space; and identity change (Chapter 4) is its cost, because a mind that can solve a more valuable problem is a more complex mind.

#### The Focus Formula: purpose, process, priority

Koe's most worked-out packaging of the principle is the **Focus Formula**, first presented in January 2023 and repeated in a 2024 compilation. "I've been working on this for a long time. This is going to be called the focus formula and it is how you take back control of your life." He showed it as a graphic he was proud of and asked viewers to screenshot; the transcripts do not allow its visual layout to be reconstructed, but its three steps, discussed in order, are **purpose**, **process** and **priority** (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The premise of the formula is a sentence Koe would repeat for years: "If you don't know what you want, you will be told what you want and you will believe it." Unless your ego is completely dissolved and you plan to live in the woods like a monk with no human connection, you need a purpose, "and even then humans still have a purpose; your purpose then is peace." And because "success is relative and dependent on a hierarchy of goals, you need to create your own." He created the formula, he says, because books that tell you "you need a purpose" do not explain how to get one (**Source:** as above).

The premise connects this chapter to Part I. "You will be told what you want" is the mechanism of the Matrix and the default path (Chapters 1 and 2) restated as a law of goals: a mind cannot hold no goals, so if you do not supply them, the environment will, and you will experience them as your own. The monk example is a limit case that confirms the rule rather than breaking it: even the most radical withdrawal has a goal (peace). And the phrase "success is relative and dependent on a hierarchy of goals" means that there is no neutral measure of success to fall back on; success is always success relative to some hierarchy, so whoever controls the hierarchy controls the meaning of success.

The three steps are as follows.

**Purpose** is the goal, which in the formula's presentation is equated with "the most pressing problem" that orders the mind. Koe develops this definition at length in the same video, and section 8.6 treats it in full.

**Process** is the clarity step. "After purpose we have process: we need to gain clarity and understand how to achieve whatever purpose we have; repeat the boring fundamentals." He illustrates the boring fundamentals with a line he uses often: "A sushi chef spends years perfecting the preparation of rice, a tennis player obsesses over the angle of their serve. Mastery sets you apart in a world that can't see beyond the surface." The method for gaining clarity is the string of goals of section 8.1: if a goal is not immediate, "you need to gain clarity by attaching a string of goals to it like a quest in a video game, that helps narrow your attention, block distractions and gets you obsessed with solving it," with the same fitness example (learn to go to the gym, then learn what to eat and what equipment to buy, then pursue a specific target such as twenty pounds of muscle in a year, aligning all actions and executing it as a process). "You need to study the principles of these things" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). **Boring fundamentals** is another of Koe's coinages: actions repeated daily that look boring only from outside, and that are principles rather than tactics (the same distinction as Emerson's, section 8.1).

**Priority** is the daily step: the lever-moving actions, framed, in Koe's phrase, as "performance versus vanity." Section 8.4 treats this framing in detail.

Koe insists that the formula is not only for life as a whole. "This does not only apply to your life, this applies to every single goal you plan to actualize": a project, or an article, for which "you need a purpose for that article, you need a process for writing it and you need priority actions to take." "This is like a global framework for framing your attention and eliminating distraction" from unimportant problems, which he calls "noise" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21; `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). The scale-independence is the same as that of the hierarchy of goals ("any plane of reality"), and it is what makes the formula a *principle* rather than a life-planning template: the same three questions (what for? how? what now?) organize a decade and a paragraph.

#### Focus as the habit of habits

The formula is called a *focus* formula for a reason Koe gave in July 2023, when he defined focus with a phrase of his own: "Focus is the habit of habits; it is the thing that comes before habits, the habit that comes before any other habit in your life. Focus is the root of your entire being; it is how you channel your awareness, consciousness and attention toward value or distraction." Conscious focus on your long- to short-term goals "is how you program your own mind" for success and daily quality of life (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11).

**Habit of habits** names a priority of order. Habits are what one does repeatedly; focus decides what one attends to, and therefore which habits get formed at all. If attention is the "root control" of Chapter 6, focus directed at a hierarchy of goals is the root habit. The Focus Formula is thus the tool by which the root habit is pointed: purpose decides what attention is for, process gives it a path, priority gives it its daily object. "Programming your own mind" is the positive counterpart of the conditioning described in Part I: the same plasticity that lets the environment install goals lets you install your own, if you focus deliberately.

#### Games as a model: the diagnostic

In September 2023 Koe re-derived the formula from games. "We need to understand how to create our own games." If games have a desired outcome (winning), a path to get there (progression) and habitual actions (priorities), then "we can create games out of any situation in life." "This universal principle of purpose, process and priority is the underlying framework of human behavior." From it he drew a three-part diagnostic: "if you don't have a goal you don't have a vision; if you don't have a path you don't have clarity; if you don't have a task you don't have focus." And a claim of scope: "Most of your problems in life can be solved by understanding and applying this principle" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05).

| Missing element | Resulting deficit |
|---|---|
| Goal (purpose) | No vision: no direction to move in |
| Path (process) | No clarity: overwhelm, anxiety |
| Task (priority) | No focus: attention has nothing to hold it now |

The diagnostic is the most practical form of the principle. Someone who feels directionless lacks a goal; someone who knows what they want but feels overwhelmed lacks a path; someone who knows the goal and the path but drifts through the day lacks a task. Each symptom points to a different repair, which is why the same advice (more motivation, more discipline) fails for most of them. The claim that "most problems" yield to this principle is Koe's opinion; the diagnostic itself is a useful sorting device whether or not the claim holds in full.

#### The same principle under many names

Koe has repackaged the principle several times, which can make his corpus look more varied than it is. The following table lists the main versions, in order; Chapter 10 discusses the game versions, and section 8.3 the creation pyramid and the 12 rules.

| Name | Date | Components |

| Creation pyramid | 2022-06 | Experience, vision, goals, process, drive, focus, resting on state of consciousness |
| Focus Formula | 2023-01 (reissued 2024-03) | Purpose, process, priority |

Koe gives a reason only for the first renaming (the creation hierarchy was turned into a pyramid to package it "simply but with depth"); for the others he gives none. Two trends are visible. "Process" becomes "path," which better conveys a structure that evolves rather than a procedure; and the anti-vision is placed in front of the vision from 2024 on. Read together, the versions are complementary views of the same goal–path–problem principle.

#### The hunting skill set

The July 2024 version deserves separate treatment because it adds elements the others lack. Koe calls it the steps "for learning to hunt," in a video about relevance in the age of AI: (1) a vision, for direction; (2) goals, for clarity; (3) systems, for progress; (4) open-mindedness, for awareness; (5) being high-agency, to identify problems in the here and now; (6) creativity, for unique solutions. "That's all you need: vision, goals, systems, open-mindedness, agency and creativity, from the top down... you're creatively solving problems on a day-to-day basis, high leverage problems." To these he adds "one last skill that is a meta skill for learning other tangible skills": making money (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`, 2024-07-13).

The sequence is held together by explicit bridges. Vision and goals are "fickle... guides... so far out, so we need to bring it closer," which is the job of systems. After vision, goals and systems, "now we're open-minded, we need to use that open-mindedness in the here and now to identify problems," which is the job of agency. The sequence descends from the most distant (vision) to the most immediate (creative solutions to today's problems), and its lower half adds what the Focus Formula leaves implicit: the capacity to *see* problems (open-mindedness, agency) and to solve them in new ways (creativity). The metaphor of hunting, developed in section 8.6, is the opposite of being assigned problems by someone else. Making money is called a meta-skill because, in Koe's view, learning to earn on your own forces you to learn the other skills; Chapter 34 develops the idea.

#### Three steps out of feeling lost

An earlier version of the principle, from October 2022, is framed as a remedy for a feeling rather than as a planning tool. To stop feeling lost, anxious or uncertain about the future: (1) set a meaningful goal; (2) create a system that bridges the gap toward the goal; (3) channel all your energy toward that goal within that process. The order matters: the goal provides intrinsic energy, the system provides clarity on how to get there, and channeling reframes every situation through the goal (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16).

The third step is the distinctive one. A meaningful goal already provides energy: "that's dopamine, that's fuel," which Koe calls *intrinsic* energy. "The next type of energy is extrinsic": channeling all your thoughts, emotions and actions toward that specific goal. "You're going to reframe every single thing in every single situation that you encounter in relationship to your goal," perceiving the situation or problem "from the perspective of your goal." His example: if you want a healthy physique and go out to eat, you view the situation from that reality. Which food will you choose, and if you choose it, will you care what people think, or eat it anyway "and not let them project their own goals and reality on me"? As you go about the day, think about the why behind your actions and what energy you channel into your goals with each one (**Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`, 2022-10-16).

A terminological caution is needed. "Extrinsic energy" here does not mean external rewards. It names a deliberate act of channeling: the person directs thoughts, emotions and actions toward the goal. In another video of the same year Koe uses "extrinsic" in the ordinary sense, for fickle motivation from outside, which he contrasts unfavorably with intrinsic drive. The same word thus designates two different things, and he does not comment on the slippage. In this passage the useful idea is the *reframing*: once a goal exists, every situation (a restaurant menu, a social invitation, other people's opinions) can be read as a move in the goal's game, and other people's goals ("their own goals and reality") lose their power to define the situation for you.

This 2022 sequence, in which the first step is to *set* a meaningful goal, is one Koe later revised. In 2024 he told viewers to "stop focusing on the goal" until a problem gives it gravity. Section 8.6 presents the change.

#### Purpose, path, priority in other people: the marketing version

The principle also has an outward face. In 2023 Koe presented the same three terms, with "process" replaced by "path," as the basis of marketing, and he made the extension explicit: "Your marketing revolves around the universal principle of human behavior, which is purpose, path and priority." **Purpose** is "a compelling reason to move toward a better future." **Path** is "a process, system or steps that bring clarity to their actions." **Priority** is "the burning problem they face at their level of awareness." "This is everything," he says; it is "an entire section of my book" and applies to everything related to human behavior, and buying a product is human behavior. "You need a problem, you need a goal and you need to bounce between the two; your product is the path that helps them get from one to another, from point A to point B, that's what people buy." His test: think of all the products you have bought; if you can apply this principle to one, you know it is a good product (**Source:** `Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md`, 2023-09-17).

Earlier the same year he had given the fullest version, under the heading of value creation (**Source:** `Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md`, 2023-04-09):

- **Purpose.** Behavior change starts with becoming aware of a problem and a goal revealing itself to overcome it. "A transformation goal equals purpose, purpose equals desire, desire equals how problems come into existence." Stories work this way: they open a curiosity loop by implying or stating a problem and hook attention through highs and lows on the way to the transformation and solution (he finds the same pattern in architecture and image design). Landing pages, YouTube videos and any content start the same way: a problem implied, stated and amplified to hook attention and lead into a solution. In personal life, once you become very aware of a problem and how it spreads into other areas, you get fed up and work to transform. The lesson: "by shining a light and making people aware of their problems you create purpose not only in yourself but in others."
- **Path.** Once aware of a problem, people need clarity on how to overcome it; otherwise "chaos ensues: overwhelm, overthinking, anxiety." You must order consciousness through structured information, creating a path, in content or products, that bridges the gap between the problem and the desired transformation.
- **Priority.** Problems are determined by perceived importance: a problem for your friend may not be one for you. If a problem does not register (you are not speaking the person's language, or did not research the market), it will not catch attention or make money. "Humans perceive problems from the lens of their identity," which is why you target or create a customer avatar.

He adds that this is "the same framework" he used in his video on taking back control of your life (the Focus Formula): "a universal principle of value creation for yourself and others."

The marketing version is the Focus Formula turned toward another person, and two of its details enrich the personal version. The first is the sentence that awareness of a problem *creates* purpose. It anticipates the reversal of section 8.6 (a problem creates a goal) and shows why Koe can treat marketing and self-development with the same theory: in both, the first move is to make a problem visible enough to generate a goal. The second is the definition of priority as *perceived* importance relative to identity. In the personal version, priority meant the daily task; here it means the problem that matters most from where the person stands. The two meanings meet in the idea that one's most important task is set by one's most pressing problem.

A 2024 passage gives the path a social dimension. Among the components of a worldview, the third is "potential paths": "How do you actually solve the problems?" "When people don't have clarity on their next steps, they feel anxious, overwhelmed, or bored. This is what causes arguments in relationships, dissonance in business and battles between political parties. People who don't share the same worldviews are missing pieces of the other party's path, narrative, or system that allows them to make sense of that situation." So when you are the niche, "your job is to create a holistic step-by-step path that you can share with readers who have your same worldview, usually in the form of a product or service that you can be paid for," so that they get results and understand you (**Source:** `Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md`, 2024-08-25). The extension is bold: the same lack of a path that produces individual anxiety produces conflict between people, because each side is missing a piece of the other's path. Chapter 19 develops the consequences for the niche.

Finally, Koe reduces the marketing version to an equation: "In marketing you're selling purpose, and a purpose equals goal equals desired outcome." His examples come from the health space. One person might sell a running product to twenty- to thirty-year-olds who want a healthy social circle and to get involved with their community; another might sell a weight-training program to business professionals who want to "get jacked with only three days in the gym," because they are building a business and think they lack time, so they can have "both money and muscle" and live a fun life. "Who wouldn't buy that?" The businessman makes money to have fun, does not have to spend much time in the gym, ends up liking it anyway, and his life gets better. "That's what you want, you want a better life" (**Source:** `Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md`, 2023-04-30). The equation purpose = goal = desired outcome makes the identity of the two faces explicit: what a person pursues for themselves and what they buy from someone else are the same object, a better state on the far side of a problem. Chapters 31 to 33 build the theory of value and product on this identity.

### 8.3 The Plan: If You Don't Create One, You Will Be Assigned One

#### "If you don't have one, society does"

Section 8.2 ended on a premise: if you don't know what you want, you will be told what you want. Koe applies the same logic to the plan. In *The Art of Focus* he wrote: "You need a plan because if you don't have one, society does, and they've been planning your life for decades" (quoted in **Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). In January 2025 he returned to the sentence and developed it: "You need a plan, there isn't any other way, because if you don't have one, society does." The problem, he says, is that people conform to the goals of their parents or of society and stop there (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12).

The sentence does not say that society deliberately plans individual lives, though its wording ("they've been planning your life") invites that reading. Its logical content is the same as that of the Focus Formula's premise: a life without a plan is not a life without structure but a life whose structure was supplied by default (school, job, retirement; Chapter 2). The choice is not between planning and spontaneity but between one's own plan and an inherited one.

What, then, is a plan? Koe's 2025 definition rejects the common understanding: "A plan is not a list of things that won't happen; it is an evolving blueprint that structures your mind to notice ideas that lead to progress." Later in the same video he adds that a plan is not just a list of steps but "a surface area for luck, a surface area for discovery," and a frame (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12). The **evolving blueprint** redefines the plan along the lines of the goal-as-lens of Chapter 6. Its value is not that it will be executed as written (it probably will not be) but that it changes what the mind notices. A plan increases the "surface area" on which luck and discovery can land, because it gives incoming information something to attach to.

Two months later he gave the plan a second image, paired with the vision: the vision can seem far away, like a **North Star**, and "a plan is a guess that starts out dumb and becomes educated," narrowing focus on the steps that move the needle forward (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). The North Star is a reference for orientation, not a destination one expects to reach soon; the plan is the part that moves. (Koe also uses "North Star" in a nearer sense, for the purpose of the moment, as section 7.1 noted; section 8.6 returns to that second use.) Calling the plan a guess removes the pressure to get it right the first time. Its first version is expected to be wrong, and it becomes educated only through action and feedback, the error-correction loop of Chapter 6.

These 2025 definitions mark a shift in Koe's view of planning. In 2022 he described a "mad scientist" who plans "every single thing for the next week," extremely specifically, so as not to fall victim to entropy. By 2025 the plan is a conjecture subordinate to action, and in September of that year he went further, telling viewers not to focus on the plan or the strategy, because without action and iteration they are "a list of things that aren't going to happen." The change is one of emphasis rather than a reversal: the plan loses weight as a script and gains weight as a frame of perception. Detailed weekly planning may still have its place at the start of a reinvention (it appears in that context), but the mature view treats the plan as something that serves action, not something action serves.

The April 2024 video that quotes *The Art of Focus* also explains why people lack a plan, in terms that use the vocabulary of Part III. "Your psyche craves actualization and transcendence. The depth of your being wants these things, but your ego is distracted by things it thinks it wants, and that's the problem. You don't have a way to focus your mind. You don't have a plan for the future that holds more gravity than the distractions in your life, so you struggle to maintain a long-term time horizon and get trapped in never-ending short-term stress-inducing tasks." His proposal is one single framework to refer to whenever one is lost: the 12 rules presented later in this section (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14).

The argument has three parts. There is a split between a deep layer that wants growth ("actualization and transcendence," terms that echo Maslow's top levels and that Chapter 38 places in Koe's developmental maps) and a surface layer, the ego, that wants whatever is in front of it. The decisive variable is *gravity*: the plan must pull harder than the distractions. And the symptom of insufficient gravity is a collapsed time horizon, a life spent on urgent, short-term, stressful tasks because nothing long-term is weighty enough to compete. The plan is thus not primarily a schedule; it is a counterweight.

#### The assigned routine

The same structure ("if you don't create it, you will be assigned it") governs Koe's view of routine. In November 2023 he opened a video with a triple: "If you don't create a routine you will be assigned one; if you don't create a purpose you will be assigned one; if you don't create a career you will be assigned one." People are "too quick to adopt the structure that somebody else created to ease life's uncertainty." If you think you don't need a routine, "you may not realize that you have already been assigned one by society, or your routine is not having a routine." Without a routine you created, "your life will slowly fall faster and faster down a chaotic hole into a life of responsibilities, work, people and a personality that you despise." Most people are "progressing the dreams of someone else rather than their own." In contrast, a powerful routine, "no matter how long, prevents overwhelm as you progress towards your goals" (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19).

The **assigned routine** is the daily version of the assigned hierarchy of goals of section 8.1, and it has the same motive: structure eases uncertainty, so people accept whatever structure is offered. Koe's phrase "your routine is not having a routine" closes the escape route of those who believe they are free because they follow no schedule: the absence of a chosen routine is itself a routine, made of reactions to whatever demands attention.

In June 2026 he repeated the sentence, now quoting it from his tweet and from *The Art of Focus*, in a different setting: a video on becoming valuable in the age of AI. Most people have been trained to learn things they don't want, to get a job they don't care about, working for people they would never associate with. But AI, technology and social media have accelerated the understanding that there is more than the default path: seeing people do it on social media is sign enough that it is possible, AI gives individuals more leverage than ever, and people are tired of "the sheer meaninglessness of modern life" (**Source:** `How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md`, 2026-06-28). The sentence itself has not changed between 2023 and 2026; its context has, from personal self-help to the economics of the individual (Part XII). In both settings the claim is the same: a structure will exist either way, and the only question is who authors it. (Koe uses the same construction for goals: "if you don't create a goal, you will be assigned one.")

How does one design a routine of one's own? Koe's answers are informal, but they apply the hierarchy of goals to the day. Asked whether he uses a sauna or a cold plunge, he said no: "one, because I just don't like those things." The sauna "isn't that fun, cold plunge is just pure torture," and he lacks the time to fill his routine with everything: "I don't know how people get things done if they're doing cold plunge in the morning, run in the morning, sauna sometime in the afternoon." His method: "I like to think from a big picture: what is it that I'm trying to accomplish, what is the ideal future that I'm leaning towards." For him that means 10,000 to 15,000 steps a day, the gym, and occasional cardio in place of a walk. Sunlight "is absolutely necessary": he would rather sit in the sun than in a cold plunge or a sauna, and walking shirtless in the sun (on his roof, later in the day) gives him a base tan. Walks also count as creative work: "I would consider walks a part of my work and my life." And walking burns calories; his maintenance, he says, is around 4,000 calories a day (**Source:** `A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md`, 2024-04-02).

The anecdote is personal, and the numbers are his (his recommended step counts for others shift over the years, from around 20,000 for himself to 7,000–8,000 as a "sweet spot" for beginners; Chapter 40 discusses them). What generalizes is the method: habits are selected top-down from the ideal future, not bottom-up from a list of what optimization culture recommends. A habit that does not serve the picture, or that one dislikes, is excluded however popular it is, and a habit can serve several layers at once (the walk is health, sunlight and creative work).

In 2025 he added a condition about work itself. In his "lion work" model (Chapter 6 and Chapter 12) there are two moving pieces: first, leverage your unique strengths that give you an **asymmetric advantage**; second, choose work that lets you put **lifestyle first**, so that you can work according to your energy cycles and consciously choose what to work on. Some creatives worked late at night, others preferred mornings. And the corollary: "if someone tells you what to work on you can't really change that, and your first priority must be to leave that work" (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). This is the assigned routine applied to employment: if the content and hours of your work are set by someone else, you cannot design your routine around your energy, so changing the work becomes the first item of the plan. (The remark about night-working creatives stands in tension with a statement from two months earlier, in which Koe said he did not believe anyone is truly a night owl, since everyone depends on the circadian rhythm. He does not reconcile the two.)

#### The creation pyramid

The oldest of Koe's frameworks for turning a vision into action is the **creation pyramid**, presented in June 2022. He had been working on the concept for "the past two or three years," and he originally called it the **creation hierarchy**: a set of steps starting with a vision and broken down into "big goal, small goal, intention, consistency, curiosity," "and I had all these things there." He knew there was "something more to it," a way to package it simply but with depth, and that packaging became the pyramid. He said he was writing "an entire book on this thing" and that it could not be explained in one graphic or one video (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

The pyramid was shown on screen, and viewers were asked to screenshot it and study it, making connections in their daily experience. From the top down its layers are: (1) **experience** ("the little white part" at the top); (2) **vision**; (3) **goals**; (4) **process**; (5) **drive**; (6) **focus**. "Under that you have your state of consciousness, the energy to create, intrinsic alignment and everything to go along with that." He also mentions "things on the side" of the graphic. He calls the pyramid "the over-encompassing concept" that will "form the foundation of every other video I've posted ever" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

| Layer (top to bottom) | Koe's explanation in the video |
|---|---|
| Experience | What you actually want: the experience that getting something would bring |
| Vision | A "crystal clear" picture of the future, built over time |
| Goals | The vision broken down for clarity; "microvisions" |
| Process | "A process that we can repeatedly execute on in order to actualize that vision"; treated as a science experiment |
| Drive | Your "why"; intrinsic, not extrinsic, drive; focusing on it brings its energy |
| Focus | Where attention is directed; what gives access to each layer's energy; mapped with the "focus matrix" |
| Base | "State of consciousness, the energy to create, intrinsic alignment" |

The transcript does not allow the base and side elements of the graphic to be reconstructed with certainty: it is unclear whether "state of consciousness," "the energy to create" and "intrinsic alignment" are three separate elements or one, and the exact layout of the "things on the side" is not described. The three middle layers, by contrast, are developed at length in the same video, and each is treated elsewhere in this book. The **process** layer is a repeatable process for actualizing the vision, which Koe frames as a science experiment (observe, research, hypothesis, experiment, analyze, result) that demands patience, surrender and faith, because without them the uncertainty of testing sends people back to the predictable job; Chapter 15 develops it. The **drive** layer is intrinsic rather than extrinsic motivation, for which he borrows Steven Kotler's five intrinsic drivers (curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy, mastery), stressing the first three at the start; Chapter 10 develops it. The **focus** layer is the "focus matrix," which crosses deep (narrow, convergent) and wide (open, divergent) focus with unconscious and conscious attention; Chapter 6 develops it. What this section adds is the top layer and the order of the whole.

The top layer is the pyramid's most original element, and Koe argues for it directly. "You don't want that fancy mansion, you don't want that fancy car, you don't want that hot sexy girlfriend, you don't want that big muscular body." He immediately qualifies: "you do want those things, I'm not saying you absolutely don't want those things," but you may be misinterpreting what you actually want, because "there's only one thing that you have and that is your present experience." What you want is the experience that comes from getting them. You have been conditioned your whole life: you see a man with a mansion driving a sports car, adrenaline flowing, and in seeing it you get a bit of that experience; that is what you want. Hence the top of the pyramid is "experience" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). The argument relocates the object of desire from things to states, which has a practical consequence developed in section 8.5: if what one wants is an experience, some of it may be available now, without the object. Koe later qualified the argument from the other side, saying in 2023 that "money doesn't buy happiness" is a half-truth and that material goods have real utility when they are not superficial pursuits; the two positions are compatible if the object is valued as a route to experience and learning rather than as a status signal.

From there the video moves into more speculative language. Visualize your future and the experience that comes with it, as when you imagine a past vacation and can feel its feelings and emotions now: "That's energy." When you bring focus, attention and consciousness to what you want to create, "you can pull energy into the now in order to help you actualize it." Where you bring focus, "there is potential energy there." He states: "everything is energy, everything is information, everything is frequencies, frequencies hold information," and your ability to process and interpret that information positively, "in order to raise your vibration, so to say," lets you bring that experience into the now and live and create a better life (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). This passage is a worldview statement, not an empirical one, and Koe offers no evidence for it; it is best read as a metaphorical description of what focus does to motivation (the vacation example is a familiar experience of imagined emotion), expressed in a vocabulary he borrowed from spiritual and self-help traditions.

The same video contains an explicit reservation about that vocabulary. At its opening Koe says he is "iffy on the concept of like affirmations and manifestation," because it can quickly be misinterpreted, especially "if you aren't congruent with the voice that you are hearing those words from," into law-of-attraction imagining without action. His own reading: what you are really doing is "framing your focus" and "aligning your actions, whether it be consciously, subconsciously or unconsciously, to manifest what you want in your life" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). So in his account, manifestation is not imagining without acting but focus that organizes action. The tension is internal to the video: he distances himself from manifestation in its first minutes and later speaks of energy, frequencies, "raising your vibration" and "manifesting." He does not address the tension. The defensible core, consistent with the rest of his work, is **framing your focus**: attention directed at a desired experience reorganizes perception and action toward it.

Below experience come the layers that the rest of this chapter has already developed. On the second layer, the vision, Koe issues a warning that anticipates his later idea of a "minimum viable vision": when he says "crystal clear," "don't overthink this, don't project," because "this is a process of time; right now you are kind of reaching into the unknown and trying to create something with the information and energy that is there." You are forming something that lets you tap into the experience, and clarity accrues (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). Koe's statements on how clear a vision must be shift over the years: from this "process of time" (2022) through "a very vague idea of a good life... a general aim" (2025) to "a minimum viable vision" and, in the same period of 2026, "it is not a vague wish; it must be as specific as it can possibly be." The two late positions can be reconciled by function: a vague direction is enough to start moving, while specificity is needed to evaluate decisions and tactics (the 2026 statements come from his turn toward strategic thinking, where a strategy requires clarity). On all versions the vision is indispensable as a direction and never finished (Chapter 7).

The third layer, goals, is "breaking it down and trying to get clarity." Goals can also be thought of as an outline: you create them to gain clarity and to make more connections in everyday experience. How far you break the vision down depends on how big or small it is. "You can even think of the goals as microvisions in themselves." On the long life span: a ten-year goal, a one-year goal, a monthly goal, a weekly goal, even a daily goal, "or just priorities, like everything is a goal" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). **Microvisions** is the 2022 name for the idea of section 8.1: each level of the hierarchy is itself a small vision, a picture of a near future, so the same lens effect operates at every scale.

Returning to the "things on the side" of the graphic later in the video, Koe gives the pyramid's practical use. If your focus goes to your vision, you can tap into that energy source; if it goes to your goals ("micro visions, per se"), you tap into their experience; the same holds for process and drive. "Can you focus on your why?" If you remember and focus on your why, it brings the energy and experience associated with it. "You can quite literally manage and manipulate and control your attention to lead and create a better reality for yourself" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). Read without the energy vocabulary, the instruction is that one can deliberately choose which level of the hierarchy to attend to, and that each level produces a different state: the vision produces meaning, the goals produce clarity, the process produces engagement, the why produces motivation. When one level fails (the process feels tedious), attention can move to another (the why). And he gives the pyramid a final role as a recovery tool: "the next time you feel lost in any dimension, whether it be finances, mental health, physical health... relationships... spiritual dimension," refer to the creation pyramid to gain clarity on the life, situation or experience you want to create. He closes by encouraging viewers to share their visions, because "it helps you gain clarity," while insisting that they must act (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

The creation pyramid is the ancestor of the Focus Formula, and comparing them shows what changed. The pyramid starts from experience and ends in focus, and it is phrased largely in the language of energy and consciousness. The Focus Formula, six months later, drops experience and the energy vocabulary, reduces the layers to three, and anchors them in problems and daily actions. The movement is from a contemplative model to an operational one.

#### The 12 rules of creation

In April 2024 Koe presented a larger framework, the **12 rules of creation**, which he described as the product of "a decade of dancing between creative success and failure." He says he built it through multidisciplinary study, pulling principles and patterns from marketing, sales, human behavior, peak performance, psychology, stories, games, the structure of billion-dollar companies, successful product development and anything involved in creating and distributing value. Its intended use is universal: as "the guiding light of your life," to change your life, start a business, build a product, write a tweet, newsletter or landing page, or have a beneficial conversation, "anytime you do absolutely anything, so that you make sure you are creating something and not doing something that's already been done." When you are lost (relationships failing, a business not taking off), "run through all 12 of those rules" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14).

In September 2025 he published a second "12 rules" video with a different list. The two versions can be compared in a table. The 2024 list is given in full in the video; the 2025 list has to be reconstructed from the passages of that video that survive in the corpus, and two of its rules (6 and 7) cannot be identified with confidence from them.

Koe gives no reason for the changes. Several of the 2024 rules have already appeared in this chapter: Rule 3 (mission) and Rule 8 (limitations) in the six-component plan, Rule 5 (goals) in "big goals for direction," Rule 9 (levers) in the daily levers. The rules on standards, projects, education and challenge are developed in other chapters (Chapters 4, 9, 14 and 10 respectively). Three further rules belong here because they concern the relation between direction and energy.

**Rule 2 of 2025, commit to excellence.** Where the 2024 list had "vision," the 2025 list has a commitment. From observing what he did not want, Koe says, "I had to commit to excellence. I had to do something great." His own version had three parts. Becoming an entrepreneur no matter how many failures ("it took seven failures"), so that he could control how long and on what he works. Building "a strong aesthetic and energetic body by making training and nutrition a non-negotiable," becoming his own teacher and personal trainer; he still loves studying how chemicals and nutrients interact with the body, and once you understand, decisions become automatic: knowing the long-term downstream effects, "you just don't want to put it in your mouth no matter how good it tastes. You see it as slop." And nurturing "a strong mind through the progressive overload of uncertainty and emotional labor." These examples may not resonate with everyone, he concedes, but "everyone wants some form of those things" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). Two details carry theory. The nutrition example illustrates the lens effect of knowledge: understanding changes perception so thoroughly that the temptation disappears ("slop"), which is a stronger mechanism than willpower. And **progressive overload of uncertainty and emotional labor** transfers the training principle of the gym (gradually increasing load) to the mind: one strengthens the mind by taking on gradually more uncertainty, the theme of Chapter 10's "living on the edge."

**The code.** Halfway through the 2025 video Koe summarized the rules so far: "So far our frame, the thing that is going to bring enjoyment to our life, the code that we operate by, is composed of anti-vision, then vision, then standards, then projects, then levers that lead to progress away from mediocrity and toward excellence, creating a tight feedback loop that makes life an enjoyable game for the most part." That is the foundation that pushes you deeper into the unknown; the remaining rules address navigating the highs, lows, emotions and uncertainty (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The **code** is the shortest statement of Koe's mature architecture: two poles (anti-vision and vision), a filter (standards), a container for the work (projects) and a daily action (levers), closed into a feedback loop. Compared with the Focus Formula, the code places the anti-vision first and inserts standards (what one is willing to tolerate) between the vision and the work.

**Rule 10 of 2025, engineer enthusiasm.** We often hear that you need to be obsessed with your goal; that is how people describe successful people with an intense spirit. But an obsessive personality "can really hurt your life unless you learn to direct it away from people and toward other things like a skill or an interest or a business." "The real word for this is enthusiasm. That's what we should aim for. That just doesn't feel as cool or edgy as obsession." Koe gives the etymology: the word comes from the Greek *enthousiasmos*, literally "having God within" or "God-inspired," being possessed or inspired by a divine spirit, suggesting that true creative or spiritual power came from beyond the individual self. And he concludes: "The master key to the good life is to fill your average day with that which makes you enthusiastic" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). This rule revises Koe's own vocabulary. "Obsession" appears throughout his earlier work as a positive sign (the six-component plan of 2025 promises that the right hierarchy leaves "no other option but to become obsessed"). Here he keeps the energy and drops the word's edge, naming the risk of an obsessive personality pointed at people, and moves the target from extraordinary days to the "average day." Section 8.7 returns to the idea.

**Complementary context:** the Greek *enthousiasmos* derives from *entheos*, "having a god within" (*en* + *theos*). In ancient usage it described divine inspiration or possession, as in the poet or prophet seized by a god; the modern sense of eager interest is a later weakening.

**Rule 11 of 2024, curiosity.** "Be willing to steer off course and discover new potential, because it is too easy to lock ourselves in a mechanical routine like the one we were trying to escape. Be curious, dive deep into your interests, let few questions go unanswered, avoid getting locked into paradigms and beliefs that narrow your mind on one idolized path. Your vision is like a battery: you must fuel it with experience, education and misdirection" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). The rule is a safeguard built into the framework against the framework. A hierarchy of goals that one follows mechanically becomes another assigned routine, the very thing one set out to escape. **Vision as a battery** says that a vision does not sustain itself; it discharges as it is used and must be recharged, and "misdirection" (detours, wrong turns, experiments that fail) is listed as one of the sources of charge. The image completes the account of the vision begun in Chapter 7: it is not a fixed picture but a store of energy that must be fed with new input, which is why curiosity has a place among rules otherwise about focus.

### 8.4 Constraints, Performance and Vanity

A hierarchy of goals says what to move toward. It does not say what one may give up on the way. Left unbounded, a goal can absorb everything: health, family, sleep, the rest of a life. This section presents the boundary Koe places on goals (the sacrifices one refuses to make), his argument that such boundaries make one more creative rather than less, the deadlines that a balanced life creates, and the way he asks goals and actions to be phrased so that they depend on the person pursuing them.

#### Anti-goals: the sacrifices you refuse to make

In May 2024, presenting "view life as a game" as a step toward self-discipline, Koe described a concept he had become "fascinated by": **anti-goals**. They are "not goals that you don't want to achieve; they are the sacrifices you are not willing to make to achieve a goal." His example: if I want to build a billion-dollar company, what am I not willing to sacrifice? Most people with lofty goals pay no attention to what they sacrifice; they are so focused and specialized that they neglect their health, family and other areas, and then "look back and be like, look at everything I destroyed, rather than look at everything that I built." "If you're smart you understand that you can still achieve that goal and maintain or even build other aspects of your life. You don't have to lose your health... your family in order to build a business; if you think that, then that's what's going to happen." Accepting the constraint is "the creative challenge": "how do I build this billion-dollar company while still being able to maintain those things? That's what creates the game, that's what makes it fun." It takes more time and work, "but what else is there to do?" The alternative is to "let your life burn right in front of you because you didn't have the restraint or discipline to take on more responsibility in a wise way" (**Source:** `It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12). Koe does not attribute the concept to anyone in this transcript.

**Complementary context:** the term "anti-goals" circulated in startup and productivity writing before Koe used it, notably in an essay by Andrew Wilkinson (co-founder of the holding company Tiny) describing the conditions a founder refuses to accept. Koe presents the concept without attribution, and his definition (sacrifices one will not make) is close to that usage.

The definition separates anti-goals from the anti-vision of Chapter 7. The anti-vision is an outcome one wants to avoid (the life one refuses to live). Anti-goals are *means* one refuses to use: they constrain the path, not the destination. The sentence "if you think that, then that's what's going to happen" applies the lens of Chapter 6: the belief that health must be sacrificed shapes the strategy one perceives and therefore the life one builds. And the word "game" is used precisely: a game is made by its rules, and anti-goals are rules; without them, "building a company" is a task, with them it is a challenge that requires skill.

A year later Koe repeated the idea with a sharper practical edge. "One of the most useful things" is to set anti-goals: what are you not willing to sacrifice for success? Your health? If not, sixteen-hour workdays are automatically ruled out. Time with a significant other, intellectual development, social life? People say you can't have it all; for Koe that is "a lack of skill, strategy and effort in other areas." Some see this as limiting (to maintain your health you cannot stare at a screen sixteen hours a day), but it is freeing and becomes your unique advantage, because "creativity thrives within constraints." The health constraint forces you to (1) choose a career or business model that favors it, (2) say no to more commitments, which frees focus, and (3) prioritize quality over quantity. When you cannot extend your hours indefinitely, you make the most of the time you have, yielding more focused and often better results (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). The three consequences show how a single constraint propagates through a plan: it filters options at the top (which business model), at the middle (which commitments) and at the bottom (how each hour is used).

Koe has given the same idea four names, which is why it is easy to miss that they are one concept.

| Name | Date | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-goals | 2024-05; 2025-05 | Self-discipline; working less |
| Limitations | 2024-04 | Rule 8 of the 12 rules |
| Constraints | 2025-01; 2025-12 | Creativity; sixth component of the plan |
| Rules of the game | 2025-05 | Life as a game |
| Anti-standards | 2025-09 | 12 rules, 2025 version |

The last renaming is the only one he explains. In the 2025 version of the 12 rules, after presenting standards, he says "let's just call them anti-standards for the sake of cohesiveness": "what you are not willing to do or sacrifice to achieve your goals." The effect, he says, is to narrow the frame further, so that you cannot get distracted, can get into flow and enjoy life. His own case: one goal among many is building a business, "but I am not willing to sacrifice my health like most people do, or my relationships like most people do." Most people think that is natural ("you have to work long hours"). "No, you don't." "Setting constraints is how you actually get creative results": refusing to sacrifice health, relationships or work hours forces creative thinking, your own insights and conclusions, and a real understanding of your goals (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The phrase "a real understanding of your goals" is the important addition: a constraint forces one to understand what the goal actually requires, because the easy, brute-force path is closed.

In January 2025 Koe explained why constraints breed creativity with an example from writing. With absolute freedom you stare at a blank page and do not act. Writing with a topic, a goal, a story, quotes to include, a framework: that boundary breeds creativity. Constraints, applied to life, are the sacrifices you won't make: "I want to build this business, but I don't want to sacrifice family time." The person who just wants the business need not be creative in the way the constrained person must; the constrained person pays far more attention to how to structure days, when to take meetings, when to stop working (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12). The blank page is the general case: freedom without constraint produces paralysis, because nothing narrows the field of possible actions.

#### Creativity requires a goal and a limit

The claim that constraints breed creativity rests on a more general principle that Koe states in his writing videos. "You can only be creative when you have a goal, something to be creative towards, and limitations that force creativity," like a framework. His example returns to the business: building a business in four hours a day without sacrificing health and relationships demands more creativity than building one by giving up everything else. When people say something is impossible, it is because they are not creative enough to achieve it, "of course some things are impossible, but you get what I mean" (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08). The hedge is worth preserving: the principle is a heuristic for rejecting false impossibilities, not a claim that constraint can overcome anything.

The two requirements (a goal and a limit) are the two sides of this chapter. The goal gives creativity a direction; the limit gives it a shape. Without a goal, constraints are mere restrictions; without constraints, the goal can be reached by brute force, and brute force needs no creativity.

In 2025 Koe addressed creative people who resist this. "Creative people are allergic to frameworks for no good reason. They think that frameworks ruin their authenticity or their freedom... but creativity thrives within constraints, specifically constraints that you set: agency, autonomy." Assigned constraints (someone else's project, client work) are not fun "unless you have a shared vision and a long-term stake," but "when you're building your own projects and you set the constraints, it's like the most fun thing to do in the world." "You can't think outside of the box if there is no box. If you don't have constraints, how can you be creative within that thing?" His recommendation: look up copywriting, writing and speech frameworks, study them and try them (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19). The distinction between self-set and assigned constraints mirrors the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic hierarchies in section 8.1. The same structure (a goal with limits) is enjoyable or oppressive depending on who set it, and the exception Koe names (a shared vision with a long-term stake) is the case in which an assigned constraint becomes partly one's own. Chapter 18 develops the role of frameworks in originality.

#### A responsible life has deadlines built in

Anti-goals have a further effect that Koe describes in his 2025 critique of hustle culture: they create deadlines. If you truly value intellect, physique, connection to others and reality, and a standard of health enveloping all of them, you cannot sustain sixteen hours of work. Those who do so day in, day out "expose" that they do not value those things, and you should question your trust in their methods. His own examples:

- **The gym.** His performance diminishes if he trains at 4 or 5 p.m.; he trains best around 11 a.m. to noon. Valuing the gym therefore gives him a real deadline: finish the morning's work before then. (He can work afterward; it depends on how one structures the day.)
- **The relationship.** He has felt that, after a stressful full workday, lying in bed thinking about work is bad for him and for his partner. So he needs a buffer period, which is another deadline: if he does not stop now, the night is ruined, and over years, the relationship.

This is "an upgrade, not a downgrade": deadlines narrow the mind and create urgency, "rocket fuel for deep work." Striving to be "multi-dimensionally jacked" (developed in body, mind, relationships and work at once; Chapter 40) as a standard creates morning urgency to complete tasks with the creative fuel of the previous day's rest. Results, he adds, take a few weeks of adaptation. His summary: "a responsible life has deadlines built in" (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04).

The argument inverts the usual view of balance. Commitments outside work are normally seen as costs to productivity. In Koe's account they are causes of productivity, because they supply the deadline that work itself cannot supply. A workday with no external boundary expands to fill all available time (the dynamic usually called Parkinson's law, which Chapter 12 discusses), while a workday that must end at 11 a.m. for training forces the mind to concentrate.

The same month he stated the converse. "If you don't value your health or your relationships, and you don't have priority tasks that you do for those things throughout your day, if you don't have good habits, then your productivity will suffer, because you don't have a reason to stop working. You don't have a deadline that kicks you into flow." And of the people who claim the opposite: "Most hustlers that brag about working 16 to 20 hours a day, it's just all distracted work and they don't even know it. And they'll defend themselves into the end of the world because it's their religion. It's their culture. It's the only thing that they know. It's their badge of honor. It's their status symbol to work so long and get so little done, but feel like they're making progress," without knowing how much more progress a different route would bring (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). The claim that most such hours are "distracted work" is an opinion based on observation, not a measurement. Its explanatory part is the link to identity: long hours persist not because they work but because they have become a **badge of honor** and a status symbol, and identities are defended (Chapter 3).

Koe is not entirely consistent here. In other passages from late 2024 and early 2025 he expressed respect for people who genuinely work very long hours as part of who they are (Alex Hormozi working twelve hours well because it is his identity; "there is no right way to work"). The two registers can coexist: a general critique of hustle as a status display, and respect for an individual choice that is genuinely one's own. He does not draw that distinction explicitly.

He also describes how he turns a deadline into a transition. "At the end of my work sessions I go to the gym; that is my hard transition from work to rest, and when I get back to my morning focused work my mind is just refreshed and the writing flows so much more seamlessly, and the rest of my work feels manageable, and I can get it done on a consistent basis that leads to exponential results over time when the focus does compound" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). A **hard transition** is an activity that marks the end of work unambiguously, so that the mind does not carry work into rest. The gym serves three purposes at once in Koe's day: it is a valued domain (health), a deadline (the work must end before it), and the transition (it clears the mind for the next morning). The claim that this produces "exponential results" through compounding focus is his interpretation of his own trajectory. (Koe also uses "hard reset" for a different practice, a thirty-minute procedure to reorder one's life; the two should not be confused.)

#### Performance versus vanity

The last boundary concerns how a goal or an action is phrased. It entered the corpus as the third step of the Focus Formula, the priority step. "The best way to frame your actions, whether it be on a daily or even a higher goal like a monthly goal, is performance versus vanity," favoring performance. For a beginner, trying to gain fifty followers a day is "iffy," "not guaranteed," and "you don't have control over it," so "your mind is much more likely to become disordered and overwhelmed and anxious about actually taking action." Instead: write a thousand words a day (aligned with what you are actually doing), or three high-quality tweets a day, or one Instagram post a day plus twenty replies under an account to bring followers to your page (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

| Framing | Example | Controllable? | Effect on the mind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanity | 50 followers a day | No: depends on others and on algorithms | Disorder, overwhelm, anxiety about acting |
| Performance | 1,000 words a day; 3 quality tweets a day; 1 post + 20 replies a day | Yes | A clear, completable task |

The distinction is between outcomes that depend on the world and actions that depend on oneself. The argument for performance is not moral but psychological: an uncontrollable target disorders the mind (psychic entropy, Chapter 5), because each day's result is uncertain and each failure feels like a verdict. A controllable target can be completed every day, which produces the feedback loop of quantified tasks (section 8.1). The performance examples are not arbitrary either: the twenty replies are chosen because they plausibly bring followers, so performance targets are the levers that, over time, move the vanity numbers. The vanity metric is not ignored; it is simply not used as the daily target.

Koe tells a story from his own life that shows the distinction at work on the experience of an activity. In 2022 his current projects were a new writing product and "getting back into the gym." He had been going to the gym the whole time, but it was not as fulfilling. Becoming aware of the dynamic between goals and flow, he set a new goal "based on performance rather than vanity." Looks are important, he says, but he wants to progress on his lifts "simply because it's just something I want to do." That makes him happy and "pumped"; it becomes "kind of ritualistic to get into the flow, get in the car, go to the gym and then just lift a bunch of heavy" weight (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28; repeated in `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). The case illustrates something the follower example does not. The activity (lifting) stayed the same; only the goal changed, from an aesthetic result to a self-chosen measure of progress, and with it the experience changed from routine to ritual and flow. It is the hierarchy-of-goals principle at the smallest scale: the goal decides not only what one does but what doing it feels like.

### 8.5 Bringing the Ideal Future into the Now

The hierarchy of goals runs from the future to the present: a vision decades away, broken down until it reaches today's levers. This section presents a complementary movement that runs in the other direction. Instead of only working *toward* the ideal future, Koe asks people to bring a piece of it *into* the present: to do now, at a small scale, what they would do if they already lived the life they want.

#### The idea and its first example

"One of the most powerful ideas I've been thinking about recently is **bringing your ideal future into the now**," Koe said in March 2023, in a passage he repeated verbatim in a 2024 compilation. It means "performing the same actions that you would in your ideal lifestyle, but on a much smaller scale." His example is his own writing. He wants to write two hours every morning for the rest of his life (which, he notes, he already does). What he did in the past was start writing five minutes a day, then fifteen, then thirty, then an hour, "slowly increasing over the years until I can sustain the two hour a day writing lifestyle." The reasoning: "it doesn't make sense to wait and put off what you're already going to be doing," since those actions contribute directly to your life's work; and if you are not doing them already, it will be much harder when you finally decide to start, because you will have to start small and taper up anyway (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The argument has a simple structure. The ideal life consists partly of activities (writing, training, building), not only of possessions or circumstances. Those activities have to be learned and scaled up gradually in any case. So postponing them until the circumstances are right does not save effort; it only delays the start of an unavoidable ramp. The verb Koe uses, **taper up**, describes the ramp: the activity is the same at every stage, only its scale grows.

#### Two daily practices

A month earlier, in a video on networking, Koe gave the same idea a social application. "Bringing your ideal future, the actions you're going to be taking then anyways, into the now" is something you should be doing. If you want to be a writer and are not writing ten or twenty minutes or an hour a day, start, and scale up as it becomes sustainable. And if you will have to message people anyway once you build your own thing, do it now: start by direct-messaging two people a day whose content you like, then five, then ten. "I promise that alone will just change everything" (**Source:** `The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md`, 2023-02-12). The example matters because it shows that the principle applies to uncomfortable activities as well as enjoyable ones. Reaching out to strangers is not usually part of anyone's fantasy of an ideal life, but it is part of the life of someone who runs their own business, so it belongs in the present at a small scale. (Chapter 26 develops the networking process.)

In 2024 he reduced the principle to a daily minimum: "Every day set aside one hour to build a project that actualizes your ideal future. That is the only way to escape building projects for someone else" (**Source:** `Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md`, 2024-03-31; the transcript leaves it ambiguous whether this sentence is Koe's own or part of a quotation he was reading). The second sentence ties the practice to the assigned-structure argument of section 8.3: the alternative to building your own project for an hour is not rest but building someone else's.

#### Error correction and the evolving work of art

In 2025 Koe added a reason that connects the principle to the cybernetic model of Chapter 6. "The best way to create your ideal life is to live it right now but on a smaller scale." If you want to be a writer and are not writing, you never will; start with even thirty minutes and slowly titrate up. Without writing, designing, creating or coding, you are not in the process of **error correction**: you are not encountering the real-world feedback that makes you better. Waiting until you are "more knowledgeable" only delays the point at which you will be good enough to do it full-time. And to work less (the context of the video), every decision must be filtered through your ideal lifestyle. But that image, he adds, is not a static target: it is "an evolving work of art," built from self-reflection on past experiences you do not want to relive, and from gradual trial and error toward future experiences you think you want, until those become past experiences you can see clearly (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04).

The 2025 version adds two things to the 2023 one. First, the reason is no longer only efficiency (don't delay an inevitable ramp) but learning: practising now is what generates the feedback that makes one competent, and competence cannot be acquired by waiting. Second, the ideal itself is revised by the practice. The image of the ideal lifestyle is built from both directions: from the past (what one has lived and does not want again, the material of the anti-vision) and from the future (what one tries and finds one wants). Bringing the future into the now is thus also a way of *testing* the future. Some pieces of the imagined life will not survive contact with reality, and that discovery is part of the value.

#### Life itself is a practice

Koe's most general statement of the principle comes from a 2023 video on reinventing one's life. Most people fail to make tangible progress because they do not realize that "a lifelong habit of education, skill acquisition and practice is how you build a better life. This is not optional." Without time carved out each day to practise your craft, you will not get anywhere meaningful. "Life itself is a practice, and most people aren't showing up"; they distract themselves with mindless entertainment. "You don't have your ideal lifestyle because you aren't living that lifestyle right now, but on a smaller scale." As you get better, the time you spend increases, the impact grows in less time, "and money will become inevitable." His example: a designer or writer designs or writes for an hour every morning; as they improve, that hour's work takes twenty minutes, so they do more and better work and can incorporate other things, such as building a business, slowly increasing the time until it becomes their lifestyle. The same holds for the gym and every healthy habit. Successful people "didn't just poof land into this lifestyle after they had a successful business; it was their lifestyle that allowed them to build the business" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25).

The last sentence reverses a common causal assumption. People who admire a successful person's lifestyle (early writing, daily training, deep work) tend to assume it is the reward of success. Koe argues that it was its cause: the lifestyle was the practice through which the business was built. The claim "money will become inevitable" is an expression of confidence, not a demonstrated law; the defensible part of the argument is the compounding mechanism (the same hour produces more as skill grows, freeing time for the next layer).

The clearest statement of what "ideal lifestyle" means in this context comes from Koe's 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, co-founder of the writing program Ship 30 for 30. Asked about retiring at sixty-five, Koe says he is living his ideal lifestyle now and would rather do what he does every day than "something fun." "Right now I would hope that I'm doing the thing that I'm going to do when I'm 65; if I'm not, why am I doing it?... If I want to become a writer, why am I not writing every day right now?" "You're only increasing the scale at which you do it": as a beginner without time or resources, "it's going to be 30 minutes in the morning regardless of whether you're getting paid or not." Some people wait until they can get paid, and "you're missing a crucial piece... you have to start before you can learn or before you can get paid... you have to live your ideal lifestyle right now, or at least seek to create it, so that you can sustain it." Bush, for his part, says he sees himself sustaining a two-hour writing habit at sixty-five (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

The **sixty-five test** is a practical way to apply the principle: imagine what you would want to be doing every day at retirement age, and ask why you are not doing a small version of it now. It also exposes the logic of the default path (Chapter 2), in which the activities one enjoys are deferred to retirement and the decades before it are spent on activities one does not. Koe's alternative is not to retire early but to make the present and the retirement contain the same activities at different scales.

#### The digital nomad trap

The principle has a corollary that Koe illustrates with his own experience: many of the lifestyles people imagine as ideal turn out not to be. In 2022 (and again in the 2024 "full guide" version of the same video) he said he had also fallen into the trap of wanting to live other people's lives: beachfront views, sand in his toes, a tan "like David Hasselhoff," sipping coconuts on the beach while working from a laptop, until he experienced it. He does not want to trash digital nomad culture ("I think it's great"), but working on the beach gets old quickly. You cannot see your screen because of the sun's glare, it gets too hot, you have to reapply sunscreen, you get thirsty, and you would rather be inside working. The Wi-Fi in those places gets old, the partying gets old, the language barrier gets old, and the social-media "highlight reel" version of it gets old. The lesson: you go through trial and error to understand what you want; "you have to figure out what you don't want in order to figure out what you want" (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md`, 2022-10-23; `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

The story is an anecdote, offered as such, and its lesson is the subtractive method of Chapter 7 ("you will never know exactly what you want"): experience eliminates options that imagination could not. In the 2022 video Koe draws a general conclusion from it. The glamour of the lifestyles people promote "is dopamine": you see something you want, and once you achieve it, if you do not appreciate it and make it part of your life, it fades very quickly. Because the glamorous things are not truly sustainable, the purpose of the video is moderation and sustainability: to present a business model for creating your ideal lifestyle through practice, experimentation and iteration, a creative income source built on your skills, interests and expertise, and not limited to people who already have them (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md`, 2022-10-23).

The analysis connects the trap to the dopamine model of Chapter 11. A glamorous lifestyle seen from outside is a cue for anticipation, and anticipation is what dopamine tracks; once the lifestyle is reached, the anticipation is gone, and unless the new circumstance is appreciated and integrated, it stops producing satisfaction. The remedy (moderation and sustainability) is also the criterion for an ideal lifestyle worth bringing into the now: it should be something one could do every day for decades, which is the sixty-five test again.

In the same conversation with Koe, Dickie Bush offered a related observation from his own experience. Very nice dinners cost a couple of hundred dollars if you are not drinking thousand-dollar bottles of wine. (He mentions that he has gone fifteen months without alcohol; before that, he says, he had probably not gone more than a week without it in the ten years since early high school, which he calls "extremely normalized.") A solo dinner at a top steakhouse, trying everything, cost about $300, which would have seemed absurd to him two or three years earlier. "There are a lot of things that are not that far out of reach when you reach a certain level," and not much changes for a long time after that: you can travel, fly first class and stay in nice hotels, but "you need 100 million dollars plus to have a private plane or a yacht," and to reach that "you have to go take shots." His realization: "the things I really really like do not cost an absurd amount of money," whereas at the beginning "you're like, oh, I need this, this, this." Koe adds that he himself prefers cheap thin steaks or shredded chicken to fatty steak (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

The observation is Bush's, not Koe's, and it rests on one person's experience at a particular level of income. Its relevance to this section is that it lowers the bar of the ideal lifestyle. If what one really enjoys costs much less than the glamorous image suggests, then much of the ideal lifestyle is reachable early, and most of the rest is a matter of time and practice rather than of enormous wealth, which strengthens the case for living it now at a small scale.

#### A decade-long time scale

The principle implies a long horizon, and Koe states it explicitly. "Think of it on, like, a decade-long time scale; this isn't for the quick fix people." That, he says, is why his videos are long and sometimes rambling: people who get value from them will not click away "simply because I didn't stroke their little ego or pleasure reward center for five minutes." He adds a disclaimer that is useful to keep in mind throughout this book: he is not "an almighty guru that knows everything" giving 100% accurate advice; what he offers is his level of understanding and what he is working toward (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22).

The decade horizon and the small daily scale are two halves of the same idea. Five minutes of writing a day is insignificant on a horizon of weeks and decisive on a horizon of ten years, because it is the beginning of a ramp. Section 8.7 returns to the question of how long one must persist before judging a path, and finds that Koe's numbers vary considerably.

### 8.6 Purpose Is the Most Pressing Problem

Every framework in this chapter has a slot labelled "purpose" or "vision" at its top, and so far the chapter has treated that slot as given (Chapter 7 explained how to write a first vision). This section addresses the question that most people find hardest: what if one does not know one's purpose? Koe's answer is a redefinition. Purpose is not a calling to be discovered but a problem to be faced, and the one to face first is the most pressing problem in one's life right now.

#### The redefinition

Koe introduced the redefinition in January 2023, in the same video as the Focus Formula, and repeated it in 2024. He created it, he says, because what books say about purpose "doesn't really make sense": you understand what a purpose is, but you do not know how to find one. His simplification has two parts. First, "there is not only one purpose... there are lesser and greater purposes, and you have to achieve one purpose in order to unlock the next." Second, "a purpose is a goal, and a goal introduces a problem, and so your purpose is the most pressing problem in your life right now." If you have done nothing with your life, "your purpose is not to spread love and be selfless," because "if you haven't self-developed and self-transcended, then it's impossible to be truly absolutely selfless." The question to ask is: "what is your purpose right now? What is the problem or the pain that you have been avoiding confronting?" (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

His example is deliberately unglamorous. If you are overweight and lack energy (you cannot walk downstairs comfortably, keep up with your friends or work certain jobs that would let you earn more), that is your purpose. Laser all your attention on it: it is your **North Star**, and all your actions (what you eat, whether you walk or sit on the couch) align with it (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). This is the second of the two senses of "North Star" distinguished in section 7.1: here it names the purpose of the moment, a near problem to be solved, whereas in the 2025 passage of section 8.3 it named the distant vision, a reference "not a destination one expects to reach soon." The two uses are compatible only in function (both orient attention), not in distance, and the reader should keep them apart.

**Purpose** is thus a common word with a defined sense in Koe's work. The chain "a purpose is a goal, a goal introduces a problem" is the goal–path–problem principle of section 8.2 applied to the largest question. It turns the search for purpose into something tractable: one does not need to know one's ultimate calling, only one's most pressing current problem, which almost everyone can name if they are honest. The redefinition also removes the shame of having a "small" purpose. A weight problem, a debt or a job one hates is not a distraction from one's purpose; at this stage it *is* one's purpose.

The phrase about selflessness is the most provocative part, and it rests on a developmental claim. Selflessness, in Koe's view, presupposes a developed self: one cannot transcend what one has not built. A person who has not solved their own basic problems and who adopts "spreading love" as a purpose is, on this account, either borrowing an assigned purpose or avoiding the pain of their actual problems. Chapter 9 develops the claim that one cannot skip levels.

In the summary of *The Art of Focus* (2024) Koe gave the definition its book form. "Your purpose is the most pressing problem in your life right now, the one you've been putting off solving." And from the book: "Your life's purpose is the big problem you want to solve in the world, but most people don't have the personal, professional or social development to pursue that. Purpose evolves from superficial to metaphysical, and you cannot skip steps." Before solving big problems in the world, solve the big problems in your life: health, wealth and relationships are where burning problems exist. Fixing them opens room for curiosity, creativity and consciousness, because your mind is no longer plagued with the stress of survival (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). Earlier in the same summary: "Start superficial so you can stop living in a narrow-minded state of stress: fix your health, fix your money problems, fix your relationships, and then dive into the depths of your ever-evolving purpose." Most people do not find their purpose because they do not see it "staring them in the face": they must solve the burning problems that prevent their mind from expanding beyond surface-level living. "You do not have one purpose; you move from lesser to greater purposes with time, effort and evolution of who you are" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The book version distinguishes two levels with two names: *purpose* (the most pressing problem in your life now) and *life's purpose* (the big problem you want to solve in the world). The second is real, but it is reached through the first. The mechanism is the narrowing effect of stress described in Chapter 6: survival problems keep attention zoomed in, and only when they are solved does attention become free to zoom out toward curiosity and contribution. That is why the superficial problems come first: not because they matter more, but because they block the view of the deeper ones.

In 2023 Koe turned the definition into a criterion for choosing one's first project. Pick the project that solves the most prevalent problem in your life: "this is your purpose; if you're trying to find your purpose, here it is." Sit with it, contemplate it, create a plan of attack. His questions: are you overweight, broke, stressed all the time, hating your job, lacking energy? "What are you sweeping under the rug so you don't have to face the pain that comes with growth?" "If solved, what problem would unlock the next level of enjoyment in your life?" Then deconstruct that goal into actions to incorporate into each focused hour (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11). The last question is the most useful one: it selects among several problems by asking which one, once solved, would open the next level, which is the logic of "unlocking" the next purpose.

#### The burning problem and the problem-solver mindset

In March 2023 Koe framed the same idea as a mindset. To stop operating within the known and start making progress, adopt the mindset of a problem solver: "that's what every single successful person does." If you study the patterns of successful people, he says, you will see that they are obsessed with solving the problems in their lives, "because the problems are the gate to becoming exposed to the potentials of your life." Where to start? With the biggest problem in your life, the one you are putting off because you think you should be working on your passion, or on something that will make you "make a leap in consciousness and progress" without taking the steps to get there. If you have never done this, start with the shallow problems. Be honest and find "the burning problem": Are you overweight? Do you hate your job? Are you tired all the time? Did you just go through a breakup? Are you plagued with negative emotions? Sit with the discomfort; do not avoid it. And once you have found the problem, the goal is its "polar end" (section 8.2), but you cannot go straight into solving it; you must first learn how (**Source:** `Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md`, 2023-03-30).

The **burning problem** is Koe's name for the problem that is most urgent and most avoided. The two criteria often coincide, because the most urgent problems are painful to face, and the pain is what keeps them under the rug. The instruction to "sit with the discomfort" connects to what he elsewhere calls marinating in the problem: letting oneself feel how the problem spreads through one's life until one is fed up, and using that energy as fuel. The claim about "every single successful person" is a generalization from his observation, not a study; its practical content is that problems are not obstacles on the way to one's potential but the doors through which it is reached.

#### Concentric circles of purpose

In May 2023 Koe gave the progression from lesser to greater purposes a spatial image. Step one of recreating yourself is to pursue a goal that is challenging enough. Start small and superficial: fix your health, your finances, your mindset. "One foot into the unknown, take the first step": the only thing nobody else can do for you ("I can't grab your foot and step it out there for you"). Put one foot in the unknown "so that you can feel the stress required to continue." This gives you "a lesser purpose to actualize." People who attack their core first are trying to solve their life's purpose "without breaking through the concentric circles inward, from lesser purpose to greater purpose to life's purpose." When you cannot even see your life's purpose, you have no clue where to begin, so you start with the lesser purpose, "which is just a superficial goal" (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19).

The **concentric circles of purpose** run from the outside in: lesser purpose (a superficial goal) at the outer ring, greater purpose in the middle, life's purpose at the core. The image explains why the core cannot be reached directly: one has to break through the outer rings. The transcript does not define the greater purpose further; it is the intermediate ring, and the image implies only that it lies between a superficial goal and a life's work. Chapter 9 presents Koe's later and more articulated version of these levels (survival, status, creativity, contribution). The phrase "feel the stress required to continue" is also significant: a challenging-enough goal is chosen partly because its stress is what keeps one going, which anticipates the challenge/skill balance of Chapter 10.

#### Two formulations: "not to spread love" and "a positive impact on others"

In July 2024 Koe gave a definition of purpose that seems to contradict the one above. Free people "are expected to learn many things throughout their life, because goals aren't static, they change, they evolve as you do, as you learn and you gain experience." "It doesn't make sense to have one goal, or a hierarchy of goals, of go to school, get a job, retire at 65 since the day you're born; to have that goal is just weird, because that's not how life works." And: "Hopefully, a purpose is a goal that has a positive impact on others." "Most people don't choose their own goals; they are programmed at a young age to have an employee mindset, to do what they're told and only learn in that narrow domain" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28).

| Formulation | Date | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| "Your purpose is the most pressing problem in your life right now... not to spread love and be selfless" | 2023-01; 2024-01; 2024-03 | One's own problems first |
| "Hopefully a purpose is a goal that has a positive impact on others" | 2024-07 | Contribution |

Koe does not retract either formulation, and the "hopefully" in the second is a signal that he is describing a direction rather than a requirement. The developmental model reconciles them: one's own problems come first, contribution comes later, and "you cannot skip steps." A passage from February 2024 shows how he connects the two levels in a single definition. "Our purpose is to solve the problems that lead to the dissolution of barriers in our own minds, so that we can pass those lessons down and solve problems in other people's lives to remove the limits from their minds. That's what a problem is: it is a limit on your mind and potential, and once it is solved you increase your level of mind, or level of consciousness, so that you can navigate the world better and lead to a better humanity." Done individually across the globe, one vessel for this is "a personal brand, the creator economy, writing, putting your word out there to help other people, selling a product so you earn a meaningful income... by solving your own problems and selling the solution" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18).

This passage contains Koe's definition of **problem**: "a limit on your mind and potential." The definition explains why solving one's own problems is not selfish in his scheme. Each problem solved raises one's "level of mind" (the developmental level of Chapters 38 and 39), and what was learned in solving it can be passed on to someone facing the same limit. The sequence is: solve your own problem, rise a level, teach the solution, help others rise. It is the bridge between the self-focused purpose and the other-focused one, and it is also the seed of Koe's business model ("solve your own problems and sell the solution"), which Chapters 19 and 28 develop.

In 2026 Koe gave this sequence a name, citing his free book *Purpose and Profit*: the **creative way of life**. Identify problems, solve them (which removes the limits on your potential and forces you to learn), and then turn the solution into something you can give someone else. It is "arguably the most meaningful way to live," both an advantage for the future and deeply meaningful (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md`, 2026-02-12). The creative way of life is the definition of purpose turned into a way of living: an endless cycle of problems, solutions and gifts.

#### A problem creates a goal

In section 8.2 the procedure for getting unstuck began with "set a meaningful goal" (2022). In March 2024 Koe reversed that order, in a video for people whose lives feel out of control. His argument is a chain: "You feel lost because you don't know what to do next"; "you don't know what to do next because you don't have a goal"; "you don't have a goal that you care about because you aren't aware of a problem that demands your focus." Therefore: "stop worrying about setting goals until you become aware of the next problem in your life that you want to solve. A problem creates a goal. Stop focusing on the goal." The mechanism: "if the problem isn't strong enough or apparent enough or aware enough to add gravity to the goal that makes you actually want to pursue it, then it's kind of useless to set the goal, and you end up like the 99% of other people that never achieve their goals" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10).

**A problem creates a goal** is one of Koe's most important formulations, and it explains a common experience: goals that are set but never pursued. A goal derived from no felt problem has no gravity (section 8.1); it competes with distractions on equal terms and loses. A goal that is the "polar end" of a problem one feels every day inherits the problem's pressure. The practical instruction follows: before setting goals, find the problem.

The change from 2022 to 2024 is a real revision, and Koe's positions on it oscillate over a few months.

| Date | Position |
|---|---|
| 2022-10 | Step one out of feeling lost: "set a meaningful goal" |
| 2023-01 | Purpose is the most pressing problem (problem first) |
| 2024-03 | "Stop focusing on the goal": a problem creates a goal; without a felt problem the goal has no gravity |
| 2024-03 (same video) | Goals "don't start out magnetic"; interest is built by investing energy |
| 2024-05 | If the mind focuses only on the problem, you dig deeper into it; focus on the goal to activate pattern recognition; "step one: just have a goal" (a minimum viable goal) |

He gives a reason only for the March 2024 position (without a sufficiently conscious problem, the goal is useless). The positions can be read as applying to different situations: awareness of a problem is what is needed to *choose* a goal (for someone who perceives no problem worth solving), while focus on the goal is what is needed to *solve* the problem once one is moving (because dwelling on a problem amplifies it, as Chapter 6 argues with the lens). That reading reconciles the sequence, but the corpus presents the positions as successive advice rather than as a reconciled model.

The same March 2024 video gives the full cycle in a single sentence: "To get what you want out of life: pursue what you think you want, realize what you don't want, become aware of that problem, sit with it, study it, dissect it, expand your mind to beyond it, solve the problem to evolve one level, repeat the process until you reach the essence of life itself" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10).

The sentence is a procedure, and its steps can be laid out:

1. **Pursue** what you think you want (begin with an imperfect goal, as in Chapter 7).
2. **Realize** what you don't want (experience produces negative information).
3. **Become aware** of that problem (name it).
4. **Sit with** it (do not avoid the discomfort).
5. **Study** and **dissect** it (understand its parts and causes).
6. **Expand your mind** beyond it (acquire the perspective in which it is solvable).
7. **Solve** it, and **evolve one level**.
8. **Repeat**, "until you reach the essence of life itself."

The procedure begins with a goal and ends with a problem solved, so it contains both of the positions in the table above: a goal starts the movement, but it is the problem discovered along the way that drives the evolution. **Evolve one level** names the result of each loop. And "the essence of life itself" is the horizon of the process, which Koe leaves undefined here.

A few minutes earlier in the same video he explains why people get stuck. "You're not getting results because you don't have the skill required to get those results; you don't know what's possible because you haven't exposed yourself to the perspective that makes it possible." "What you want right now may be completely different tomorrow, or next month, or next year; nothing lasts forever." "Meaning is found in endless problem solving until you drill so deep you find what you're looking for" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). The two deficits (skill and perspective) correspond to steps five and six of the procedure, and the sentence on meaning locates it in the process rather than in any final answer.

#### An endless string of problems

If purpose is a problem, then a solved purpose leaves one without a purpose, unless a new problem appears. Koe's view is that it always does. In January 2023: "Problems don't ever go away, they just get better and they get more fun to solve." All we ever do is solve problems or avoid them, and avoidance is the cause of pain. There are three modes: leaning into the battle, the conflict, the problem; sitting back "marinating" in it, not moving; and, aside from those, being in the present moment, meditating, practising, "getting ready for that next round of heat, which is problem solving" (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15).

| Mode | What one is doing | Koe's evaluation (as implied) |
|---|---|---|
| Leaning in | Engaging the battle, the conflict, the problem | Progress |
| Marinating | Sitting in the problem without moving | Can become avoidance; also the stage where one gets "fed up" |
| Preparing | Present moment, meditation, practice | Readiness for "the next round of heat" |

The three modes describe a rhythm rather than a ranking: engagement, a pause that can turn either into resolve or into avoidance, and preparation for the next problem. Koe nuances the claim elsewhere, saying in 2023 that problems are only problems if you interpret them as such from a narrow perspective, which relativizes the discomfort (Chapter 6, on zooming out); by 2025–2026, writing about thinking, he moves toward training the mind to think "problem, problem, problem," where problems are the starting point of direction. The two views apply at different points: zooming out relativizes mere discomfort, and a problem-first mindset turns real problems into direction.

In March 2024 he gave the endlessness of problems an image: "Problems never stop, there will never be a day when problems go away, there will always be another. Don't stress, don't react, don't expect it to be any other way. The joy of life is to train your creative ability and swim, not drown, in this beautiful black hole of problems" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). The **black hole of problems** is meant to be beautiful, not frightening: it is infinite, but it is also the medium in which one's creative ability is trained. Swimming rather than drowning is a matter of skill, and the skill grows with each problem solved.

Two months later he connected endless problems to mental growth. "Life is an endless string of problems. Problems are the polar end of purpose; one does not exist without the other." "When you stop solving new problems, your mind stops expanding." "With each problem you solve you reach a new level of mind, because you have to zoom out into the unknown, collect resources and use the creative ability of your mind to reach the next stage." This, he says, is a key lesson of *The Art of Focus*, "a principle in every single chapter of that book" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). Here the polarity of section 8.2 is stated from the other side: earlier the goal was "the polar end of the problem"; now problems are "the polar end of purpose." The relation is symmetrical, which is why "one does not exist without the other." The sentence "when you stop solving new problems, your mind stops expanding" links the theme to the entropy of Chapter 5: there is no staying the same, and a mind without new problems contracts.

In September 2025 he gave the claim a near-axiomatic form. "We know two things. One, problems are infinite, and two, problems are soluble." No matter how developed we become, there will always be a new problem, "because evolution creates more complexity." His example is AI, which gives access to more information: "that's not better, to an extent, that's more chaotic, that introduces more problems," and those problems create jobs or entrepreneurial opportunities for further solutions that build on the current ones, "forever and ever until the sun dies out, unless we solve that problem" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). In this video Koe does not name a source, but a month earlier he had attributed the same idea to David Deutsch (below).

**Complementary context:** David Deutsch is a British physicist known for his work on quantum computation and for *The Beginning of Infinity* (2011), a book on explanation and progress. Two of its central slogans are "problems are inevitable" and "problems are soluble": there will always be problems, because knowledge is always incomplete, and every problem that does not violate the laws of physics can in principle be solved with the right knowledge. Koe's "problems are infinite and problems are soluble" is a close paraphrase.

#### Chains of problems: lifestyle design

How do endless problems show up in practice? As chains, in which solving one problem reveals or requires the next. In 2022 Koe defined **lifestyle design** in these terms: "being able to design a life of your own doing and being able to solve your problems." Problems chain: "here's a problem, but I'm unable to solve it because I don't have enough time, because I'm in a job that doesn't allow much autonomy." That stems another problem, whose solutions are to search for another job, increase your skill set, start a business, or do something else that allows the first problem to be solved, "until you have full control and freedom over your life" (**Source:** `Why You Can’t Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02). Lifestyle design is a common phrase that Koe uses in this defined sense: not the aesthetic arrangement of a pleasant life but the successive removal of the obstacles that prevent one from solving one's own problems.

In January 2023 he gave the same logic as a heuristic. Once you have a rough vision, identify the problems standing in the way and solve them in chain: "if you can't because you have a job, then you need to start a business; if you don't have time to start a business, then you need to get a better job, and so on and so forth." It will take a lot of time. And it leads to his claim that "all of your success boils down to endlessly solving the right problems" (**Source:** `How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md`, 2023-01-12). The example is useful because it does not move in a straight line: a business is the solution to a restrictive job, but if starting a business is impossible for lack of time, the next step is a *better job*, which looks like a step back from the vision and is in fact the next link in the chain.

A third version, from the same month (repeated in 2024), shows how a mistake starts a chain. You create a vision for your future by reflecting and piecing together "the puzzle" from past mistakes and from what went well. From mistakes you learn what not to repeat and move in a better direction; but a mistake also "presents a problem that could potentially be your next purpose." His example: blowing money on a compulsive car purchase and being unable to afford anything for three months. Then: if I can't afford the lifestyle I want, I need more money; so I need to start a business or increase my skill set for a better job; and those present subsequent problems you must lean into. If I don't know how to build a business, I need to learn; if I know and haven't done anything, I need to act. "Here's a problem, I need to solve it, I need to also learn how to solve it, and then I'm going to solve it." "All of this stuff is so simple if you just get out of your head" (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15). In the 2024 version he adds that "there are no right or wrong actions here": if you want something surface-level and status-based (a business, money, a good car), do it, then self-reflect on whether it was a mistake, because "I can always come back and correct it": sell the car, restructure the business or the lifestyle. But you need the control to do that, and if you lack it (a job that leaves you no time), "that's a problem, solve it" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). He also gives a smaller example of reflection producing direction: someone who does not know where to move can reflect on the places they enjoyed on vacation and find inklings (a big city, walkable).

The car story contains, in miniature, the method of this whole section. A mistake is not a failure to be regretted but information: it reveals a problem, and the problem is a candidate purpose. The instruction "there are no right or wrong actions" does not mean that all actions are equally good; it means that actions are correctable, so the cost of trying a superficial goal is low, provided one keeps the control needed to correct it. That proviso explains why autonomy (time, money, freedom from a controlling job) keeps appearing as the first problem in Koe's chains: it is the meta-problem that makes the others solvable.

In 2025 he summarized the logic in its simplest form. Becoming deeply aware of a problem is extremely powerful, "because most people don't want to solve their problems. They don't realize that life is just a series of problems, and you build solutions and you get better. That's how you make more money. You solve your own problems. The way you solve a problem is with a project. The project is a solution. You can turn that into a product and put it online." Fitness people, business people, anyone creating a product "solve a painful problem in their own life or someone else's." So solve your own problems "for life," without getting distracted from the series "laid out in front of you from shallow to deep." The shallow problems now are getting into the gym, getting fit, eating healthily, being overweight, sluggish, lacking energy, having no money, needing to start a business; solving them gives you the experience to make more money (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). This passage is the hinge to Chapter 9. A problem is solved by a **project**, and the project can become a product: the personal purpose of this section becomes, through the project, the life's work and business of Parts IX and X. The phrase "from shallow to deep" restates the concentric circles as a sequence in time.

#### Chapters, puzzles and exhausted visions

If purposes are solved one after another, life has a rhythm, and Koe describes it with several images. In his 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush he speaks of seasons: "I like to think of it as like chapters in a book... each chapter has a purpose... that you're pursuing." When you achieve a purpose, you often get "launched into this phase of feeling lost... you don't see the light at the end of the tunnel because it's too early in the chapter." So "stay mindful, test goals, learn from certain lenses, move forward." Purpose and clarity are "built over time, just by going about life consciously, like noting those little things that can apply to a better future," like a puzzle: you piece it together, it takes shape, one piece reveals part of an image ("I need to move further in that direction"), and with the last piece: "we're going all in on this... I'm obsessed for the next six months... nothing is going to get in my way" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). He illustrates it with his own transitions, from an off-grid phase to the clarity of the one-person business, and from writing a book to building software.

The **chapters** image explains a phenomenon that the definition of purpose might otherwise make puzzling: why achieving a purpose can feel bad. The completion of a chapter removes the problem that organized one's attention, and the next problem is not yet visible. The **puzzle** image explains how the next purpose appears: not all at once, by insight, but piece by piece, through noticing small things, until one piece makes the picture clear enough to commit.

In January 2024 Koe described the same rhythm as a cycle with phases. For a few months you feel on top of the world, unable to stop making progress. The next few months slow down: you maintain things, you feel good, "but it's not the same." Then you hit a wall: "your vision is exhausted, you feel lost," negative thoughts flood your mind and can cloud it, preventing you "from collecting hints at your next purpose in life that sends you down a rabbit hole of obsession." The video, he says, is for people in this last phase, those who cannot find joy in reading a book or walking in nature (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07). **Vision is exhausted** names the end of a chapter from the inside: the vision has done its work, its energy is spent, and the danger is that the low mood prevents one from collecting the "hints" (the puzzle pieces) that would form the next one. Koe's models of these phases change over the years (waves, seasons, chapters; clarity, intensity, consistency, lost; and, in 2025, limbo, vision, flow, resistance); Chapter 39 traces their evolution. What they share is the claim that feeling lost between purposes is a normal phase rather than a sign of failure.

What should one do in that phase? In 2023 Koe offered a set of questions to use as **attention anchors**. If you are absolutely lost, ask better questions, because "questions dictate the quality of answers, quality of answers dictate the quality of your life." Hold the questions in the back of your awareness; meaningful questions are not answered immediately, because you must make mistakes and let struggle and pain lead you to the answers. Experience life, self-reflect, and see which answers piece together in alignment with the questions. His list:

- What do I really want out of life?
- What is important to me?
- What is the highest version of myself?
- What does my ideal day look like?
- What do I have to accomplish in order to get there?
- Does that solve a problem in the world?
- What product or service aligns with both?
- What is the next phase of my purpose?

Write these down (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15).

The term "attention anchors" captures how the questions work: they are not answered in a sitting but held, like the vision of section 8.1, as a lens through which experience is filtered until answers accumulate. The reader should note that the same phrase, in the singular, means something else in a 2025 passage discussed in section 7.2: there "an attention anchor" is the vision itself, the "sturdy mental house" that pulls attention away from doubt and insecurity. In this 2023 usage the anchors are plural and are questions, not an image of the future; what the two senses share is the function of holding attention steady in the background while daily life goes on. The sequence of the list also reproduces the chapter's movement: from desire (what do I want) through the ideal day (section 8.5) and the necessary accomplishments (the hierarchy) to the world's problems and a product (the creative way of life), ending with the next phase.

In a 2024 interview, the writer Devon Eriksen gave a complementary method, which he called the "deeper level answer," "a little more difficult to grasp but very important." Ask what the real reason was that you had a goal. Why did you want to be an artist? To give vent to a creative impulse (there are still lots of ways to do that)? To entertain people? To show the world what exists in your head? To be famous and make a lot of money ("that's just as good as any other goal... that's not shallow")? "What is it that you wanted to experience in your own life, in your own psyche, that made you decide I want to be an artist, I want to be an author, I want to be an entrepreneur?" "When we boil it down, most of our goals are about the core things that make us happy." When the skills that form your identity hit a dead end (because they are obsolete, or "because they aren't serving your real purpose"), stop and ask: "what is my real purpose? What is it that I wanted out of this, and how do I find some way to get it that is more directly related to what I really want?" (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`, 2024-12-08).

The method is Eriksen's, and he illustrates it with his own move away from a career in software. It fits Koe's framework closely: it is the "experience" layer at the top of the creation pyramid (section 8.3), applied when a path has closed. If what one wanted from a goal was an experience, then a blocked path to the goal is not a blocked path to the experience; there may be a more direct route. Eriksen's refusal to call fame or money "shallow" is also consistent with Koe's insistence on starting with superficial goals.

#### The hero's journey without burning the boats

Koe's emphasis on problem solving often leads him to recommend entrepreneurship, and on this point a guest qualified him. In a 2025 conversation, Sahil Bloom, author of *The 5 Types of Wealth*, closed the "mental wealth" section of his framework with a call to action: "You are the hero in your hero's journey. That is not a grandiose statement. That is true. You get one shot at all of this. So engage in something that matters to you." And he set an explicit limit: "That doesn't mean that you have to go burn the boats and go build your own business. It might mean that you are going to engage in your purpose of providing for the people that you love... by showing up to your factory job every single day... showing up with energy to the job that you don't like, so that you can do the things that you like on the weekends." The requirement is to "live your path, find your version of this hero's journey... Take the actions on a daily basis. Don't accept the blind version. Create a life around the things that you truly care about, not what other people tell you" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30).

The passage is Bloom's, and it nuances a position that Koe states in the same conversation and elsewhere (that everyone should at least try entrepreneurship). Koe's own record on the question is mixed: he has said that "the only way to escape is to build your own thing," that he does "not even recommend starting a business" as a separate act from documenting one's development, and that one's life's work will eventually require a business. Bloom's version is compatible with Koe's definition of purpose, and arguably follows from it: if purpose is the most pressing problem in one's life, and the most pressing problem is providing for one's family, then a factory job done with energy is a purpose, not a betrayal of one. What both reject is "the blind version": a life whose goals were never examined.

**Complementary context:** the "hero's journey" is the narrative pattern described by the mythologist Joseph Campbell in *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949): departure from the ordinary world, trials, transformation and return. "Burning the boats" alludes to the story, attributed to several commanders (most often Hernán Cortés), of destroying one's ships to make retreat impossible.

#### Problems are infinite: why fear of replacement misreads the future

The claim that problems are endless has an economic consequence, which Koe draws in his videos on work and AI. "The fundamental misunderstanding with people worried about replacement is that there will always be problems to solve." But "you don't know how to hunt: you're trapped in a repetitive string of known tasks assigned by someone else; it's obvious why you are a prime candidate for replacement. You don't grow or evolve, there's no novelty, no iteration and no building toward a vision that exposes you to solvable problems that can't be found when you are at a standstill." "If you rely on someone else to give you a problem to solve, you will be replaced. You need agency, creativity and a purpose" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28).

**Knowing how to hunt** is the capacity to find one's own problems, which is exactly what the definition of purpose trains. Its opposite is waiting to be assigned problems by an employer. The argument connects this section to the "hunting skill set" of section 8.2: vision, goals, systems, open-mindedness, agency and creativity are the faculties of someone who finds problems rather than receiving them. The replaceable worker, on this account, is not replaceable because of a lack of skill but because their problems come from outside: a machine that can solve the assigned problem makes them unnecessary, whereas someone who generates new problems from their own vision is the source of the work.

Two weeks earlier he had put the same point in terms of speed. "Machines can solve a problem in 30 seconds that used to take humans hundreds of hours to solve. That means if you follow a string of assignments at a massive corporation, it isn't a question of if you will be replaced, it's a question of when." "What most worried people don't understand is that problems will never go away. AI may help solve for our basic needs, but what about our growth needs? What about the new problems that people can't predict, that emerge from advancements and technological efficiency?" "This isn't anything new; it's just happening faster than it has before, meaning the chances for replacement are happening more than before, unless you adapt" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`, 2024-07-13). The distinction between **basic needs** and **growth needs** is borrowed from Maslow's hierarchy (Chapter 38), and Koe uses it to locate where human work will remain: not in satisfying needs that machines can meet efficiently, but in the open-ended problems of growth and in the new problems that efficiency itself creates.

In August 2025 he named the source of the idea. If you do not understand that problems are infinite, he says, take a year to read and study David Deutsch's *The Beginning of Infinity* (he summarizes it in his free book *Purpose and Profit*). His derivation: if problems are infinite, then solutions, value and wealth creation are infinite too; therefore money is infinite. It is not limited to the US dollar; it is changing with Bitcoin; in the past it was seashells; in the future it will take whatever shape culture and society give it, but it is still infinite, "because it represents value exchange, and that's innate to human nature" (**Source:** `You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md`, 2025-08-17). The starting premise is Deutsch's; the chain of derivations (from infinite problems to infinite money as value exchange) is Koe's adaptation, and it goes beyond what Deutsch argues. Chapter 34 develops Koe's theory of money as value exchange. For this chapter, the relevant conclusion is the one that closes the loop: a person who defines purpose as solving problems will never run out of purpose, and an economy built on solving problems will never run out of work for people who can find them.

### 8.7 Passion Is Created

The common advice is to "find your passion" and then build a life around it. Koe's view, consistent with everything above, is the reverse: passion is not found but created, by investing energy in a goal, and it is sustained by keeping the goal large enough that the investment never runs out. This final section reconstructs that argument, the techniques he gives for building motivation where none exists, his account of curiosity as the raw material of passion, and his advice on how long to commit before judging.

#### Invested energy: the argument

Koe's fullest version of the argument appears in March 2023, directly after the passage on bringing the ideal future into the now, and was repeated in a 2024 compilation. It proceeds by steps.

1. Bringing your future actions into the now means you "begin to invest mental energy in a goal through focus on that goal," whether fifteen minutes, an hour or four hours a day: "you're investing energy or currency, in a metaphorical sense."
2. "The more you do it, the more you feel obligated to do it; that's how you build momentum."
3. "And that's how you find passion. You don't just find an action that you are super passionate about; instead you start investing mental energy into a goal, you do the actions that are required... and then you cultivate passion along the way, because you discover things that you wouldn't have discovered without that direct experience."
4. When you do not work on it, "you will feel as if you are wasting that currency... that investment," and body and mind push you back toward it, so that you do not lose "the progress, the momentum, the passion."
5. "That's how you sustain a passionate, fulfilling and momentum-full life."

(**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21.)

The metaphor of **currency** carries the argument. Attention invested in a goal behaves like money invested in an asset: it creates a stake, and the stake changes behavior. One feels obligated to continue not because of an external rule but because stopping would waste what has been put in. The argument contains two distinct mechanisms. One is commitment through sunk investment (step 4), which psychologists would recognize as a version of the sunk-cost effect, here harnessed deliberately rather than avoided. The other is discovery (step 3): direct experience reveals features of an activity that could not be known in advance, and some of them turn out to be engaging. The first mechanism keeps one going; the second supplies the content of the passion. Koe presents the whole chain as his own reasoning from experience, not as a research finding.

In July 2024 he restated the claim in the vocabulary of Chapter 5. To create a life of enjoyment and flow, "you need to invest mental energy into self-generated goals, stick it out long enough to create passion (you don't just find passion), create new systems or routines for your mind to operate on; that's how you reverse psychic entropy and chaos." You invest energy every day, with thoughts, emotions, attention and actions, in goals that create your ideal life through work, study and practice (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). Passion is here placed between two other items: self-generated goals (section 8.1) and new routines (section 8.3). The three together are what reverses psychic entropy, so passion is not merely a pleasant feeling but part of the ordering of consciousness.

#### Passion as investing energy into a goal

In May 2024 Koe compressed the argument into a definition: "Passion is investing energy into a goal." From it he draws a warning about skills. "You can be passionate about the journey of mastering a skill, but once you've mastered it, that passion fades." "You must keep that passion alive by channeling energy into the skills, goals and projects that will achieve your vision." And: "Passion is found in the process of creating your vision." "You can enjoy a skill that you use along the way to help you create that vision, but if you become attached to or make an identity out of that skill, that's how you get trapped." "You aren't passionate about being a designer, you're passionate about the problems design allows you to solve, until your life and work limit what problems you solve" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26).

The designer example applies the identity theory of Part II to passion. A skill is a tool; when it becomes an identity ("I am a designer"), it is defended even when it no longer serves (Chapter 3), and the passion, which was really for the problems the skill could solve, quietly dies as the problems become routine. The definition locates passion in the relation between energy and a goal, not in any activity, which explains both why it fades (the goal is reached, the skill mastered) and how it can be renewed (a new goal, a larger problem).

What goal never fades? In the same video Koe answers with an image: "Your vision is the large goal in your life," so "you can invest as much energy as you want into your vision if you want to live a very passionate life." The vision is "an infinite bank for energy storage" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). The **infinite bank for energy storage** completes the currency metaphor. A skill is an account that fills up: once mastered, it absorbs no more energy. A vision is an account without a ceiling: because it evolves (Chapter 7), it is never "mastered," and it can absorb all the energy one is willing to put into it. A passionate life, on this account, is not one that found the right activity but one that has a goal large enough to keep investing in. (This is also a reason Koe's rule on curiosity calls the vision a battery that must be recharged; the bank and the battery are two images of the same store.)

The same video opens a section with a principle Koe returns to later in it: "If you don't want to do something, but you have to do it in order to do what you want, then you want to do it." Most starving artists go wrong here: "you're going to have to learn skills that you think you don't want to learn, you're going to have to do things you think you don't want to do, but the only reason you don't want to do them is because you're not aligning it with your vision for the future" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). The context suggests his typical case: the artist or writer who wants to create but refuses to learn marketing or sales. The principle is logically simple and practically demanding. If a task is a necessary means to a goal one wants, then wanting the goal entails wanting the task; feeling otherwise is a sign that the task has not been connected to the goal. The repair is not willpower but alignment: seeing the task as part of the vision. This is the passion argument applied to its hardest case, the tasks that will never be enjoyable for their own sake.

#### The neurochemical cocktail

Koe's earliest explanation of passion, from October 2022, is phrased in neurochemical terms. The dopamine response of pursuing a goal is, he says, "why I'm so big on creating a vision for your future." What people call "passion for their work" is "this neurochemical cocktail in your head" that happens because they have a vision of what they are trying to actualize in the now. All the neurotransmitters "are going crazy" to help you bridge the gap, because you have:

1. a desired outcome;
2. clarity on how to get there, with a plan;
3. deconstructed goals, reverse-engineered from that vision;
4. priority tasks you know will hit those goals if done day after day, which become "conditioned and structured into your routine."

The problem state is the opposite: a desired lifestyle far away, with no plan or path, which produces overwhelm and anxiety instead of fuel to "act in the now with passion and excitement" (**Source:** `Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md`, 2022-10-09).

The four items are this chapter's structure, from vision to daily levers, and the passage makes a striking claim about it: the experience people call passion *is* the experience of having that structure in place. Passion, on this account, is not a property of the work but a by-product of a well-built hierarchy of goals. The neurochemical language is Koe's interpretation, influenced by the dopamine literature he discusses in Chapter 11 (in which dopamine is associated with pursuit and anticipation rather than with pleasure as such); he does not cite studies for the specific claim, and "neurochemical cocktail" is a figure of speech rather than a pharmacological description. The useful contrast is the last one: the same distant goal produces fuel when a path exists and anxiety when it does not. It is the canyon and the bridge of section 8.1, described from the inside.

#### Gravity: why distractions are not distractions without a goal

In July 2025 Koe drew a consequence from invested energy that explains a common frustration with self-improvement advice. You will not recognize distractions as mistakes "unless you have a goal that you are actively working toward." For a goal to have gravity (for discipline or motivation to work), "you have to have some form of energy invested into it." So spend a month working toward a goal, so that you feel the pain of no longer working toward it, or so that you can register weed, laziness or junk food as a mistake. If the gym is not your goal, alcohol does not take away your gym performance; if you are not working on a business, partying does not affect your mental clarity for making money. "Most people aren't working towards meaningful goals. So all of the distractions that they're engaging in aren't distractions to them" (**Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`, 2025-07-27).

The argument is the cybernetic model of Chapter 6 applied to temptation. A deviation can only be registered against a target; without a target there is no error signal. Advice that tells people to stop scrolling, drinking or eating junk fails when they have no invested goal, because from their point of view these activities cost nothing. The remedy is not to fight the distractions directly but to invest in a goal for long enough (a month, in this version) that the distractions acquire a cost. **Gravity**, which appeared in section 8.1 ("the more attention you invest in your goals, the stronger their gravity becomes") and in section 8.3 (a plan "that holds more gravity than the distractions"), is here given its operating condition: energy must already have been invested.

#### Shallow pursuits and the new why

If passion is created, where does the first investment come from, before there is any passion? Koe's answer is that it comes from shallow motives, and that this is normal. In 2022, describing the lessons of building his business, he said that the curious mindset led him to try things he fell in love with, and all of them "started out as surface-level, shallow pursuits; that's how it starts out for everyone." He tried the gym to get over a breakup and to get jacked, fell in love with it, and eventually attached a **new why**, a new purpose, so that it could become a sustainable and meaningful pursuit. He tried new skills that people promoted, fell in love with learning, failed a lot, fell off the path, but eventually his knowledge, skill set and experience compounded until everything started to make sense and he could navigate life and business more artfully. With a vision for the future, small consistent actions and failures ("the entire hero's journey"), he built his lifestyle (**Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`, 2022-11-06).

The sequence (shallow motive, investment, love of the activity, a new and deeper why) is the passion argument told as autobiography, and it reproduces the concentric circles of section 8.6 at the scale of a single pursuit. A shallow reason is enough to start; the investment produces engagement; and the engagement creates the conditions for a deeper reason to attach itself. The lesson for someone without passion is that a shallow motive is not a disqualification but the usual entry point.

#### The iceberg of whys

A single reason, shallow or deep, is fragile. In January 2025 Koe explained why New Year's resolutions fail. Getting out of a bad situation is not as simple as setting a new goal or a resolution: resolutions are often conformity (doing it because everyone is, or because someone told you to), not something done for an intrinsic reason, and without "an iceberg of whys" creating intrinsic motivation it is easy to quit. The new goal motivates for a few hours, and then you forget, or get trapped in the same "cloud of distractions." To avoid a robotic, predetermined life, "equip your mind with the right tools to think for yourself," which is the fastest way to change your life; the journey, he warns, may and probably will be painful (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12).

Later in the same video he gave the method. When you lack motivation, create a stack of reasons, "an iceberg of whys." His own big life changes started with being fed up and disgusted with where he had let himself go, which made him search for reasons to get out. The method has two directions. First, search for the benefits of what you want (for example, google "what are the benefits of" a diet or a food you want to adopt). Then do the opposite: find reasons *not* to do the things you want to stop ("what is so bad about fried foods"), so that you come to fear them. This creates what he calls "polar in-betweenness," which pushes you toward the positive end. And he generalizes: "nobody does one thing for one reason." Critics say a creator does it "only for the money," but it is part of their life; Koe does it because he enjoys it more than the alternative. Become aware of this, he says, so that you can reverse-engineer your own stack of reasons for things that are not immediately gratifying (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12).

The **iceberg of whys** is a deliberate construction of the gravity that, in other passages, comes from invested energy or a felt problem. Its two directions correspond to the vision and the anti-vision of Chapter 7: reasons toward what you want (pull) and reasons away from what you do not (push). **Polar in-betweenness** names the position of someone between those poles, pushed and pulled in the same direction. The image of the iceberg adds that most of the reasons are below the surface: a visible motive ("for the money") rests on a mass of others that observers, and often the person, do not see. The method makes them visible on purpose, so that motivation for an unpleasant task does not depend on a single reason that can be argued away.

#### The rabbit hole of discovery

The passion argument starts from investment. A second line in Koe's thought, older and more exploratory, starts from curiosity. In November 2022 he described how knowledge compounds. If you are just starting out, try everything and see what genuinely interests you. When you catch "that whiff of excitement or dopamine that tries to pull you down the rabbit hole of discovery," do not deny it: go into it and see whether you get obsessed. Once you find it, repeat the process for life. Obsession with one thing expands your awareness in that domain, so you notice another thing you would never have noticed had you not tried or committed; commit to that, go further, and so on. Compounding, then, is trying things, diving deep on one, "living in that area or experiencing life from that state of heightened knowledge," branching off, then branching again, then coming back and seeing the whole picture. He closed that part of the video with a line that echoes section 8.3: "if you don't make time to build, you're assigned time to build for someone else" (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

Later in the same video he gave the recipe in steps: research what you feel pulled to research; do not get distracted; let it pull you deep into a rabbit hole of discovery; understand that "sustained curiosity plus excitement equals passion"; practise sustaining that passion with everything discussed; note down your findings; build a project; begin teaching others (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

The two passages give passion a second formula: **sustained curiosity plus excitement equals passion**. It is compatible with the investment formula of 2024 ("passion is investing energy into a goal") but describes an earlier moment. Curiosity and excitement are the signal that tells one *where* to invest; investment is what turns the signal into passion. The **rabbit hole of discovery** is the place where the two meet: a domain one has entered following a whiff of excitement and in which one has stayed long enough for the investment to compound. The recipe ends with building and teaching, which ties the rabbit hole to the project (Chapter 9) and to learning by teaching (Chapter 14).

In his conversation with Dickie Bush, Koe located passion in the process of learning itself. For him, fulfillment is learning new things. Many people become passionate about one skill, "but that's because they were passionate about learning it... the psychological processes behind that, that made you enjoy the skill, is what you actually enjoyed. You enjoyed the process, but you mislabeled it for the end result." The reframe: "you're passionate about life itself," rather than trying to find a passion. Bush recognized his whole life in this. He described speedcubing, a year and a half of playing *Call of Duty* professionally in seventh grade (among the best in Florida), and reaching the game's tenth prestige while home for three weeks with pneumonia. He loves playing a new game, getting up to speed, feeling he has mastered it and moving on, and "that isn't like Call of Duty... it's everything." His current question is what business model or "operating vehicle" lets him obsess over something new, learn it and step away while *storing the energy* (unlike the hours of *Call of Duty*, which pay nothing now); perhaps a portfolio of businesses. "Leaning into that is kind of my personal superpower." (Koe, for his part, mentions that his best Rubik's cube time was around fifty-six seconds.) (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16.)

The idea that passion attaches to the process of learning rather than to the skill is Koe's; the extended illustration is Bush's. Together they explain the fading of skill-passion described above: once the learning curve flattens, the process that was enjoyed is over. Bush's question about "storing the energy" is a practical version of Koe's infinite bank: someone whose passion is learning needs a vehicle in which each new obsession adds to a lasting asset instead of evaporating when it ends. For Koe, the one-person business built around a personal brand is such a vehicle (Chapter 28).

Koe also contrasts two attitudes toward learning that decide whether a rabbit hole is entered at all. Quoting a tweet of his: "most smart people I know, when they want to learn something, immediately Google what they want and go down a rabbit hole; everyone else asks for all the information to be given to them, rarely gets a complete answer and gets distracted within five minutes" (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23). The opinion is a generalization from his acquaintances, but its point is consistent with the agency theme of section 8.6: waiting to be given information is the learning equivalent of waiting to be assigned problems.

In 2025 he specified where the best rabbit holes are. "The best ideas I've found are the ones collecting dust in little-known books, podcasts, videos and posts." "Most people aren't original; they're just talking about ideas you haven't seen before. If you're consuming the same information that everyone else is, it's no wonder why you feel like you're saying the same thing." So stop reading only mainstream books and content, stop getting information only from the algorithm, and "actively hunt for ideas." He nuances: mainstream books and movies are fine for entertainment ("your intention with those is entertainment... not learning... you need to separate those two things"). It takes effort, not page one of Google: "listen to two-, three-hour-long podcasts that nobody has heard before, that have like 2,000 views on YouTube... go and find those channels." "The point... is to just go down more rabbit holes" (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19). The passage belongs mainly to the theory of originality (Chapter 18), but it adds a criterion to the rabbit hole: depth and obscurity, not popularity, are what make the exploration productive, and the intention (learning versus entertainment) must be decided before consuming.

His own consumption, which he described in 2026, shows the rabbit hole in practice, and it shows that depth can also mean repetition. "I could see the same video title every single day from a different creator and I would probably watch it. I do watch it, because I want to hear their opinion." When he falls down a rabbit hole (Claude Code, OpenAI, the future of work), "I'm just going to watch the same topic over and over again so that I get all of the nuances." The same with fitness, now "honestly, my main priority": "if I had an ounce of muscle for every time that I've watched a nutrition 101 video or just some creator's opinion on nutrition, I'd be Ronnie Coleman" (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md`, 2026-05-14). The joke (Ronnie Coleman is an eight-time Mr. Olympia bodybuilder) makes a serious point about how obsession works. Repeated exposure to the same subject from different perspectives is not wasted time inside a rabbit hole; it is how nuance is acquired, and it is one of the forms that invested energy takes. There is an apparent tension with the advice to seek obscure sources: one recommends the little-known, the other repeats the familiar. Both describe depth in a single domain rather than breadth across the mainstream.

#### Commit long enough: the first hump

The investment theory of passion has a practical implication: one cannot judge whether a path is right before enough has been invested in it. Koe returns to this repeatedly, with a recurring image. In 2023: "Valuing something, or finding importance in something, or being passionate about something doesn't come from not doing it." "You have to start, you have to get over the first hump, because then you will find the reason": when you self-reflect and realize "this actually made my life better, I want to continue doing this, I care about this now," you continue (**Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`, 2023-05-08). The **first hump** is the initial stretch in which an activity is unfamiliar, unrewarding and difficult; the reason to continue appears only on its far side. Judging an activity from before the hump means judging it at its worst.

In December 2024 he explained what happens on the far side with an analogy and a reference to flow. You need enough time to invest energy until you see the thing as important, "like a song you don't like, but when you listen to it enough it becomes your favorite song." The gym and a new skill are not fun or easy the first time, but as skill comes to match challenge, "you begin to find the game extremely addicting." People learning new things say "this is overwhelming"; yes, it is unknown, scary and dark. "Overwhelm and struggle and mistakes... are signals that you're moving in the right direction." If it were comfortable, it would not be worth doing or push you to grow. His instruction: "give yourself three months, not two weeks" (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15). The song analogy describes acquired taste (familiarity changing preference), and the reference to skill matching challenge describes the onset of flow (Chapter 10). The two mechanisms reinforce each other: exposure makes the activity familiar, and practice makes it a game one can win. Reading overwhelm as a signal of direction rather than of error is a reframing Koe uses throughout his work; it is a heuristic, and it can mislead if applied to every discomfort, but within the first months of a chosen path it protects against quitting at the hump.

A related point from 2023 concerns pain tolerance. You must train your pain tolerance by taking on an increasing level of challenge. You would not try to meditate for an hour at the start: it would be so difficult, and your tolerance so low, that you would not even reach what he calls the "perception threshold," the point at which the practice starts to show you anything (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05). The principle of progressive challenge applies to commitment itself: one should start with a version of the activity small enough to be tolerated (the five minutes of writing of section 8.5) and grow it, rather than start at a level of difficulty that guarantees quitting.

#### How long is long enough?

Koe's recommended minimum commitment varies with context, and the variation is worth laying out because readers often take one number as his rule.

| Recommendation | Date | Context |
|---|---|---|
| "Give it two weeks, give it a month, and then see if you become obsessed with it" | 2022-10 | Trying a new interest (content creation) |
| "For even just three months" | 2023-01 | Acquiring enough skill to know what is possible |
| "1% of 50 years is 6 months, and most people can't last two weeks" | 2023-02; 2023-11 | Not quitting a lifelong path in its "tutorial phase" |
| "Give yourself three months, not two weeks" | 2024-12 | Learning a new skill or habit |
| "Give yourself the full 60 days" | 2024-09 | Starting a one-person business |
| One month of work toward a goal | 2025-07 | Making distractions register as costs |

He gives no explicit reason for the variation, but the numbers depend on the object: weeks to see whether an interest sparks, months to acquire enough skill to judge, years for a lifelong path. The first entry, from 2022, also contains the promise of the payoff. Stick out the initial period in which you do not know what you are doing (like a game with no instruction manual, which is not fun until you pick it up); "give it two weeks, give it a month, and then see if you become obsessed with it." You will see "that first spark of growth" ("this is possible") and double down, and "eventually, three years later, you'll be at close to 500k to a million followers across platforms." He mentions having passed 10,000 subscribers on YouTube, which grew more slowly than his other platforms and is special to him for providing "aha moments and mindset shifts" (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31). The follower numbers describe his own trajectory and should be read as an anecdote, not a forecast.

The six-month figure has the most memorable form. In 2023: "The longer you do it, the more attention you hold. Quitting after one to six months is stupid. 1% of 50 years is 6 months. If this is the work you see yourself doing for your entire life, don't quit during the tutorial phase" (**Source:** `Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md`, 2023-11-26). Earlier that year he had repeated what he called a saying from an earlier video: "one percent of 50 years is six months, and most people can't last two weeks." All of this takes a lot of time; "broaden your time horizon" and think. Compare it with school: four to twelve years of education plus five to ten of climbing a career ladder. Sticking out your own path for two to four years puts you far ahead, and you monetize along the way. "Weigh the pros and cons and call yourself out" (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). The **tutorial phase** is the opening section of a video game in which the player learns the controls; quitting there means quitting before the game has begun. The arithmetic (six months is one percent of fifty years) is a reframing device: it makes a period that feels long look short against the length of a working life, and the comparison with schooling makes the same point against the default path.

The 2024 business version adds a pattern that Koe considers general. You will get quite a few "no"s at the start; "that's just how you get into business: failure." Sixty days is a long time, and he would "guarantee anyone can do this if they actually do it for 60 days and they aren't stupid when it comes to repeating themselves": if something does not work and you do not improve it, it keeps not working. You will feel confused and overwhelmed learning something new: "your mind is struggling to expand and grow, like how you get sore after lifting weights." "Most of your results are going to come after day 30"; there is a natural filter for people who are not serious. He posted eighty YouTube videos before one went viral and he felt his channel growing. "With all things worth having, nothing happens, then everything happens": you feel no progress, then experience accumulates and compounds into, say, one person saying yes. "Stop being a dopamine junkie and give yourself the full 60 days" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md`, 2024-09-15).

**Nothing happens, then everything happens** is Koe's name for the nonlinear shape of progress: a long flat stretch in which effort seems to produce nothing, followed by a sudden rise. In 2023 he had used the same phrase for a cognitive version of the pattern. Illustrating circles of knowledge expanding until they overlap, he said that you will not understand how things connect until you have developed yourself enough in each area; once they overlap, "it's like putting that final piece into the puzzle, and then a messy image turns into a clear image in your head" (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). The puzzle is the same image that described the arrival of a new purpose in section 8.6. In both cases the pieces accumulate invisibly, and the picture appears only when enough of them are in place, which is why quitting during the flat stretch is so costly: it abandons the investment just before it would have paid. The **dopamine junkie** is the person who needs a reward from each action and therefore cannot survive the flat stretch; Chapter 11 develops the term.

#### Why there are no results: the causal chain

In January 2023 Koe gave a chain of causes that connects commitment to the rest of the chapter. "If you aren't seeing results in your life, it's because you're distracted." You don't know what you want because you haven't tried anything. You haven't tried anything because you make excuses for others' results and how they got them. Because you haven't tried, you haven't committed, so you haven't stuck it out long enough to have enough skill to get results. And you don't know what's possible because you haven't moved forward by trying something, committing to it "for even just three months," and acquiring some skill in that domain. Since what you want may change tomorrow, in a week, a month, a year or a decade, you must consistently self-correct (**Source:** `How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md`, 2023-01-12).

| Link | Statement |
|---|---|
| Symptom | No results |
| Cause 1 | Distraction |
| Cause 2 | Not knowing what you want, because you have not tried anything |
| Cause 3 | Not trying, because you make excuses for other people's results |
| Cause 4 | Not committing long enough to acquire skill |
| Cause 5 | Not knowing what is possible, because skill is what reveals possibility |
| Correction | Try, commit at least three months, self-correct as wants change |

The chain shows how the parts of this chapter depend on one another. Knowing what one wants (the top of the hierarchy) is not a precondition of action but a product of it; commitment produces skill, skill reveals what is possible, and possibility informs what one wants. The circularity is intentional, and it is the same loop as the error-correction loop of section 8.5 and the cycle of problems of section 8.6: act, learn, revise, act again. The "excuses for others' results" deserve notice: attributing someone else's success to luck or privilege is, in this chain, a way of protecting oneself from trying, and therefore from finding out.

#### Try hard

Finally, Koe addresses a cultural obstacle to commitment. In a 2024 aside on intensity, he says it is unfortunate that people think trying hard is bad or stigmatized ("you're a try-hard"). "The only way to see any kind of success in anything is to be serious and to try hard." Comedians, who joke all day, sit down every night writing and scrapping jokes; even comedy must be taken seriously. "The whole chill guy, or don't try hard, thing that is going on right now will just make you complacent and dumb" (**Source:** `Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md`, 2024-12-01). The opinion is directed at a specific online fashion of its moment, and its tone is polemical. Its substantive point connects to the rest of the section: invested energy is the source of passion, and a culture that treats visible effort as embarrassing discourages exactly the investment that would create it. This is not a contradiction of Koe's critique of hustle (section 8.4). Trying hard, in his sense, means taking a chosen goal seriously within constraints; hustle, in his critique, means long, distracted hours worn as a badge. One is intensity of investment, the other a display of duration.

The arc of this chapter ends where Chapter 9 begins. A vision broken into a hierarchy of goals, bounded by constraints, practised at a small scale, aimed at the most pressing problem and fuelled by invested energy, takes a concrete form: a *project*. The next chapter shows why that project should begin with superficial problems, how purpose evolves through levels, and how the project becomes, in its largest form, a life's work.

### Exercises

1. **Audit your hierarchy.** Write down, without editing, the hierarchy of goals your days actually serve: the final goal, the intermediate goals and the daily tasks, as an observer watching your week would infer them. Then mark each level as intrinsic (derived from your own curiosity and vision) or extrinsic (supplied by an employer, a school, family or the algorithm). Where the hierarchy is extrinsic, apply Koe's 2024 test: do these goals evolve, or have they stayed fixed for years? Finally, consider whether his claim that "every single thing you do revolves around a hierarchy of goals" survives your audit, or whether you found activities that serve no goal at all. What would he say about them, and would you agree?

2. **Build the six-component sheet, then attack it.** Write the six components of section 8.1 for your own life (anti-vision, vision, one-year goal, one-month project, daily levers, constraints), using the three "lens" questions of 2025 for the year, the month and the day. Then critique your own sheet with three of the chapter's tests: the two-week rule (would your levers produce noticeable progress in two weeks?), the performance-versus-vanity distinction (does any goal depend on outcomes you do not control?) and the constraint test (does your plan quietly sacrifice something you listed as a constraint?). Revise the sheet and explain the most important change.

3. **Problem first or goal first?** Koe advised "set a meaningful goal" in 2022, "stop focusing on the goal; a problem creates a goal" in March 2024, and "step one: just have a goal" two months later. Choose two people you know well (or two versions of yourself at different times) whose situations differ: one who perceives no pressing problem, and one who is already moving toward something. For each, argue which advice fits and why. Then decide whether the three positions can be reconciled into a single rule, and state it in your own words, or explain why they cannot.

4. **Find your burning problem.** Using the questions in section 8.6 ("What are you sweeping under the rug so you don't have to face the pain that comes with growth?", "If solved, what problem would unlock the next level of enjoyment in your life?"), identify your current most pressing problem. Trace the chain of problems it belongs to, in the manner of Koe's car example or his job-to-business chain: what problem lies behind it, and what problem will its solution probably reveal? Then evaluate Koe's claim that "your purpose is not to spread love and be selfless" until you have developed yourself. Is the claim persuasive for your case? What would Sahil Bloom's factory-job example add to your answer?

5. **Constraints as a creative engine.** Pick a goal you are pursuing and list three sacrifices you have been making, or are tempted to make, to reach it. Turn each into an anti-goal and work out, concretely, what the constraint forces you to change in your strategy, your commitments and your use of time (the three effects Koe derives from a health constraint). Then identify a case in which a constraint would make a goal genuinely impossible rather than merely more demanding of creativity, and explain where Koe's heuristic ("when people say something is impossible, it's because they aren't creative enough") stops applying.

6. **Bring a piece of the future into the now.** Apply the sixty-five test: describe a typical day you would want to be living at retirement age, and identify the activities in it (not the possessions or circumstances). Choose one and design a taper-up schedule starting at a scale you could sustain tomorrow, in the manner of Koe's five-minutes-to-two-hours writing ramp or his two-to-ten daily messages. Then apply the digital nomad lesson: which element of your imagined day might, like the beach office, turn out not to be wanted once lived? How could a small-scale trial test it before you commit more?

7. **Passion: found or created?** Koe gives two formulas, "passion is investing energy into a goal" and "sustained curiosity plus excitement equals passion," and a neurochemical account (passion as the experience of having a vision, plan, goals and priority tasks in place). Choose an activity you are, or were, passionate about, and reconstruct how the passion arose. Which formula fits your history better? Then choose one that faded, and test Koe's explanation (the skill was mastered; the learning process ended; the skill became an identity). Finally, construct an "iceberg of whys" for one task you need to do but do not want to, listing reasons in both directions, and evaluate whether the principle "if you have to do it to do what you want, then you want to do it" changes how the task feels.

8. **How long before you judge?** Koe's minimum commitment varies from two weeks to six months to sixty days, depending on context. For a path you abandoned in the past, reconstruct when you quit, what you had invested by then and whether you were still before the "first hump" or in the "tutorial phase." Then design a commitment rule for a path you are considering now: what period, what minimum daily investment, and what evidence at the end would count as a genuine reason to stop rather than the discomfort of the flat stretch? Consider also the opposite error: how would you recognize that "nothing happens, then everything happens" has become an excuse for persisting in a path that should be abandoned?
