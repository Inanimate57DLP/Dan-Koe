## Chapter 10 — Life as a Game: Flow and the Edge

The four previous chapters built a set of parts. Chapter 6 established attention as the only real root control a person has and the goal as the lens through which the mind filters reality. Chapter 7 produced a direction (anti-vision and vision), Chapter 8 broke that direction into a hierarchy of goals with daily levers, and Chapter 9 gave it a practical unit, the project, and a largest form, the life's work. What none of those chapters explained is why a person who has all of these parts in place would *want* to keep using them on an ordinary morning, or why the same person can sit for twelve hours in front of a video game without any effort of will and cannot sit for one hour in front of a project they say they care about. This chapter is Dan Koe's answer, and it reassembles the parts into a single image: life is a game, and the hierarchy of goals, the rules you set yourself and the feedback you receive are its mechanics.

The chapter proceeds in seven steps. Section 10.1 presents the central metaphor and the reason Koe gives for it: games, businesses and the constructions of society share a structure that orders attention, so treating life as a game is a way of taking control of attention. Section 10.2 takes the game apart into its mechanics (the macro game and the micro games, the frame, the rules, the mechanics proper, feedback, the map and the skill tree) and shows how Koe turns them into a method for designing one's own game. Section 10.3 asks why video games are so absorbing and what that reveals about the way most people live, including Koe's own years of addiction to them. Section 10.4 defines the state that games produce and that Koe calls the key to life: flow, mostly as he takes it from Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi and Steven Kotler. Section 10.5 develops the condition flow depends on, the match between skill and challenge, which Koe calls living at your edge, and reads boredom and anxiety as signals. Section 10.6 presents the most extreme and most dangerous of his techniques for staying at that edge, tactical stress. Section 10.7 closes with infinite games: games without an end, whose challenge keeps rising, and Koe's claim that stagnation is equivalent to death. The chapter prepares Part V, which treats the daily self-government (dopamine, focused work, rest, routines) through which this game is actually played.

A note on status before starting. Koe insists that life is a video game "in a metaphorical sense." He is not, he says, claiming that "aliens built a giant computer" running a simulation, although he admits the idea is "quite similar" and that he is not completely closed to it ("who actually knows") (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05). Throughout this chapter, then, the game is an explanatory metaphor, and in this author the metaphor is often the mechanism of the explanation: the claims that matter are about attention, goals and skill, and the game is the vocabulary in which they are made concrete.

### 10.1 Life Is a Video Game

#### The author's gaming years

The metaphor has a biography behind it. Koe remembers waking up at three in the morning, before school, to play video games: *Halo 3*, "all the Call of Duties," *League of Legends*. "I was addicted to say the least," he says, and he sums up the consequence in one line: "increasing my level in video games took priority over increasing my level in real life." He adds that he would not change a thing, because those years allowed him to make the video in which he says it (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05).

The game that marked him most was *World of Warcraft*, an MMORPG (massively multiplayer online role-playing game), which "consumed far too much of my life": late nights, dungeons, raids, grinding quests, farming. He often found it more exciting than the real world. After his summer job as a lifeguard he would put on headphones and disappear into it; "nothing else mattered but my mind and the lines of code that kept it sane" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

He returns again and again to one specific memory: downloading the game at about fifteen and spending "a straight 2 hours just creating my character." The choices were race (human? orc?), class (warrior, mage, priest), role (tank, damage, healer), and appearance down to the hair, and while making them he was already thinking about how each would favor his personality and play style in order to win and handle the game's challenges. Around level fifteen you could ride a horse and choose a profession: blacksmith, armor creator, leatherworker, tailor, or gem crafting for weapon power-ups. "Once I made that choice it opened up a series of talents and traits that I was able to choose after that," and leveling those to a certain point opened choices that had not been available at the start: more dungeons, raiding with friends, acceptance into guilds that had requirements (did they need a healer, damage or a tank, and of what level and experience?) (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; repeated almost word for word in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

In the 2024 telling he adds the step that comes before race: faction. A beginner chooses between the Alliance and the Horde, and many players were passionate and ideological about that choice. Koe played Alliance but thought "the digital religion was a bit too much" and did not hate the other side. Then come race (he mostly played humans or night elves; the options included gnomes, orcs, pandaren and undead), class and appearance. Class is the role and play style: the tank keeps the enemy attacking them, the healer keeps the group alive, the damage dealer fights at close range with blades or at long range with magic. And "the cool thing about a video game is that you can experiment with the character you play and start over if you don't enjoy it" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

He draws two lessons from the memory, and they are the hinge between the biography and the theory. The first is that in life "we don't really have that choice" of race, but the viewer should "metaphorically paint a picture" for themselves: what character are you playing, and what did that character's choices unlock? The second, from the faction episode, is a reflection prompt: notice how identity influences opportunity and potential, and how attachment to a single political, religious or social ideology (a "digital religion") makes it hard to reach maximum enjoyment. Both lessons connect to Part II of this book. The character is Koe's game-vocabulary name for the identity, and the guild requirements are a picture of something Chapter 3 described abstractly: who you are determines which doors are even visible.

#### Why games order attention: four shared properties

The theoretical core of the metaphor is a claim about attention. To the question "how do we gain control of our attention," Koe answers: "by treating life like the game it is." He observes that his generation is "notorious for transferring obsession from video games to real life or just business success," and he gives the reason. Games, business and "constructions of the external world, in other words the Matrix," share four properties (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21):

1. They "present a desirable hierarchy of goals."
2. They "have a structure that frames your attention."
3. They "introduce a challenge to narrow your attention further."
4. They "require a player to have the skill that meets the requirements to play the game."

These four properties are the backbone of everything else in the chapter. The hierarchy of goals is the element developed in Chapter 8, now reread as the structure of a game. The frame (with its rules, mechanics and feedback) is the subject of section 10.2. The challenge and the skill requirement, taken together, become the skill–challenge balance of section 10.5. The list therefore works as a table of contents for Koe's whole theory of the game.

Notice what kind of claim this is. Koe is not saying that life resembles a game in some loose, inspirational way. He is saying that games, businesses and society are the same kind of object, structures that order attention, and that this is why the obsession of a teenage gamer can be transferred whole to a business. The Matrix of Chapter 1 appears here not only as the source of conditioning but as a game in its own right, with its own hierarchy of goals, frame, challenge and skill requirements. The difference between being played by it and playing your own game is not the presence or absence of structure; it is who designed the structure.

In a 2024 formulation that opens his productivity system "under the philosophy of the 4-Hour Workday," Koe compresses the point into a single phrase: "humans find meaning in stories, humans love playing games, why? Because they present a clear hierarchy of goals that our mind can adopt as an **indistractable frame**" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The indistractable frame is his coinage for what a game offers the mind: a goal structure clear enough that distractions have nowhere to land. The 4-Hour Workday, treated in Chapter 12, is in this reading an attempt to build such a frame around a few hours of work.

#### Playing rather than being played

From the four properties follows the principle that gives the metaphor its practical use: "What few people understand is that you can create a game out of any situation in life as long as you can mold your mind to create the certain aspects that a game entails." If that game is fun to you, "life becomes more enjoyable because you start **playing rather than being played**" (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The phrase is one of Koe's coinages and it has a precise sense. Being played means occupying a role in someone else's structure: a hierarchy of goals you did not choose, rules you did not set, feedback designed to keep you inside. Playing means supplying those elements yourself. The condition is mental: you "mold your mind" to provide the hierarchy, the frame and the challenge, which is why this principle depends on everything Part III said about attention as something you direct.

Koe's examples are deliberately small. He used to hate walks, even though he knew they were healthy, until he created rules for them. He does not like watching football: "I don't like watching sports, I just don't like football, I don't understand it, and that is the exact point, I don't understand the game." If he knew the game and could direct his attention to follow it, he says, he might enjoy it. And if your partner likes looking at antiques on a Saturday afternoon while you drag your feet, notice what she is doing: she has made a game of it ("I like the price on this one, I like the look of this one") and is in "some degree of a flow state." You can do the same. Zoom out, gain perspective and create your own game, "even if it's as simple as finding the stupidest rock you can find or the stupidest antique," so that you both have fun playing your own games (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The examples teach two different things. The football example says that boredom is often a failure of understanding: a situation is dull because you cannot see its goals, rules and moves, and learning the game is one way to make it absorbing. The antique-shop example says something more radical: when you cannot or do not want to learn someone else's game, you can invent one on top of the same situation. Both are applications of the four properties: the first supplies the missing frame by learning it, the second by creating it.

#### Society's default game

If everything is a game, then so is the life most people are already living. "Everything is a game. Society is a game and you know how to win": go to school, get a job, retire. That is what you have practiced your whole life unless you consciously chose to break out of it. "If you don't think you're on it and you haven't made the conscious choice to break out of it, then you're just unaware that you're on that path." Hence the conclusion: you have to learn to create your own games (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

This is the **default path** of Chapter 2 seen through the metaphor, and the metaphor adds something to the diagnosis. In the language of Chapter 1, the default path is an assigned hierarchy of goals. In the language of this chapter, it is a game with clear win conditions that you are already playing, competently, without having chosen it. That is why it is hard to leave: it has every property that makes a game absorbing, and the only thing wrong with it is that you did not design it.

Koe develops the point in 2025 with an image of levels. In a game you start with the goal in mind (win) and then execute quests in the order your experience allows: level 1, level 2, and beyond. From a higher level you look back with all your knowledge and skill and devise how to reach the next goal. "The higher level you are, the more fun life becomes because you get to choose the challenging yet meaningful goal you take on next. It is not assigned to you as if you were in a tutorial phase." This is why life can feel out of control: you reached level 10 (childhood, school, job) and now you are stuck, and "the game isn't fun anymore because the game makers don't benefit from you going to a higher level, so they incentivize you to stay there." You end up in a loop of boredom and anxiety: the tasks are repetitive and mindless, and any further challenge overwhelms you "because you do not know how to learn." His conclusion: "The most important boss fight of your life is to pursue your own path" (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md`, 2025-12-21).

Three elements of this passage will return. The **tutorial phase** (the stage in which goals are assigned rather than chosen) reappears in section 10.2 and in the critique of employment in section 10.7. The **loop of boredom and anxiety** is the subject of section 10.5. And the claim that people are stuck "because you do not know how to learn" anticipates Part VI. The "game makers" are not named; the passage reads as a reference to the institutions and incentives Chapter 2 described, and should be taken as a metaphor for incentive structures rather than as a claim about a coordinated design.

#### Player and opponent

A 2026 formulation adds a strategic dimension. "In the game of life, you are both the opponent and the player. Every decision you make changes your own position, and most people are just completely blind to where they are." If the average person's life were a chess match, he argues, "they probably have gotten into a game-winning move before, but they didn't even realize it… it was just unconscious to them." They lacked the "cognitive flexibility or architecture" to know when they could execute a specific tactic for a big reward. "That's why strategy has to come first." Most people "spend zero time strategizing. They don't engage in intentional strategic thinking." His diagnostic question: "When you're just going about your life, are you actually thinking or are you just reacting to thoughts and emotions?" His examples of such missed moves are a side business, a creator business, an aesthetic physique, health (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

Being both player and opponent means that the obstacle in the game is not only external. Your own previous decisions created your current position, and your own blindness to it is what keeps winning moves invisible. The passage belongs to the late strand of the corpus on strategic thinking (Chapter 17), and it will reappear in section 10.6, where the same video argues that comfort prevents strategic depth and calls for "tactical stress."

A related image, from 2023, explains a familiar illusion about one's own skill: "Even if I think I'm good at it, it's usually because I'm playing against a **low level character**." If you make $10,000 a month surrounded by people who make $10,000 a month, you feel you play the game well. Then you are exposed to someone making a million a month ("yes, it is a thing") and "it's like, oh, I suck at this game of business" (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04). The point is not that comparison is bad. It is that your sense of skill is calibrated by the level of the opponents you happen to meet, so a narrow environment produces an inflated self-assessment. This is the game-language version of a theme from Chapter 4: exposure to higher levels is one of the main triggers of identity change.

#### Feeling lost as a level problem

The metaphor also reinterprets one of the most common complaints in the corpus, feeling lost. "It's like a video game: when you're lost you're at a point where the next quest is not available to you. You're like this far from hitting the next level, and you can't get to the next quest until you hit that level. So you need to gain experience. You need to just try things, you need to kill a bunch of monsters and do a bunch of random side quests in order to, in our case, gain the clarity on what to pursue fully and get into that flow state that a game provides by pursuing a quest" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10).

The reframe changes what being lost implies. In the ordinary reading, being lost means something is wrong with you, or that you must think harder until you discover your purpose. In the game reading, it means you are slightly under-leveled for the next quest, and the remedy is not reflection but experience: small, varied actions (side quests) that accumulate the level the next quest requires. This is consistent with the experimental, subtractive account of wanting given in Chapter 7 (you never know exactly what you want; exposure reveals it), and it explains why Koe treats action as a prerequisite of clarity rather than its consequence.

How do you recognize the next quest when it unlocks? Koe offers a heuristic. In the game, when you first complete certain quests, learn to fly or do your first dungeon, it is new and you are extremely excited to do it *right then*. You would not procrastinate on that "work," because you know exactly what to do, it is the only thing on your mind, and it is a new and fun challenge. In the real world, "if you find this one thing you may even pull an all-nighter or let other important things in your life that you thought were important go," realizing that this matters more, and "that's a great hint at where you should be going in life" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The signal is the disappearance of procrastination together with a spontaneous reordering of priorities. It is a heuristic, not a guarantee; the same symptoms describe a compulsion, and section 10.3 will show Koe drawing that line for games themselves.

#### The character and the player

Underneath all of these images is a single principle: "Life is a game and your character determines the outcome." The first realization you need, Koe says, is that "your character is malleable": you can change, "no matter how painful or no matter how much your mind tells you that you can't," and no matter how much you want to cling to the current comforts of your life "that are causing more destruction than you think" (**Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19).

In his lexicon, **character** is his game-vocabulary term for the concept of self: who you think you are, the amalgamation of ideas, beliefs, experiences and information from which you interact with reality. The **player** is the same thing seen from the side of action: the self-concept that perceives the situation and chooses the moves.

The argument for why the character matters is a chain. Learning is the foundation of the human experience. When we are young we do not know better: "we learn these things just through repetition, they are conditioned into our psyche, and a concept of the self is created that we didn't really have much control over." Then: "learning through any medium influences our thoughts and our thoughts influence our behavior," so "anything you learn or consume is going to impact either directly or indirectly how you act and move within this world and what opportunities you perceive as available to you." Therefore "winning the game of life, business or any present moment situation you are in is dependent on how your character perceives and acts within that specific situation," and "if we want to achieve or create the life of our dreams then we have to create the character or the player that will lead to that outcome" (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The chain restates, in compressed form, the model of Parts I and II: conditioning installs a self-concept; the self-concept filters perception and steers behavior; perception and behavior determine outcomes. What the game metaphor adds is the word *create*. In *World of Warcraft* the character is something you spend two hours building on purpose. In life it was built for you by repetition before you could choose. The project of the game metaphor is to reverse that order.

Koe connects this project to a quotation he presents as Csikszentmihalyi's: "at this point in history it should be possible for an individual to build a self that is not simply the outcome of biological drives and cultural habits but a conscious personal creation." In the 2022 video he introduces the WoW story with that quote and names its author; in the 2024 video the quote appears without attribution. Around it he places a blunter claim. You cannot go from a 9-to-5 you hate to millions from your life's work instantly, "but there is a quest you can create and follow in your life to reach any goal you want" (weight loss, good looks, charisma, money, enjoyable work), and "the only thing standing in the way is you." You are not where you want to be because you did not make the choices that lead to a purposeful career, fulfilling relationships, a healthy and aesthetic body: "You right now are the manifestation of your past choices" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21; `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11).

The last sentence is a strong formulation and should be read in context. It sits next to a long account in Chapters 1 and 2 of how much of a person's character was *not* chosen. The two claims are reconciled by the time frame: the starting character was assigned; what you do with it from the moment you become aware of the assignment is your responsibility. "The manifestation of your past choices" is a statement about agency going forward, not a denial of conditioning.

How the character is actually created, the method Koe gives, depends on the mechanics of the game, so it is reconstructed at the end of section 10.2.

#### Games as a school of agency

In December 2025 Koe added a different reason to take games seriously: they are where agency is practiced. He quotes "the book *Games*" without naming its author: "In ordinary practical life we usually take the means for the sake of the ends. But in games we can take up an end for the sake of the means. Playing games can be a motivational inversion of ordinary life." And: "painting lets us record sights, music lets us record sounds, stories let us record narratives, and games let us record agencies." His adaptation: "Agency is art. You develop agency by practicing other people's agencies until you can create your own." You play by the rules until you can create your own rules, and "the most important high agency trait is knowing when to break free of conformity" (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md`, 2025-12-21).

**Complementary context:** the book is C. Thi Nguyen's *Games: Agency as Art* (2020), a work of philosophy arguing that games are an art form whose medium is agency itself: the designer specifies goals, abilities and obstacles, and the player temporarily adopts the agency so specified. The "motivational inversion" refers to what Nguyen calls striving play, in which a player adopts a goal (winning) for the sake of the activity of pursuing it.

Koe's use of the idea is an adaptation in a specific direction. Nguyen's point is about the value of temporarily adopting designed agencies; Koe turns it into a developmental sequence: imitate other people's agencies, internalize them, then design your own. That sequence matches the account of "intelligent imitation" in Chapter 1 and the "practice other people's agencies until you can create your own" applies equally to how he describes learning to write (Part VIII). There is also a terminological tension in the same video, which the corpus records: the video's title and earlier sections call agency "the most important skill," while this passage calls it "not a skill or a trait, but an art form." Koe does not reconcile the two labels. Later still (2026) he would downgrade agency to "not the only one thing," one ingredient among five; Chapter 35 follows that evolution.

The "motivational inversion" also clarifies what the whole chapter is attempting. In ordinary practical life, work is a means to ends (money, status); in games, the end is adopted for the sake of the activity. Koe's proposal, in effect, is to import the second attitude into the first domain: to adopt the end (the vision, the goal hierarchy) partly for the sake of the absorbing activity it makes possible. Section 10.4 will give this attitude its technical name, autotelic.

#### The great modern game

If life is a game, which game should a person in the present era play? Koe's answer, repeated over three years with growing elaboration, is the internet, and above all social media used as a place to learn, build and sell.

In 2025 he calls it by name. He loves social media first because it is where attention is: you will not build your life's work with radio ads, TV or handwritten letters to prospects; "you're going to write content, obviously." But beyond being an accessible, low-cost, low-risk vessel, "learning and agency are baked in. Social media, the internet is the **great modern game**." You can study other people's agencies and their content, guides and courses; you can experiment in public and get direct feedback; you quickly identify what works; you are forced to learn a future-proof skill stack (writing, persuasion, marketing, sales, storytelling); and you have to truly learn whatever you want to talk about (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md`, 2025-12-21).

Read against the four properties, the claim is precise. Social media supplies a hierarchy of goals (followers, customers, income), a frame (platforms with their formats), a challenge (competition for attention), a skill requirement (the skill stack) and, above all, the element that real life usually lacks: fast, direct feedback. It is also a place where "practicing other people's agencies" is literally possible, because their work is public.

The earlier formulations show the same idea from other angles. In 2023: "Social media is your public school, public resume and public portfolio. It holds the **holy trifecta of the good life**: learning, building and selling." You "cultivate your value by learning new knowledge; you solidify your value by building new projects; you distribute your value by selling those projects. All three result in a more valuable skill set and mindset" (**Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`, 2023-11-12). In 2024, in a video on dopamine, he lists three "syntropic" uses: learning almost anything (challenging your understanding and seeking new perspectives "to get closer to truth," especially with longer content); building a business or independent income with "indirect access to 5.5 billion people" you can help "in exchange for another form of value like money or attention," which can lead more people toward long-term gratification in "a ripple effect"; and finding employers, investors, team members or connections, because "creating high value content does the heavy lifting for you." All three "are syntropic: they take time and effort, they reverse the entropy and chaos you are so used to by providing meaningful inputs and outputs; they are earned dopamine," leading to "greater complexity and order" so that you "spot more opportunities, solve more problems, create more value and profit in a meaningful way." He frames the list with the Alan Watts line that "sensible people get paid for doing what they enjoy doing," and with his own phrase "paid to be yourself" (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24).

The word **syntropic** is Koe's: it names the opposite of the entropic, chaos-increasing uses of media that Chapter 5 analyzed and Chapter 11 will treat in detail. The distinction does not lie in the platform but in the use: the same app can be cheap dopamine or earned dopamine depending on whether you consume or learn, build and sell.

In 2025, in a video arguing that the creator economy is changing under AI, he places social media in an evolutionary frame: "Evolution is creative and thus it solves problems." A long-brewing set of problems (work you hate, a lifestyle you do not control, lack of access to opportunity and income) found social media as a solution, although people see it as toxic. Its underappreciated good sides are three: anyone can learn ("intelligence on tap," especially with AI); anyone can create (no permission needed to post who you are, what you do and why); anyone can be discovered (no publisher, job board or record label needed to write a book, find work or make music). "Your personal brand is your public resume" (**Source:** `The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md`, 2025-02-16).

In 2023 he gave the same idea a more modest shape, as a step in a method for recreating oneself: "turn your valuable pursuits into contributions." Most people limit themselves to college or local opportunities until they get online and realize "this is infinite." Social media is "a public forum where you can share your knowledge online and share products and services in exchange for other value." For most people value means money until "the superficial money problem" is solved, but it can be anything. His example: with a million followers he could message a retreat or resort offering promotion in exchange for a free week, as they do with influencers, although he says he has not done it and that it feels weird (**Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). And in 2022, at the beginning of the series, he gave the material precondition: no-code tools. Coders "made their own jobs in a sense" by creating drag-and-drop website builders; anyone can take what they are curious about, build a website, an email list and free social accounts ("it's all free") and attract an audience by learning psychology, how to catch attention, and how to "dissect the social media platform." Gaining followers "is not just posting content: you have to get eyes on your profile" (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21).

Put in order, the four years show an idea that did not change in substance but kept gaining layers: free tools (2022); a forum for exchanging knowledge for value and the trifecta of learning, building and selling (2023); a syntropic counterweight to entropy (2024); and an evolutionary solution and "great modern game" with agency built in (2025). Parts VII through IX develop the practical side: what to write, how to grow, how to build a personal brand. Here the point is the one the metaphor requires: among all the games available, Koe recommends one whose structure maximizes learning, feedback and the transfer of value, and which, unlike most games, produces real rather than virtual progress.

### 10.2 Frame, Rules, Feedback and the Map

#### One metaphor, several versions

Before taking the game apart, it helps to see that Koe has assembled it differently at different times. The metaphor is constant from 2022 to 2025, but each version reassigns which part of life corresponds to which part of the game. The record of these versions is a refinement, not a series of contradictions:

| Date | Version | What it emphasizes |
|---|---|---|
| 2022-06 | Video games are addictive because they hand you a hierarchy of goals, like a quest | The hierarchy of goals |
| 2022-12 ("How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game") | Macro game (open world, infinite paths) and micro games with three components: hierarchy of goals, frame of the game (rules, mechanics, feedback), balance between anxiety and boredom; then "create your character"; seven-point recap | The full mechanics |
| 2023-09 ("Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix)") | Four perspectives: psychology, metaphysics, self-help, business | Range of the metaphor |
| 2024-01 (*The Art of Focus* summary) | Macro game = vision as goal, life's work as path, micro games as leveling; metagame vs mind game | Vision and life's work as game structure |
| 2024-05 | Anti-goals check the five boxes of flow: challenge, skill, clarity, feedback, rules | Rules as constraints |
| 2025-05 ("How To Gamify Your Life") | Design the game: goal hierarchy, rules (what you won't sacrifice), quantifiable tasks as feedback; stay at the edge of the unknown | A design procedure |
| 2025-12 | Six components (including boss, mission, quests, rules) and a "force field" | Mentioned in the corpus record; not developed in this chapter |

The 2023 video lays out the ambition of the metaphor most explicitly. It explains why life is a video game from four perspectives, in order: psychology (flow, psychic entropy, the skill–challenge match), metaphysics (Donald Hoffman's interface theory of perception and the idea of a survival-based simulation, treated in Chapter 1), self-help (stack gold, acquire skills, purpose–process–priority, behavior change) and business, "the most practical" (the creator economy and the one-person business). Its promised outcomes are a list of the chapter's themes: make progress toward goals and fall in love with the process, tap into optimal states of consciousness, stop taking life so seriously, create your ideal future, "reinvent yourself or the character that plays the game of life," and leverage that character to pursue your life's work and earn a meaningful income (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05).

What follows reconstructs the mechanics mostly from the 2022 version, which is the most complete, and folds in the later ones where they add a piece.

#### The macro game: an open-world strategy game

Koe introduces "the macro game of life" through open-world strategy games like *World of Warcraft*, whose recognizable patterns are "stacking gold, choosing a profession, leveling up your character and the progression that goes along with those things." He knows that "life is a video game" is a familiar line, and proposes to "take it a bit deeper" through three points: the game is already programmed (the collective psyche, treated in Chapter 2), there are infinite paths, and leveling up increases the complexity of the self (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

In *The Art of Focus* (2024) the open-world image becomes a full allegory. In the game you choose race, class and appearance; you are launched into a starting zone; you are taught the fundamentals, rules and mechanics; you practice and level up, beginning as the main character; you unlock skills, traits, profession options, dungeons for specific loot, membership in a guild, and flight to travel faster. Those choices determine your opportunities and whether you enjoy playing: you may dislike a support role but love playing a tank and leading. And then the decisive difference: "in real life we can make the choice to start our game over at any point." You can change who you are and what you pursue, and stack the resources (skills, network, environment) to achieve that new reality (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The "restart" is the game-language version of Chapter 4's thesis that identity can change, and it carries the same caveat that chapter developed: restarting does not erase the experience accumulated so far. That caveat is built into the next image.

#### Infinite paths: the birth–now–death graphic

The second point of the macro game is that "there are infinite paths that you can take in an open-world strategy game." Koe describes a graphic, shown on screen in 2022 and again in 2024. On the left is birth, on the right death, and a vertical line in the middle marks "now." The path traced from birth to now is your experience. To the right of "now," darker circles show the many paths you can take toward a green dot labeled "success," or "winning the game of the external world." The transcripts describe the graphic only verbally, so its exact layout cannot be reconstructed with certainty; what is clear is the asymmetry between a single path behind and many paths ahead.

The game equivalent is *World of Warcraft*'s choices: appearance, class (warrior, mage, rogue), profession, quest path, solo or group play, which guild to join. The life equivalent is choosing which sport to play growing up or, for someone starting a business, which skill to learn or which business model to choose: "they're all viable but they take a different skill set and they lead people down a different path" (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The point is anti-dogmatic. There is no single correct path to "winning the external game," which is one reason Koe distrusts one-size-fits-all advice: a path is viable relative to a skill set, and the skill set relative to a character. The graphic also gives a precise meaning to "experience": it is the path already traveled, which is fixed, and which determines from which point the next paths branch.

#### Infinite mountains

He adds a second image for the same idea. Five people stand at the bottom of a mountain; they look up and each draws a different path to the first peak. Life is "infinite mountains" to climb. "Once you reach that peak you can look down and you can help people navigate that path up in a better way." And: "That's what business is: helping people solve their problems or climb the mountain in a faster way to achieve their desired outcome, which is reaching that peak that you've reached" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The image links the game metaphor to the economic model of Part X. The peak you have just reached is a problem you have solved; the people below are those who still have it; the business is the guidance you can offer. It is the same logic as "solve your own problems and sell the solution" from Chapter 9, now drawn as a landscape. The adjective *infinite* matters: each peak reveals another, which anticipates the infinite games of section 10.7.

#### The external game and the internal game

A 2023 formulation divides the macro game in two. "Life is a game that houses infinite games," and two macro games are the most fruitful. The **external game** is what you choose to do with your life: the self-generated goals you pursue that impact humanity, your life's work, what you dedicate time, focus and money to building "as a modality for value creation." The **internal game** is philosophical mastery: "if your external pursuits are not backed by a philosophical sense of mastery they will remain superficial and meaningless." The connecting principle: "for every goal you pursue outward there is a lesson you must unlock inward" (**Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19).

This division prevents a misreading of everything above. "Winning the game of the external world" is not the whole game. Each external achievement has an internal counterpart (a fear faced, a belief revised, a level of mind reached), and without it the achievement stays on the surface. Chapter 9 made the same point with the levels of purpose, and Part XIV will make it with the levels of consciousness: the outward pursuit is the vehicle through which the inward development happens.

#### Macro game and micro games

Having described the open world, the 2022 video turns to a smaller scale: "we can talk about the micro games of life on a situation to situation basis." The micro game has three components: (1) set a hierarchy of goals; (2) understand the frame of the game (rules and mechanics, together with feedback and practice); and (3), "arguably the most important," the delicate balance between anxiety and boredom, that is, the match between skill and challenge. After those three, the video explains how to create your character by integrating them with self-education and self-reflection (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11).

The 2024 book summary relates the two scales more tightly. "The macro game of your life is your vision as the goal, life's work as the path, and micro games as the way to level up along the way." The micro games are everyday situations: approach each with an open mind, create your own goal, and try to win the games that are conducive to your vision. "Everything else is a distraction; do not play those games, and if you do, learn from them fast" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

In the same text Koe gives the two scales their abstract names. "From a macro perspective this **metagame** is society; from a micro perspective everything is a **mind game**: every situation has a series of goals, problems and paths in between that every individual perceives according to their identity or perspective." If you mold your mind to see a situation as a game (create your own goal, construct a path, decipher the most advantageous actions), "even mundane situations can become enjoyable." Games are enjoyable because they focus the mind and eliminate distractions that could otherwise replicate until boredom or anxiety appear, and they do so by presenting a "descending series of goals," each with a certain challenge (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

"Mind game" here has a specific sense: a situation perceived as a structure of goal, problem and path. Elsewhere in the corpus the same phrase designates the battle between the lower self and the higher self; the two senses should not be confused. The structure goal–problem–path is the principle of Chapter 8 applied at the scale of a single situation, and it explains why perception depends on identity: two people in the same room see different games because they bring different goals.

The resulting architecture can be summarized as follows:

| Scale | Koe's name | Goal | Path | What counts as a distraction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Macro | Macro game of life (society = the metagame) | Your vision | Your life's work | Any game not conducive to the vision ("do not play those games, and if you do, learn from them fast") |
| Micro | Micro games / mind games | A goal you create for the situation | The most advantageous actions you decipher | Whatever breaks the descending series of goals |

#### The first element: a hierarchy of goals with locked quests

The first component of the micro game is the hierarchy of goals, which Chapter 8 developed in detail. The game metaphor adds one property that the abstract treatment lacks: some goals are *locked*.

"Games are a hierarchy of goals": you need to know how to win (the main goal), how to progress through each sub-goal, and what character you need (upgrades, skills, traits) to reach each goal, each of which demands a higher level of skill because it presents a higher challenge. When you start a game you go through a **tutorial phase** that introduces the rules, goals and skills. An absolute beginner "has no business" challenging a higher-level player: "the game is locked, you're not able to accept that quest." Applied to life: you cannot start a business because you have not learned the skills to understand how. The remedy is to "learn and build according to your skill level," whereas many try to learn and build according to a millionaire's skill level. "You have to start where you are." And, as the conclusion of the passage: to get immersed in your game of life, you must first create it (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

The locked quest is a useful corrective to the "big irrational goals" of Chapter 7. The big goal sets direction, but the quest you can accept today is the one your current level unlocks. Koe's later separation (big goals for direction, small goals and near challenges for clarity and action) is the same principle in non-game language; section 10.5 returns to it.

#### The frame of the game: a camera

The second component is the frame. "Your perspective or your worldview is the frame in which you view reality. It's like a camera, so the field of view, even if the background is blurred, constrains what registers in the frame itself." By molding your perspective in any situation, "you allow yourself to perceive what used to be a huge problem as a minor road bump." And if your attention is narrowed on your hierarchy of goals, it is difficult for a distraction "to register in the camera itself" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

In a game, the frame is provided by the game itself; in reality, by your perspective (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The camera image condenses the theory of perception from Chapter 6 (the goal as a lens that decides what enters awareness) into a single picture. It has two consequences. The first is that the size of a problem depends on the frame: what fills the whole picture in a wide, unfocused frame is a road bump in a frame centered on a goal. The second is that distraction-resistance is not mainly a matter of willpower; it is a matter of how narrow and well-aimed the frame is.

**Frame of the game** is one of Koe's coined terms. In a video game, he says, two things create the frame and the order of consciousness inside it: rules and mechanics. Feedback, the third piece, tells you how well the frame is holding.

#### Rules: the here and now with the goal in the back of the mind

"Every single video game has a set of rules that you must follow." The function of rules is to let us "use our limited conscious attention or focus to remain focused on the here and now while keeping our vision or the end goal of winning in the back of our head" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21; `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11).

The sentence describes a dual structure of attention that recurs across the corpus: a distant goal held in the background and a narrow present held in the foreground. Rules are what make that structure stable, because they remove from consideration everything the game does not allow. In 2024 Koe adds a shorter justification: "Rules prevent you from cheating, they make the game worth playing" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

He also gives a neurochemical account of why the structure feels good, and it should be read carefully because he marks part of it as his own interpretation. "In psychology," he says, projecting into the future or desiring something you do not have releases dopamine, while focusing on the here and now releases "the here and now chemicals like oxytocin, serotonin and other neurochemicals like that." He then cites Steven Kotler's *The Art of Impossible*: "it doesn't say this directly, but it mentions that flow, the flow state, is kind of a neurochemical cocktail of all of these different neurotransmitters going to your brain," and Koe claims that this cocktail "can be accomplished by exactly this," that is, by the combination of a future goal (dopamine) with present focus enforced by rules (here-and-now chemicals) (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

Three layers need separating here. The general distinction between dopamine as a "future" chemical and serotonin and oxytocin as "here and now" chemicals is the one Koe takes elsewhere from Lieberman and Long's *The Molecule of More* (Chapter 11). The idea of flow as a neurochemical cocktail is Kotler's. The link between the two (rules plus a background goal produce the cocktail) is Koe's own hypothesis, and he says so ("it doesn't say this directly"). It should be read as an explanatory model, not as an established finding.

**Complementary context:** in Kotler's popular account, the neurochemistry of flow involves norepinephrine, dopamine, endorphins, anandamide and serotonin; oxytocin is mentioned in some of his descriptions of group flow. The "here and now" grouping of serotonin and oxytocin comes from *The Molecule of More*, which contrasts dopamine (anticipation of what one does not yet have) with chemicals associated with enjoyment of what is present. Neither source, as Koe himself notes for Kotler, states that game rules produce the cocktail.

#### Rules in real life: values and self-made constraints

What are the rules of the game of life? Koe answers at two scales. At the macro scale, "in the real world your rules are your values." At the micro scale you can create rules for a particular situation: on a walk, never step on a crack, or walk 2,500 steps. When you act in alignment with your values and constrain your attention to what aligns with them, "life becomes a bit more enjoyable." And on the micro scale of everyday situations, "clarity comes from eliminating environmental distractions," which "pretty much means creating a frame": the usual deep-work advice of closing browser tabs, setting a timer such as a Pomodoro, having clear steps for your work, and using noise-cancelling headphones (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

In 2024 he states the same mapping at the level of the whole life: "in life the rules are what you deem important and meaningful: your priorities, your values" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

The equation "rules = values" is significant. Values in this account are not abstract virtues but constraints on attention: they decide which moves are permitted. And the micro rules (no cracks, 2,500 steps) show what rules do to experience. They add an arbitrary but clear structure to an activity that had none, and that structure is enough to turn a disliked walk into a game. This is how Koe stopped hating walks.

#### Mechanics: channeling senses, thoughts and experience

The second element of the frame is mechanics. A game you have not played is not fun: "your aim is going to suck," you practice on low-level challenges, and you are awestruck by top players. "Every game has a specific way for you to channel more of your senses, thoughts and prior experience to play it better." In video games this means pressing series of keys, buttons or mouse clicks; in board games, the effectiveness, creativity or forward thinking of strategy; in sports, conditioning your body and moving it in accordance with the goal. "In all of the above it's your perception of the situation or your frame of the game that allows you to choose in a way that moves you towards winning" (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). In the 2024 version: "mechanics are skill-based." At first you feel clumsy with the keyboard, cannot move well or land shots, but you practice and improve as the challenges increase. And in life, "the mechanics are the skills and habits you use to achieve the prioritized goals in your life" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

How do you improve mechanics? By practice, and specifically by the kind of repetitive practice that games reward. In *World of Warcraft*, people log in every day to perform a repetitive series of tasks: farm gold, create armor, mine ore, farm experience by running dungeons over and over. "You have to program the specific mechanic in your brain to the point of where you're getting results with less effort, in other words habit formation." And then: if you can "automate to an extent the decisions that you make on a daily basis that are conducive with your hierarchy of goals and achieving your end vision, then success kind of becomes inevitable" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The "kind of" is Koe's own hedge and is worth keeping. The claim is not that habits guarantee success but that habits aligned with the goal hierarchy remove the daily decisions through which most people drift away from their goals. Chapter 13 develops this as routine; Chapter 15 develops the mechanics of skill acquisition.

#### Feedback: when the frame widens

The third piece is feedback. "Another form of information is feedback," and it matters "to know how well you're doing in the game," because games are "ordering our consciousness, giving us structured information to pay attention to, and our mind likes that." The mechanism of failure is described precisely: when you do something and know it was a wrong move or a mistake, "your perspective or your frame widens to the point of allowing distractions to penetrate that field," and if you give your attention to the distraction you go "off the game" and enjoy it less (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

This is the first appearance of an idea that becomes central in section 10.5: the frame is not fixed. It narrows when things go well and widens when they go badly, and a wide frame is the doorway for distraction. A mistake is dangerous not mainly because of its cost but because of what it does to the frame: it turns attention toward the self ("I messed that up"), and through that opening the rest of the world comes in.

The three elements and their real-life equivalents can now be placed side by side:

| Element of the game | Function in the game | Equivalent in life (Koe) |
|---|---|---|
| Frame | Determines what registers | Your perspective or worldview |
| Hierarchy of goals | Narrows the mind toward winning | Vision, long-term and short-term goals |
| Rules | Keep attention on the here and now with the goal in the background; prevent cheating | Values and priorities (macro); self-made constraints (micro) |
| Mechanics | Channel senses, thoughts and prior experience into moves | Skills and habits |
| Feedback | Structured information on how you are doing | Signals of progress (quantifiable tasks completed); mistakes that widen the frame |

#### Designing the game: "gamify your life"

The mechanics above describe games as they are. The method Koe derives from them describes how to build one. Its theoretical starting point is a question he asks in 2024: why are video games enjoyable to the point of addiction, can we replicate that enjoyment for our own goals, and can we structure life so that we are so immersed we forget we are playing? His premise: "The mind craves order, and an ordered mind is a happy mind." Games have quests, leveling and skill development to narrow your mind on the big goal and eliminate distractions. He then quotes, without naming the author, a passage from Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow*: games "have rules that require the learning of skills, they set up goals, they provide feedback, they make control possible, they facilitate concentration and involvement by making the activity as distinct as possible from the so-called paramount reality of everyday existence." And he underlines one word: "The key word there is *designed*." The question to hold is: "How can I design a life, month, week and day that gives me a sense of control over my life?" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

**Complementary context:** "paramount reality" is a term from the phenomenological tradition (William James, and after him Alfred Schutz) for the everyday world of work and practical concerns, which people treat as "the" reality against which dreams, play and fiction are measured. Csikszentmihalyi's point in the quoted passage is that flow activities, games above all, are deliberately set apart from that everyday reality.

The 2025 video turns the question into a procedure. Its premise is that "your mind runs on a storyline, and games are preconstructed stories with certain mechanisms that narrow your focus and make progress enjoyable": a clear hierarchy of goals (you know how to win), direct feedback (you know when you are progressing), and rules that add challenge and skill development. "All of these are core components of flow psychology." That, he says, is why "social media companies and gaming companies and entertainment companies spend billions upon billions of dollars investing in research for how to replicate these mechanisms… so that we become addicted to it." Few people realize that you can study the same mechanisms and replicate them in your own life, "so you're actually making progress on something real rather than thinking you're making progress on something that's fake" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08).

The argument has a symmetry that is easy to miss. The mechanisms of flow are neutral; what changes is who designs them and toward what. The same design principles that make an app addictive can make a project absorbing. "Gamify your life" is the decision to stop being the target of someone else's game design and become the designer.

The design procedure has three steps, which Koe says many people write off as a self-help or productivity hack (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08):

1. **Create a hierarchy of goals** composed of an end goal, long-term goals and short-term goals: something out there you would want; a one-year goal that helps you get there; a one-month goal; a one-week goal; and further breakdowns.
2. **Create the rules of the game** by asking "what are you not willing to sacrifice in your life to make progress toward the end goal?" Your health, your relationships, working long hours? "This adds constraints. It makes the game creative": you can succeed without long hours and without sacrificing health or relationships. Most people do not choose that path because they do not think it is possible, or because they "don't know what they don't know" and will not admit it or learn it.
3. **Use quantifiable priority tasks as feedback loops**: write 1,000 words a day, read 10 pages, reach out to five potential clients. "Those are your levers." The ten-year, one-year and one-month goals give direction; the quantifiable task "is just how you get there," and completing it is "the feedback loop that lets you know you're making progress."

The second step deserves emphasis because it inverts a common intuition. Rules look like limitations on the pursuit of the goal, and in one sense they are; but Koe's claim is that the limitation is what makes the game *creative*. If you allow yourself to sacrifice health and relationships, the obvious path (grind) is available and you will take it. If you forbid it, you have to find a better path, and that search is where creativity comes in. The same logic appears in Chapter 8's treatment of constraints and in Chapter 12's four-hour limit on work.

#### Anti-goals as the rules of the game

A year earlier, in 2024, Koe had made the same move under a different name, **anti-goals**, and had argued that they turn life into a game because they "check the five boxes of flow" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12):

1. **Challenge**: a goal within reach that tests your skill.
2. **Skill**: if it is too low for the challenge you get anxious; if too high, bored, "indicating that you need to choose a greater or lesser challenge rather than give up." If you are anxious you are probably pursuing a goal that is too high; identify a goal before it, so that it is easier to achieve and to order your mind ("you may be projecting too far into the future"). If you are bored, you are either not pursuing a goal, pursuing the wrong one, or stuck after achieving a goal without setting another.
3. **Clarity**: "a hierarchy of greater to lesser goals makes it easier to start moving towards your vision."
4. **Feedback**: you know exactly when you are making progress, which feels good, and you do not feel trapped in repetitive tasks that lead nowhere.
5. **Rules**: "rules or boundaries frame how you perceive the world; your mind has more space to notice information that aids in the achievement of your goals." The anti-goals act as the rules.

The five boxes are an adaptation of flow theory (the transcript names no author), and they are a useful bridge between this section and the next three: challenge and skill belong to section 10.5, clarity and feedback to the mechanics above, and rules to the frame. The concept itself has several names in the corpus that refer to the same content (the sacrifices you refuse to make for a goal): "anti-goals" (2024), "constraints" (2025-01), "rules of the game" (2025-05) and "anti-standards" (2025-09), the last renamed, in Koe's words, "for the sake of cohesiveness." Chapters 7 and 8 treat the term's origin and its use in planning.

Note also the remedy for anxiety in box 2: pick a nearer goal rather than abandon the far one. It anticipates the heuristics of section 10.5 and shows that, for Koe, the cure for overwhelm is a change of level, not of direction.

#### The map and the light of awareness

Two more images from games complete the mechanics: the map and the skill tree. Both describe what experience does to the space of possibilities.

"In video games you have a map: the light of awareness reveals where you have been and what you have experienced." That knowledge lets you make better decisions. "The darkness of the map is the unknown," and you would not launch yourself too far from the known: "you must slowly puncture the unknown, let the experience normalize through skill acquisition and emotional management, and press forward." Koe draws a large conclusion from the image: "One purpose of life is to raise your consciousness, and this is only possible by living at your edge, embracing the story of life, the highs, lows, successes and catastrophic failures. The most miserable place you can be is in the middle" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05).

The image predates that video. In 2022, reviewing Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow* among seven books, Koe brought back "the analogy of a map" in a video game: where you are is bright and you are aware of everything in it; venture into the unknown or the darkness and shine "the light of awareness" on it, and it opens up and gets stored in your consciousness, so you can pull from that experience later. He connects this to the number of bits of information a person can consciously process in a lifetime (in this 2022 source, 185 billion): that number "is your potential as a human being," and the depth of information stored matches "your potential or your experience level in a game." If you go through the motions with a "very shallow and average existence," not making connections or diving deeper into areas where connections are made that few have discovered ("by doing and acting and building businesses and… getting fit in the gym"), you do not grow; if you do, "you start to operate at a much higher level and life becomes much more meaningful and fulfilling." He calls this the main lesson of the book (**Source:** `7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md`, 2022-04-23).

The figure for lifetime bits varies across the corpus (185 billion here; 125 or 128 billion in 2023–2024), and Chapter 6 discusses those numbers and their relation to Csikszentmihalyi's estimates. The variation does not affect the argument, which is qualitative: potential is the depth of experience you have actually processed, and exploration is how it grows.

In 2024 the map returns in a video on self-discipline. "When you turn your life into a game you become obsessed with progress. There are quests, there are challenges, there are dungeons," and depending on your level, places on the map "become bright": you can see them, navigate them, enter them and discover things you did not have and did not know existed. "So if you aren't pursuing new goals in your life and being self-disciplined, you're closing yourself off to so much experience in life" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12).

The map adds two ideas to the hierarchy of goals. The first is that parts of reality are literally invisible until you reach a certain level: not forbidden but dark. This is the locked quest seen from the side of perception. The second is the pace of exploration: slowly, by puncturing the edge, not by jumping into the middle of the darkness. Section 10.5 develops both under the name of living at your edge.

The phrase "the most miserable place you can be is in the middle" creates an apparent conflict with the rest of the chapter, because the same 2023 video places flow "right in the middle" of the skill–challenge graphic. The conflict is terminological and is resolved in section 10.5; for now it is enough to note that "middle" here means a lukewarm life that avoids both highs and lows.

#### The skill tree

The second image is the skill tree, in which choosing one skill opens branches to others. Koe uses it to argue that the domains of life gate one another. "The financial domain of life is a domain just like any area of life"; it is "intertwined with all other domains"; "it's spiritual, it's physical, it's mental." "Like in a skill tree in a video game where one skill can only go as high as the other allows it: I can't unlock the next level of mind if I don't unlock a certain level of finances, or I can't unlock a certain level of health" if "I can't afford the right foods," or do not have time and "have to pay for McDonald's every day." "There's specific thresholds that you have to reach in each domain of life, finances being one of them, to even reach a certain level in something else like spirituality; you have to overcome and integrate that part of life." Money is "value exchange," "connection with human beings or society," and "when you ignore the need for money you limit how far you can go in your mental, physical and even spiritual practices" (**Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`, 2023-11-12).

In 2025 the same image is used in the opposite direction. In a skill tree, some skills remain "grayed out" until you reach the right level and unlock the right sequence. So "you don't even know what you're capable of in business or how far you can go or how much you're missing out on because you haven't prioritized any part of your mind, your body, or your spirit. You're just focused on vocation" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19).

The two uses are complementary rather than contradictory. In 2023 the warning is aimed at the spiritually minded person who neglects money; in 2025 at the business-minded person who neglects mind, body and spirit. The common principle is interdependence with thresholds: progress in one branch is capped by the level of others. It is the game-language form of the "multi-dimensionally jacked" ideal of Chapter 40 and of the sequencing argument in Chapter 9 (body, then mind, then business, with each depending on the one below).

#### How to create your character

With the mechanics in place, the method for creating the character can be reconstructed. Koe gives it in two parts (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The first part is integration. Combine everything described so far: "an intrinsic hierarchy of goals"; the ability to frame life at macro and micro scales; rules created to narrow attention; and the consistent practice of "learn, practice, learn, practice, or learn, build, learn, build," which raises skill so you can take on higher challenges. The word *intrinsic* matters: the corpus distinguishes an intrinsic hierarchy of goals (your own, starting from curiosity) from an extrinsic one (imposed by culture or a company), and only the first creates a character of your own.

The second part is self-education and self-reflection. All information is at our fingertips; for most problems you can find a solution online or in a book. You find these ideas as you build a better life and run into problems, by consuming valuable information without getting distracted. "Sometimes you have to sift through dirt to find gold": you read things you were not interested in, and those who get bored doing this "will normally just distract themselves instead." Self-reflection, in turn, guides future decisions, because 100% certainty is impossible: the effects of actions are not immediate. You can compare yourself to other people's advice and results, but the only way to know for certain is to reflect and pivot, because the people who advise you operate from a different worldview and environment. His example: taking advice from Socrates "from hundreds of years ago" for today's environment, "where money rules the world," may not be practical; you still have to filter it through direct experience.

The method is the learn–build cycle of Part VI in miniature: integrate a structure, act within it, learn from what you meet, reflect on the results, adjust. Its originality lies less in the steps than in the claim that this cycle is how a self-concept is deliberately built. The character is not created by deciding who to be, but by repeatedly playing games designed around that decision and letting the results shape the player.

#### The author's seven-point recap

The 2022 video ends with a recap in Koe's own words, which he reused verbatim in a 2024 compilation. Its seven points summarize the theory of the game as he first formulated it (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21):

1. "Games are a way of ordering consciousness to the point of obsession."
2. "When you're obsessed you stop caring what people think and play to win according to your values."
3. "Games are enjoyable and every situation in life can be mentally molded into a game."
4. "If you want to avoid mental turmoil your skill needs to match the challenge that any situation presents."
5. "Your perspective will determine the information available to you, and if it's not structured you will misperceive it."
6. "Your self-concept is the player."
7. "With time you want to create the character that can win the games that it is best at."

Points 1 and 2 introduce a theme that sections 10.4 and 10.5 develop: obsession as the extreme of ordered consciousness, and its social consequence, freedom from other people's opinions. Point 4 is the skill–challenge match of section 10.5. Point 5 restates the frame. Points 6 and 7 restate the character. The last point contains a qualification that is easy to miss: not "the character that can win every game" but "the games that it is best at." The goal is not omnicompetence but a fit between a deliberately built character and the games chosen for it, which is the principle of "you are the niche" developed in Part VII.

### 10.3 Why Video Games Hook Us

#### The quest: a hierarchy of goals someone else built

Section 10.2 showed what games are made of. This section asks why that construction is so effective, and what that effectiveness reveals about ordinary life. Koe's first answer, from 2022, connects games to everything else people do: video games are addictive, and we do what we do in general, because "either culture or ourselves or our parents or other things have constructed a hierarchy of goals that allows us to actualize a specific vision that those goals stem from." "It's like a quest in a game: do this, do this, do this, and you will receive this vision," the vision you can imagine and the experience that comes with it (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

The passage generalizes the quest. A quest is a hierarchy of goals with a promised reward at the end; a career, a degree, a religion and a diet are quests in the same sense. What distinguishes them is not the structure but who built it, which is the distinction between assigned and self-generated goals from Chapter 1, restated in game language.

#### Two reasons, then five

In 2025, introducing a routine for deep work, Koe reduces the addictiveness of games to two reasons. (1) "They have a clear hierarchy of goals, and these each increase in challenge, which means they demand an increase in skill. So you know how to win and you become someone in the process. You have a **clear bridge**." (2) "They have rules and feedback loops. And these keep your attention in the game and minimize distraction." He mentions in passing that the previous week's video, on gamifying life as a whole, was "unfortunately not doing that well," and that this one applies the same ideas to deep work sessions (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

The phrase "you become someone in the process" is the important addition. A game does not only tell you what to do next; it changes who you are as you do it, because each level demands more skill. The "clear bridge" is a bridge between the present self and a future self, with every plank visible. Chapter 4 described identity change as the slow, uncertain construction of such a bridge; games are addictive, in this reading, because they make that construction certain and visible.

A year earlier, in a video on walking, he had given a longer list of five design features: (1) a clear hierarchy of goals (how to win, quests); (2) clear feedback for progress (leveling up, milestones); (3) rules that narrow attention and remove distractions; (4) tutorials to get you up to speed; (5) challenges matched to skill level, so you do not get anxious or overwhelmed. "In a nutshell, video games are addictive because they put us into the flow state," characterized by "absolute clarity, zero distractions, pure progress." You can tap into that state with most habits, and especially with walking: you know what to do, how and when; it is neither easy nor widely practiced, so it is meaningful; and novelty adds extra dopamine. To form and get addicted to a walking habit, he concludes, pull patterns and principles from video games into habit formation, which he says he did without realizing it (**Source:** `The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md`, 2024-06-16).

The two lists (five features in 2024, two reasons in 2025) do not contradict each other; the shorter one compresses the longer. Their common core is the same set of elements from section 10.2, and their common conclusion anticipates section 10.4: what games deliver is flow.

#### Projects as quests

The practical consequence is a way of seeing projects. "You can also view projects as quests in a video game": what makes games enjoyable is that they narrow your mind on the hierarchy of goals composing the quest. When you work this way, you begin to control your attention every morning, you move with purpose through the world, "and people notice it in your walk and how you hold yourself." Koe marks one difference: the game of life has "actual real pain," but the pleasure derived from it is much greater (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11).

In 2024 he adds a note about time: "Like a video game, you will have to progress from beginner to advanced by completing a series of quests to level up. It will take time." The sentence frames the guidelines of his productivity system, whose aim is "maximum enjoyment in your focus in your work" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18).

The project of Chapter 9 and the quest of this chapter are the same object. Chapter 9 called the project a lens, an anchor and a magnet; here it acquires a fourth function: it is the structure that makes work absorbing, because it contains the hierarchy of goals that a quest contains. The warning about time ("it will take time") and the warning about pain ("actual real pain") are two caveats that separate the quests of life from the quests of a game, and the next subsections examine that separation.

#### Virtual risk and real risk

Koe's 2023 video on the four perspectives states the difference between games and life most compactly. From the self-help perspective, "life is a game: stack gold, acquire skills, gain experience, unlock new levels and eventually get to a point where you have the resources to do whatever you want." When your operating frame is focused on the process that will actualize your future, "your focus is impenetrable and you live in a world of your own design." What is the difference between a video game and real life? "Virtual risk versus real risk." What is the same? Learning, practice and "becoming a dopamine junkie." He then maps the elements of games onto life (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05):

| In video games | In real life |
|---|---|
| Tutorials | Childhood |
| Professions | Career paths |
| Quests | Responsibilities |
| The unknown to explore | Potentials you are unaware of but which, once discovered, can drastically change how you think, act and work |

Understanding these patterns, he says, lets you correct your behavior in a way that is conducive to your future.

The table is useful mainly for its last row. The other three restate the default path in game terms; the last introduces the reason to play differently: there are regions of the map (potentials) you do not yet know exist, and they are reachable only by exploration. The difference in risk explains why so few people explore them: in a game, exploration costs nothing.

#### "My life sucked because I let it suck"

Koe's own past is his main illustration of what happens when the virtual game replaces the real one. One of his darkest periods, he says, did not feel dark at the time; it was his normal, and "my good days then are my terrible days now." Many people do not realize that their normal state is "bottom of the barrel." In college, taking online classes, he would start the day at 10 a.m., drive to McDonald's for two Sausage and Egg McMuffins and a large iced coffee with cream and sugar, feel like garbage, lie back down and binge *The Office* until he was hungry, then overeat again (eating out or frozen burritos). He saw the freedom of online classes as an excuse for self-destructive habits; he barely did his schoolwork, logged in at the last minute, and still passed with A's and B's ("that says a lot about the education system"). At night he played five to seven hours of *League of Legends*, with or without friends, numbing his mind to escape whatever he was hiding from (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22).

His analysis of that period turns the virtual/real distinction into a diagnosis. The difference between video games and real life is fake versus real risk: real pain, fear and sacrifice, which are what allow pleasure, love and results to exist. In games you fail without repercussion and respawn; in life you cannot hide behind a screen hoping to avoid the failure that growth requires. Games "trick the mind into thinking it's progressing": levels, skills, traits, unlocked dungeons and quests trigger the reward system, so we enjoy "diving into the fake unknown" for a false reward. "It's just all backwards." He was hiding from necessary failure and from the push into the unknown that brings true progress, getting his reward externally from games and food. Sleeping in, fast food and Netflix binges kept him in "a **drip-fed dopamine sedation**," so he felt no need to pursue anything greater. "Everything was fine because I let it be fine. My life sucked because I let it suck" (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22).

The argument is the reverse side of everything in section 10.2. The same mechanics that make games a model for designing life make them a substitute for it. A game provides the experience of progress (levels, unlocked content) without the reality of progress (skills that matter outside the game), and it provides the experience of exploring the unknown without its risk. Because the reward system responds to the experience, not the reality, a person can feel satisfied while going nowhere. "Fake risk / real risk" and "drip-fed dopamine sedation" are Koe's coinages for this mechanism; Chapter 11 treats it in the general vocabulary of cheap dopamine.

#### The defense test

Koe's later position on games is moderate in substance and hard in tone. In 2025 he recalled that in a previous video he had said "social media is the fast food of socialization" and "video games are the fast food of entertainment," and that people disliked it. His point, he explains, is not prohibition: he eats fast food sometimes, and that is fine because he understands its overall impact on his progress. There are games "that aren't inherently entropic to your mind." But for most people, and for his own past self clocking "12 hours a day of World of Warcraft, just ruining my life," it is not something to be defended. Then the heuristic: "If you have the desire to tell me or justify why video games are good for you, then that's kind of a surefire sign that they're not," because with a big-picture, nuanced understanding of where they sit in your life "you wouldn't feel the need to defend yourself." You only think that eating clean, or looking at McDonald's or video games with disgust, sounds stupid "because your standards are abysmal." Someone who values fitness finds it painful *not* to eat clean; eating clean is enjoyable to them (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14).

Two qualifications are in order. First, the self-quotation is inexact: in the videos of the corpus Koe called video games "the fast food of achievement," while the "fast food of entertainment" analogy was applied to Netflix. The variation is in the author's own formulation and does not change the argument. Second, the earlier record shows the same moderation without the harsh tone: in 2023 he said he played two to five hours of games a week and went out every two to four weeks, because his priorities were clear. The position is consistent (conscious moderation), with a rhetorical hardening over time.

The defense test is a heuristic, not an argument, and it should be evaluated as such. Its logic is that justification signals an unresolved conflict: someone at peace with a habit's place in their life does not need to defend it. That is often true, but the test can misfire, because people also defend things that are genuinely good for them when those things are attacked. The closing point about standards is more robust and connects to Chapter 11: what feels like deprivation from one level of standards feels like enjoyment from another.

#### You already focus for twelve hours a day

The most useful lesson Koe draws from games is not a warning but an observation about capacity. "You already have motivation; you can already focus for 12 hours a day when you're playing a video game or scrolling on your phone. It's automatic; you don't need to discipline yourself." But deep down you know you are ruining your life: "your subconscious knows that you could be doing better, and that's why you feel terrible." The remedy is to create a physical, mental and spiritual environment that makes working on your dreams seamless, and it has two prerequisites. First, understand that twelve-hour days are not always possible, feasible or worth it; you need the right phase of life, and understanding phases lets you guide yourself into a **season of intensity** in which you enjoy working that long. Second, remove the blockers that prevent you from seeing and wanting to play the video game of life: "your life needs to become more interesting than the games that you're already playing," including phone scrolling (winning "the game of cheap dopamine") and almost anything else with a game structure (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13).

The reframe is important for the rest of the book. The common explanation for why people do not work on their goals is a lack of discipline or motivation. Koe's explanation is a competition between games: attention flows to the most absorbing structure available, and for most people the most absorbing structure available is designed by someone else. The remedy is therefore not more willpower applied to an unabsorbing life, but a life redesigned to be more absorbing than the competing games.

He answers the obvious objection directly. "But Dan, those things are comfortable. Playing a game is comfortable… immediate gratification." His reply: "Writing every morning is very comfortable to me. It gives me immediate gratification. You just haven't turned the goals you want to achieve into a game. It's more painful for me not to write every morning than it is to write every morning," and the same holds for the gym, 10,000 steps and eating healthy (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24).

And he adds a story that shows the same reversal from the other side. He is not judging gamers ("I was the same person"). As a kid he hit tenth prestige in *Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 2* because he was out of school for a week with pneumonia and played all day "with a 12-pack of Dr. Pepper near me." A few years ago he downloaded the new *Call of Duty*, "but it was just too much… it just wasn't fun. I would play a match or two and then it's like, I have something better that I could be doing." The lesson: "Most people have it backwards. They start working on their goals and then they think, I have something better that I could be doing. You're missing the psychology of skill and challenge" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24).

**Complementary context:** in the *Call of Duty* series, "prestige" is an optional reset in multiplayer mode: a player who reaches the maximum level can return to level one in exchange for an insignia; tenth prestige was the highest in *Modern Warfare 2* and required repeating the full progression ten times.

The story completes the argument. The comfort of a game is not a property of games; it is a property of the relation between a person's goals and the activity. When his own goals were in place, the game that once absorbed him for a week felt like a distraction from them. Discipline, in this account, is not a force that overcomes the attraction of easy things; it is what it looks like from the outside when someone's own game has become more absorbing than the alternatives. The phrase "the psychology of skill and challenge" points ahead to section 10.5, which explains why.

#### The four focus blockers

If attention flows to the most absorbing game, why does it not flow to one's own goals once they are set? Koe's answer, in the same 2024 video, is that something is blocking it. "You can't focus because your mind, life and priorities are a mess and you haven't done anything about it." So the first step is to remove what he calls **focus blockers**: "focus is about removing anything that prevents focus." This is why you feel lost, distracted and low-energy. The moment you sense boredom or anxiety, you fill it with the closest relief: "your mind is guiding you toward a better life, but instead of listening to the signals you attempt to numb the pain of not being everything you could be," which makes it worse in the long term. The four blockers apply to what that video calls the lost phase: they prevent you from moving into curiosity or intensity (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13).

Notice the status Koe gives boredom and anxiety: signals that guide you, which most people numb. Section 10.5 develops this reading in detail. The four blockers are:

1. **The purpose blocker.** You feel lost because you lack purpose: you do not know what problem you are solving, what goal you are pursuing, or what your part is in a whole greater than yourself. Your mind is conditioned on society's goals and the path is known (school, job, retire); you drag your feet, you get bored because you do not care, and you fill the boredom with laziness and pleasure instead of building toward your own goal. The remedy is to allow yourself to be bored; to expose yourself to higher potentials; to drop everything and see what sticks; to sit with your thoughts in order to "catch an **inkling of vision**." Vision clarity does not come all at once: "you collect the little puzzle pieces" until one fits and you start to see the image ("oh crap"), and then you solve faster, see the whole image and build it.
2. **Environmental blockers.** "Your environment creates who you are, but most people forget that they're in full control of their environment." Surrounded by people, opinions and distractions in the physical and digital worlds, it is no wonder you cannot focus. "Rip the Band-Aid off": take a day and literally throw away anything that (1) is not important and (2) distracts you from what is. Do not hesitate.
3. **Metabolism or food blockers.** You lack motivation because you eat too much in one sitting, digest poorly, or eat so much junk that you feel bad and cannot focus. Koe's personal data point: as a 225-pound man, if he eats more than 300 to maybe 600 calories in a meal, his focus starts to go down. The mechanism he gives: when fed, you enter the "rest and digest" state rather than the "fight or flight" focus state (not that you should always be in fight or flight); eat too much and the body moves energy toward digestion, leaving less for work. His suggested experiment: intermittent fasting, or smaller meals in the morning and bigger ones later.
4. **Knowledge blockers.** You do not know enough to get the work done, you do not know what to do now, or you lack the skill for the challenge. The solution is to "bake in learning and building": learn in unison with building the project, for example by listening to an audiobook or a lecture on walks, or by exchanging phone scrolling for reading or a course.

(**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13.)

The four blockers are heterogeneous (one existential, one environmental, one physiological, one cognitive), and that heterogeneity is the point: the obstacle to focus is rarely a single deficit of will. Each blocker also connects to a later part of the book: purpose to Part IV, environment to Chapter 11, metabolism to Chapter 40, knowledge to Part VI. The calorie figure is personal experience offered as an experiment, not a general rule, and Koe presents it that way. The fourth blocker, finally, is the bridge to section 10.5: lacking the skill for the challenge is precisely the condition in which anxiety appears.

### 10.4 Flow

#### The optimal human experience

Every argument so far has pointed at one state: the absorption that games produce and that Koe wants to transfer to real goals. He usually introduces it by description before naming it. It is "the optimal human experience": the feeling when nothing else matters, when you feel extra confident, as if you could take on the world, "at one with the task," whether in a sport, a game or writing at your computer. "Some people call this state the flow state": a state of consciousness in which nothing matters except what your attention is immersed in. In it, "you lose the sense of self-consciousness and self-centeredness that leads to anxiety or boredom or just suffering in general, as many of the spiritual teachers and ancient masters would say." You stop caring what other people think, become one with the task, know exactly what to do next and how well you are doing, and gain a deep sense of satisfaction (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

A 2024 description adds the vocabulary of performance. Flow is the state of consciousness that professional athletes, spiritual masters and culture-shifting artists "swear by as the key to their success." It feels like unbreakable confidence, being light on your feet and secure with your future, "the moment where skill becomes art and you no longer care what people think," the feeling that you do not exist and cannot think of anything you would rather be doing. "It's called the flow state, which is a state of peak experience, maximum enjoyment and effortless productivity" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

And a third description, from the same period, returns to games. Everyone has played a game they were completely immersed in: they forget everything else, enter flow, and may not even realize they are in "peak experience," which Koe describes as "the beautiful spot where new information is digested as reality flows through them." That state is what you want to replicate by creating the game of your life (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

The three descriptions share a structure that is worth making explicit, because Koe's later claims depend on it:

- **Absorption**: attention entirely on the task; nothing else registers.
- **Loss of self-reference**: self-consciousness and self-centeredness disappear, and with them concern for others' opinions.
- **Clarity**: you know what to do next and how you are doing.
- **Enjoyment**: the state is intrinsically satisfying (maximum enjoyment, deep satisfaction).
- **Effortlessness**: skill becomes art; productivity does not feel like effort.

The second feature is the one Koe develops most. In his account, self-consciousness and self-centeredness are not just absent in flow; they are the specific mechanisms of the two states flow is opposed to, anxiety and boredom. Section 10.5 shows how. The phrase "as the spiritual teachers and ancient masters would say" signals that he sees the loss of self-reference as the same thing those traditions describe as the end of suffering, a connection he makes explicit below.

#### Csikszentmihalyi: order in consciousness

The source Koe relies on most for the concept is Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, whom he calls "the godfather of flow psychology." In 2023 he read the definition from *Flow* on screen: "The optimal state of inner experience is one in which there is order in consciousness. This happens when psychic energy, or attention, is invested in realistic goals, and when skills match the opportunities for action. The pursuit of a goal brings order in awareness because a person must concentrate attention on the task at hand and momentarily forget everything else" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05; the transcript renders the last sentence as "brings order and awareness," and the name of the author as "mihai chicken").

The definition contains, in one paragraph, the conditions of the whole chapter: attention invested in goals (the hierarchy of goals), goals that are realistic and skills that match the opportunities for action (the skill–challenge match), and the narrowing of attention to the task at hand (the frame). Koe's gloss is spiritual: flow is the optimal state of consciousness that "all mystics, ancients and psychologists" point to ("I don't have any worries… I'm so focused at work… so at one with nature"); it is being at one with being or doing, "when they collapse into one."

In another video he quotes a second passage from the same book to justify choosing a goal that is challenging enough: "when all a person's relevant skills are needed to cope with the challenges of a situation, that person's attention is completely absorbed by the activity. There is no excess psychic energy left over to process any information but what the activity offers. All the attention is concentrated on the relevant stimuli." His comment: "this is the path to maximum enjoyment in life" (**Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19).

This second quotation explains *why* challenge produces absorption. Distraction requires spare attention. A task that uses all your relevant skills leaves none over, so nothing else can register. A task that uses only part of them leaves a remainder, and the remainder wanders. The mechanism of boredom in section 10.5 is already contained here.

In 2025 Koe compresses the theory into an equation. Csikszentmihalyi argued, he says, that happiness comes from taking control over the contents of our consciousness, creating the flow state: "periods of complete absorption where people experience deep enjoyment, creativity, and the total involvement with life." Koe's restatement: "**Order in consciousness equals enjoyment**. Chaos in consciousness, or disorder in the mind, or mental disorder, equals not enjoyment" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14).

This equation links the chapter to Chapter 5, where psychic entropy (disorder in consciousness) was presented as the default state of the mind and ordering consciousness as the remedy. Flow is the positive pole of that same axis. "Mental disorder" is used here as a play on words (disorder in the mind) rather than in the clinical sense; the reader should not take it as a psychiatric claim.

**Complementary context:** Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934–2021) was a Hungarian-American psychologist whose research, summarized in *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (1990), studied the experiences people describe as most enjoyable across very different activities. He identified recurring conditions (clear goals, immediate feedback, a balance between challenge and skill) and recurring features (concentration, loss of self-consciousness, altered sense of time, the activity becoming its own reward). "Psychic entropy" and "psychic energy" are his terms for disorder in consciousness and for attention as the energy that orders it.

#### Autotelic: the doing is the reward

Koe borrows one technical term from Csikszentmihalyi's framework and explains its etymology twice. When you are at one with the task, he says in 2023, the activity is called **autotelic**: "auto meaning self, telic meaning goal, a self-contained goal," and in this state "you lose self-consciousness and move through life without a worry in the world" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05). In 2024 he gives the fuller definition, attributing it to Csikszentmihalyi: flow is an autotelic activity, from two Greek words, *auto* (self) and *telos* (goal). "It refers to a self-contained activity, one that is done not with the expectation of some future benefit, but simply because the doing itself is the reward" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

The concept resolves an apparent tension in the chapter. Koe insists on goals, hierarchies and visions, all of which point to the future, and at the same time describes the ideal state as one in which the doing itself is the reward. The resolution is the dual structure described under "rules" in section 10.2: the goal sits in the background and gives the activity its shape, while attention in the foreground is entirely on the present activity. The goal is what makes the activity autotelic, by narrowing attention onto it; it is not what makes the activity rewarding moment to moment. This is also the "motivational inversion" of *Games: Agency as Art* (section 10.1): taking up an end for the sake of the means.

#### Flow, the now and the spiritual traditions

Koe repeatedly connects flow to the spiritual vocabulary of presence. In 2022, ranking *Flow* last in a list of seven books, he said it "put all of the pieces together for me. If you read all of these books in this succession, you would make incredible connections when you read *Flow* afterwards." Csikszentmihalyi's life's work, he says, was dissecting the psychology of the flow state, and Koe links it to ancient teachers and spiritual gurus: "The present moment, the now, no mind… it's [a] degree of this flow state" (**Source:** `7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md`, 2022-04-23).

The claim is an identification: what spiritual traditions describe as presence, "the now" or "no mind" is, in Koe's reading, a degree of flow. This is his interpretation, not Csikszentmihalyi's, and it has consequences for how he relates spirituality and productivity. If the deepest spiritual state and the most productive working state are degrees of the same thing, then work done in flow is not opposed to spiritual practice but one of its forms. Chapter 40 returns to this connection; elsewhere in the corpus Koe also contrasts presence and flow, so the identification should be read as one strand of his thought rather than a settled doctrine.

**Complementary context:** "The now" is Eckhart Tolle's vocabulary (*The Power of Now*), which Koe credits elsewhere as a turning point in his life; "no mind" (Japanese *mushin*) is a Zen term for a mind free of deliberative thought, used in martial arts to describe action without hesitation.

In 2024 he broadens the claim to the whole of life: a state of consciousness like flow "connects to all of life; it's not just neuroscience, it's metaphysics, psychology, self-improvement and productivity all in one," because it comes into play in anything you do and gives "incredible hints at how to live the good life" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). This is an opinion offered as a closing reflection, and it explains why flow occupies the place it does in the architecture of this book: it is the point at which the theory of mind (Part III), the theory of productivity (Part V) and the theory of the good life (Part XIV) meet.

#### Productivity mode is not the enemy

One practical corollary concerns a distinction Koe draws in the 4-Hour Workday between focused "productivity mode" and more open states. Productivity mode, he says, is not bad: it is "one of the most enjoyable states you can be in," the flow state, although flow also transfers into open focus (being in nature, some kinds of challenge). It is enjoyable because it is "so secure, certain and focused that negative thoughts don't replicate in your mind" (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18).

The reason he gives (security and certainty that block negative thoughts) is the same mechanism as the "no excess psychic energy" quotation above, stated from the other side: negative thoughts need room to replicate, and a fully engaged mind does not give them any. Chapter 12 builds on this when it treats focused work as something to be enjoyed rather than endured.

#### "Arguably the key to life": self-chosen goals

Why does flow matter so much to Koe? His answer comes from his own history. After studying flow and psychology, he says, he understood why always having a side project had made him happy (his coding projects, businesses and digital art between roughly 16 and 25). He had a vision and a goal, and he could "focus my consciousness," be absorbed by the skill he was learning or the goal he was pursuing. It was intrinsically motivating because the skill or project had meaning to him: nobody else told him which project or skill to pursue; it was "by my choice, by my design." "Me creating and living in my own reality is what caused me to continue to be obsessed with and somewhat addicted to those projects" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28).

The account is a causal chain, and it is worth spelling out because the rest of the section depends on it: own choice → meaning → absorption (flow) → intrinsic motivation → sustained obsession → happiness. The first link is the decisive one. The same activity chosen by someone else breaks the chain at its start, which is why the "assigned goals" of Chapter 1 can produce competence without producing flow.

From this he draws his strongest claim: "You want to be in flow for a lot of your life. That is arguably the key to life": pursuing your own goals and educating yourself to the point of consistently progressing in them. And this, he says, is why so many people are unhappy: very few set and stick to their own individual goals, as opposed to what they "have been conditioned to want to create" for themselves. He adds that you could "pull up a random statistic online" about how many people set and stick to their goals, without giving a figure (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28; repeated in `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

The claim should be read with its own hedges. "Arguably" is Koe's word, and the explanation of widespread unhappiness is a hypothesis drawn from his experience and his reading of flow psychology, not a finding he supports with data (the missing statistic makes that clear). What the claim does establish is the structure of his system: if flow is the key to life, and flow requires self-chosen goals, then everything in Part IV (vision, goals, projects) is in service of making flow possible.

#### The five intrinsic drivers

Koe's main elaboration of the conditions of flow comes from Steven Kotler, and its history in the corpus is a clear case of an idea moving from citation to adaptation to unattributed framework.

In April 2022 he describes Kotler's *The Art of Impossible* as a book about peak performance, entering the peak flow state and accomplishing "impossible" things. He assumes "impossible" means what the average person thinks is impossible, such as working for yourself with an online business only two hours a day: "yes, you can. It's extremely possible." What stuck with him was "the five drivers of flow. Believe that's what it was from him": curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy and mastery. They play into and lead to each other. Curiosity is "the first step" and leads to the others; the book covers cultivating passion around your interests and curiosities, assigning purpose to that passion, needing autonomy to pursue them fully, and mastery as "the step above that." The rest of the book covers skill acquisition and creativity, and he notes that it and *Flow* "play into each other very, very well" (**Source:** `7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md`, 2022-04-23).

In May and June 2022 he cites "Steven Kotler's five intrinsic drivers" as what to take into account ("bonus points") when building a vision and a hierarchy of goals (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28), and as the fifth layer of a process for building a better life: drive, "the intrinsic drive, not extrinsic drive and motivation, not things that are fickle and have no meaning to you and are just there for a quick dopamine hit." Intrinsic drive is "a sustainable and powerful way of fueling your daily actions and making those actions seamless." At the very beginning, the relevant drivers are curiosity, passion and purpose, because "all of these bake in inspiration": reading something you are genuinely curious about, and thinking "oh my god, that makes sense," gives an energy you "can't beat," and you live off it to fuel daily action (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

In April 2023 the list appears without attribution, as "the five intrinsic drivers," and is linked to business: "If you want to live a fulfilling life you need to leverage all five intrinsic drivers: curiosity, purpose, passion, autonomy and mastery. You need to create and have full control over the lifestyle you wish to live. Business is how you get there." That video lists them without defining them (**Source:** `Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md`, 2023-04-20).

In July 2024 Koe attributes them to Kotler again and defines each one. Kotler, he says, believes there is a formula for achieving flow and thus the impossible, and aligning motivations is the first step. The five drivers are: curiosity ("our compass into the unknown"); passion ("finding that one thing you can't pull yourself away from"); purpose ("how your passion will impact the world, something greater than yourself"); autonomy ("the desire for freedom required to pursue your passion and purpose"); and mastery ("honing your skill set so you can monetize your passion"; the monetization framing may be Koe's rather than Kotler's). According to Kotler, as Koe presents him, each contributes neurotransmitters (dopamine, oxytocin, endorphins, serotonin and one more that the transcript renders as "in neur and nephrine," probably norepinephrine), and together they form a neurochemical cocktail that creates flow. Koe recommends the book but says he has his own methods (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

He then gives his own adaptation, which reverses the order. "In my eyes this is best tackled from the top down" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21):

| Driver | Kotler (as Koe summarizes him) | Koe's top-down adaptation: where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Mastery | Honing your skill set | Your life's work, your vision for the future |
| Autonomy | Freedom to pursue passion and purpose | Entrepreneurship, the ability to create your own work |
| Purpose | How your passion impacts the world | Projects turned into products: "a product is how you help more people than just yourself, that's how you transcend yourself" |
| Passion | The thing you can't pull yourself away from | Connecting the dots, finding the pieces of the puzzle you are creating |
| Curiosity | Your compass into the unknown | The fuel: "the desire to acquire the knowledge to reach the next level" |

The adaptation turns a psychological sequence into an institutional one. In Kotler's account, as Koe first summarized it, the drivers build upward from curiosity. In Koe's account, each driver is anchored in a structure of his system: mastery in the life's work (Chapter 9), autonomy in entrepreneurship (Part X), purpose in the product (Chapter 33), passion in the creative connection of ideas (Chapter 18), and curiosity as what keeps the whole moving. On purpose he adds a note about value: you give and create for others in exchange for another form of value, not necessarily money (status, peace or enjoyment), and those forms of value are neutral until assigned to someone good or evil.

A month later, in August 2024, he draws the practical consequence. Perhaps it is time to take a brutal look at your situation and "stop trying to force finding meaning in the meaningless"; to take the leap you know is the only logical long-term option but avoid because you would sacrifice comforts that can be replaced by something more fulfilling. When you build your own thing, you actually want to work; it only feels like work during a lull, and even then it is work you can tolerate. In control of the project, "you control the variables for creating the flow state," which he calls "the key to achieving the impossible": curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy, mastery (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

Finally, in June 2025, the list returns without attribution as "five intrinsic motivators," explained by neurochemistry. Intensity and obsession, he says, create a neurochemical cocktail. People fall into a rut seeking extrinsic motivators; obsessed with a goal, you are fueled by intrinsic motivators, which "stack and strengthen each other in a way that sustains some degree of flow" ("the state of optimal experience") (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29):

1. **Curiosity**: the desire to explore the unknown, learn how to change, fill knowledge gaps. It yields "good dopamine" from novelty and norepinephrine, which heightens attention and prepares you to learn.
2. **Passion**: intense enthusiasm for the path that lets you change your life; more good dopamine and norepinephrine.
3. **Purpose**: the feeling that your actions contribute to something larger than yourself. Achieving goals yields dopamine that reinforces behavior; serotonin comes from significance and belonging; oxytocin from connection.
4. **Autonomy**: the desire to direct your own life and work, to control your choices, actions and environment. It yields dopamine and reduced cortisol (the stress of feeling put in a box), allowing creative decision-making.
5. **Mastery**: learning and growing as its own reward; sustainable good dopamine that keeps you in the game.

These, he says, create obsession, and they come into play in the six-month plan that video lays out.

The evolution can be summarized: the list itself never changes; the attribution moves from a hesitant citation of Kotler (2022) to Kotler as the source of a framework Koe reorders as his own (2024), to an unattributed list explained by neurochemistry (2025) and, in 2026, to "the five drivers of the flow state" without a source (Chapter 9 records that last use). The neurochemical mapping of 2025 is presented without citations and should be read as Koe's explanatory model, informed by the popular literature on flow, rather than as an established finding. There is also a small terminological slippage to note: in 2022 Koe uses "extrinsic" for fickle, meaningless motivation opposed to intrinsic drive, while in another 2022 video he uses "extrinsic energy" for deliberately channeling thoughts and actions toward a goal. The same word designates different things; the reader should rely on context.

**Complementary context:** Steven Kotler is an American author on peak performance and co-founder of the Flow Research Collective. In *The Art of Impossible* (2021) he presents curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy and mastery as a "motivation stack" of intrinsic drivers that, layered together, increase the likelihood of flow. The idea of autonomy and mastery (with purpose) as intrinsic motivators is also associated with Daniel Pink's *Drive* (2009), which draws on self-determination theory.

#### Motivation, learning, creativity, flow

Koe uses one more line from Kotler, twice, to open the same step of his info-product process ("become above average" in your skills and interests): "Motivation gets you into this game, learning is what helps you continue to play, creativity is how you steer, flow is how you turbo boost the results beyond all rational standards and reasonable expectations" (**Source:** `You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md`, 2022-12-04; repeated in `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

The quotation is a compact map of Kotler's book, and it fits Koe's metaphor exactly, since it is already phrased as a game. Its order also matches the order of this book: motivation (Part IV), learning (Part VI), creativity (Chapter 18). Flow sits at the end, not as an ingredient but as a multiplier of the others.

#### The recipe for the good life

In January 2024 Koe gave his own sequence, which overlaps with the drivers but follows a different logic. "You must push into the unknown to fuel your mind": (1) expose yourself to new information and experience, which is curiosity; (2) create something of value with that information in order to digest it, which is passion; (3) expand your mind as that experience becomes part of you through practice, which is mastery; (4) transcend that phase of life by passing that value down to others, which is connection. "That's the recipe for the good life: **create, expand and transcend** with the experience you acquire by pursuing self-generated goals that lead you into the unknown." Winning, he concludes, is a process of using information, training and fueling that training (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

The recipe is Koe's own (it is not attributed to Kotler) and it differs from the five drivers in an instructive way. The drivers are motivations; the recipe is a cycle of experience. Curiosity brings in new information; passion is defined not as a feeling but as the act of creating something with that information; mastery is what happens when the created thing becomes part of you through practice; connection is the transfer of what you have mastered to others. The cycle then restarts at a higher level, which is why the last step is called transcending "that phase of life." The same structure (learn, build, teach) runs through Parts VI to X.

#### Detecting intrinsic curiosity: reread your favorite book

If curiosity is the fuel, how do you tell intrinsic curiosity from passing interest? Koe offers a simple exercise. Reread your favorite book, the one that excited you so much you read it entirely, whose author you love and recommend to everyone. Two things will happen. You will pick up a completely different message the second time. And you will be able to notice and understand: "okay, yes, I am curious about this, this is intrinsic, I can replicate this in other things that I am consuming" (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28).

The exercise works by contrast. The first reading was driven by novelty; the second, with the novelty gone, shows what still pulls you. What survives the loss of novelty is the intrinsic part, and once identified it can be used as a filter for what to consume and pursue.

#### Obsession

The extreme of flow sustained over time is what Koe calls obsession, and he treats it both as the energy behind unconventional lives and as something that cannot be chosen in advance.

In 2022 he opened a video by noting a common theme he had seen over the previous month in his own life and in the lives of many people he was connected with: obsession. As a kid, "I was obsessed with finding a better way of living." He frames this childhood obsession as the root of his later choices (questioning the conventional career path, trying business models, building online), and the video operationalizes it as curiosity (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21).

At the end of 2023 he gave obsession a three-step prescription. First, "expose yourself to more experience," explicitly "not distraction." Second, "let curiosity pull you down a **rabbit hole of discovery**." Third, once you find the thing you cannot pull yourself away from, work to make it a sustainable part of your life. Obsession is the key to greatness, but it is discovered through exposure and curiosity, not chosen beforehand, and only afterwards turned into something that sustains you economically and practically. Later in the same video he restates the sequence as a chain: curiosity leads to learning, learning leads to obsession, obsession leads to "a life that you never thought was possible" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31).

The distinction between experience and distraction in the first step is essential and connects to Chapter 11. Both expose you to new things; the difference is that experience is engaged with (you act, learn, reflect), while distraction is consumed passively. Only the first can lead into a rabbit hole of discovery, because only the first builds the knowledge that makes the next discovery possible.

Why does novelty pull so strongly? Koe gives an evolutionary image. "Your psyche is wired to hunt. Imagine our ancestors walking by a berry bush every day. It doesn't serve their future at that time, but one day it grows new berries. Somebody walks by it, they notice, and that novelty causes excitement; they either gather the berries or remember them later so they can survive longer" (**Source:** `How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md`, 2023-08-01). The novelty response, in his reading, evolved to drive discovery tied to survival.

The berry bush is a metaphor, offered as a just-so story rather than as evidence, and its role in the argument is to explain two things at once: why curiosity feels good (it was once rewarded because it found food) and why it can be hijacked (anything that delivers novelty triggers the same response, including feeds designed to deliver it endlessly). Obsession, in this account, is the hunting instinct aimed at a goal worth hunting. Section 10.6 will show what happens when that instinct is deliberately put under pressure.

### 10.5 Living at the Edge

#### The skill–challenge graphic

Of the three components of the micro game, Koe calls one "arguably the most important": "the delicate balance between anxiety and boredom" (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11). He explains it with a graphic he has shown "multiple times," adapted, in his words, from Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow* and *The Evolving Self*, both of which he recommends.

The fullest description comes from 2024: "On the y-axis, up and down, there's the challenge level; on the x-axis, left and right, there's the skill level. If the challenge is too high but your skill is too low, you are going to get anxious or self-conscious, and if your skill is too high but the challenge is too low, you're going to get bored, or your focus is going to be self-centered. But when you live at your edge, when you live at the edge of your abilities and you pursue a challenge or a problem that you want to solve and your skill is up to par with that, this is when you feel good" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18).

The 2022 version describes the regions by position: in the top quadrant, anxiety or self-consciousness; in the middle, flow or *selflessness*; at the bottom, boredom or self-centeredness (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11). In the 2024 compilation of that video, Koe corrects himself mid-sentence about which axis is on the left, so the orientation is ambiguous in that transcript; the 2024 description above, and those of 2023 (challenge on the vertical axis, skill on the horizontal) resolve the ambiguity (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21; `Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md`, 2023-11-26; `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18).

"The skill–challenge match is extremely important here," he says, and the first application he draws from it in 2022 concerns beginnings. When you start a game, especially without having read the rule book, it is not fun; even knowing the rules, it takes time to grasp what is going on. Hence his advice to "just start": start with what you know, learn on the go, get real-world experience, and do not get trapped in "tutorial hell," the endless preparation that never meets a real challenge (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11). The advice anticipates the "build to learn" principle of Chapter 14: the discomfort of beginning is the normal experience of the anxiety region, and it is reduced by acquiring skill through action, not by postponing action until the skill arrives.

Reconstructed from these descriptions, the graphic has the following regions:

| Region | Condition | Emotional state | Orientation of attention (Koe's adaptation) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above the channel | Challenge too high for skill | Anxiety | Self-consciousness: attention turns toward the self |
| The channel ("the middle," "the edge") | Skill matches challenge | Flow | Selflessness: attention on the task |
| Below the channel | Skill too high for challenge | Boredom | Self-centeredness: attention turns to other desires |

The last column is Koe's addition. The pairing of anxiety with self-consciousness and of boredom with self-centeredness appears to be his own layer on top of the flow model (Csikszentmihalyi does describe loss of self-consciousness as a feature of flow, but the specific pairing of the two failure states with two forms of self-reference is Koe's elaboration). It is the part of the model he develops most, and it is examined below.

**Complementary context:** in Csikszentmihalyi's original model, flow occurs in a diagonal "channel" where challenge and skill rise together; later versions divide the space into eight regions (including apathy, worry, relaxation, control and arousal), with flow where both challenge and skill are high relative to one's average. Koe uses the simpler three-region version.

Koe's attribution of the graphic varies over the years. He credits Csikszentmihalyi in 2022, in 2023 and in 2024; in a 2023 video he names no source; and in 2026 he jokes about it: enjoyment from living at the edge of your abilities is "pretty well studied," he says, but refuses to cite sources ("I made it up, picture") (**Source:** `How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md`, 2026-06-28). The joke should not be read as a claim of authorship. The model is Csikszentmihalyi's, the self-consciousness/self-centeredness reading is Koe's adaptation, and the 2026 remark is irony.

#### Why the channel must keep moving

The graphic is static, but Koe insists that the situation it describes is not. "What people don't realize" is that the match "must keep evolving." A challenge eventually becomes normal and easy, and then boredom sets in; aim too high without matching skill and you land in anxiety (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). Staying in flow is therefore not a position but a trajectory: the challenge must rise, and skill must rise with it.

He illustrates the trajectory with games. Video games order consciousness and teach lessons about skill acquisition, learning and self-confidence. When you start, you do not know what you are doing; you go through a tutorial so that you are not exposed to everything at once; you practice at level one until it becomes boring; you are introduced to more skills, traits and abilities; you progressively overload the challenge until you stop playing. "There must be a skill-challenge match in order to maintain order in consciousness." A game, he says, represents any situation in life, especially those in which you lack confidence: learn the rules first, then practice the mechanics; there is a series of steps or quests that provide education and practice in unison (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05).

The sequence (tutorial, practice until boring, new abilities, progressive overload) is a curriculum, and it explains why games teach so effectively: they control the rate at which challenge rises. Most real-life situations do not, which is why a person has to do for themselves what the game designer does for the player. The phrase "practice at level one until it becomes boring" is also a criterion: boredom is the signal that a level has been mastered.

In *The Art of Focus* (2024) the same idea is stated in a more intense register: "If your skill doesn't match the challenge that the game presents, you're in for a bad time. If your skill is high and the challenge low, you will get bored. If your skill is low and the challenge is high, you will get anxious. When the challenge is just above your skill level but you have absolute confidence in your ability to execute, you tap into what is arguably the most enjoyable point in the human experience that is accessible without drugs, herbs or other external compounds" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

Two details refine the model. The optimal challenge is "just above" skill, not equal to it: the channel is slightly tilted toward difficulty. And the condition includes a subjective element, "absolute confidence in your ability to execute." Skill alone is not enough; the person must also believe it is enough. That subjective condition is what the anxiety mechanism below disrupts.

In 2026 he calls this the main lever of the whole system: enjoyment comes from a challenge just above your skill, not so hard that you get anxious and not so easy that you get bored. Video games exploit this: quests are "just challenging enough"; a level-one character on a level-100 quest would die immediately, hate the game and quit. This match is "the single greatest driving factor of getting into the flow state," and if the structure of your life increases the likelihood of this **flow trigger**, you have abundant enjoyment (**Source:** `How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md`, 2026-06-28).

#### Boredom as self-centeredness, anxiety as self-consciousness

Koe's distinctive contribution to the model is an account of the *mechanism* of the two failure states, which he repeats almost word for word from 2022 to 2025.

"If your skill is high and the challenge is low, you're going to get bored, and if your skill is low and the challenge is high, you're going to get anxious." **Boredom stems from self-centeredness**: your focus breaks from the task and another desire comes to mind. If you are bored at work, it is usually because you are not immersed in the task, and you sit there "thinking self-centeredly about the better things you could be doing with your time." **Anxiety comes from self-consciousness**: when the challenge is too high and your skill too low, attention turns toward the concept of self, which lets distractions or problems penetrate your conscious field, and attention "starts going downhill from there." His examples of self-conscious thoughts are "wow, I'm not as good as I thought," "I really need to work on my back swing," and "that girl is way out of my league": in each, you are paying attention to your current skill relative to the skill the game requires (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

A 2023 version, given without naming a source, adds the case that most of his audience would recognize. When the challenge is too high and skill too low, you "think about yourself and how you aren't good enough." When the challenge is too low and skill too high, you become self-centered ("what could I be doing better with my time"): bored at a repetitive job, not realizing that you need to evolve to find fulfillment, you spend all day thinking about better things to do. "The middle, where skill and challenge match, that's when you get into the flow state" (**Source:** `Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md`, 2023-11-26). The example matters because it locates the boredom region not in idle moments but in the core of many working lives, which section 10.7 develops.

In 2025 the same model is presented as an early-warning system: "There are two ways to identify distractions before they happen, so you can correct yourself and get back into flow. That's what this is from: flow state psychology." Boredom: "your focus breaks, a new desire pops into your head, and related thoughts start to fill your attention"; you think of better things you could be doing. Anxiety: "your focus turns inward at yourself, and the negative thoughts start to flood in about how you're not good enough" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

The two mechanisms are symmetrical, and the symmetry is the insight. In both cases attention leaves the task and turns toward the self, but toward different aspects of it. In boredom, it turns toward the self's *desires* (what I would rather be doing); in anxiety, toward the self's *adequacy* (whether I am good enough). Flow is the state in which neither happens, which is why Koe labels it "selflessness." This explains his earlier claim that in flow you lose "the self-consciousness and self-centeredness that leads to anxiety or boredom or just suffering in general": the two words name the two exits from the channel.

#### The blemish cascade and the rogue programs

Koe's most vivid illustration of the anxiety mechanism is a small one. You look in the mirror and notice a blemish: "step one." Attention turns to more flaws and can "take over your mind and ruin your self-image." "My diet isn't the best right now. Maybe that's what it is." Then: "I'm kind of getting a bit overweight and I don't like this little pudgy stomach." Then: "In order to get rid of that I have to start going on a run or going to the gym." The mind slowly floods, "because you don't have clarity on what to do." And the counterfactual: "If you had an immediate and effective solution for the blemish through your knowledge, skill and education, then you wouldn't have to think twice about fixing that problem" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

A year earlier he had described the same event in computing terms. "If you look in the mirror and spot a pimple or hair, that is going to be on your mind for the rest of the day. It will impact every other area of your life. The RAM of your mental supercomputer will be consumed by **rogue programs** until your performance suffers as a whole." And then the conclusion: "The chaos induced by boredom and anxiety can only be cured by clarity. When you have the skill and knowledge that matches the challenge of a task, life becomes a video game of quests that you love to play" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18).

The example is an application of the RAM metaphor from Chapter 6 and of the pimple example of psychic entropy from Chapter 5. What it adds here is a diagnosis in terms of skill and challenge. The blemish is a small challenge; it becomes a cascade only because there is no skill (knowledge, a known solution) to match it. With the skill, the challenge is dispatched and attention returns to the task; without it, the unmatched challenge turns attention toward the self, and self-consciousness spreads from the skin to the diet to the body to the gym. "Rogue programs" is Koe's name for these self-propagating thoughts; elsewhere in the corpus he calls them "rogue thoughts."

#### Anxiety as a lack of clarity

The model has an earlier variant that matters for practice. In 2022, discussing the difficulties of building a better life, Koe brought up "Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's flow model" and read the two states differently. If you are anxious, "that usually means that you are trying to tackle something that you don't have too much clarity on, so the solution to that is self-education": "learning, learning, learning and applying in real life," to gain the awareness that begets the next step. If you are bored while executing, you are doing something you are too skilled for: "there's no challenge there." When you fall out of the "skill to challenge ratio," it is very hard to be present, because you lack clarity; you need to mold your mind to immerse yourself in the present and tap into flow (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). His example in that video is anxiety about landing a first client, whose remedy is to self-educate.

The two readings of anxiety (lack of clarity in June 2022; self-consciousness from December 2022 onward) are compatible rather than competing. Lack of skill or of clarity is the cause; the turn of attention toward the self is the mechanism through which the cause produces the feeling. The 2024–2025 statements join them explicitly: "the chaos induced by boredom and anxiety can only be cured by clarity." And the practical consequence of the first reading remains the most useful heuristic Koe offers for anxiety: do not push harder at the task, learn what you are missing.

#### Clarity, and the level-1 problem

What does curing chaos with clarity mean in practice? The 2025 formulation is the fullest: "The chaos induced by boredom and anxiety can only be cured with clarity. You must refocus your mind not only on the task in front of you, but on the ideal outcome of your life as a whole. When you have the skill and knowledge that matches the challenge of the task or situation at hand, life becomes an interesting video game. You're not a level one going against a level 50. You're a level one going against a level two, three, four. You're increasing your experience" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

Clarity here has two scales, matching the dual structure of attention: clarity about the task (what to do next) and clarity about the whole (where the task leads). And the remedy for overmatched challenge is to choose challenges just above your level.

In 2022 Koe had offered a different remedy for the same problem. A level 1 fighting a level 50 in *World of Warcraft* loses immediately, which is not fun. "But you can create a better frame for that game": instead of making beating the level 50 the goal, "what if the goal was to see how fast you could lose against the level 50? Then it'd be kind of fun and you'd be interested in playing the game." The same applies to chess: a beginner playing a friend with years of experience, or entering a competition without practice, is "just not going to be a good time" unless the frame changes (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The corpus record treats these as two answers to the same situation: in the earlier version, keep the overmatched challenge and reframe the goal; in the later one, choose challenges just above your level. They are not strictly contradictory, because they apply to different circumstances. Reframing is a tool for situations you cannot choose (a game you have been dragged into, a competition you have already entered); choosing the level is a tool for designing your own path. The broader question of goal size (enormous goals versus challenges just above your level) runs through the whole corpus, and Koe's own resolution is the one given in Chapter 8: big goals for direction, near goals and challenges for clarity and action. The reframe heuristic also illustrates the power of the frame described in section 10.2: by changing only the goal, the same objective situation moves from the anxiety region into the channel.

#### Heuristics for staying in the channel

From these elements Koe draws a set of short rules that recur across the corpus. They are worth gathering because each addresses a slightly different situation.

**Step back, or step up.** "If you are faced with a large challenge, then you need to step back, learn and practice at your level; if you are bored with your current level of challenge, you need to expose yourself to the education of the next level." Slowly step into the unknown, practice new skills, "and enjoy the neurochemical cocktail with a double of dopamine as you transcend into the next phase of your life" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05).

**Play at your level, aim for the next.** If you could achieve your goals now, they would not be challenging, "and challenge gives you a place to focus and order your mind; that's what makes life enjoyable," because challenge plays a crucial role in flow, enjoyment and dopamine, "but only if you take on challenges that aren't too boring or difficult. Play at your level but try to make it to the next level" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25).

**Neither novice against master.** "When a novice plays against a master, neither of them have fun": the novice becomes anxious, the master bored. "When your skill is the perfect match for the challenge of a situation, the world goes quiet and you move forward with grace. Challenge is the source of enjoyment. Enjoyment is found on the tightrope between boredom and anxiety at the edge of your abilities. The path to meaningful living is often found in a simple shift in perspective" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14).

**Test your skills with challenge.** In the context of turning a skill into income (helping others for free or paid, in public or private, through freelancing, coaching or content): "Skills must be tested with challenge. If you're bored along this path it's because you're not challenged; if you're anxious it's because you're aiming for a challenge too high and your skill hasn't matched that yet." You go through phases: beginner, intermediate, advanced (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19).

**The golden zone.** In 2025, discussing how to grow an income, Koe gave the match a number: "the golden zone for challenge… is at about 85% difficulty. So not so high that you get anxious, but not so low that you get bored." He adds a mechanism: "the stress from the progressive overload of challenge releases norepinephrine, which enhances learning further and helps you adapt into that new state of mind." In that context, challenge in the real world is what solidifies a visualized goal (there, an "aspirational hourly rate") (**Source:** `What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md`, 2025-08-03). He gives no source for the figure.

**Complementary context:** a 2019 study by Robert Wilson and colleagues ("The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning," *Nature Communications*) found that, for a class of learning algorithms and some related experiments, learning is fastest when the learner succeeds about 85% of the time, that is, with an error rate of roughly 15%. If Koe's figure derives from this line of work, it should be noted that the study's number refers to the rate of *success* at a task, so "85% difficulty" is not a direct translation of it; Koe does not cite the study, and his usage should be read as a rule of thumb for "hard but mostly manageable."

Taken together, the heuristics describe a two-way adjustment. When the signal is anxiety, lower the challenge or raise the skill (step back, learn at your level, pick a nearer goal, reframe the goal). When the signal is boredom, raise the challenge (expose yourself to the next level's education, take a harder quest). In neither case is the remedy to quit, which echoes the "five boxes" of section 10.2: boredom and anxiety indicate "that you need to choose a greater or lesser challenge rather than give up."

#### Immersion, and why overstimulation destroys it

Two more conditions complete the model. The first is immersion. "Video games immerse you so far into the fabricated reality that it becomes your reality," the only thing consuming your attention: absolute clarity, zero distractions. "This is the key to maximum enjoyment: maintaining the sense that one's skills are enough to match the challenge of the situation," and it is made easier by creating "an environment or a system that allows your mind to solely focus on that," with no problems or distractions pulling you away (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The skill–challenge match is necessary but not sufficient; the environment must also protect it. That is the bridge between this chapter and the environment design of Chapter 11 and the work blocks of Chapter 12.

The second condition concerns the baseline of stimulation. In 2025 Koe cited Csikszentmihalyi again: flow states ("the optimal state of human experience, or just pure enjoyment") require "a specific skill to challenge ratio"; challenge too high for your skill produces anxiety, too low produces boredom; "you wouldn't fight a level 100 character when you are a level one." Then his application: "Overstimulation destroys the potential for flow states or enjoyment because it raises the bar for what you consider stimulating. Everything that is more boring than your phone, gambling, porn, partying and the rest is automatically labeled as boring by your brain." That, he says, is why a dopamine detox "has become a well-marketed way of resetting your baseline for stimulation" (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26).

This is an important extension of the model. Boredom, in the original model, depends on the relation between skill and challenge. Koe adds a third variable: the level of stimulation the person is accustomed to. Someone whose baseline has been raised by highly stimulating media experiences an appropriately challenging task as boring, not because it is below their skill but because it is below their stimulation threshold. The channel is still there, but it has been made to feel dull. Chapter 11 is built on this observation.

#### Reverse-engineering a distraction

Koe also turns the model into a diagnostic procedure. When attention is narrowed on a task, distractions are hard to register. But if you do not know what to do next, and next, it falls apart: "if the next task is hard, you get anxious; if the next task is too easy, you get bored." Then attention splits away from the task, the present moment splits from your vision, and distractions register. Use this in reverse: when you notice a distraction, ask what led to it. If it came from anxiety, perhaps the task is too difficult, or you lack knowledge, skill or clarity. If it came from boredom, ask how to make the task more challenging. Aim for "**the middle**, where I just can't help but focus." Part of this, he adds, is simply creating a plan (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02).

The procedure inverts the usual approach to distraction. The usual approach treats distraction as the problem and tries to block it. Koe treats it as a symptom of a mismatch between the task and the person, and adjusts the task. Blocking distractions (Chapter 11) and adjusting the task are complementary: the first protects the channel, the second puts you in it.

#### The known and the unknown

So far "the edge" has meant the edge of your abilities. Koe also uses it in a second, broader sense: the edge between the known and the unknown. The two senses describe the same structure at different scales.

In 2022 he showed a graphic in which the unknown is a big black area surrounding the known. If you launch yourself too far into it, pushing too far out of the known, you get anxious. If you stay too far within the known (the Matrix we have locked ourselves into), you get bored, think life has no meaning and start believing there is no point. The answer is to live at the edge. When fear kicks in, will you hide back in the known, "your little comfortable home in your mind," or push out a bit and expose yourself to deeper experiences? Later in the same video he reread the graphic: the known is your mind, and the line around it is the closed-minded nature of your mind; pushing into the unknown expands that boundary until it encompasses much more than what you were taught in school or the experiences everyone else has gone through (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20). As with the birth–now–death graphic, the transcript describes a visual that cannot be fully reconstructed.

The method he attaches to the graphic is gradual. You have to begin exposing yourself to deeper experiences to unlock the experience you can draw on when it is time to think deeply, creatively or freely. At the start, "our goal is to just get a bit uncomfortable. We want to **puncture the known**, we want to puncture it with consistent effort and let obsession pull us deeper." When you make a novel discovery you get a hit of excitement, of dopamine. His example: struggling to read something, you come across a point you would not have caught by skimming, and it makes you want to research another thing, and another; without being conscious of it you start exploring, going from connection to connection, reflecting and understanding the patterns (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20).

The two steps (consistent effort, then obsession) describe a change in the source of motivation. At first, pushing into the unknown requires effort, because it is uncomfortable. Once a discovery is made, the novelty response (the berry bush of section 10.4) takes over and pulls you further. The effort is needed only to reach the point where curiosity becomes self-sustaining.

In 2025 he translated the graphic back into the game. Step three of gamifying your life is to "stay at the edge of the unknown." In some games the mini map shows explored areas as light and bright and unexplored areas as dark; these indirectly represent your skill level and experience. Dark places are higher-level, with quests you cannot take, higher-level monsters and players. If you somehow teleported into the middle of the unknown (there is no path there yet), "it's like you're jumping into the middle of the ocean and you don't know how to swim": a higher-level character one-shots you, monsters two-shot you, you cannot do the quests or dungeons, and you keep running back to safety, anxious. If you stay too long in the known areas, you get bored: in *Pokémon*, catching low-level Pokémon; in *World of Warcraft*, one-shotting monsters for a few bits of gold. In the unknown, the gold and experience are higher. Reality works the same way: with a challenge too high, you are anxious and make little progress; doing the same task every day without novelty or challenge, you are bored and wonder why you are not doing something else. Neither makes substantial progress. "The solution is to stay right at the edge of your challenge level." To stay in the range of optimal experience, "you need to consistently cultivate your skill set and take on higher challenges. That's where the meaningful flow of information is maximized. That's where you feel as if you are always learning something new. That's where your life becomes an enjoyable blur of progress, a spiritual state of clear perception where things just feel right" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08).

The mini map unifies the two senses of "edge." The light area is what you know and can do; the dark area is what you do not yet know and cannot yet do; the boundary is both the edge of your knowledge and the edge of your abilities. The skill–challenge graphic and the known–unknown graphic are the same picture drawn with different axes.

#### Meaningful events at the edge

What happens at the edge, in Koe's account, is not only enjoyment but meaning. In 2023: the balance between the known and the unknown, "living at your edge," "is where meaningful events occur" and where you feel best: "when you aren't too comfortable and you aren't too uncomfortable, you're right in the middle, where skill matches the challenge of the situation." When the flow of information is maximized "through the balance of consumption and creation, you will encounter meaningful events; you will feel a deep sense of excitement for the future, fulfillment in the present and gratitude for the past." The corollary: "You aren't where you want to be because you've been where you are for a bit too long. You have to push yourself into the unknown," set your own conscious, self-generated goals, and pursue them "with actual effort. Yes, it takes effort; it's going to be hard" (**Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`, 2023-09-24).

In 2024 he repeated the formula and added a tolerance for overwhelm. "The lesson or solution to this in all areas of your life is to live at your edge, the edge of the known or the edge of the unknown." Do something challenging, but not so challenging that you cannot think; "a little bit of overwhelm is very good, because it shows that you're challenging yourself" and expanding your mind to register new opportunities, potentials and ideas. Reading a new book, much of it will not make sense, but one idea alone can produce the excitement you feel when you know you can change your future (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11).

The phrase "the balance of consumption and creation" adds a variable to the edge. Information comes in through consumption and is metabolized through creation; too much consumption without creation is overwhelm, too much creation without new input is stagnation. The temporal triad (excitement for the future, fulfillment in the present, gratitude for the past) describes what a life at the edge feels like when all three time frames are in order: the goal pulls, the task absorbs, and the path already traveled makes sense.

#### The mind as a digestive system of reality

That variable has its own metaphor, one of Koe's most characteristic. He builds a bridge between "food metabolism or digestion and information metabolism or digestion," because "life is experience and experience is information." Metaphysically, he says, "everything is in formation" in the present moment (infinite consciousness, however you phrase it), and our brains are processing all that information. "Your mind dictates your quality of life because your development dictates how you process information, or how you process life." Overwhelmed with information, you feel anxious; underwhelmed, bored. "To maximize the flow of information, think of your mind as a **digestive system of reality**." And he develops the parallel: if you eat too much or do not move enough, you do not metabolize the information; if you eat too little or move too much, you digest it too easily and neither use the extra information nor sit "at the edge, where you're in this growth phase" (**Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`, 2023-09-24).

In 2024 he connected the metaphor to the lifetime bits of conscious attention (here 125 billion), which he asks you to imagine as "your life's potential." Your mind is "the digestive system for reality itself." Eat too much information and you get anxious; eat too little and you get bored. To maximize enjoyment, maximize the time spent "at our edge": not bored, not anxious, in the middle, where everything becomes meaningful and you are "using what we're learning by building" (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07).

And later in 2024 he gave it its most human version. "I like to think of the mind as the metabolism for experience": eat too much and you are sluggish, too little and you are on edge; overwhelmed with information, anxious; underwhelmed, bored. Humans often bite off more than they can chew: they accept more work when already neck-deep, try to learn too much and expect no overwhelm, say yes when they know they should say no so that the other person does not feel bad. "The optimal state of mind is when you metabolize life as it comes: you learn and do without thinking and emerge as a new person." People in flow often do not remember it, but when their sense of self returns, it feels as if they came back a new person (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

The metaphor reframes the skill–challenge match as a matter of intake. Challenge is information arriving faster than skill can process it; boredom is too little information for the capacity available. "Metabolize life as it comes" is then the ideal: a rate of experience that matches the rate of processing. The examples of overcommitment (taking on more work, saying yes to avoid discomfort) show that the anxiety region is often entered not by bold choices but by an inability to refuse. And "emerge as a new person" connects flow to identity change: digestion is how the outside becomes part of you, and the self that returns after flow has been changed by what it absorbed. In Koe's lexicon the image goes by several names that designate the same idea: information metabolism, metabolism for experience, digestive system of (or for) reality.

#### "The most miserable place is the middle"

The vocabulary of this section creates an apparent contradiction that the reader should resolve rather than ignore. In the same 2023 video, Koe says that "the most miserable place you can be is in the middle" (section 10.2) and that flow lies "right in the middle," where skill matches challenge. In 2023, 2024 and 2025 he again describes the edge as "the middle" ("right in the middle, where skill matches the challenge"; "not bored, not anxious, in the middle"; "the middle, where I just can't help but focus"). Is the middle the worst place or the best?

The word designates two different things. In the first statement, "the middle" means a lukewarm life that avoids both the highs and the lows, the successes and the catastrophic failures, a life lived far inside the known. In the second, it means the point of balance between boredom and anxiety, which is by definition at the boundary of the known. The miserable middle is the center of the light area of the map; the flow middle is its edge. Koe does not point out the double use himself; the reconciliation follows from reading each statement in its context, and the corpus record classifies it as a terminological, not a substantive, conflict.

#### Meaning at the edge: true education

The edge is also where Koe locates meaning and learning, and the two claims are joined. "Meaning is found at the edge of your abilities, the edge of the unknown, not so deep that you are met by chaos, but not so shallow that you close yourself off to the depth": the sweet spot in which your skills are enough to navigate the challenge. You have been there: feeling incredible for no reason, knowing exactly what to do, unable to stop having ideas, a vision so clear that you must act. "The flow state of consciousness is where the flow of information is maximized": all thoughts, ideas and mental energy are useful for your meaningful hierarchy of goals, "maximum signal, minimum noise, maximum focus, minimum distraction." "This is **true education**: learning, doing and discovering in unison," as opposed to being told what to learn for a task you do not care about on a path hordes of people have gone down (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

"True education" is a phrase Koe uses in several senses across the corpus (Part VI and Chapter 35 collect them); here its sense is precise: learning that happens in flow, at the edge, driven by a goal you chose. It is defined against school, which assigns the goal and separates learning from doing. The claim that flow "maximizes the flow of information" is Koe's reinterpretation of Csikszentmihalyi's "order in consciousness" in the information vocabulary of Chapter 5: in flow, everything that enters attention is signal because the goal hierarchy decides what counts.

In 2023 he connected the edge to the expansion of what he calls the domain of mastery. A graphic about fulfillment (only partly reconstructible from the transcript) shows that you must push the boundary of the known: acquire skills to match a challenge, consistently learn and practice on the challenges that arise in pursuit of your goals, and find fulfillment in challenging problems "because they are intrinsically aligned with you." Doing so, "you're shining the light of awareness on what used to be the unknown," increasing the volume of your domain of mastery, the field in which you can solve creative problems. If the unknown is the market and the known is your niche, your niche slowly expands, and the skills you acquire let you target a problem within it (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). The "light of awareness" of the map (section 10.2) here becomes the mechanism of a business: the niche of Part VII is the lit area of your map, and it grows at its edge.

#### Purpose revealed in layers

The edge is also where purpose, in Koe's sense, becomes visible. In 2023 he read an unattributed quotation: as you open yourself to living at your edge, your deepest purpose slowly makes itself known, and meanwhile you experience layer after layer of purposes, each closer to the fullness of your deepest purpose. To reach new levels of mind, he continues, you must encounter problems and live at your edge, going after goals and problems you are actually able to solve. Problems and goals "don't exist without each other": encountering a problem implies a goal to overcome it. A goal gives you an expectation ("I want to make $100,000"), and the gap closes by solving subsequent problems one after another, "tiny change after tiny change, choice after choice," because you now view problems through the lens of the goal, a better perspective. To solve them you must increase your skill and acquire the knowledge to see the problem in a better light (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05).

The passage joins three ideas from earlier chapters: purpose as the most urgent problem (Chapter 8), purpose evolving through levels (Chapter 9), and the goal as a lens (Chapter 6). The edge is the place where they become one process: you see the next problem only from the lens of a goal, you can solve it only at the edge of your skill, and solving it reveals the next layer of purpose.

#### No mistakes without a goal: learning to swim

A late formulation states the dependence of perception on goals in its starkest form. "You can't perceive what is a mistake if you don't have a goal that allows you to." Your current life is fine to you until you set and cultivate a new goal, develop reasons for it and understand what you do not want; only then do you have a frame in which to see and fix the mistakes in your lifestyle. And then the method: "You must launch into the unknown, feel as if you are drowning, and learn how to swim." This is how you learn anything, he says, and why most people learn nothing: they jump in the deep end, complain that it is hard as if it should be otherwise, or as if hard meant bad rather than simply reality, and blame anyone but themselves for being unable to do anything without it being given to them (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15).

The drowning image is in tension with the mini map, where "jumping into the middle of the ocean" without knowing how to swim was precisely the mistake. The difference is one of degree and of attitude. The mini map warns against teleporting far beyond your level; the drowning image describes the normal discomfort of the edge, which feels like drowning but is survivable if you accept it as the condition of learning rather than as a sign that something is wrong. Section 10.6 takes this tension further: tactical stress is a deliberate jump into water deeper than comfortable, justified only by the knowledge that you can, in fact, swim.

#### Never stop evolving

The section closes where the chapter's argument about the character began. Echoing his diagnosis that most people stop changing, learning and building, Koe says that continuous growth "has to happen if you want an enjoyable life": "you need something to immerse your attention in and continuously progress at that throughout life and develop yourself into a **complex being**," able to take on bigger and broader challenges and to have more opportunities to choose from and "play games." He quotes Csikszentmihalyi again: "At this point in our history it should be possible for an individual to build a self that is not simply the outcome of biological drives and cultural habits but a conscious personal creation" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21; the same prevention advice closes the 2022 passage on self-centeredness and self-consciousness in `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`).

The phrase "complex being" links flow to Chapter 5's account of order and complexity. Each time the challenge rises and skill rises to meet it, the person becomes more complex: more skills, more perspectives, more of the map lit. That complexity is what makes the next, larger challenge perceivable and accessible. The skill–challenge channel is therefore not only a recipe for enjoyment; it is the mechanism by which a self becomes "a conscious personal creation." Section 10.7 draws the conclusion for a whole life.

### 10.6 Tactical Stress

#### A deliberate jump to the edge

Section 10.5 described the edge as something you approach gradually: puncture the known with consistent effort, step into the unknown slowly, take challenges just above your level. Koe also has a second, opposite way of reaching it, which he calls **tactical stress**. It is his own coinage, he uses it from 2022 to 2026, and he consistently presents it as both his most effective strategy and a dangerous one. It belongs in this chapter because it is a technique for forcing the skill–challenge match: when comfort has pulled you into the boredom region, tactical stress throws you deliberately toward the anxiety region, at a distance you judge you can close.

#### The definitions

The term has been defined several times, and the changes between definitions are part of its meaning.

The earliest, from a 2022 tweet Koe reads aloud: "Tactical stress is the conscious decision to put yourself in a do-or-die situation knowing that you have the skill to make it work and will accept nothing less from success." In practice, he says, once he had clarity and vision about what he wanted, he arranged things so that he had to act with urgency. Two conditions are built into the definition: the decision is conscious, and it presupposes that you already have the skill. He adds immediately: "use common sense here," "make a wise decision with what I'm saying here" (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

In a 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, Koe describes the same idea more loosely: "most of the growth in my life has come from forcing myself to push to that point and let my actions follow back up." He had scarcity around rent (from dorms to four roommates at $300 a month) and then saw a place at $1,700 a month, "nothing I've ever paid before," but he knew he could afford and sustain it, and "if it's uncomfortable I can make myself comfortable." "It's like the opposite of lifestyle inflation": he moves in a way that offsets inflation, because he has the skill to make ten times that and the move launches him there, "gradually increasing that ladder." His warning: "that can get dangerous very quick, clearly, but it hasn't in my life yet." The same applies to coaching: "I know I can make something work, I don't know what I'm missing… so I'm going to drop 5K on this coaching," and every time he gets what he needs and usually stops showing up because he has already made it work: "the money was kind of just a way to force that." Dickie Bush's response gives the idea a second frame: "there needs to be a term for the opposite of lifestyle inflation, where you make an investment that forces you to grow into that," like signing a lease that is slightly uncomfortable and asking "what does that type of person do, who makes those types of choices?" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). In the same conversation Koe distinguishes tactical stress from what he calls the ignorance tax, describing them as similar but different.

**Complementary context:** "lifestyle inflation" (or lifestyle creep) is the common personal-finance term for spending that rises with income, so that higher earnings never translate into savings or freedom. Bush's question, in the voice of the future self ("what does that type of person do?"), echoes the identity-based framing popularized by James Clear, whom Bush quotes in his conversations with Koe.

In October 2023 Koe gave the definition he would treat as canonical: "Tactical stress is the conscious decision to force yourself into a do-or-die situation, knowing that you have the skill to make it work, and have no choice but to overcome your fears. The result is a **season of intensity** that propels you into the next level of your life." He calls it "the secret to abnormal results" and "a very extreme and potentially dangerous strategy," based on a pattern he noticed many times in his life. Its use: when you identify that you are in a rut ("things are getting very stagnant"), you now have a tool. Normally you would feel stuck and wait until "life happens and you eventually get out," "but this is a way to force your way out" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

In 2024 he states its relation to pain: "Pain is sometimes a necessary requirement for reaching a new stage of development, and even if it's not a prerequisite to it, it's usually the byproduct of transformation." Tactical stress is launching yourself into a very stressful situation to force yourself to evolve, which he did "by moving to a place that I couldn't afford, forcing me to do better in my business and grow and expand through those negative emotions." And the limit: "that's not for the faint-hearted; many people can't handle that." Understanding the stages of development, he adds, "helps you see what lies ahead… helps you orient your development" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03).

In 2025 he redefines it in the vocabulary of environment design: "Tactical stress is when you put yourself in a high-pressure situation that creates a strong deadline to achieve the goal you've been meaning to achieve," "a form of **environment engineering**" (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10). And in 2026 he uses it in the vocabulary of strategy: "You need pressure. You need tactical stress. You need to put yourself in a position where you must craft a strategy or you lose" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

|---|---|---|
| 2022-06 | Conscious do-or-die situation, knowing you have the skill, accepting "nothing less from success" | Original; conscious choice and existing skill as conditions |
| 2023-03 | Push to an uncomfortable commitment and let your actions catch up; "the opposite of lifestyle inflation" (with Dickie Bush) | Financial framing; the investment that forces growth |

The core never changes: a voluntary situation of real pressure, entered with skills you already have, that you cannot easily back out of. What changes is the vocabulary in which it is justified (urgency, finance, development, environment, strategy) and the warnings that surround it, which grow over time. Elsewhere in the corpus, Koe also uses "tactical stress" for a cycle of rest and sprints that he compares to how a lion works; that sense is not developed here.

#### Why comfort stalls you

The first part of the argument is a diagnosis of comfort. Moving, Koe says, was "a great way to push myself into the unknown," because "around every six months is when things start to really become normal to you. You fall into a routine, you know the friend group… the places you can eat, the places you can walk… you've pretty much explored everything physical to you." The mental realm can be explored infinitely through the internet, but "you can get into a comfortable spot with the place that you're living and it can really impact how far you progress in life, simply because you just become comfortable without knowing it." People think comfort is good because it lowers everyday stress, "which is a good thing," "but you're not pushing yourself into the unknown in every domain that you can, whether it be physical or mental or financial" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

The six-month figure is a personal observation, and it recurs in the corpus: the job's "tutorial phase" ends after about six months (section 10.7), and people repeat "the same six months" for years. In the vocabulary of section 10.5, comfort is the slow slide from the channel into the boredom region, which happens not because the person changes but because the challenge, once mastered, stops being one. The danger he points to is that the slide is invisible: "you just become comfortable without knowing it."

#### The goldfish and Parkinson's law

Koe's favorite image for the remedy is a goldfish. "A goldfish only grows as large as the tank that it is put in. If you put it in a small tank it will only grow to a certain amount; if you put it into a large tank it will grow to fit that." Tactical stress, he says, is "quite similar": the environment, the tank, sets the size you grow into (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). In 2025 he compresses it into an instruction: "You can only grow as much as the tank you were put in, so put yourself in a new tank" (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02).

**Complementary context:** the belief that goldfish grow only to the size of their tank is popular but only partly accurate. Goldfish in small tanks often do grow less, but mainly because of poor water quality and stunting, which harm the fish, rather than because they adapt their size to the available space. Koe uses the image as a metaphor for the effect of environment on growth, not as a biological claim, and it should be read that way.

In late 2024 he gave a three-part explanation of why "calculated risk" of this kind works (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15):

1. **The goldfish.** "Goldfish grow to fit the tank you put them in, but if you keep them in a small bowl they never grow"; the same is true of your mind.
2. **Parkinson's law.** "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." Launching into the unknown threatens survival and gives you less time to succeed, so the work contracts to fit.
3. **Going full circle.** Koe asks you to imagine a gradient line from black to white bent into a circle, so that the extremes meet and one flips instantly into the other: the joke so unfunny it becomes funny, the person so dumb they become insightful, "someone who is in so much pain that they have no option but to find enjoyment."

The psychology, he concludes: a challenging goal that you know deep down you can achieve creates a real deadline, which eliminates distractions and leverages survival instincts to learn what is needed to stay alive; the mind focuses only on important things.

The three reasons work at different levels. The goldfish is about environment setting the ceiling of growth. Parkinson's law (Chapter 6 introduced it; Koe does not name its author, C. Northcote Parkinson) is about time: a shorter deadline compresses the work. "Full circle" is Koe's own expression, used in several senses in the corpus; here it describes an extreme state flipping into its opposite, so that pain pushed far enough becomes the condition of enjoyment. The common thread is the "real deadline," and it explains the connection to the previous section: a real deadline raises the challenge and simultaneously removes the spare attention through which boredom and distraction enter.

#### What stress does to the mind: signal and noise

Koe describes the effect of tactical stress on his own work in terms that echo the description of flow. "This is where I thrive, this is where I make the most progress. It forces me to publish anything that I've been putting off, or act on the ideas that I've had in my mind." If he is moving and knows his bank account will fall below his standard, "my survival is going to be threatened, I'm going to be put in this state of stress, and I'm going to have to work to survive." A condition is attached: without a way of creating resources, "like having a business," it is very hard to progress or achieve autonomy and freedom. In this time he is extremely productive: flow of information, idea generation, focused output, "because I have that singular goal in the back of my mind." "Life becomes so much more meaningful because it's easy to filter the signal from noise in the day-to-day situations… because you're so dead focused on accomplishing a goal that you're forcing yourself to hit by throwing yourself into the unknown, doing something that you can't back out of, like signing a lease" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

The mechanism is the dual structure of attention from section 10.2 under high pressure: a singular goal in the background, everything in the foreground judged by whether it serves the goal. The phrase "it's easy to filter the signal from noise" is the same "maximum signal, minimum noise" that defined true education in section 10.5. Tactical stress, on this account, is a way of forcing the conditions of flow when they do not arise on their own.

In 2025 Koe connects the technique to a state he calls **pure focus**, which he presents as the requirement of rapid learning, and which he doubts many people today have experienced: "Just you and the task and the skill you're trying to learn. No thought, no emotion, no distraction." He defines distraction strictly: "a distraction is any person, place, or thing that pulls any thread of attention away from your goal." In pure focus "you literally cease to exist and you become one with reality. Input and output merge": every slice of information is instantly transformed into raw material put back into reality, "like a lock and key." It is "a period of intensity where your mind collapses on a clear goal"; pattern recognition goes into overdrive; books, content, music and conversations gain a new dimension; "reality becomes the source of inspiration, as it always has been," without distractions pulling you away from "that connection with what is." For most people, most of the time, this does not happen, and learning is "a slow grind that you hate" (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10).

Pure focus is described in the same terms as flow (loss of self, merging with the task) but with an emphasis on learning speed and on intensity. The definition of distraction is unusually strict ("any thread of attention") and only makes sense relative to a goal: the same conversation can be a distraction or raw material depending on whether it serves the goal. In the same video he explains why he needs a technique to reach this state.

#### Skill depends on state

He observes, as "some concept here," that skill level is not fixed. Your skills can be at a specific level ("level 50 in Photoshop"), but if you use the skill from a negative, narrow state caused by stress, anxiety and negative thoughts, "that skill level decreases by a lot. You're operating at a 10, a level 10 on Photoshop, when you're actually a level 50 when you operate from a place of calm." It is "a double whammy": you have learned all this about business, but with a narrow, stressed mind "you still feel like a beginner" (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10).

This observation sits in apparent tension with the praise of stress, and the tension is instructive. The stress that degrades skill is the narrow, anxious state of section 10.5, where attention turns toward the self. The stress Koe recommends is the stress of a clear external deadline that pulls attention *away* from the self and onto the goal. The difference is not the amount of pressure but where attention goes under it, which is why the definition of tactical stress always includes the clause "knowing that you have the skill." Without confidence in the skill, pressure produces self-consciousness, and skill drops from 50 to 10.

#### The idle state

Why is a technique needed at all? Koe's 2025 answer introduces another coinage. His "trick" for kicking himself into overdrive is deployed "when my mind is filled with dissonance": tired of the life he has been living, with a somewhat clear idea of where he wants to be, "stuck in the middle." His examples of tactical stress here are quitting your job to make your business work, moving into an apartment you cannot afford in order to push for a new career, and saying hi to the person you are attracted to, so that you must at least start "some kind of jumbled conversation": "then you either make it work or you hesitate and get crushed." Why it works: most people fall into "the **idle state**," numbing themselves before they hit rock bottom, "never allowing the pain of where they are to outweigh the pain of where they want to be." Deploy the technique when you notice that your current situation's pain is holding you back, "to avoid repeating the same six months for the rest of your life" (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10).

This connects tactical stress to the account of rock bottom in Chapter 4. There, change was often triggered when the pain of staying exceeded the pain of changing. The idle state is the condition in which that never happens, because numbing (the cheap dopamine of Chapter 11) keeps the pain of the present just below the threshold. Tactical stress manufactures the pressure that rock bottom would otherwise provide, without waiting for it.

#### The one-rep max

Koe's most careful image for the technique comes from the gym. Testing your one-rep max on the bench is not supposed to be easy. It is a calculated decision ("I think I can achieve this big milestone"): you put yourself under the bar, and if you do not use everything you have trained for, you get crushed and feel bad (you can try again later). You hype yourself up, then go all in. Mimic this in your environment, he says: like holding your one-rep max, "but you know you can lift it," and not for one to ten seconds but as "a 3 to six month thing," under mental and emotional pressure while grinding through. It feels good (you love testing your max because "that's where everything you've trained for comes into play"), but "it's bad to do it too often because you might get injured." It is something to do "once or twice a year to kick yourself, to see how far you've come." If you are stuck in a slow, boring grind, test your one-rep max (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10).

The metaphor carries three constraints that the earlier definitions only implied. The weight must be one you can lift: a test of accumulated training, not a substitute for it. The duration is bounded: months, not a permanent way of life. And the frequency is limited: once or twice a year, because the cost of overuse is injury. Read together with the skill–challenge model, tactical stress is a brief, deliberate excursion to the top of the channel, not a permanent residence above it.

#### The author's record of "stupid" decisions

Most of the evidence Koe offers for tactical stress is his own history, and he tells it as a chronology.

- **2018.** "I wasted my life savings and maxed out my first credit card in hopes that my dreams of building a massive e-commerce business would work."
- **2019.** "I got rid of everything I owned because of the situation that I was in," flew across the country, stayed a while, realized it was not for him (there was no Wi-Fi and his Zoom calls with clients did not work), flew back and moved into a place. During that time he also quit his job, knowing that each of the freelance clients that let him quit "could disappear at any time" (not a large probability, he says, but it sat in the back of his mind).
- **2020.** "I signed a lease that was two times more than anything that I've ever paid before and anything that I thought that I could afford," which forced the mindset that he had to make his business work.
- **2021 onward.** He moved about every six months, not as a conscious plan, each time "into a place that was just a bit above my comfort zone in terms of what I could afford." He was fine with it "because I knew that the only limits in the digital world in terms of business were the ones that I believed."

(**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02.) In a 2024 retelling, the 2019 episode is described as getting rid of everything except a bag of clothes and his laptop and flying to a different country (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15); the 2023 version says "across the country." The difference is a detail of the telling.

The most cited episode is the lease. In 2022, without a date, he told it as follows. He wanted to move to a new place ("a step down from this, a step up from my last one") with a nice pool and a rent "way above my level." He knew he had the skill to make more money on the internet with his skill set (branding, marketing, sales), because he had exposed himself to it beforehand: "I had the ideas that beget this new idea of what I'm able to do with those skills, so I knew the money was there." The only thing left was to put himself in that environment. "I signed the lease even though I wasn't able to afford it at the time." That made him take action "left and right," moving the levers that let him make more money, free up time and afford the place: "that is when I hit my first 50k month as a consultant." He repeats the warning: use common sense, make a wise decision (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12).

In 2025 he gives the same episode an explicit interpretation. He "moved into an apartment that was two times what I could afford, to kind of go all-in on my business and force myself to make more and be comfortable with that amount." "On the surface it looked like I was just another victim of lifestyle creep"; friends and family "don't even have to say it for you to understand what they think": "why would you make that stupid decision?" "What they don't understand is that most of the time when I was making those irresponsible purchases, I was imposing a deadline on a challenge that I knew I could take on, that would force me to learn skills and acquire knowledge so that that purchase was no longer irrational or stupid. I was physically putting myself in the **environment of my future self** and allowing time to bridge the gap" (**Source:** `What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md`, 2025-08-03).

The two coinages in this passage, **imposing a deadline on a challenge** and **the environment of your future self**, are the most precise description Koe gives of what tactical stress is supposed to do. The purchase is not consumption; it is a commitment device that creates a challenge with a deadline, and the challenge forces the acquisition of skills that make the commitment rational after the fact. "Environment of your future self" connects it to Chapter 4's account of identity change through environment: instead of waiting to become the person who can afford the apartment, you live in it and let the gap force the becoming.

The record is not perfectly consistent, and the reader should know where. The 2022 telling describes a lease "way above my level" that led to his first $50,000 month; the 2023 and later tellings date it to 2020 and describe it as "two times" anything he had paid. The magnitude differs between tellings, though not in a way that changes the argument. And the 2025 reading, in which an "irresponsible purchase" is a deliberate forcing function, can be read against a 2024 passage (Chapter 9) in which superficial purchases at the status stage are something to "realize fast" and correct. Koe's distinction between the two is the function of the purchase: a purchase that forces skill development is a deadline; a purchase for status is a trap. The distinction is clear in principle; in practice, as he admits, only the person making the purchase can tell which it is.

A smaller story shows the same mechanism at lower stakes. Around the time his business was first starting, he wanted an email marketing software that cost about $1,500 a year, when his business "wasn't doing that good; that was a lot of money to me." "It deeply pained me to see $1,500 go out of my bank account to invest in something that I didn't even know if I could make work. But I trusted myself and I paid for the software." "I felt this pressure, this good pressure, this **good stress**, to learn the skills and acquire the knowledge that would allow me to maximize the use of that software." That meant learning landing page design, funnel design and copywriting, "and those things paid off massively" (**Source:** `What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md`, 2025-08-03).

What did the larger moves produce? Moving and "knowing that I couldn't afford certain things," he says, forced him to grow the business "from 50,000 a month to a hundred thousand a month to 200,000 a month plus, and having surges here and there of upwards of seven hundred thousand dollars a month." And he refuses to credit any single source: "It would be hard for me to credit any of this to any single business video or book or podcast or… course, where people think that they can just get all of the information. That's not how it works. You need experience, you need something that pushes you forward, you need to figure things out for yourself." "You can't just acquire knowledge and then… sit and do nothing with the knowledge because you're comfortable" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

The figures are self-reported and come from a single source; they illustrate the scale Koe attributes to the strategy, not a measured effect of it. The causal claim is also his interpretation: a business growing in the same period might have grown for other reasons, and Koe himself, by refusing to credit any single input, acknowledges that the growth had many causes. What the passage establishes clearly is his view of the relation between knowledge and pressure: knowledge without pressure stays inert.

One of the moves illustrates the role of environment beyond money. He moved to Austin, Texas, "with people like JK Molina and Dakota Robertson and my editor Devin"; they stayed six months, "figured out the location, we got what we needed out of it, and we made a ton of progress in business, especially being around those right people." Then they broke their lease in Texas "just because we wanted to get out, we wanted to experience something new," and he moved to Scottsdale, Arizona, a place he had always perceived "as, like, too high above me." At the time of filming he was in a temporary place (previously in Phoenix) and was about to sign a nine-month lease, "but honestly, I'll probably break it in six" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). The "right people" are part of the tank: the environment that sets the ceiling of growth is social as well as physical.

A final example shows tactical stress applied to skill rather than money. Koe learned After Effects, "a very complex software," in two weeks, because he filled a YouTube video's storyboard with animations he did not yet know how to make but wanted. He has to put out a YouTube video every week: "It's tied to my survival… my income… my sense of identity." If he does not, "entropy releases"; two weeks without posting and his mind races. "I have to put a video out because that's the life that I've built myself into" (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10). The weekly deadline is a permanent, low-intensity tactical stress built into the structure of his work: the storyboard raises the challenge above his current skill, and the deadline makes the gap impossible to postpone.

#### "The payoff of that risk is the mistake"

Koe frames the whole record with a principle about failure. "If it wasn't a mistake, you didn't learn a thing." His best decisions are ones most people thought were stupid; he gives himself permission to do "stupid things" and discovers a great deal, though he grants that many people are smart to hold back if they could not pull through or are ruining their lives ("be smart"). Most of his massive mistakes led to most of his growth. These decisions start as risks, and "the payoff of that risk is the mistake": if a risk led only to success, you would learn nothing and could not replicate it (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15).

The principle completes the account of feedback from section 10.2. A mistake widens the frame and lets distractions in; it is also the richest feedback there is, because it shows where skill fell short of challenge. A risk that succeeds teaches little because it gives no information about the limits of your skill. The value of tactical stress, in this reading, lies partly in its failures.

Koe carries a reminder of this on his body: an ouroboros tattoo on his right arm, a symbol of alchemy showing a snake eating its own tail in the shape of an infinity sign. It reminds him that "creation demands destruction," that one cannot exist without the other: "progress demands sacrifice" (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11). The image condenses the logic of tactical stress: the comfortable version of yourself (and its comforts) is what is destroyed so that the next version can be created.

#### The strategies

In the 2023 video, Koe lists the strategies that have worked for him, with an explicit caveat: "This is where things get touchy, so I would encourage you to keep an open mind throughout this, but… these are not the only things you can do to implement this strategy. There's quite a few things, and I encourage you just to discover your own, but these are just the ones that have worked for me." The list: (1) launching a product before building it, (2) location hopping, (3) investing in education, (4) material items, (5) degeneracy, (6) emotional transmutation (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). Four of them are developed in the material of this chapter; launching a product before building it belongs to the treatment of products in Part XI, investing in education was illustrated above by the coaching and software purchases, and emotional transmutation is not developed in these sources.

**Location hopping.** He moved "every six months or so, if not less, if not more," whenever he "became way too acclimated to the location, I became too comfortable, I wasn't making much progress, my income stagnated, my business stagnated, my ideas stagnated." The cause, he says, was that he was no longer in a physical and mental environment conducive to progress ("they play into each other a lot"). He knew all the restaurants, most of the people he would run into, whether befriending certain people would benefit his future; his walks were no longer new. "That's a great way to generate ideas: just walk down a new path, throw yourself into the unknown, take a different route to work." In the end, "there was no further room for discovery for me" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). The small version ("take a different route to work") shows that the principle scales down: what matters is novelty that reopens the map, not the size of the move.

**Material items.** "Money doesn't buy happiness… you don't need a fancy car, a fancy watch, a fancy house… that's a half-truth. I agree to an extent, but there is utility behind those things as long as they aren't, like, shallow, surface-level pursuits." "A lot of the good things in my life came from being what other people would perceive as materialistic, and they taught me about the thing that I purchased." His story: he bought a $40,000 watch, got home, put it on "and just hated what I saw," and returned it the next day (it had a return policy). The lesson he drew was never to buy a fancy watch again unless it really means something, he knows its history, he truly enjoys it, it is a hobby like watch collecting, or he has so much money he does not have to worry about it (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). This passage nuances an earlier position in the corpus (you don't want the mansion, you want the experience underneath it; looking at it is cheap dopamine) by granting material objects real utility as a path to experience and learning. The watch story is an example of what that utility looks like: the purchase taught him something about himself that he could not have learned by imagining it, and the lesson was paid for by a mistake he could reverse.

**Degeneracy.** Koe uses this word with a sense of his own. "If you feel all pent up, and you feel like you aren't making progress but you're working towards a goal, then maybe you need to go out, maybe you need to have some fun, maybe you need to let loose, maybe you need to let loose a little bit too much, because the next day when you wake up and you feel like [garbage] and you really see what it's like to not be able to make progress towards your goals, you feel the struggle that it takes, you don't have ideas… you're just sitting there stuck, and you're like, why did I do that? And then the next day or two days after, when you're feeling better, that surge of energy allows you to attack at full force" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). The condition is essential: you are already working toward a goal and feel pent up. Degeneracy works, in his account, by contrast: a day of being unable to progress makes progress felt as a privilege, and the rebound provides energy. It is the "full circle" logic applied at the scale of a weekend. Of all the strategies, this is the one most obviously open to self-deception, since "letting loose" can be justified as strategy after the fact; Koe's condition (an existing goal and pent-up energy) is the only safeguard he gives.

**Irreversible acts: war mode.** In 2025 tactical stress became step one of what Koe calls war mode (Chapter 4): "Commit and shave your head." It is not required (especially for women), he says, but the point stands: do something physical, representative of your commitment, that you cannot turn back from. Too many people want to change without consequences; they have too many plan B's and are not fully invested, because they fear leaving a comfort that "will always prove to end up more uncomfortable than committing to a life you want." His examples: buy new clothes and burn your old ones; sign a lease on an apartment you cannot afford, so that you are forced to make your business work; get on a plane across the country with a few dollars and a plan; put your last few dollars into marketing. "Money doesn't buy happiness, but it buys the ability to make mistakes, which leads to growth, which leads to happiness." "This is tactical stress": do the thing that makes your self-doubt real, because in your head it is scarier than reality (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02).

The last sentence identifies a second mechanism beside the deadline. Self-doubt is largely imagined; the irreversible act replaces the imagined consequences with actual ones, which are usually smaller. The remark about money is a further revision of "money doesn't buy happiness": money's value lies in the room it gives for mistakes, and mistakes, as above, are where learning happens.

#### Tactic-level thinking and the general

The 2026 strategic-thinking video gives tactical stress its most general justification. "Plain tactic level thinking is very disastrous." "The average person lives in a mindless cycle of struggles they can't escape." "Thank goodness you aren't actually on a battlefield, because you'd probably be dead." Most people commit "highly predictable strategic mistakes" that winners would study in order to do the opposite: getting married too early or too late; not developing mastery in anything; trusting that AI can do everything for you; chasing money over meaning; irresponsibly going into debt; staying in a bad relationship; not investing in education; climbing a corporate ladder that leads nowhere. On AI he adds that he is "not anti-AI completely, because everything is a religion nowadays": the "flavor of the day drama… every three months" is something people get obsessed with, cry about, play victim with and virtue-signal on, and "you can see the signs of lower levels of thinking in those people and you can avoid them" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

Then the remedy. "You're living under this guise of modern comfort and convenience where… you're allowed to just coast through life." "If you were on a battlefield, think of the general. His own life was on the line. That forced strategic depth." "You need pressure. You need tactical stress. You need to put yourself in a position where you must craft a strategy or you lose. Or else you're just going to lose either way." His concrete moves: move into an apartment you cannot afford; book the plane ticket to somewhere you have never been; invest in the business software, course or coaching "even if you think it's a scam, because at least your finances will be put on the line and you can't live in a shroud of false comfort anymore" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

The argument links tactical stress to the "player and opponent" passage of section 10.1, which comes from the same video: comfort lets people react instead of think, and pressure forces them to think. "**Tactic-level thinking**" is his name for life lived move by move without strategy, and "false comfort" for the condition that permits it.

The same video also contains a principle that points the other way, and the corpus record flags the conflict. Among its principles of strategy is preparation: do not "rush into battle"; build a financial runway, reduce expenses and hedge risk, a risk that grows as you move up the hierarchy of goals. Koe does not reconcile the two in the video. One plausible reading, offered here as interpretation rather than as his position, is that they apply to different people and moments: pressure for the person stagnating in comfort, preparation for the person about to make a major change (leaving a job, ending a relationship) whose downside would be severe.

#### Warnings and conditions

Koe surrounds the technique with warnings, and they are as much a part of it as the definitions.

The first is a prerequisite: a track record. "That's the prerequisite here: if you don't have a track record of being ambitious and taking action on certain things, this strategy isn't for you. Dude, do not, please do not do this, because I know it's going to come back and it's like, 'oh, Dan told me to fly across the country and waste my entire life savings and now I'm homeless,' so don't do that" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

The second is calculation. He is not telling you to do something absolutely stupid that you know you should not do; you need "at least a shred of self-belief" that you can make it work. "Don't double your rent thinking it'll magically 10x your business": that is the quick-fix mindset. You have to understand the process of putting yourself in a stressful situation: "it has to be calculated to the point where you know that you don't have any other option but success" (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15).

The third is temperament: "not for the faint-hearted; many people can't handle that" (2024). The fourth is frequency and duration, from the one-rep max: three to six months, once or twice a year. The fifth runs through every version: common sense, and the existing skill that makes the situation survivable.

Gathered together, the conditions describe a narrow window. Tactical stress, as Koe defines it, applies to a person who (1) already has the skill to close the gap, (2) has a track record of acting on ambition, (3) has a calculated belief that success is possible, (4) is stuck in comfort rather than in crisis, and (5) uses it occasionally and for a bounded period. Outside that window, the same moves are not tactical stress but recklessness, and the skill–challenge model explains why: without the skill, the forced jump lands far above the channel, in the anxiety region where, as the Photoshop example showed, even existing skill collapses.

Two broader tensions in the corpus frame the technique. The first concerns the pace of change. Across the years Koe alternates between a gradual register (identity as a city from which you remove small pieces, "small calculated taps," progressive overload in small increments, "take it gradually") and an extreme register ("rip the Band-Aid off," "give yourself permission to be extreme," "it's easier to change everything at once," war mode). He never explicitly retracts either, and in 2025 he even says that "changing your life doesn't mean packing up, moving across the country," in contrast to the war-mode examples. The reasons he gives at various points suggest a division of labor: the plan B approach "can work, absolutely," and his own preference for the extreme is personal; pressure increases risk for people at lower levels of consciousness; and the urgency of a particular moment can supply the pressure on its own. A reasonable reading is that the gradual register applies to central pieces of identity, specific habits and people with little margin, and the extreme register to brief, deliberate shifts in environment and intensity. Chapter 4 treats this tension in full.

The second concerns pain. In 2023 Koe says that "man cannot remake himself without suffering" and that "a season of internal struggle is the requirement." In 2024 the claim softens to "sometimes a necessary requirement… and even if it's not a prerequisite, it's usually the byproduct." By 2025 pain is "the signal that change is happening," something to use deliberately ("make the pain of where you are outweigh any other pain"), and a clear vision makes the chaos "tolerable." The direction of the refinement is from pain as an inevitable cost to pain as a signal that can be used on purpose, and tactical stress is the clearest case of that use.

### 10.7 Infinite Games

#### Finite and infinite games

The skill–challenge model has a consequence for a whole life, not only for a single task: if the challenge must keep rising for flow to continue, then the games worth playing are those in which it can keep rising indefinitely. Koe states this as a guideline: "Play infinite games. Infinite games are ones that you don't stop playing; there is no end. Finite games are games that have a winner and a loser, so that means that there are rules and a goal for people to finally achieve, but then the game is over after that." "There are finite games within infinite games," and in infinite games "you can continue changing the rules and the goal so that it continues going further and further; it evolves." His examples are business or entrepreneurship, health and relationships (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). He cites no source for the distinction.

**Complementary context:** the distinction between finite and infinite games comes from James P. Carse's *Finite and Infinite Games* (1986): "A finite game is played for the purpose of winning, an infinite game for the purpose of continuing the play." Carse's book is philosophical and wide-ranging; the distinction later became popular in business writing, notably through Simon Sinek's *The Infinite Game* (2019). Koe uses the vocabulary without attribution and adapts it to goals, relationships, diets and work.

The nesting ("finite games within infinite games") is what makes the distinction usable. A launch, a competition or a quarter's target is a finite game with a clear end; a business, a body or a marriage is the infinite game that contains a succession of them. The mistake Koe warns against is treating a finite game as the whole: winning it, finding nothing after it, and stopping.

Two years earlier, in 2022, he had made the same point with an image of building. "You aren't seeing progress because you're obsessed with the polished end result, not the process that created it." You are building a mansion. With a construction team it might take a year or two, but you do not have a team ("you're only you"), so building your mansion will take your entire lifetime, "and that's exactly the point": "fall in love with infinite games, not finite status games" (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

The phrase "finite status games" names the specific kind of finite game Koe has in mind: games whose prize is a position relative to others (a title, an income bracket, a possession). Their end is built in, because once the position is reached there is nothing further to do except defend it. The mansion that takes a lifetime is the opposite: a game whose purpose is the building itself, which is the autotelic attitude of section 10.4 extended across decades.

#### The psyche craves challenge

Why should the game be infinite? Koe's answer is psychological, and he gives it most sharply in an argument about employment. "Very intelligent people are like, 'I love the comfort of a job, I love the stability,' but what they don't realize is that as an entrepreneur, or starting a business, you create your own stability; the stability would be better." "The reason you're unhappy and you want to leave that job is because the challenge stopped increasing; **your psyche craves challenge**." As a child everything was "fresh and new and novel"; in a corporate job, climbing the ladder, "it slowed down drastically," leaving "this robotic routine of tasks without any further challenge." "The only way to create the new challenge" is to keep "hopping from job to job" or to "start your business and become an entrepreneur." "Just do what you were meant to do, pursue your life's work, and then you have an infinite level of challenge; it's an infinite game at that point" (**Source:** `You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md`, 2024-09-29).

In 2023 he had built the same claim as a chain of steps, in a passage that links flow to evolution (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18):

1. We all feel the pull toward something greater than ourselves.
2. As we are pulled and desire a higher state of existence, we meet problems that must be solved.
3. Challenge narrows the mind on a problem standing in the way of a goal, and that is the driver of evolution.
4. If we ignore problems other than money problems because we work twelve to fourteen hours a day, we will not reach that point, or "fulfill our purpose of being on this Earth."
5. Challenges must evolve, and skill must keep increasing to match them: "**stagnation equals death**."
6. To reach new levels of challenge you must never stop learning, stack new skills and perspectives, and "increase the complexity of your character to be able to even perceive the challenge," solving deeper problems so that you do not get trapped in the superficial.

The chain is the skill–challenge model of section 10.5 raised to the scale of a life and given an evolutionary meaning. Step 3 joins the goal–problem structure of Chapter 8 with the narrowing of attention of this chapter: challenge is what a problem feels like from inside, and its function is to focus the mind. Step 6 contains the most interesting claim: some challenges are not merely too hard but *invisible* until the character is complex enough to perceive them. This is the locked quest and the dark region of the map once more, now as a description of personal development. "Stagnation equals death" is a hyperbole, but its sense is precise: a self whose challenge has stopped rising has stopped developing, and in Koe's frame that is the end of the game that matters.

#### Why the 14-hour hustler and the 9-to-5er are "the exact same"

Step 4 of that chain points at a target Koe's audience might not expect: the hard-working entrepreneur. "Most hard-working entrepreneurs would scoff" at the claim that they are "the exact same as a nine-to-five worker," he says, but they are, and the skill–challenge match explains why. Both working twelve to fourteen hours a day as an entrepreneur and doing the same mechanical 9-to-5 every day prevent personal evolution and are "dangerous for your psyche": "there is no higher ladder rung." The entrepreneur may make more progress in business and money, "but what about the things that matter that money contributes to?" He is careful not to dismiss money ("it matters a lot"); his point is that other things need cultivation, appreciation and attention (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18).

The argument is not about hours as such but about the shape of challenge. Both lives have narrowed to a single domain in which the challenge has stopped rising in kind, even if it rises in quantity. More of the same work is not a higher challenge; it is the same challenge for longer. The infinite game requires the challenge to change in kind, which, by the skill tree of section 10.2, usually means developing other domains (mind, body, relationships) that the single-domain grind neglects. This is the argument behind the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12: limiting work hours is a way of freeing the attention that other domains require.

#### Basic needs, then actualization

In 2025 Koe placed the same idea in a hierarchy of needs. "First, you fulfill your basic needs and then you pursue your actualization needs." To stay in "some kind of a flow state," "you need to gradually increase the challenge" you take on, which demands developing skills to match. Being stuck in repetitive work with no more challenge, repeating the same day, is neither fulfilling nor enjoyable; you are not learning, evolving or growing. This matters especially because eight hours a day, a third of life, goes there; a third goes to sleep; and the other third goes to scrolling "because you don't have anything better to dedicate your time to." So "you need to start building things" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md`, 2025-05-25).

**Complementary context:** the sequence from basic needs to actualization needs echoes Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs (physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization). Koe names neither Maslow nor Csikszentmihalyi in this passage; both the needs hierarchy and the requirement of rising challenge are presented as general psychological patterns.

The thirds-of-life arithmetic is rhetorical rather than measured, but it makes a structural point: when the largest block of waking life offers no rising challenge, the remaining time fills with the cheapest available game. The remedy, "start building things," is the project of Chapter 9 reintroduced as the source of challenge that the job no longer provides.

#### Entrepreneurship as an infinite game

The infinite-game argument leads Koe to his most polarizing recommendation, and its several formulations need to be read together.

In 2024: "everyone can be their own CEO, especially in today's world with the one-person business and technology," whereas in employment "you reach a specific rank in the corporate hierarchy" and "not everyone can be the CEO of that company." Entrepreneurship is infinite "because you can choose, you make the choice of what games you want to play." "Employment is where you win the game and then everything becomes boring. There's no new challenge, there's no evolution. You sit there performing the same repetitive task day in, day out like a specialized robot. You don't discover the depth of life and you get trapped there," trapped "on the surface, since there is no further goal to pursue in your life; you are stuck chasing the cheap pleasures" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30).

In 2025, addressed only to viewers who have agreed with him so far: "you need to start a business… right now… and you need to make it a success." That viewer will not object that "not everyone should start a business," because they want full control over their time, energy, focus and money. They understand that "the good life and flow states and anything good in life stems from an increasing level of challenge that is matched by an increasing level of development in your mind, body, spirit, finances." At 99% of jobs (not the 1% that most people will not get), the challenge stops after about six months: you have gone through the job's **tutorial phase**, it becomes normal, a repetitive routine, "a great way to waste 40 years of your life. It psychologically castrates you." He suggests researching psychology for a year to see it (**Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`, 2025-07-27).

In the same video he answers an objection: "I just want to make as much as I need." That is "perfectly fine," he says; you can be mostly happy and satisfied, and many people live great lives that way. "But you are limiting the level of challenge that you can take on in any domain of your life." His audience, he thinks, probably resonates with not wanting to relive the same six months for years. Few things allow continuous progress besides your work, mind, body and spirit, all intertwined, and you need to survive. "Entrepreneurship and value exchange is an infinite game" (**Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`, 2025-07-27).

And in a 2025 conversation with Sahil Bloom, he states it as a personal belief: "I think everyone should be an entrepreneur [for] the rest of their life. I think everyone should try it." His causal chain, tied to the idea that many people "die at 25": (1) people leave school and "lose their source of challenge. They aren't going to grade ten, 11, 12. They aren't learning new things"; (2) in a job "they learn for quite a bit of time… potentially go up some form of a ladder"; (3) "then after that, it kind of just drops off a cliff"; (4) responsibilities pile on (family, bills); (5) what is lost is "that novelty and challenge that leads to the good dopamine, leads to that feeling of progression, leads to needing to develop your skill set in your mind to match that new level that you're trying to reach." His proposed solution is "going off and doing your own thing and kind of jumping into the unknown," and he immediately admits: "I don't know if that's even a feasible solution for everyone" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). In the same conversation Bloom adds a layer: being enterprising can be practiced within a 9-to-5; "you don't have to burn the boats."

Finally, a 2023 heuristic narrows the difference between job and business to one variable. "With a job, I think it's good practice to job hop every six months to a year to increase your salary if you can, but you can also get stuck in a job and just get trapped in this robotic, anti-human state of living." In business "you can do that too, but you have full control: you can throw yourself out there by launching a product and force your business to grow. You have full control over how far into the unknown you push" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

How strong is the claim? The corpus shows Koe oscillating, without a chronological turn, between two tones about employment: the job as "the worst outcome," "a death sentence" or a form of slavery, and the job as "an absolutely incredible stepping stone," with explicit statements that he is "not anti-nine to five" and that "not all jobs are bad." He also moves, over time, from treating employee and entrepreneur as opposite trajectories to treating them as states of mind ("you can still be an employee that has high agency"; "entrepreneurship is not a role or a title, it's a state of mind"). And on whether to start a business, he said in 2024 "I don't even recommend starting a business" (meaning: as an act separate from documented personal development) while elsewhere calling entrepreneurship "the only logical option for long-term thinkers." Chapters 2, 9 and 28 trace these oscillations in full.

Read through the lens of this chapter, the oscillations have a common core that does not oscillate. What Koe objects to is not employment as such but a life in which the challenge has stopped rising and the person has stopped developing; the job is the most common case of that, not the only one (the 14-hour entrepreneur is another). What he recommends is not a legal form of work but a game in which you control how far into the unknown you push. The business is, for him, the most reliable way to have that control; the "state of mind" formulation and Bloom's layer acknowledge that some people can have it inside an organization. His own hedges ("I don't know if that's even a feasible solution for everyone"; "perfectly fine") mark the recommendation as his opinion and his personal rule, addressed to people who want more challenge, rather than as a universal prescription.

The objection about earning "as much as I need" also connects to a change of emphasis in the corpus. In 2024 Koe's ideal reader was a creative who wanted to earn more than enough, with rest, free time and meaningful work, without hustle; in 2025 he legitimizes explicitly the obsession with getting rich ("the concentration of force is the only way to get rich"), while still describing a spectrum between rich and comfortable on which "both goals are fine." The 2025 reply to "as much as I need" sits between the two: the modest goal is legitimate, and its cost, in his terms, is a ceiling on challenge.

#### Relationships as infinite games

The same logic applies, in Koe's account, to relationships, and it begins with a trap. In 2022, applying his model of dopamine (the "up world" of anticipation and the "down world" of possession, Chapter 11) to relationships, he says that a high school relationship will be dysfunctional almost "100% of the time" if you do not take time to develop yourself. "There is a trap lying within this whole thing": if you latch onto something because of the dopamine at the start, then "call it quits" and do not build or nurture what you have, it goes bad, because the up world is dopamine and the things you possess belong to the other neurotransmitters. People get into a relationship, "call it quits on their life, let themselves go," and their life and relationship suffer because they fell into "that initial **honeymoon phase**" with whatever they possessed, "whether it be a car, a person" (he clarifies that he is not saying you possess a person, only that they become part of your life). He contrasts two patterns: shortening the gap ("I have it now, but I haven't developed the depth I need to sustain that"), or bridging it and appreciating the whole process, "falling in love with the journey of developing depth, but also playing the attention-grabbing game so that you can actually have people see that depth" (**Source:** `Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md`, 2022-10-09).

In 2023 he names the cost of settling: if you are fine with a partner or a job that is merely comfortable and you do not know what more is possible, you are using it as an excuse to stay the same so that you do not have to improve. You are not pursuing something (or someone) better than you in other areas, which would force you to change who you are. "Instead you settle so that you don't have to improve, and instead you degress [regress], and you go deeper and deeper into a **hole of mediocrity**" (**Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17).

And in 2024 he states the positive version as an infinite game. "Long-term relationships are infinite games: you continue playing them and you change the rules as you reach new stages of the relationship. That's how you bring meaning, fulfillment and energy and passion into the relationship." "Flings here and there, that's a quick fix; that's how you bounce around on the surface and never invest long-term energy into one thing that brings out, or allows you to dive deeper and discover more in life" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30).

The three passages apply the full apparatus of the chapter to a domain that seems far from games. The honeymoon phase is a finite game: the challenge of winning someone is over once they are won, and if no new game replaces it, the relationship slides into the boredom region. Settling is the choice of a comfortable game below one's level to avoid the challenge that a better one would impose. The infinite game is the solution: changing the rules at each new stage keeps the challenge rising and the relationship in the channel. "Bouncing around on the surface" is the relational equivalent of finite status games, and its opposite, depth, is what only a long game can produce. The phrase "playing the attention-grabbing game so that people can see that depth," finally, anticipates Part IX: depth that no one perceives does not, in Koe's economy, create value for anyone else.

#### A predictable life is a meaningless story

The last idea of the chapter is the one that justifies the uncertainty the whole game involves. In 2022, among seven lessons drawn from seven failed online businesses, Koe gave this one: embrace the uncertain path. Nothing worth having is predictable. "A predictable life is not a good or meaningful life": you live the same actions day after day with nothing creating meaning or interest. Life is like a story: a story is not predictable (how will this battle go, who wins, how does it end), and that is what makes it interesting, provided you approach it from a zoomed-out lens, a "meta perspective," in which you stay present, see things for what they are and are not consumed by emotions (**Source:** `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md`, 2022-06-18).

The story image completes the game image. Games and stories, as section 10.1 noted, both offer the mind an indistractable frame; they differ in that a game's outcome depends on the player and a story's on the author. In an infinite game the two coincide: you play a game whose rules you keep rewriting, and you live a story whose ending you do not know. The condition Koe attaches (a meta perspective, present and not consumed by emotions) is the same zoom-out that, in section 10.1, let a bored partner in an antique shop invent a game. Uncertainty is tolerable, and even enjoyable, from that height; from inside the frame, without it, it is merely anxiety.

The chapter has assembled the parts of Part IV into a game: a vision as the win condition, a life's work as the path, a hierarchy of goals as the quests, values and anti-goals as the rules, skills and habits as the mechanics, quantifiable tasks as feedback, flow as the state the game produces, boredom and anxiety as the signals that you have left the channel, tactical stress as a forced return to its edge, and the infinite game as the form of a life whose challenge never stops rising. What it has not yet described is how the game is played on an ordinary day: what to do with the phone that competes for your attention, how to structure the hours of focused work, how to rest, and how to keep the order you have built. That is the subject of Part V.

### Exercises

1. **Audit your current game.** Choose one domain of your life (work, health, a relationship, learning). Using the four properties Koe says games, businesses and society share (a desirable hierarchy of goals, a structure that frames attention, a challenge that narrows it, a skill requirement), describe the game you are currently playing in that domain. Who designed each element: you, an institution, an app, someone else? Then decide whether, in Koe's terms, you are playing or being played in that domain, and justify your answer with specific evidence rather than impressions.

2. **Design a game with the 2025 procedure.** Take one goal you actually hold and build the three layers of "gamify your life": an end goal broken down into one-year, one-month and one-week goals; the rules of the game (what you refuse to sacrifice to reach it); and two or three quantifiable priority tasks that will serve as feedback loops. Then explain how the rules you chose make the game "creative" in Koe's sense, and identify which path to the goal they close off.

3. **Diagnose a distraction.** For three days, each time you notice yourself distracted from a task, record whether the distraction was preceded by boredom (a new desire, thoughts about better things to do) or by anxiety (thoughts about not being good enough, self-evaluation). At the end, use Koe's reverse-engineering heuristic to decide, for each case, whether the task needed more challenge, more skill, more clarity or a plan. What pattern emerges, and does it support or complicate his claim that boredom stems from self-centeredness and anxiety from self-consciousness?

4. **Reconcile the two "middles."** Koe says that "the most miserable place you can be is in the middle" and that flow happens "right in the middle," where skill matches challenge. Explain in your own words why these two statements do not contradict each other, using the map image (light, darkness and edge) to do it. Then describe one period of your own life that fits each sense of "the middle."

5. **Evaluate tactical stress as a decision.** Imagine someone who asks you whether they should sign a lease at twice what they can currently afford in order to "force" their business to grow. Using every condition Koe attaches to tactical stress (existing skill, track record, calculation, "a shred of self-belief," bounded duration, common sense) and the preparation principle from the same 2026 video, construct the questions you would ask before giving an opinion. Then describe one profile of person for whom the move would fit Koe's definition and one for whom it would be, by his own criteria, the "quick-fix mindset."

6. **Test the claim that games are "backwards."** Koe argues that video games provide fake risk and fake progress and that a person who feels the need to defend them is showing a sign that they are not good for them, while also granting that some games "aren't inherently entropic to your mind." Build the strongest case you can for a specific game or hobby that produces real rather than fake progress. Does your case survive his distinction between virtual and real risk? Does the "defense test" correctly classify your case, or does it misfire?

7. **Map your finite and infinite games.** List the main pursuits of your life and classify each as a finite game or an infinite game in the sense Koe uses (with Carse's distinction in the background). For each finite game, identify the infinite game it belongs to, if any. For one pursuit you consider infinite, describe how its rules have changed, or should change, at its next stage. Finally, examine Koe's claim that a job is usually a finite game whose challenge stops after about six months: under what conditions would that claim be false for a particular job?

8. **Trace your own drivers.** Koe adapts Kotler's five intrinsic drivers (curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy, mastery) by anchoring each in a structure: mastery in the life's work, autonomy in entrepreneurship, purpose in products, passion in connecting the dots, curiosity as fuel. Apply his "reread your favorite book" exercise to identify one genuinely intrinsic curiosity of yours, then describe what each of the other four drivers would look like if that curiosity were developed into a project. Which of the five is weakest in your current life, and is Koe's structural anchor for it the only way to strengthen it?
