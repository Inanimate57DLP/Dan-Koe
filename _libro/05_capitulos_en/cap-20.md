## Chapter 20 — The Generalist and the Second Renaissance

Chapter 19 ended with a claim about markets: the niche is the person, because the person's goals, problems and path already define a market made of everyone who shares them. That claim has a corollary about skills that Koe develops at length in a separate body of work. If the niche is a whole person rather than a segment, then the person who serves it cannot be a fragment either. A creator who documents a path through health, wealth, relationships and happiness needs to write, design, sell, film, think and build, and needs interests broad enough to see connections a single discipline would hide. "You are the niche" therefore implies "you are a generalist," and this chapter is Koe's argument for that second proposition.

The argument has a negative half and a positive half. The negative half, Section 20.1, is a critique of specialization: how it took over the economy, whom it serves, why Koe thinks it leads to replacement and even "extinction," and why the arrival of AI sharpens the danger. It continues the institutional critique of Chapter 2, where school and employment were shown to reproduce the programmed mind of Chapter 1; here the same institutions are examined for what they do to the *range* of a mind. The positive half has three steps. Section 20.2 defines Koe's **generalist** (a common word he uses in a sense of his own: someone who has an aim and learns whatever that aim requires), shows the mechanism by which a generalist accumulates capability (skill stacking), traces the hybrid formulas with which Koe tried to reconcile breadth and depth, and introduces Naval Ravikant's **specific knowledge** as the generalist's real asset. Section 20.3 turns from skills to interests: why Koe treats multiple interests as a "superpower" rather than a defect, and why he believes the present is a **second Renaissance** in which, as after the printing press, the economy favors people who combine domains. Section 20.4 closes with the aspiration that gives the whole argument its point: getting paid to play, the claim that the future of work is creative, chosen and enjoyable, and that "everyone is an entrepreneur."

One warning belongs at the start, because it affects how everything that follows should be read. Koe's position on generalists and specialists is not fixed. Between 2022 and 2026 it oscillates between a radical defense of the generalist ("become a generalist, not a specialist... the worst thing you can do") and a hybrid that keeps changing its name ("specialized generalism," "generalized specialists," "specialized generalist," "master or strategist"). The chapter presents the versions in order and shows where they converge. Likewise, his claim that we live in a second Renaissance moves from confident assertion to an explicitly speculative pattern "worth holding without accepting as truth." The reader should follow the arguments while keeping track of their status.

The chapter prepares Part VIII. Chapter 22 will ask what to write about; its answer depends on accepting that one's crossed interests are a legitimate source of content rather than a lack of focus. Chapter 27 returns to the brand as the vessel that holds many interests; Chapter 36 returns to the economic forecast about AI and work, which this chapter touches only where it bears on the generalist.

### 20.1 The Critique of Specialization

#### How specialization took over: the pin factory and productivity as God

Koe's critique begins with a historical account of why specialization became the default. He takes his central example from Adam Smith. "Specialization took over because in a pin factory, for example ... one worker doing every step could produce 20 pins a day. And then many workers, each doing one step rather than every step, could produce 48,000." The efficiency gain was enormous, and, in Koe's telling, the world reorganized itself around it: "We built an entire world around this model. We just siloed people into doing individual, repetitive, robotic tasks, and you can wonder why we're at threat of replacement from robots right now. It's because humans became assembly lines working 9-5 jobs."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

**Complementary context:** The example is from Book I of Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). Smith wrote that a single untrained worker "could scarce, perhaps, with his utmost industry, make one pin in a day, and certainly could not make twenty," whereas ten workers dividing the eighteen operations among them made upward of 48,000 pins a day. Koe's "20 pins" is the upper bound Smith gives for the lone worker. As noted in Chapter 2, Smith himself also warned, in Book V, that a life spent performing a few simple operations degrades the worker's mind.

The last sentence of the passage is the hinge of the whole critique. The same property that made specialized labor efficient (breaking work into repeatable steps, each performed by someone who needs to know only that step) is the property that makes it automatable. A task simple enough to be assigned to one person for life is simple enough to be assigned to a machine. Koe's later arguments about AI are extensions of this observation.

In a 2026 video he gives the same history a second angle. He names it **productivity as a priority**, and calls it "a losing game." "The concept of the 9-to-5 job became a thing just a few hundred years ago during industrialization. Before, people directed their own work. They were artisans, farmers, apprentices." With the 9-to-5, "productivity became the highest value. It became God. And then everyone became a specialist in a factory that knew one piece of the puzzle... because if they understood how to create the entire image themselves, they would be an entrepreneur, not an employee."

**Source:** If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md

The puzzle image carries a definition that matters for the rest of the chapter. The difference between an employee and an entrepreneur, in this passage, is not courage or capital but *scope of understanding*: the employee knows one piece; the entrepreneur knows how the pieces form the picture. Specialization is therefore not only an economic arrangement but a cognitive one. It keeps people from seeing the whole, and seeing the whole is what would let them direct their own work. The same idea returns in Section 20.2, where Koe defines every founder as a generalist because they "understand enough about marketing to direct it, enough about product to build it, and enough about people to lead them."

#### Whom specialization serves: institutions and the school

Why did the arrangement persist after it stopped being the only option? Koe's answer, in the same 2026 video on multiple interests, is about interests in the economic sense. "Governments don't serve the national interest, the interest of the people, they serve their own interest." He immediately blocks the obvious political reading: "The opposite of capitalism also isn't the answer." The same logic applies to corporations, which serve not their employees but their own interest, profit. Because those two forces form the base of Western economy and culture, "schools were also designed to serve that interest. Their sole purpose was to create factory workers who were obedient," and, he adds, they have not changed much. His conclusion is phrased as an ironic instruction: "If you want to have specialized knowledge so that you could never run an operation, especially your own operation, then be dependent on schools for your education and jobs for your wage. Be duped into believing that specialization is what makes a human valuable when it is clear that the system does not need you specifically to perform that task."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The final clause, "the system does not need you specifically," is the core of the argument against specialization as a source of value. The specialist is told that their specialty makes them valuable. But a specialty is defined precisely by being transferable from one interchangeable person to another; that is what makes it trainable and certifiable. What the system values is the function, not the person performing it.

A 2024 video develops the psychological side of the same diagnosis. The student was placed in front of "a government trained expert trained by government trained experts" who are not doing what the student wants in life, six hours a day, told what to learn and how to act, and nudged toward the status symbols of a job and a degree. Koe connects this to the model of the mind built in Parts II and III: the goals that compose a worldview (fancy degrees, high-paying jobs) "determine your mind's potential development and freedom." The student was trained to be a specialist (a doctor, a lawyer, an artist, a designer, an engineer) and this specialty became a **self-limiting identity** that shaped what the student could perceive and learn. What the student failed to develop was **agency**: the capacity to explore, make mistakes, discover and build by one's own desire, which Koe calls "the singular trait of a free individual." In the past, he says, free people were expected to act in their own interest, to explore, navigate and discover, and therefore to learn many things. Focusing on one skill, one degree or one job confines learning to a narrow box, "and your mind becomes that," even though the same mind can render reality and is, in his phrase, "the fastest supercomputer in the world."

**Source:** How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md

This passage links the critique of specialization to two earlier chapters. From Chapter 3 it takes the idea that identity filters perception: a person who *is* a lawyer notices what lawyers notice. From Chapter 6 it takes the goal as a lens: the goal "high-paying job" lets in only what serves that goal. Specialization, in this reading, is not a neutral division of labor but a goal installed early, before the person could choose one, which then narrows the field of perception for decades. Agency, which Chapter 35 treats as its own subject, appears here as the opposite capacity: the ability to choose what to learn.

Koe's own life supplies the counter-evidence he cites most often. In 2023 he wrote: "When you go to school your mind is narrowed on the select few skills that will make you employable or replaceable, and with every skill that I learned outside of school I can spot multiple opportunities that allow me to make an income outside of a job, and this effect compounds." He gives a figure that he presents as a personal capability rather than a statistic: "With the skills I've developed I can create 50 different products that I know will be profitable within the first six months of my effort, simply because I've invested my attention in myself."

**Source:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md

The pairing "employable or replaceable" is deliberate. The same skills that make one employable make one replaceable, because both properties depend on the skill being standard. Skills learned outside the curriculum work differently: each one widens the field of opportunities the person can perceive, and the widening compounds because each new skill combines with the previous ones. This compounding is the mechanism Section 20.2 will call skill stacking.

#### The Great Pirates: Buckminster Fuller's metaphor

The most elaborate version of Koe's critique is a story he borrows from Buckminster Fuller. He introduces Fuller as a "certified genius philosopher and innovator," the designer of the geodesic dome ("incredibly stable while also being lightweight"), and retells, in full, the metaphor of the **Great Pirates** from Fuller's *Operating Manual for Spaceship Earth*. Because Koe reconstructs it as a sequence and draws a lesson from the sequence, it is worth following step by step.

1. The great pirates understood many things: geography, celestial navigation, biology, the men on their ships, the ships themselves, history and science. All of this was necessary for trade and dominion.
2. The men they brought to work on the ships "were stupid on purpose." The pirates did not want them to understand their strategies and contrive to overtake them; they were expected to follow orders without question.
3. The pirates had global knowledge because they could sail to different lands. They were generalists.
4. The rulers of the land knew only their own land and whatever the pirates told them, "and the Pirates can lie." They were specialists.
5. This gave the pirates great power and made the land rulers dependent on them for trade and knowledge.
6. The pirates influenced the land rulers to give prestigious roles to the brightest minds of each kingdom (royal historian, treasurer) so that those minds would spend their days studying a single niche, "rendering themselves only useful to the land rulers and the pirates," exactly like the men on the pirates' ships.
7. This prevented the bright minds from overtaking the pirates, because they were specialists.
8. Everyone was content: the bright minds with their prestige, the land rulers with their smart servants, and the pirates with control over both across many lands.

The lesson Koe draws is one of his most quoted sentences: "schools were created to enslave the brightest Minds by promising The Prestige of specialization that way they remained narrow-minded didn't choose their own purpose and therefore didn't learn multiple interests and skills that would allow them to overthrow those who assigned their purpose."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

**Complementary context:** R. Buckminster Fuller (1895–1983) was an American architect, inventor and systems theorist, best known for popularizing the geodesic dome. *Operating Manual for Spaceship Earth* (1969) contains his chapter on the "Great Pirates," which he uses to explain how specialization arose as a means of control and to argue for comprehensive, "anticipatory" thinking. Koe's retelling follows Fuller's outline closely; the application to modern schooling is Koe's.

The term **prestige of specialization** comes from this retelling. Its mechanism is subtle: the bright minds are not coerced; they are rewarded. Prestige is the payment that makes narrowness feel like success. This is why Koe can say, a few minutes later, that the arrangement left "everyone content." The control works because nobody experiences it as control.

Koe softens the claim at once. "While this is just a metaphor it illustrates a very important point it illustrates why specialization is still highly encouraged whether it's a conspiracy theory or not." He then offers a non-conspiratorial explanation: "That's just the byproduct of the schools people don't like competition people don't want others to do better than them or outshine them they don't want you to outshine the master." This explains, he says, why high-paying jobs are the first thing on young people's minds, and why "their first goal results in a narrow dumb mind unless they go to school but they also study outside of it they self-educate and pursue curiosity." His summary is the phrase **programmed to be replaced**: "Most people are just programmed to be replaced most people just did what they were told throughout their childhood and that's okay," but someone has to give them the awareness that there is a better way so they can see the opportunity and change. "Let's learn how to reverse the damage that's already been done."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

In December 2025 he returns to the sentence, this time quoting himself. Introducing the third idea of a video, "why generalists win in the AI age," he reads from his book *Purpose and Profit* (free on his Substack): "Schools were created to enslave the brightest minds by promising the prestige of specialization so they remain narrow-minded and didn't overthrow the true rulers." He admits that it is "taken quite a bit out of context" but calls it "largely true." He adds an observation about his critics: those who attack his generalist content rarely offer a coherent argument, do not "steelman" his position, and "inadvertently prove me right."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

The same video, however, also contains the systemic version of the claim, in Koe's own words: "If you understand systems, you understand that the system takes the shape of that which most benefits the end goal, which in society's case is keeping you sick and dumb, whether it's intentional or not. It doesn't have to be a conspiracy theory for the system to naturally take shape of the subconscious desires of the humans at the top of the pyramid." Chapter 2 discusses this passage. Read together, the two statements show that December 2025 is not a hardening of the conspiratorial reading. In one video Koe calls the strong sentence "largely true" and also explains that no conspiracy is needed for the result it describes.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

The status of this claim needs to be stated precisely, because the corpus contains two versions of it in parallel and never resolves them. One is the strong rhetorical version ("schools were created to enslave"); the other is the systemic version, in which no one planned the outcome and narrowness is a "property of the system" that emerges from the interests and fears of the people in it. Chapter 2 (Section 2.2) presented the systemic version through the history of Horace Mann and the Prussian model. Here the reader sees the strong version and the author's own qualification of it in the same breath: "just a metaphor," "whether it's a conspiracy theory or not," "taken out of context," and, in the December 2025 video, "it doesn't have to be a conspiracy theory." The most defensible reading is the one Koe declares when he stops to qualify: the Great Pirates is an explanatory metaphor for a structural effect, not a historical claim about a plot. The rhetorical version functions as a hook; the systemic version functions as the explanation. Across the corpus the tension remains unresolved, and nothing in these videos settles it.

#### "Only slaves were expected to do one thing"

A second historical image accompanies the pirates. In a 2024 video Koe cites a reference he "really loves," without identifying its origin: "only slaves were expected to do one thing their entire lives." Free or sovereign men, by contrast, were meant to do many things throughout their lives, in alignment with a broader, overarching goal of living a good life and impacting others with the value they create. "That's what you attract people to," he adds, linking the image to the brand: a person who lives a many-sided life in pursuit of one overarching aim is more attractive to an audience than a person defined by one function.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

In December 2025 the image becomes a premise from which he draws a conclusion: "If slaves were expected to do one thing throughout the entirety of their lives so that their minds were closed to learning more, which are specialists, then you as a free individual are meant to do many things throughout your life. You're meant to be a generalist." The practical consequence is a triple instruction: revolt against the path set at birth, pursue an **interest-based education**, and use your capabilities wisely.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

The slave/free framing echoes Devon Eriksen's distinction, discussed in Chapter 1, between an education that produces useful workers and one that produces free individuals; the material records the 2025 formulation as adapted from that framing. Two cautions apply. First, the "only slaves" line is a remembered reference of unspecified origin and should be read as a rhetorical contrast, not as a historical generalization the author has checked. Second, the 2025 sentence ("you're meant to be a generalist") is stronger than some of Koe's other statements on the subject. In March 2024 he said that "the generalists don't win, the specialists don't win, it's the generalized specialists." Section 20.2 returns to this oscillation.

#### Overspecialization leads to extinction: the long-beaked bird

Koe's most memorable image for the danger of specialization comes from biology, and it is his own illustrative story rather than a description of a named species. "In the animal kingdom the main cause of extinction is over specialization." Imagine a bird with a long beak that lets it reach fish in shallow waters. The bird stays in that environment. Only the birds with the longest beaks find enough food, survive and mate, so each generation's beaks grow longer, until "the beaks become so heavy that they can't fly and the children's beaks become so small that there isn't any food left for them." Restricted to one environment by the very trait that made them successful in it, they die out. The same, Koe says, holds for people who have overspecialized for a long time: "their extinction has finally come."

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md; The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The parable's structure is worth noticing because it explains why specialization is not simply a lack of options. Specialization is *rewarded* at every step. Each increment of beak length is an advantage in the present environment; the cost appears only when the environment changes or the trait passes a threshold. That is the shape of a specialist career as Koe describes it: each year of narrower expertise brings a raise, a title, more prestige, until the task itself is automated.

In the 2024 video on the future of work, Koe draws the contrast with humans: "the difference between an animal and a human is that animals specialize or Niche down to survive the problem is that overspecialization leads to Extinction in certain species." "Humans build tools to make up for their shortcomings": in the past we learned to throw spears so we could eat; today we text our social circle so we are not seen as outcasts. "All technological advancement around you was a hedge against the extinction of the human race in other words humans are generalists that solve problems that prevent our Extinction we don't Niche down resulting in extinction." The business application is posed as a question: "are you building something valuable to prevent your replacement or are you content with a single skill as a cog in someone else's machine awaiting Extinction?"

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

Note the verb "niche down" in the animal sentence. Koe is deliberately using the vocabulary of Chapter 19 (where "niche down" was the core advice of the business matrix) in its ecological sense. The pun is an argument: the business advice to occupy one narrow niche is, by analogy, the evolutionary strategy that ends in extinction when the environment shifts. Section 20.2 develops the positive side of the same image, humans as tool-building generalists.

#### The fragility of the specialist: the one-trick pony and the injured bodybuilder

Two smaller examples from a 2024 video, "The Age Of The Generalist," translate the extinction parable into everyday terms. The first comes from video games, which Koe uses throughout his work (Chapter 10). In League of Legends there is the **one-trick pony**, the player who picks the same champion over and over because they are very good with it. Koe uses the term, a common phrase, for the fragile specialist, and lists three disadvantages. Only one person can be the best in the world at that thing. If an opponent counters you, it is very hard to win. And if someone else picks your character, you cannot play it at all: you are at a disadvantage, you get angry and you quit. The general point he draws is that "specialists compete with other specialists," now more than ever.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The second example comes from fitness. If you are only a bodybuilder (no running, no cardio, no hikes) and you get injured, you either get bored and hit a terrible low point, or you must start completely from scratch in running, CrossFit, cycling, hiking or yoga, because you were not well-rounded in fitness to begin with. The lesson: being a specialist sets you up for many disadvantages, "especially when work consisting of a repetitive string of tasks can be replaced."

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The three images (the bird, the gamer, the bodybuilder) describe three different failure modes of specialization. The bird fails when the *environment* changes. The one-trick pony fails through *competition*: the specialist's field is crowded with other specialists, and a single counter or a single rival choosing the same tool removes the advantage. The bodybuilder fails through *disruption to the self*: an injury removes the one capability on which everything depended. In each case the specialist's strength and fragility are the same thing.

#### "Specialization is for insects"

The earliest version of Koe's position is also the most radical. In the October 2022 video on the one-person business model (republished in a 2024 compilation), he argues from his own observation of who earns the most. The highest-paid people he has seen are not those with looks and glamour, who bank on sponsorships and, he says, don't make much. "The people who make the most are generalists": not one-dimensional but **multi-dimensional**, "the generalists that hire the Specialists." Everyone tells you to become a specialist, and Koe calls this "the worst thing you can do," because specialization means doing a specific task with a specific skill, which can be trained into you and is therefore "primed for automation." Such tasks are being automated "left and right"; social media managers, for example, will be outsourced to software. What you need instead is to be a generalist who can "move artfully and gracefully within a specific domain of interest." His rule of thumb: "if you work like a robot then you are a robot and you will be outsourced to robots and your job will be replaced."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

Even this early, radical version contains a qualifier that is easy to miss: the generalist moves "within a specific domain of interest." Breadth of skill is not the same as lack of direction. Later in the same video Koe adds that "you need to truly become an expert and that does not mean being a specialist," which refines rather than contradicts the argument. Expertise, for him, is depth of understanding in a domain; specialization is confinement to one task within it.

He supports the argument with what he calls a "classic quote" from the science-fiction writer Robert A. Heinlein, which he reads in full in three different videos (2022, 2024 and 2025): "A human being should be able to change a diaper, plan an invasion, butcher a hog, conn a ship, design a building, write a sonnet, balance accounts, build a wall, set a bone, comfort the dying, take orders, give orders, cooperate, act alone, solve equations, analyze a new problem, pitch manure, program a computer, cook a tasty meal, fight efficiently, die gallantly. Specialization is for insects." In the 2025 version he turns the list into a self-test: count how many you can do. He says he hit quite a few but realized he needs to learn some, admitting, "I personally can't change [a] diaper."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md; How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md

**Complementary context:** The passage comes from Heinlein's novel *Time Enough for Love* (1973), where it appears among the aphorisms of the character Lazarus Long. It is widely quoted as a manifesto for versatility.

The quotation is third-party material; Koe uses it as backing, not as his own formulation. Its list is worth reading as Koe reads it: it mixes manual tasks, intellectual tasks, social roles (taking and giving orders, cooperating, acting alone) and existential ones (comforting the dying, dying gallantly). The point is not that every person should master all of them but that a human life is meant to range across many kinds of competence, and that confinement to one is a reduction of the human to something else, an insect in a hive, a cog in a machine, a robot.

#### Specialists are tools; generalists are attached to the vision

By 2025 the argument has acquired its sharpest form, shaped by the arrival of capable AI. "Society wants you to be a specialist because useful workers serve Society, but every single person you admire is not a specialist. Specialists are tools. Tools get replaced. AI is doing the replacing. Every free and successful person is a generalist, a polymath, even if they put on the guise of a specialist." Koe proposes a test for anyone who seems very good at one thing: ask what allows them to be good at it, and how you even know about them. The answer is that "they had other traits qualities and skills that allowed them to make that one thing work." To actualize a grand vision, a person must learn and do many things; "One path, one static known defined path that many people have gone down before does not cut it."

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md

The test is a useful analytic tool in its own right. A famous chef is known through media, business and personality as well as cooking; a famous scientist through writing, fundraising and teaching as well as research. What looks like specialization from outside is, on Koe's reading, a visible specialty resting on an invisible generalism. This is what he means by "the guise of a specialist."

In December 2025 he draws the distinction in terms of attachment. "Specialists are attached to the skill. Skills always get replaced and evolve as technology advances." His historical example is Photoshop. Computer-generated graphics disrupted the art industry; Photoshop is now integral to it, but the manual, hand-drawn artists of the time "were probably just screaming at the top of their lungs that this is going to ruin everything," which is exactly what is happening now with AI. Generalists do not care whether a skill is replaced or evolves, because they are focused on the goal or vision and on doing whatever is necessary to reach it, including changing the goal. They do not identify with a skill or feel passionate about it ("like being passionate about Photoshop"); "they're passionate about their direction in life and creating that." In an aside, Koe says he is "very pro AI" but sees things going "very very wrong" in content creation: people asking AI for a script and reading it to the camera, obviously AI.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

The distinction between attachment to skill and attachment to vision is the conceptual center of Section 20.1. It explains why the same technological change is experienced as catastrophe by one person and as opportunity by another. For the person whose identity is "illustrator," a tool that illustrates is a threat to the self. For the person whose identity is a direction ("I am building a world of stories"), the same tool is one more means. It also connects to the identity model of Chapter 3: a skill held as identity is defended like a part of the body, which is why technological change provokes rage rather than adaptation.

#### Deep generalist: the Schmachtenberger quotation

Koe frames several videos with a quotation that, in some of its appearances, he attributes to Daniel Schmachtenberger, the thinker whose metacrisis framework was introduced in Chapter 2 (Section 2.9): "Traditional education and hyperspecialization is a way to make people subservient to the dominant paradigm or system. Study the generalized principles of nature and be a deep generalist." He uses it to open the main section of the July 2024 video on the future of work, announcing "how becoming a deep generalist will future proof yourself"; as the opening thesis of the February 2025 video on multiple interests; and to open "Rule 6, become a deep generalist" in his September 2025 video of twelve rules for changing one's life.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md; How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md; 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md

The attribution is not uniform. In some appearances, including the opening of the February 2025 video, Koe states the line as his own thesis without naming any author, and part of the corpus records it as his. Schmachtenberger is the source the quotation points to, but the reader should not assume that Koe names him every time he uses it.

**Deep generalist** is therefore a third-party term that Koe adopts as the identity that makes a person "future proof." The adjective is what distinguishes it from the stereotype of the dabbler. A deep generalist is not someone who knows a little about many things but someone who studies "the generalized principles of nature," the patterns that hold across domains. This anticipates the convergence that Chapter 15 described in the Musashi passage and that Section 20.2 will trace: depth sufficient to find principles, and breadth sufficient to apply them everywhere.

There is a discrepancy in the corpus that should be reported rather than smoothed over. In one video (June 2024) the same quotation is given as ending "study the generalized principles of nature and *become a deep specialist*," and Koe uses it there to support the first step of a process: become a "niche of one" (Chapter 19) or create your "personal monopoly," with references to his own videos "The Age of the Generalist," "The most profitable niche is you" and "The anti-niche." The source offers no explanation for the inversion; it may be a misreading, a slip or a transcription variant. The majority of appearances use "deep generalist." The "deep specialist" variant happens to fit Koe's hybrid formula of "specialized generalism," but nothing in the corpus indicates that he chose it deliberately.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

#### Artificial stupidity and "don't be a tool"

The arrival of AI could seem to turn Koe's argument upside down: if machines can now draw, write and code, perhaps no human skill is safe, generalist or not. Koe's answer leans on a December 2024 conversation with the novelist Devon Eriksen, whose ideas about education and identity appeared in Chapters 1 and 3. The ideas in this subsection are Eriksen's; Koe's role is to adopt and restate them.

Eriksen starts from his own novel, *Theft of Fire*. Its protagonist, Marcus, describes the robot guard spiders of the character Miranda as "simple reactive predictable **artificial stupidity**." Eriksen explains the term: the spiders are essentially robots with a neural net trained on one set of tasks ("I'm going to protect this person"). In numerous scenes they do something counterproductive to Miranda's real goals, "because they only know how to do this one thing they can't think about the broader context." A human can outwit them despite lacking their speed, strength or processing power, because he understands the broader context he is operating in and acts appropriately. Marcus "can decide what tools to use based on his understanding of the total context"; the robots have only the tools provided by their training data and "are unable to say okay my whole Paradigm no longer applies so I need to learn to do something totally different." Current AI, Eriksen says, "is this kind of artificial stupidity."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

From the fiction he draws an argument about the present. "The reason people are afraid of artificial intelligence right now is they don't really understand what artificial intelligence right now is." We do not have something that can replace a human, because "the defining capability of a human is the ability to look at your environment and say I need to learn that and then go learn that and do it." We can build AI that plays chess better than us or draws better than many of us (Eriksen says he himself cannot draw: "I haven't devoted the time to learn"), "but these are task specific machines and they're no more a threat to our Central Humanity than a machine that can lift more than we can." During the Industrial Revolution, many people probably thought humans were obsolete because machines could lift and move more, "and it's not true." "What human beings do is navigate their environment and learn things and decide what to learn."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The argument connects directly to the critique of specialization. AI, as Eriksen describes it, is the ultimate specialist: superb at one task, blind to context, unable to change paradigms. Its threat is therefore aimed at whoever competes on the same terms, that is, at the human specialist. The capability AI lacks, deciding what to learn and learning it, is precisely the generalist's defining trait in Koe's definition (Section 20.2).

Eriksen sets a limit on his own argument, which should be kept to avoid overstating it. It is at least theoretically possible to make a software entity that does what humans do: if you scanned a human brain and simulated the physics of every atom of it "in a vast supercomputer the size of a solar system," it would function like a human brain. "There's nothing magical about meat." So he is not claiming that a sentient artificial person is impossible; he is saying that current AI is "a tool that does certain types of thinking like processing," very useful, and that we may "integrate with it where the line between us and the tool starts to blur as we incorporate these capabilities into ourselves technologically and that's fine."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The warning that follows is the most important part of the exchange for this chapter. Eriksen imagines an artist threatened by people prompting DALL-E through ChatGPT: "how will I get work... authors are making book covers out of AI art and they're stealing bread from my mouth and we need to boycott them and shame them." His diagnosis: "The problem you have there is that you have defined yourself as a tool. **Don't be a tool**." "When you say I am an artist that is a self-limiting belief because you have defined yourself in terms of this one capability and now you are threatened by the existence of a tool that has that same capability because you have reduced yourself to the level of a tool."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

"Don't be a tool" compresses the whole of Section 20.1 into a pun. The specialist is not only *like* a tool (trainable, replaceable, valued for one function); by identifying with the function, the specialist *becomes* one, and so enters into direct competition with every machine that performs the function. The generalist, defined by a direction rather than a capability, uses tools. Koe restates the point in his own words immediately afterward, in the image of the cheetah, the polar bear and the parka, which opens Section 20.2.

Koe returns to Eriksen in August 2026, quoting him (transcribed as "Devon Erickson") with a different emphasis: "Don't get breathless about how smart AI is. It's dumber than a sack of wet hammers. Leverage instead its ability to do rapid input and output for simple tasks on large data sets." Koe uses the line against two opposite exaggerations. He criticizes voices on Instagram claiming that using AI once makes everything you have done useless ("the whole Hank Green situation"), and announces a video on "everything is a religion," arguing that even nuanced thinkers who pick "an absolute side in the **AI religion** either anti or pro AI" have stopped thinking for themselves: "this isn't all bad or all good as all things in life." He refers viewers to his video on how to "think five-dimensionally," the progression of thinking that Chapter 17 reconstructs.

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

"AI religion," Koe's coinage, names the absolutist stance in either direction. Its relevance here is that the generalist's relation to AI is supposed to be instrumental and unattached: neither the specialist's panic at being replaced nor the enthusiast's surrender of judgment, but the use of a fast, stupid tool for what it does well.

#### Hyper-specialists and the fading of labor as leverage

Koe completes the critique with an account of what computers are and what that implies for work. "Humans are supposed to be generalists": we build tools so that we can be generalists (if we cannot hunt, we build a tool to hunt). Computers and technology, by contrast, are **hyper-specialists**: they solve specific problems and perform repeatable tasks. Work, he says, centralized and is now decentralizing, "because that's a pattern in nature," a cycle, a wave, throughout evolution. Hence the fear of job loss, the layoffs at certain companies, and the many people unfulfilled in their jobs anyway, a dissatisfaction that seeps into other areas of life and kills motivation. Young people do not know which path will generate wealth when the physical routes of oil and real estate are exhausted and require vast resources.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

The same month, in the July 2024 video on the future of work, Koe gives the term a second, complementary sense. Asked whether writers and coders will be replaced if AI can write a landing page or build an app, he answers: "yes, some of them, if they aren't in control of the vision." Then: "Computers are hyper specialists, humans are deep generalists, a great combo but not too effective on their own." In the first sense, the hyper-specialist machine is a threat that displaces the human specialist. In the second, it is a partner: the machine supplies speed on specific tasks, and the human supplies the vision that orchestrates them. The two senses fit together, since the threat falls on the human who competes with the machine at its own game, while the partnership is open to the human who directs it. Chapter 36 (Section 36.4) develops this second sense as part of Koe's argument that AI needs direction.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The appeal to a "pattern in nature" is an interpretive framework rather than an empirical claim; elsewhere Koe illustrates the same **cycle of centralization and decentralization** with the ocean that evaporates and condenses. Its role here is to make the present shift look less like an accident and more like a phase.

The economic consequence concerns **labor as leverage**, a concept Koe adapts from Naval Ravikant. Labor as leverage (employing others to work for you) was useful in the past and is less so today. Labor jobs are being phased out by AI and automation because "labor almost by definition is hyper specialization": everyone doing labor focuses on one thing, does not learn a variety of skills or manage people, is not a generalist, and therefore computers or robots will do those things first. Naval adds a second reason: everyone now wants to be the "top monkey," wants equity, does not want to do much work and wants massive salaries, which makes managing labor "a volatile status game." Koe qualifies the claim: labor can still work, and people still have employees. He himself has contractors rather than employees (at the time, one contractor, his editor, in his one-person business), and his software company Cortex also uses contractors "for now": "a bunch of one-person businesses coming together," which is, he says, what the creator economy is.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

**Complementary context:** In Naval Ravikant's "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" (2018), leverage comes in three forms: labor (people working for you), capital (money) and "permissionless" leverage (code and media), which can be multiplied without anyone's permission. Naval describes labor as the oldest and most contested form of leverage.

The two passages complete the logic of the section. Specialization made humans efficient by making them machine-like; machines are now better at being machine-like; so the most specialized forms of human work, and the leverage built on them, are the first to go. What remains is the human capacity that the specialist was trained out of: deciding what to learn, seeing the whole, building tools. Section 20.2 turns to that capacity.

### 20.2 The Generalist and Specific Knowledge

#### Humans as natural generalists: the tool builder

The positive argument begins where the extinction parable ended. Animals niche down; humans build tools. Koe states the contrast many times between 2024 and 2026, with slightly different animals and slightly different tools, and the variations are worth following because each adds a layer.

In the earliest version (June 2024) the point is survival. What makes humans powerful as generalists is that they build tools so they do not go extinct. Animals niche down into specific environments to survive, unless they over-specialize. "Computers and animals Niche down humans are generalist because they build the tools that allow them not to go extinct." An animal that cannot hunt with its physical abilities goes extinct; a human builds something to hunt with. Today's tools are technology and software, things like a camera, "whose depth for survival you may not see." The warning attached: if you do not leverage technology and modern skill acquisition, you become part of systems built by prior generations, like school, and depend on someone else for your income.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

In the conversation with Devon Eriksen (December 2024) Koe restates Eriksen's "don't be a tool" in these terms: humans are **natural generalists**, and "they shouldn't be the tool because they build the tools." The cheetah is perfect for its niche, but put it in another environment and it does not survive; the same with the polar bear. "The cool thing about being human is that we are in all of the environments because we created the parka or the coat." AI, in this frame, is one more tool that helps us survive better in some contexts.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The July 2024 video on the future of work turns the image into a principle: "humans are natural generalists this is what we do we Thrive at an overlapping intersection of interests and skills that allow us to adapt to most niches or most opportunities by building tools acquiring new knowledge and learning new skills." He then promises his viewers "a stack of skills from big picture to technical details that will allow you to think for yourself make better decisions and secure your future."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The phrase "overlapping intersection of interests and skills" is the bridge between this section and the next. The tool-building capacity is not exercised in the abstract; it is exercised from a particular combination of what a person knows and cares about. That combination is the generalist's niche, in the only sense Koe accepts.

In a December 2024 video he adds the layer that matters most: mental tools. What you want cannot come from someone else ("not even Koe"), because "humans are natural generalists." Humans build tools to adapt to different niches and environments; lions in the Sahara or polar bears in Alaska would not survive in a different niche. But beyond physical tools, humans invented mental tools (language, culture, religion, stories) with which to build, adapt and acquire the skills and knowledge for any niche. This, he says, is the ability that makes us unique, "and it is what most people have lost." His name for it: "**the greatest skill you can develop is the ability to figure it out**."

**Source:** How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md

The September 2025 version, which opens his "Rule 6" right after the Schmachtenberger quotation, gathers all the elements. Humans do not thrive in a specific niche like a polar bear in Alaska; if the polar bear went to the savannah where the lion thrives it would die, and vice versa. Humans go to Alaska because they built tools (a coat, heating) and to the savannah with a car with air conditioning or a water bottle. "You're a tool builder. Humans build tools to adapt and thrive in any environment." This extends to mental tools: "language, culture, concepts, religion, and stories," with which we adapt, build and acquire knowledge and skills to thrive anywhere. "This is the ability that makes us unique. This is the ability that most people have lost."

**Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md

In December 2025 he adds the planning step: "Humans are tool builders. We thrive in any niche because we can adapt to it. If you were to put a lion in Alaska and a polar bear in the savannah, they would die. If you were to put a human in either, they would build shelter, clothing, and hunt for something to eat because they can create a plan and execute on it."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

And in February 2025 he gives it a personal turn: "You aren't an animal you aren't a niche specialist that only thrives in the savannah like a lion or Alaska like a polar bear"; if you swapped them they would die. "You can put on a jacket because we created jackets"; and in the same way you can live the life you want because you can create the tools that allow it.

**Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md

Read together, the versions build an argument in five steps. (1) Animals are adapted to one niche and die outside it. (2) Humans are not adapted to any niche in particular; they adapt niches to themselves by building tools. (3) The tools are physical (spear, coat, car, camera) and mental (language, concepts, stories, plans). (4) The capacity to build tools, which Koe calls "the ability to figure it out," is the defining human trait and the one schooling and employment let atrophy. (5) Therefore the generalist is not an exotic personality type but the human default, and specialization is a deformation of it. The metaphor is Koe's own throughout, though the December 2024 version is explicitly a restatement of Devon Eriksen's point. It should be read as an explanatory image, not as a biological thesis: the long-beaked bird and the lion in Alaska illustrate the logic of adaptation, and Koe does not cite evolutionary research for them.

#### Applying the lion to content: "all the niching down you need"

The lion in Alaska also carries the argument back to Chapter 19's question of what to post. In January 2026: "If a lion were put in Alaska, it would not build shelter and clothing. It would die. A lion belongs in its own niche. We don't belong in one niche. Everyone's telling you to niche down when everyone lives in a separate freaking niche across the world. It doesn't make sense."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

In July 2026 he draws the practical conclusion for writing. People are told to niche down and fear talking about multiple things despite having multiple interests, as almost everyone does: "I'm not only interested in business, neither is any other businessman." "Stop limiting yourself to what you can talk about. If you find it important, that's unique because you interpreted it and decided to post it. That is all the niching down you need." The justification is the tool builder: "you are not an animal that dies when they get put into a different niche or environment, like a lion that gets put in Alaska. You are a human who learns, builds, and adapts so that they can thrive in any niche."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

This is a change of position, and both versions should be visible. In 2023 Koe taught a deliberate method for choosing topics: pick two or three interests, filter them by the goal of your ideal future, avoid the hyper-specific, and organize them into a **topic tree** (you → three interests → topics → subtopics) that you could master in six to twelve months. By 2026 the pillars have become optional: "if you want to avoid all of that, just focus on the ideas that are important to you and share those," and the criterion loosens from a deliberate intersection to personal judgment of importance ("if you find it important, that's unique"). The change is not linear, though: in June 2026 he again teaches the paper map with two or three pillars, subtopics and pain points. The two versions plausibly serve different stages. The map is scaffolding for someone who needs structure to generate ideas; "share what you find important" suits someone who already has a collection of ideas, a goal, a mission and a formed style. The reason Koe gives for loosening the criterion is the lion: humans adapt to any niche, so the topic boundary is less important than the act of interpretation. Chapter 22 develops the topic tree and its alternatives in detail.

#### Koe's definition of the generalist

With the tool builder in place, Koe can define his central term. "Generalist" is a common word, and in common usage it suggests someone who knows a little about everything. Koe uses it in a sense of his own, and the definition is stable across 2024 and 2026.

The 2024 version, from "The Age Of The Generalist": a generalist "learns all relevant knowledge and skill out of interest for achieving a goal in their life." A specialist "learns a singular skill or interest to achieve a goal," which makes the specialist one-dimensional and puts them at a disadvantage from the start. If the goal is a better career and you learn one skill, the path is harder: you are very good at one thing but not adaptable. The generalist attacks and solves the problems that arise along the way from multiple angles, because of the experience gained across skills, even without diving extremely deep into any one of them.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The 2026 version, from the video on multiple interests, adds the concept that had been missing. "The missing piece for me that I'm guessing is a missing piece for you was a **vessel**. A vessel, a way to channel your multiple interests into one thing." That, he says, is not what people consider a specialist; "it's more of a generalist." Then the definition: "a generalist is someone who has an aim for their life. They have a desired outcome. They have a future they want to achieve and they do all of the things required to achieve that." He calls this "such a huge insight that you may not understand yet." What he personally needed was something that generated an income to survive "but it umbrellaed all of my interests into it."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The two definitions agree on the essential point, and it inverts the ordinary meaning of the word. The generalist is not defined by breadth. The generalist is defined by an **aim**, and breadth follows from the aim, because any real goal requires many kinds of competence. The specialist, by contrast, is defined by a skill, and the goal is whatever that skill can reach. In this sense, the generalist is the person of Part IV: someone with a vision, a hierarchy of goals and a project, who learns whatever the project demands, exactly as Chapter 14's "build to learn" prescribed. The word **vessel** (which Koe uses across the corpus for the business, the brand or the format that channels a purpose) names the single container into which the many interests are poured. A generalist with a vessel looks, from outside, like someone doing one thing; from inside, it is many things organized by one aim. This is also why, as the evolution record notes, Koe's "generalist" and his "specialized generalist" end up designating almost the same thing: specialization in the vision, generality in the skills.

#### Big-picture understanding and surface area

The 2024 video adds what the generalist gains cognitively. The generalist prioritizes big-picture understanding over the technical details of one thing. Once you see its importance, you start noticing patterns across domains such as psychology, marketing, business and sales. Understanding their principles helps you navigate life more easily: in a conversation or a business deal, you have "much greater surface area for understanding and spotting and identifying opportunities" than a specialist in any one of those areas.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

**Surface area** is Koe's metaphor for exposure to opportunity. A specialist touches reality at one point; a generalist touches it at many, and each point of contact is a place where an opportunity can register. The idea connects to Chapter 6's account of the goal as a lens: what one perceives depends on what one is looking for. A person with knowledge in several domains has several lenses at once, so the same conversation contains more for them to see.

#### The generalist in practice: founders, Shakespeare, designers

Koe's evidence for the definition is a set of figures he reads as generalists in disguise. The first is the founder. "Every CEO or creative or founder ... they're almost always a generalist, even if it doesn't seem like it on the surface, because they understand enough about marketing to direct it, enough about product to build it, and enough about people to lead them." They must direct the ship and adapt, so they go broad rather than too deep on one thing other than their company. The benefit is cognitive and competitive at once: "creating your own **unique model of the world**," because "ideas that cross into different domains, they complement each other." That way of viewing the world lets you see ideas and opportunities others would not, and it is hard to replicate: even if someone copies what you built, "they still don't have all of the intersections of ideas that led to the specific decision-making that you made, so you are much more likely to succeed."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The argument about replication is important because it answers a natural objection. If a generalist's products can be copied, what protects them? Koe's answer is that the product is only the visible output of a model of the world built from many intersecting domains; the competitor copies the output but not the model, and so cannot make the next decision as well. The same idea appears in Naval's specific knowledge, discussed at the end of this section.

The second figure is Shakespeare. Critics of Koe's generalist content, he says, quote "the classic from Shakespeare," "a jack of all trades, master of none," unaware that "it is a misquote and it ends with 'but oftentimes better than a master of one.'" Some think of Shakespeare as a specialist playwright, "but that was simply a vessel." To write as he did he needed deep understanding of human nature, character development, language, classical literature, stagecraft, religion, philosophy, military tactics, music, navigation, the natural world, social structures, the body and medicine. "He was a synthesizer who used his diverse interests as his edge." The same is true, Koe says, of a Fortune 500 CEO, of Charles Darwin, of Steve Jobs, of any visionary: a specific vision, then the learning of whatever steps it requires. His conclusion: "Do not confuse a specific vessel or niche as being a specialist."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md

**Complementary context:** The attribution of the full phrase is uncertain. "Jack of all trades" was applied to Shakespeare early: Robert Greene's pamphlet of 1592 mocked him as "an absolute Johannes factotum." The longer form ending "but oftentimes better than a master of one" circulates widely, but its origin is not well documented. The material records the attribution to Shakespeare as Koe states it.

The Shakespeare passage contains the most useful distinction of the section: **vessel versus specialty**. A playwright writes plays, so from outside his work looks specialized. But the play is the container; what fills it is drawn from a dozen fields. The word **synthesizer**, which Chapter 18 (Section 18.4) developed as Koe's name for the creative worker who connects ideas across domains, is here applied to the paradigm case of literary genius.

The third figure is the great designer, and here Koe argues from education. The greats were not great because they attended a high-status school or studied the same curriculum better than everyone else; studying the same things as everyone, you may have different insights, but you still studied the same things. "What makes a great designer is not how well they understand Photoshop." The great designers, artists and writers were great because of experience in other domains. The **free individual**, in this passage, is the opposite of the government-trained specialist, the person viewed as "a machine for labor a useful worker or a slave."

**Source:** How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md

Photoshop appears twice in Koe's argument, and the two appearances complement each other. In Section 20.1 the hand-drawn artists who were attached to a skill were disrupted by Photoshop; here the designers who mastered Photoshop are not great because of it. In both cases the tool is the wrong thing to identify with. What makes the work valuable is what the person brings to the tool from elsewhere.

#### The one-person business requires a generalist

The figures above are exceptional. Koe's practical point is that the same structure applies to anyone running a one-person business, the model the rest of this book builds. "As a one-person business you are the marketing department, you are the sales department, you're the product lead, you're the project manager, you're the content writer, you're the social media strategist. You have to learn so many things, and you have to become a generalist even though people tell you to niche down; you still need the general skills to build a business."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

This is the bridge between Chapter 19 and this chapter. The niche advice of the business matrix, applied to skills, would have the solo founder become excellent at one department; but a one-person business *is* all departments. The advice contradicts the model it is meant to serve.

Koe adds a definition of the entrepreneur that follows from the same logic. Entrepreneurs are generalists and problem solvers with dominion over everything they run "like a well-oiled machine," and "the entrepreneurs entire goal that they're working towards is to reduce the hours they work." Some people earning five to ten million dollars with a reasonable lifestyle business do not even need to work; many choose to because they want to. Knowing such people exist, he says, means it is possible and probably replicable. Working hard for twelve hours on a replaceable task under a boss, without controlling any of the things that would make you the boss and the money, is "kind of foolish" and traps you in the range of fifty thousand to perhaps three hundred thousand dollars for most of your life. Entrepreneurship is "the only way" to tap a broader sense of leverage, leverage not only in your work but in your life, and it is the first step to unlock it.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

The income figures are Koe's illustrative ranges, not survey data. The substantive claim is structural: the employee specializes in a replaceable task, the entrepreneur generalizes across the whole operation, and the generalist's aim is not more hours of work but fewer, which links this argument to the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12.

Finally, Koe gives the generalist a contemporary face. "The rise of the generalist" is, in his account, the rise of the **value creator** and of the creator in general: "generalists nowadays are creators who have multiple interests who display themselves in public," attracting attention to their work so they can make a living doing what they enjoy.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

This definition, which opens the 2024 video, is narrower than the previous ones; it describes the form generalism takes in the creator economy rather than generalism as such. It explains why the chapter belongs where it does in this book: the creator is the generalist made visible, and Parts VIII and IX are about how that visibility is produced.

#### Skill stacking: how a generalist accumulates capability

How does a generalist actually come to have many skills? Koe's answer from 2022 to 2024 is **skill stacking**, a term he uses for stacking a dynamic set of skills, especially skills applicable in the digital landscape, so that together they can produce a creative solution to almost any problem. (Chapter 15 traces how Koe later shifted the unit of learning from the skill to the technique; skill stacking is the career-scale version of the same idea.)

The argument starts from his own history. In the January 2023 video "The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack," he says that hopping from skill to skill did not prevent him from making an income at the start; he believes it helped him make more, because "there's a cap on how much you can make per skill or with a singular skill." With a single skill like web design it is "not impossible but difficult to form a compelling marketing campaign around that," unlike copywriting and funnels, or a more holistic skill stack, which you can manipulate to deliver and promise direct results: "I can get you a hundred new leads," "I can make you ten thousand extra dollars a month." With web design alone, "it's kind of hard to do that."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The mechanism is about *results*. A client does not want a website; the client wants leads or revenue. A single skill produces an intermediate artifact whose connection to the result depends on other skills the specialist lacks. A stack can close the gap between artifact and result, and a promise of results is what can be marketed and priced high.

From this he derives a ranking that he states as a principle: "Skill stacking is greater than being a specialist, which is greater than doing nothing at all because you're afraid of saturation, automation or any other excuse that leads to distraction." We are, he says, in a "**digital Renaissance**," and skill stacking lets you build a creative solution to almost any burning problem on the market. You reach a point where you can spot a market problem and say: "I have the skills to create a system for this in order to solve it, and I have the marketing skills to patch this up and put it in front of the right people." At that point making money is much easier, and "you become in control of your income."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The ranking is easy to misread as hostility to specialists. Its middle term says otherwise: being a specialist is better than doing nothing. The real target is the third option, the person who does nothing because every path seems threatened (saturated, automatable). The ranking is aimed at paralysis as much as at specialization.

Koe then gives a metaphor for the difference, which he explicitly calls "metaphorical and abstract." He claims that he can get better results than most specialist freelancers or agencies. Specialists "know how A will impact B will impact C and that's it": they do one thing to get a very specific niche result. Through skill stacking and a holistic understanding of the entire digital domain, he understands how A impacts B impacts C, "and how that goes back and impacts A all the way throughout the alphabet in a circular motion," how all those things interconnect, "with my skill stack in the middle allowing me to pick and choose whatever I want to do."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The **alphabet in a circle** is a picture of systems thinking applied to a business. The specialist's knowledge is linear and local; the stacker's is circular and global, including the feedback loops by which downstream effects return to change the starting point. A copywriter who understands email, funnels and product design knows how a change in the headline will affect the sales call, and how the sales call will change the next headline.

A 2024 metaphor adds risk to the picture. Skill stacking is like diversifying a portfolio. A specialist invests everything in one stock, one skill: they can see a lot of success but can end in catastrophic failure. Investing in various stocks means that even if one does badly, the others keep you positive. Diversifying your bets with a range of interests or skills, all aimed at one goal of "being in the positive," makes you more likely to get there without a failure so big "that you end up drowning."

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The portfolio metaphor and the extinction parable make the same argument from two directions. The parable shows what happens to the concentrated position when the environment changes; the portfolio shows that a diversified position survives the change. Note that the portfolio still has *one* goal ("being in the positive"); diversification is of means, not of aims, which is consistent with Koe's definition of the generalist.

How are skills stacked, concretely? Koe's answer is that they come one at a time, because each new skill reveals the next. In a November 2022 video he states the principle in terms of awareness: "you can't improve what you aren't aware of, you can't take action on what you aren't aware of." If you are getting into business and see people making a lot of money, you will not have their skill set. You start with one skill, like web design; then your awareness opens to stacking it with copywriting, which makes it much more effective; then email marketing (a lead magnet or opt-in, email sequences). Skills stack over time, and you cannot reach the expert level of a complete money-making skill set until you learn the first skill.

**Source:** The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md

A 2023 video gives the same principle as a game image. Think of skill acquisition as leveling up in "The Game of Life." Video games have **skill trees**: you choose skills, practice them, gain experience, and new skills unlock. The key rule: "you are not able to access other skills until you practice the ones available to you." If you lack awareness of profitable opportunities, it is because you lack the skill to recognize, choose and act on them. The same holds in social situations: without social skills, you practice at your level and work upward; once you are past the "**beginner's hell hump**," you perceive situations for what they are, because you have experienced them many times and know how to inject yourself confidently. How good you are at something determines how well you perceive situations in it.

**Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md

The skill tree here is a different use of the image than the one in Chapter 10 (Section 10.2), where Koe used it to show that the domains of life gate one another (finances limiting spirituality, for instance). Here it describes a sequence within a field: each practiced skill unlocks perception of the next. The two uses agree on the underlying claim: perception is limited by capability, and capability grows by practice, not by planning. This is why Koe insists that a generalist cannot be designed in advance by drawing up a list of skills; the list only becomes visible as one climbs.

Two further principles explain why skill stacking does not waste effort even when projects fail. The first comes from Koe's account of his seven failed online businesses. Though he eventually stopped doing editing and photography, those years taught him design, image composition and color theory, which "transferred into everything" he does now. Many people miss that "if you try and fail and start over you're still picking up valuable skills because everything interconnects." His failed content marketing agency, his SEO agency and the others "all complemented each other because they go hand in hand."

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md

The second is muscle memory. In 2024 Koe recounts learning animation for his videos. It was "really freaking hard at first": a lot of time, overwhelm, not knowing what he was doing. He kept going and learned quickly by focusing on one project; eventually animations will be second nature, and "what used to take 8 hours will take one." Once a skill is learned, stopping for a day, a week, a month or a year makes returning harder, but muscle memory exists: after a long period away from Photoshop and digital art, it took him a little while to get back, but much less than the first time. Hence, he concludes, skill stacking and consistently learning new skills are crucial: you can adapt easily even after long disuse. He links this to control over work: if you work a job with assigned tasks and are not learning automating, outsourcing and delegating skills, you cannot control what you work on, and therefore how much you work (the theme of his 4-Hour Workday videos, Chapter 12).

**Source:** Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md

The animation story also illustrates the entropy of Chapter 5: a new skill begins in high disorder (overwhelm, confusion) and becomes ordered through focused practice on a single project. The generalist pays this entry cost many times over a career, but each payment is partially refundable, because old skills return faster than they were first learned and because each skill speeds the learning of related ones.

#### Shiny object syndrome: good and bad

Skill stacking runs into an obvious objection: isn't this just **shiny object syndrome**, the habit of jumping from one new interest to the next without finishing anything? Koe's answer in January 2023 is a distinction. Shiny object syndrome is not inherently bad; "the bad shiny object syndrome is the thing that takes you off the path of mastery." Learning a new skill that complements your others and pushes you to new levels in business is not bad: you can learn web design and copywriting and more until you have a big-picture understanding of online business. Your skills compound, and no one can take them away. Investing in self-education is "usually never a waste; it's only a waste if you aren't building something along the way," because you will not remember anything and will have nothing to show either in knowledge or in real-world tangibility. When a shiny object or a distraction registers and you narrow your focus onto it, "you close your mind to the depth of your craft"; but distractions are temporary bumps.

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The criterion is precise: the question is not whether a new interest is new but whether it *complements what you are building*. Building is the test, which connects this distinction to the "build to learn" principle of Chapter 14. A new skill acquired in the service of a project is stacking; a new skill acquired as an escape from a project is distraction.

Koe's attitude toward the label is not consistent across the corpus, and the reader should see the range. In some videos he defends shiny object syndrome (in 2023 he attributes his "unique success" to it; in 2024 he asks people not to call experimentation by that name). In others he treats it as a symptom: in August 2024 "the endless shiny object syndrome you have with finding a niche is a blatant sign that business hasn't clicked for you yet," and in 2026 he says some of his early businesses failed "thanks to shiny object syndrome," though they left him with skills. In February 2025 (Section 20.3) he criticizes the use of the label by parents, teachers and friends to disqualify multiple interests. The positions can be reconciled through a distinction the record supports: jumping from niche to niche in search of the most profitable one is different from exploring interests that stack; the early businesses failed *as businesses* (they did not acquire customers) but succeeded *as skill acquisition*. The evolution record nonetheless classifies the tension as unresolved, because Koe never states the reconciliation himself.

#### Specialized generalism and its successive names

If the generalist is defined by an aim, and the aim requires depth somewhere, then the opposition between generalist and specialist cannot be absolute. Koe saw this early, and from 2023 onward he repeatedly formulated a hybrid. The hybrid changes its name four times, and its relation to his radical statements shifts with it, so the versions are best presented in sequence.

**Specialized generalism (February 2023).** In "Become A Digital Renaissance Man," Koe says that since the pandemic "specialists are out but so are generalists." He immediately qualifies: not entirely; you will be a specialist at times (especially at the beginning) and a generalist at others, "dancing between the two," and that is what the normal world runs on until people transition into the new economy. "But we aren't here to be normal." "We live in a time of specialized generalism": the highest-paid individuals use the creative ability of their minds to focus on a specific profitable problem and deliver above-average results. The more skills and personal interests you acquire, the more awareness you have at their intersections, so you can pull from experience to laser in on a problem related to any of them or their combinations.

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md

He illustrates with a worked example. If he studied fitness and business and solved a problem in the middle (a fitness system for busy people, or for businessmen) that would be more specific and profitable than "here's a fitness program." Awareness of both domains lets him craft a more compelling marketing campaign, especially with experience in both. He contrasts this with the typical advice to "target a profitable niche," which he calls good advice in itself: if you target fitness and offer a business service without experience in either (if you have never improved your own fitness, never self-experimented with diets and training philosophies) "you're going in blind."

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md

**Specialized generalism, refined (December 2023 and January 2024).** In "The Future Of Work Is Play," the formula acquires its clearest structure: "This is not a time for specialists nor is it a time for generalists we live in a time of specialized generalism." You must "laser in on a specific vision for the future and acquire the general skill and knowledge that will actualize each ascending goal that creates that vision." Success is not as simple as learning one skill; self-education and skill acquisition for life are necessary "for creating a life of sovereignty."

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md

The structure is the key to the whole concept: **the specialization is in the vision; the generality is in the skills.** One focuses narrowly on where one is going, and broadly on how to get there. The "ascending goals" are the goal hierarchy of Chapter 8: each rung requires different skills, and the generalist acquires them as the climb demands.

The book version, *The Art of Focus* (Chapter 11, "The Skill Stack"), gives the most quoted formulation: "The state of work is changing. Specialists are out but so are generalists. We live in a time of specialized generalism. You must pursue your curiosity, become obsessed with one crevice of reality, stack skills to solve burning problems within that domain and let the base of your skill tree spread its roots into other connected domains." Becoming a specialist, the book argues, is a great way to get replaced in this "digital renaissance"; pigeonholing into one skill is not the path to independent success. As a specialist you can work for someone for a pretty paycheck, but "they are the reason you can get paid": you cannot create independent income because you have not learned the skills that let you get paid. Big businesses have marketing, sales, operations and other departments to acquire customers and deliver products; as one person, there are no limits (aside from pleasurable distractions) to what you can learn. Individuals can learn design, marketing, writing and video to run a multi-million-dollar business selling the solution to a problem they have solved in their own lives.

**Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md

The **crevice of reality** is a recurring term in Koe's work for the corner of reality that most intrigues a person and that they feel compelled to explore, often one unexplored in that particular combination. In this formulation it plays the role the "vision" played in the previous one: the narrow part. The skill tree whose roots spread into connected domains is the broad part. The image of roots is apt: the trunk is one, the root system wide.

**Generalized specialists (March 2024).** Two months later the hybrid changes its grammatical form. "We are in the middle of a second Renaissance, meaning that the generalists don't win, the specialists don't win, it's the **generalized specialists**." The line is brief and the term appears only once. It refines the January 2023 ranking ("skill stacking is greater than being a specialist"): now the winner is explicitly a hybrid.

**Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md

In the same month, in a video about feeling lost, Koe gives the hybrid a temporal order. Experimenting inward and outward creates "**span**," the breadth that experimentation produces before depth (he announces a "second Renaissance" video on generalists versus specialists). "It's not about becoming a generalist or a specialist it's about becoming both it's about becoming a generalist and then diving deep and becoming a specialist in certain areas but having that general knowledge so you can be a more powerful specialist and you can easily become and transfer your specialty ... into different domains because you have the general knowledge you have the general principles that make almost everything work."

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

"Span, then depth" adds what the earlier versions lacked: a sequence. Breadth comes first, from experimentation; depth comes second, chosen in light of the breadth; and the breadth then makes the depth transferable, because general principles "make almost everything work."

**The specialized generalist (August 2025).** The next version answers an objection. Someone points out that Mark Zuckerberg paid a hundred million dollars to hire top AI researchers, apparently a triumph of the specialist. Koe replies: "You're not a top AI researcher." And then: "The peak of human ability lies in being a specialized generalist. Not so specialized that you get replaced in an instant or taken advantage of because you are identified with a credential, but not so generalized that you are effective at effectively nothing."

**Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md

This is the most balanced statement in the series, and the only one that names the failure mode of the generalist as clearly as that of the specialist. It introduces two risks for the over-specialized (instant replacement, and exploitation through identification with a credential) and one for the over-generalized (ineffectiveness). The answer to the Zuckerberg objection is also significant: Koe concedes that extreme specialists at the top of a field can be extremely well paid, but treats them as a statistical exception irrelevant to his audience, the same logic he applies to the NBA in Section 20.3.

**The master or strategist (August 2026).** The latest version draws on Miyamoto Musashi, whom Chapter 15 introduced. "The modern idea of a generalist is usually someone who knows a little about a lot… a jack of all trades, but what Musashi is describing here is a bit different. There's the generalist, there's the specialist, and then there's the master or the strategist." The master "receives the benefits of both… the ability to be good at many things so they don't narrow their mind to one area of study limiting their potential and the ability to excel significantly at one thing so they can get outsized results, preferably in a meaningful domain." Koe adds that this "maps quite well to the stages of ego development in spiral dynamics. The strategist stage is quite achievable."

**Source:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md

**Complementary context:** "Strategist" is also the name of a late stage in Susanne Cook-Greuter's and Bill Torbert's models of ego development, which Chapter 38 discusses together with Spiral Dynamics. Koe's sentence links Musashi's figure to that developmental stage but does not elaborate the mapping, so the precise correspondence cannot be reconstructed from this passage.

In the same video, as Chapter 15 reported, Koe describes Musashi's strategist as someone who goes "so deep into one discipline that they discover something universal at the bottom of it." The 2026 version thus restores depth to a place of honor without abandoning breadth: depth in one discipline is the route to the universal principles that make breadth effective.

The following table summarizes the sequence.

| Date | Name | Formula | What it adds |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022-10 | (generalist vs specialist) | "Become a generalist, not a specialist"; specialization is "the worst thing you can do" | The radical baseline: specialists get automated, generalists hire specialists |
| 2023-02 | specialized generalism | "Specialists are out but so are generalists"; focus on a specific profitable problem from the intersections of your skills | Hybrid; "dancing between the two" in the normal world |
| 2023-12 / 2024-01 | specialized generalism | Laser in on a vision or "crevice of reality," acquire general skills for each ascending goal | Specialization in the vision, generality in the skills |
| 2024-03 | generalized specialists; span | "Generalists don't win, specialists don't win"; "becoming both," first span then depth | Sequence: breadth first, then transferable depth |
| 2025-08 | specialized generalist | "The peak of human ability"; neither replaceable/credential-bound nor "effective at effectively nothing" | Names the generalist's failure mode |
| 2026-08 | master / strategist (Musashi) | Breadth of the generalist plus outsized results of the specialist; depth reveals the universal | Depth as the path to universal principles; link to ego development |

The hybrid does not replace the radical statements; they coexist. In May 2024 Koe titled a video "The Age Of The Generalist"; in December 2025 he said "you're meant to be a generalist." The evolution record classifies the whole series as a renaming with oscillating radicalness. One reading makes the apparent contradiction mostly verbal. Because Koe defines the generalist as someone who pursues an aim and learns everything it requires, his "generalist" already contains a specialization (the aim) and a generality (the skills). When he speaks radically, he is attacking the *specialist* in the sense of Section 20.1, the person defined by a trainable task. When he speaks of the hybrid, he is acknowledging that pursuing an aim requires going deep somewhere. The two kinds of statement are aimed at different errors: the radical one at narrowness imposed by institutions, the hybrid one at diffuseness that never produces results. What does change over time is the weight given to depth, which grows steadily toward the 2026 Musashi version.

#### Specific knowledge

The last element of the generalist's equipment is a concept Koe takes from Naval Ravikant. In May 2022, introducing a "new career path," he says that the start is curiosity, and reads two statements from Naval's thread "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)." First, **specific knowledge** is knowledge you cannot be trained for: if society can train you, it can train someone else and replace you. Second, specific knowledge is found by pursuing your genuine curiosity and passion rather than whatever is hot right now. Koe's interpretation: figuring things out for yourself, as opposed to doing what other people tell you, is how you gain specific knowledge, and that knowledge is "immensely valuable."

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

**Complementary context:** Naval Ravikant is an entrepreneur and investor (co-founder of AngelList). His 2018 Twitter thread "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" introduced, among other ideas, specific knowledge, leverage through code and media, and accountability as the components of wealth creation.

The first statement links directly to the critique of specialization. Trainable knowledge is exactly what Section 20.1 called "primed for automation": what can be taught to one person can be taught to another, and now to a machine. Specific knowledge is the opposite: what cannot be packaged into a curriculum because it arises from one person's particular path of curiosity.

Koe illustrates with what he calls "a subtle flex." In the previous week he had gained 120,000 followers on Instagram, not by buying followers or through "weird hacks," but because he had been "in the game long enough and dissected the social media platform for long enough" that it "finally clicked." This, he says, is specific knowledge: you will not find it in a course or online unless he tells you, and even if he told you, it is entangled with his branding, his minimalist style and other things, so "if I told you what it was you're not going to get the same results." His conclusion: figure things out on your own, stay in the game long enough, and make discoveries others have not made.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

The example contains a precise claim about why specific knowledge cannot be transferred. It is not secret; it is *entangled*. The insight works only in combination with the rest of the person's assets (style, brand, audience, history), so extracting it destroys it. This is the same argument as the "unique model of the world" of the founder: the competitor can copy the visible move but not the intersections that made it right.

He adds an image for how specific knowledge is found: "if you dive into an iceberg of whatever you want to master that is how you get this extremely valuable specific knowledge." Surface knowledge is visible to everyone; what is valuable lies below, discovered only by going deep and staying long enough.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

The **iceberg** introduces depth into a chapter that has mostly praised breadth, and it is consistent with the hybrid formulas above: the generalist's breadth is valuable because it is combined with depth in the domains that matter to the aim. Specific knowledge lives at the bottom of icebergs and at the intersections between them.

A month later, Koe re-reads Naval's tweet on specific knowledge alongside Naval's line about "seven billion people... seven billion businesses," and adapts it: "stop thinking of it as a business but rather you amplifying your voice and your value," getting online, improving yourself and helping others, "that's what life is all about."

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md

The adaptation is characteristic of how Koe uses Naval: the economic thesis (everyone can be a company) is reframed as a personal one (everyone can amplify their own voice). Section 20.4 returns to the "seven billion" line in its stronger form.

The concept survives into 2026 with two further readings. In January 2026 Koe quotes Naval (transcribed "Ravocant") on four properties of specific knowledge: it cannot be trained for; it comes from pursuing genuine curiosity; building it feels like play to you; and it is often at the edge of knowledge. His application moves to the AI context: someone who spent ten years obsessing over productivity and then builds AI software will build something far more nuanced and detailed than someone without that time in the domain, or someone who just tells ChatGPT or Claude to build a productivity app. If that person has also built an audience on social media with that specific knowledge, they are at even more of an advantage.

**Source:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md

The 2026 example answers a question the AI age poses to the whole chapter. If anyone can now build software by prompting, what distinguishes one builder from another? The answer is the decade of specific knowledge behind the prompt. AI lowers the cost of execution; it does not supply the understanding of what to execute. The third property, "feels like play," anticipates Section 20.4: specific knowledge is built through activity that the person would pursue anyway, which is why it cannot be competed away by someone doing the same thing as work.

In August 2026, as "Step 3 — Seek specific knowledge" in a process for learning, Koe quotes a longer passage from Naval ("specific knowledge is kind of Naval's thing"): "I ultimately think that everyone should be figuring out what it is that they uniquely do best, that lines with who they are fundamentally, and that gives them authenticity, that brings them specific knowledge, that gives them a competitive advantage, that makes them irreplaceable. And they should just lean into that. And sometimes you don't know what that is until you do it." Koe stresses the last sentence. Beginners get stuck in "what do I do?"; but "you learn what to do by doing something," a paradox that does not register for them.

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

The final sentence of the quotation resolves a difficulty that could otherwise paralyze the reader of this chapter. If one's value lies in a unique combination of interests and specific knowledge, how does one find it before starting? One does not. Specific knowledge is discovered by doing, in the same way that skills in the skill tree become visible only after the previous ones are practiced. The generalist does not begin with a map of their uniqueness; they begin with an aim and a first project, and the uniqueness emerges.

The lexicon records a second use of "specific knowledge" in Koe's own work (knowledge sought to solve a concrete problem in a project, which cuts out filler), developed in Chapter 14. The two senses share the term but are distinct; this section concerns Naval's sense.

### 20.3 Multiple Interests and the Second Renaissance

#### The predicament of the person with many interests

Section 20.2 approached the generalist from the side of skills. This section approaches the same figure from the side of interests, and from the experience of a particular kind of person whom Koe addresses directly in a series of videos from 2025 and 2026: the person with many interests who has been told that this is a problem.

He begins, in February 2025, with the experience itself. For such a person, "focusing on one thing is just the bane of your existence": you want to explore "the full range of The Human Experience" and acquire as much knowledge as you can. Society has convinced you this will not lead anywhere. Parents, teachers and friends shoot you down, say "shiny object syndrome is bad," and throw "subtle Jabs at your dreams," claiming they want what is best for you, "but in reality they want what's best for them." What they want is the **safe route**: doctor, lawyer, "something respectable," a high-paying degree, drilling your mind "into this little box that they can understand." Against this, Koe makes his claim: "having multiple interests is a superpower going into the future of work"; he believes "it's the singular Edge that will make most people stand out." And the reversal: "What used to be the safe route is no longer safe at all." He does not pretend the condition is easy. Multiple interests are chaotic, and you must learn to differentiate them and package them into something valuable.

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md

Two features of this passage deserve attention. First, the criticism of shiny object syndrome here is a criticism of the *label* used as a social weapon, not of the distinction between good and bad shiny object syndrome made in Section 20.2. What Koe rejects is the use of the phrase to disqualify curiosity as such. Second, the claim that others "want what's best for them" applies the diagnosis of Chapter 1 and Chapter 2: the safe route is a goal assigned by others, legible to them, and its safety is the safety of the people who recommend it as much as of the person who takes it.

In January 2026 he restates the predicament with a historical frame. If you have multiple interests and do not know how to manage them or what options exist, "this is the greatest time to be alive." "But therein lies the problem. Society has made you feel like having multiple interests is a weakness." The story everyone knows is: go to school, get a job, retire, "but there is so much wrong with that series of events." "We don't live [in the] industrial age anymore, but we are still dealing with its consequences."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

He continues: "Specializing in only one skill is certain death." "We all know by now how dangerous mechanical living is for the psyche and soul. It's not meaningful. We've made productivity our God and that God has betrayed us." People feel that we are going through "a second renaissance," and excited multi-interest people ask how to do what they see others doing online. "Your curiosity and love for learning is not a weakness. It is a massive advantage. You just need to learn what to do with it."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

"Productivity our God" ties the predicament to the history of Section 20.1. The industrial order made productivity the highest value; the specialist was its servant; and the person with many interests, who does not fit a single productive function, was experienced as defective. The "betrayal" is that the god has now turned on its servants: the most productive, most specialized functions are the ones being automated. "Certain death" should be read as rhetoric continuous with the extinction parable, not as a forecast with a measured probability.

#### What multiple interests signal

In a June 2026 video Koe gives his most analytic account of why multiple interests are an advantage. Having multiple interests "is potentially a superpower" (note the hedge, "potentially") because it signals three things. First, "it signals that you have high agency": a person who pursues many interests on their own is already doing the self-directed exploration that school failed to cultivate. Second, "it signals that you have unique point of view": a combination of interests is a combination of lenses, and no one else has the same combination. Third, "it signals that you have everything required to turn your interests into a modern source of income," which is every multi-interest person's dream of pursuing their interests full time.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

He adds a claim that reframes the multi-interest person's daily activity: "you already do 80% of the work required to make an income, that is, you already research, you just don't think of it as that." The learner reads, watches, experiments and takes notes; the creator does the same and then publishes. What is missing is not the work but the last step, the conversion of research into something distributed. The video aims to show options for side or full-time income, "preferably by discovering and pursuing your life's work," because "it's a lot better if it's meaningful money." The "80%" is an illustrative proportion, not a measurement.

The three signals correspond to the three threads of the book. Agency is the capacity Chapter 2 found missing in the schooled mind and Chapter 35 will treat on its own. Point of view is the raw material of content that Chapters 18 and 22 develop. The modern source of income is the one-person business of Part X. Multiple interests, in other words, are presented as evidence that the reader already has the beginnings of all three.

#### Managing multiple interests: make them your work

The most common practical complaint of the multi-interest person is that there are too many interests to manage. Koe's answer, in January 2026, inverts the usual advice to prune them first. "Most people pursue multiple interests as an escape from their work, but when your interests become your work or your life's work, most of them start to filter out." Once you are on the path, "when work turns into play for you, then you tend to drop the interests that just served as an escape and you really start to focus in and clarify what you want out of life." "You may have too many interests right now, but once you actually start to get on the right path ... they will become so much more clear. That is the best way to manage multiple interests."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The principle rests on a distinction between two functions an interest can serve. Some interests are *escapes*: they are pursued because the work is unbearable, and they matter only by contrast with it. Others are *directions*: they would be pursued even if the work were good. The person stuck in mechanical work cannot tell the two apart, because every interest is experienced as relief. When the interests become the work, the escapist ones lose their function and fade on their own, and what remains is the real set. The method is therefore not "choose, then act" but "act, and the choice clarifies itself," which is the same structure as Naval's "sometimes you don't know what that is until you do it" at the end of Section 20.2.

#### A course in seven ideas

Koe structures the January 2026 video, which he calls "kind of like a full course on how to turn those into your life's work," as seven ideas. Its outline shows how the themes of this chapter connect to the chapters that follow: (1) the three ingredients of individual success and the death of the expert; (2) you are living through the second renaissance; (3) how to turn multiple interests into a lucrative way of life; (4) how to turn yourself into a business; (5) brand is an environment; (6) content is novel perspectives; (7) systems are the new product. He links it to his next video, "The Future of Work," a synthesis of his research on AI and AGI.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

Ideas 2 and 3 are the subject of this section. Idea 4 extends Chapter 19's "you are the niche"; idea 5 belongs to Chapter 27 on the brand; idea 6 to Chapter 18 (novel perspectives) and Chapter 22; idea 7 to the product chapters of Part XI. The transcript material assigned to this chapter does not develop idea 1 ("the death of the expert") in detail, so its content cannot be reconstructed here.

An earlier pointer shows how long the theme has occupied him. In the closing promotion of a January 2023 video, Koe mentions a Modern Mastery article on "how to niche down multiple interests," aimed at polymaths with multidisciplinary interests who want to package them into a brand that grows, impacts people and can be monetized. In the same promotion he describes his course *The 2 Hour Writer* as teaching "the skill of persuasive writing that transfers over into other communication-based media AKA all of social media." Only the premise is given; the method of the article is not detailed in the source.

**Source:** The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md

#### The second Renaissance: the claim

The second idea of the 2026 course is the historical thesis that gives this chapter its title: "you are living through the second renaissance and you need to take advantage of it." Koe opens it with a quotation attributed to Leonardo da Vinci: "Study the science of art. Study the art of science. Develop your senses, especially learn how to see. Realize that everything connects to everything else."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

**Complementary context:** The lines circulate widely as Leonardo's "principles for the development of a complete mind." They are a modern condensation of themes in his notebooks rather than a sentence that appears in them; the material records the quotation as "attributed."

The last line of the quotation, "everything connects to everything else," is the philosophical ground of the second Renaissance thesis, and it echoes the argument of Chapter 19 that reality is not niched down. If everything connects, then knowledge divided into specialties is knowledge with its connections cut, and the person who restores the connections sees what specialists cannot.

The thesis itself appears in many forms between 2023 and 2026. The earliest clear statement in the 2024 material is an exhortation: "do it all become a writer designer marketer filmmaker bodybuilder Runner or whatever your curiosity desires the internet gave the individual the power to learn almost anything fast and get results from diverse interests you are living through the second Renaissance." Later in the same video he calls the present a "digital Renaissance environment" and says that "creators are the New Renaissance men."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The mechanism named here is specific: the internet lowered the cost and time of learning almost anything, and therefore made it possible for one person to get *results* in several domains within one life. The second Renaissance is defined by what it enables an individual to do, not by any cultural flowering in general.

In May 2024, opening "The Age Of The Generalist," Koe lists the signals that, in his view, show we are in a second Renaissance that few people see. The creator economy is growing exponentially. People do not trust traditional jobs or schooling to secure their future. People turn to creators to learn the skills to thrive in a fast-changing digital environment. AI, automation and software have made business more accessible to people who want to do what they enjoy for a living. People work less, earn more and leverage technology to remove themselves from "the old Society." And people crave human connection online "from people with personalities not glorified search engines who talk about one specialized interest." All of these connect, he says, to the fact that technology has changed the way we work, the opportunities for work and "what justifies valuable work." The internet has decentralized wealth generation to individuals who value self-education, personal responsibility and making something of themselves by doing good work.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

These are observations and interpretations, not data; Koe offers no figures for them in this passage. The last signal is the one most tied to the generalist: an account that talks about one specialized interest is, in his phrase, a "glorified search engine," and search engines (now AI) do that better. What people seek from a human is personality, which is a combination of interests, views and experiences, that is, a generalist.

#### The conditions: programming, consciousness and intention

Koe does not claim that the second Renaissance happens to everyone. In February 2025 he attaches a condition: "Generalism alone is only one piece of this equation. We are going through a second Renaissance. We have the power of Einstein and da Vinci in our pockets. The individual, not the institution, has more power than ever to thrive with multiple interests." And yet people still do nothing with it. It does not matter how many interests you have if you sit around. "It's not because you're lazy or you lack creativity"; it is because "our minds have been programmed for 20 plus years and continue to be by a society that benefits from our stupidity." This is not something that simply goes away: there is "residue that still needs to be cleaned up for kind of your entire lifetime," Koe included.

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md

The passage connects the second Renaissance to the diagnosis of Part I. The tools are available, but the mind that would use them was shaped by the institutions of Chapter 2, and the shaping leaves **residue**. Koe's admission that he too must keep cleaning it is consistent with the developmental view of Chapter 4: identity change is a process, not an event.

In November 2024, in a video on cheap dopamine, he states the condition in terms of consciousness. "There's more to the story, dopamine isn't all bad." "We're living through a second Renaissance," he says, noting that the historical Renaissance came after the Dark Ages. "The internet and social media for conscious and intentional individuals seeking truth and growth is the key to doing work you love and taking back control of your life."

**Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md

This opinion bridges Chapter 11 and this chapter. The same tools that make the unconscious "mentally obese" (Chapter 11's diagnosis of the dopamine environment) are levers for the conscious and intentional. The second Renaissance and the digital dark ages are not two different eras but two uses of the same environment.

That duality appears in Koe's book as well. In *The Art of Focus* (Chapter 9, "The Shift") he writes: "We are living through what seems to be a second Golden Age." The evidence he gives is institutional: formal education is being challenged by independent educators, journalists, experts and artists who sought to remove the cap from their salary and physical reach; the banking system by programmers of decentralized currency; the government by remote work and the desire for multi-nation citizenship to pay less tax. Formal education "has its place," but people are losing trust in its ability to secure their future: it is common to invest four to twelve years and tens of thousands of dollars, after twelve years of public schooling, and be left with "close to nothing but a piece of paper." For those who want fulfilling work and an open schedule, a job will not give it.

**Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md

The phrase "what seems to be" is a hedge worth keeping. Elsewhere in the same corpus Koe calls the present the "digital dark ages," the era of distraction and mindless consumption. The two framings are reconciled by the image the material itself suggests: dark ages with a renaissance on the horizon, available to those who see it.

#### The printing press parallel

The most developed historical argument for the thesis comes in January 2026. Before the printing press, knowledge was scarce. Books were copied by hand; a single text could take a scribe months; libraries were very rare and literacy rarer still; to learn outside your trade you needed access to a monastery. "Then Gutenberg changed everything." Within fifty years, twenty million books flooded Europe; ideas that had taken generations to spread moved in months; literacy exploded; the cost of knowledge collapsed. "For the first time in history, a person could realistically pursue multiple domains of mastery in a single lifetime and the renaissance was the result."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

**Complementary context:** Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type press began operating in Mainz around 1450. Historians commonly estimate that some twenty million copies of books (incunabula) had been printed in Europe by 1500, which is the figure Koe uses.

He then names the people the press produced. Da Vinci "painted, sculpted, engineered, studied anatomy, designed war machines, and mapped the human body. Michelangelo was a painter, sculptor, architect, and poet." "The printing press was a catalyst for a new type of person to emerge. A person who could learn anything, combine everything, and create something that no specialist ever could. And this is happening again." "In today's world, unique minds are finally able to operate the way they were meant to": cross disciplines, synthesize connections, follow curiosity.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The argument has a clear structure: (1) a technology collapses the cost of knowledge; (2) the collapse makes multiple domains of mastery feasible within one life; (3) a new type of person, the polymath, emerges to exploit the feasibility; (4) the internet and AI are a second collapse of the same kind; (5) therefore a new generation of polymaths can emerge now. Step 4 is the contestable one, and Koe addresses the objection directly. When people say AI is not the same and does much more than the press, he answers: "the pattern is still there." But he adds a statement of epistemic status that changes how the whole thesis should be read: nobody has figured out the future; "it's just prediction, it's speculation." It is worth holding these ideas "without accepting them as truth," and using them because they can be useful now: "It's better than doing nothing."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

This is a change from earlier statements, and it should be marked as such. In 2024 Koe asserted the second Renaissance flatly ("you are living through the second Renaissance"); by late 2024 he had made it conditional on consciousness and intention; by 2026 he presents it as a pattern held without accepting it as truth. The evolution record classifies the shift as a change of emphasis: from assertion to useful hypothesis, and from an era for everyone to an era conditional on awareness. The reason he gives is that no one knows what the future will be, and that social programming keeps most people inactive. The thesis survives the change; its status does not.

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md

The candor matters. Koe presents the term as a conceptual tool for pointing at something not yet visible, not as a description of established fact. **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md

**Source:** Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md

The "mental plane" is part of Koe's broader vocabulary (Chapter 3 described identity as living almost entirely on the mental plane). What does the person who thrives in this era look like? In August 2023 Koe shows a graphic of the **modern or digital Renaissance man**, and its components can be reconstructed from his description: the person is self-educated, pursues curiosity, builds digital leverage, embarks on physical training, is health conscious, socializes, dedicates time to purposeful work, and is spiritually inclined.

The descriptions in the second column are a gloss on Koe's labels; the source gives only the labels. The list is notable for what it includes beyond skills: body, health, relationships and spirit. The digital Renaissance man is not only a professional polymath but a person developing in all domains of life, the holistic development that Chapter 40 will treat. Koe frames the list with a principle: "creativity is the path to wealth mental and financial," and creativity requires high understanding focused on specific problems. We are in "a second Golden Age" with overwhelming information; you cannot learn everything but you can learn a lot. People still live in a paradigm of getting very good at one thing because the past environment required it; now success is reserved for "the value Creator the specialized generalists the New Renaissance Man," someone who studies diverse interests, creates value from them and sustains an enjoyable lifestyle.

**Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md

The phrase "the value Creator the specialized generalists the New Renaissance Man" identifies three of Koe's terms as names for the same figure, and it is one of the places where the hybrid of Section 20.2 meets the Renaissance thesis of this section.

In June 2023 he names the one trait that makes the type irreplaceable. Developing across the subtopics of the Eternal markets (Chapter 19) is "how you become a digital renaissance man." "You must become a lifelong **autodidact** or self-learner if you want to be truly Irreplaceable"; thanks to the internet, courses and coaching (which too many people write off as lacking credentials or results), you can learn anything faster than any of your ancestors could.

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

Irreplaceability, in this sentence, comes not from any particular skill but from the habit of acquiring skills. A specialist is replaceable because their skill is fixed and therefore copyable; an autodidact is a moving target.

A March 2024 heuristic makes the advice concrete for someone still experimenting: "Try to learn writing even if you think you're not a writer go buy a web design course go buy a web development course learn something get the skill because in the Digital World in the second Renaissance I promise that those skills are going to come in handy down the road for ... Whatever ... you do."

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

This is a rule of thumb rather than a principle, and its basis is the transfer argument of Section 20.2: digital skills (writing above all) are useful across almost any future aim, so acquiring them during a phase of uncertainty is a low-risk bet. Koe's position on whether one should learn to code oscillates across the corpus (between "you don't need to learn to code" and "learning code and content is not optional," with a growing emphasis on media over code); the 2024 heuristic belongs to the period in which he recommended both. Chapter 25 (code and media as leverage) and Chapter 36 (AI) return to the question.

#### Combining interests: average in several, unique in combination

How does the second Renaissance translate into a strategy for an individual? Koe's central claim, from May 2024, removes a burden that many multi-interest people carry: "you don't have to be exceptional at one thing you have to be average or a little bit above average in multiple things." Most people think success requires being really good at one thing. Social media, he argues, distorted our perception of value: you do not need a shredded six-pack and a "10 out of 10 body" to generate attention or make a living. Most people find it impressive if you have a bit more muscle than average, can do a few pull-ups and have a life outside fitness, more impressive than the one-dimensional person with six-pack abs. Pair that with a writing hobby and some knowledge of psychology, put it online, and you attract people to your own niche: "you're creating a niche by combining the interest that you have into something that is unique and irreplaceable."

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The argument is probabilistic, and Koe makes it explicitly. If you were going to play in the NBA or the NFL or have a platinum album, you would know by now: being the best at one specific thing (music, football, web design) "is close to statistically impossible," since only one person can be the best. Being in the top 10% or even 25% in a few areas you find interesting is much easier, given what the average person does daily. He is candid that his numbers are invented ("I'm pulling things out of my butt here... this isn't a statistic"): the top 25% in health is probably one walk a day; the top 25% in financial freedom is probably setting aside a hundred dollars a month (he first says "a day," then corrects himself). "People just don't do much with their lives."

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The figures are illustrative and the author says so; what carries the argument is the structure. Excellence in one dimension is a winner-take-all contest. Combination is not, because the number of possible combinations is so large that each person's is effectively unique. A person in the top quarter of three unrelated domains may be the only person in the world with that particular intersection, and therefore has no direct competitor, which is the same escape from competition that Chapter 19 called the niche of one.

Koe then describes the sequence by which a generalist compounds interests into an audience and a product, which can be reconstructed step by step:

1. **Reach the top 25% in something**, for instance fitness, "which isn't much."
2. **Explore a new route from it**: start a fitness career by writing online and building an audience (as taught in his *2 Hour Writer*).
3. **Reach the top 25% in that new skill**, then add to your results by branching out: study philosophy to add depth to your writing, design to enhance your brand, and so on.
4. **Become followed for yourself**: people then follow you for you, not for one skill. With a specialist, "once you stop talking about it people just unfollow you."
5. **Build a product** from any of those interests once you have an audience (taught in his course *Mental Monetization*).

The motivation he gives for the last step: you want leverage, a product that sells while you sleep, so that you can focus on your craft with something sustaining it.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

Step 4 is the strategic heart of the sequence. An audience attached to a skill is fragile in exactly the way the specialist is fragile: it leaves when the skill-topic is exhausted. An audience attached to a person survives changes of topic, because what it follows is the combination. This is the audience version of the portfolio metaphor of Section 20.2.

Koe illustrates how combinations change outcomes with three parallel examples from 2026. "A person who studied psychology and design sees user behavior differently from a pure designer. Now, imagine if they tacked on something else." "A person who learns sales and philosophy closes deals differently than the pure salesman. A person who understands fitness and business builds health companies that MBAs can't understand." This, he says, is "the exact pattern that showed in the renaissance and what allowed individuals to flourish."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

Each pair follows the same logic: the second domain changes how the first is practiced, not merely what the person knows. The designer with psychology does not do design plus psychology; they do a different kind of design. This is why combinations produce uniqueness rather than mere addition.

A 2022 example shows how a combination can become a named category. Koe likes to study broad interests and distill the big picture into content. If he studies spirituality, philosophy and neuroscience, he can create a new umbrella category "that nobody else knows but me": for example "neuro philosophy," noting the connections between neuroscience and philosophy, and call himself a "neuro philosopher." This opens many topics, is niche, specific and novel, attracts more people and builds authority in the category you created.

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md

The example resolves the apparent conflict with "niche down" from the other side. Combining interests does not mean having no niche; it means creating one that did not exist. A category of one cannot be crowded.

Koe states the general principle behind all these examples in April 2023: "Curiosity is a crucial touch point." "What makes you unique is your ability to find and curate the combination of knowledge that gets you results." In business, some people are curious about e-commerce or website flipping, others about personal branding and digital products, as he is. In health, some are curious about sports and optimal performance, others about bodybuilding and biohacking. Same domain, different interests and curiosities that each person gravitates toward to solve their problem of health, finances or mindset.

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

The emphasis here falls on curation, a word that anticipates Chapter 18's later principle that "curation matters more than creation." Uniqueness is not a matter of inventing new knowledge but of selecting, from the vast available knowledge, the combination that actually produced results in one's own life. Curiosity is the selection mechanism; results are the test.

#### The cube: a thought experiment

The most ambitious attempt to picture the generalist's combinatorial advantage is a thought experiment from February 2023, which Koe himself says is difficult to explain and which few people reach in practice. The visual he drew is ambiguous in the transcript, so the reconstruction below follows his verbal description and marks where it is uncertain.

The experiment builds four squares, each with four circles at its corners, and lets the circles grow.

1. **The Eternal markets.** Four circles at the corners of a square represent health, wealth, relationships and happiness, "where all burning and profitable problems exist." This is where to start when beginning a business. Do not start with new creativity software or art unless it is positioned into one of these markets, "because you don't have leverage yet to create whatever you want"; create art that hits the problems and pains of these markets, and narrow further if you wish. As you improve yourself (his audience, he says, is "self improvers... mind body spirit business"), you solve your own problems in these markets, and the circles expand until they overlap and you start making connections between them.
2. **Evergreen skills.** A new square: marketing, sales, writing, speaking. These too expand until you understand that they work together.
3. **Modern skills.** Email marketing, Facebook ads, Google ads, web design, SEO: recently emerged "useful modalities for applying" the evergreen skills. People tell you to learn these first, "which doesn't make sense"; learn one or two.
4. **Personal interests.** His own are philosophy, epistemology, spirituality and psychology. These expand over one to three years.

The four overlapping sets "mesh together into some form of a cube or... pyramid"; Koe admits that geometrically "it doesn't really work that way." Place a problem in the middle, and you can navigate around the surface of the shape and pull from your experience to solve the problem in a unique way. Traditionally, he says, that cube was a company with departments; now "the human mind of one individual can do this now," and more so in the future, as courses become "zip files of knowledge."

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md

| Layer | Contents | Role in the cube |
|---|---|---|
| Eternal markets | Health, wealth, relationships, happiness | Where the problems are; the starting point |
| Evergreen skills | Marketing, sales, writing, speaking | Durable means of creating and communicating value |
| Modern skills | Email marketing, paid ads, web design, SEO | Current modalities for applying the evergreen skills; learn one or two |
| Personal interests | (Koe's) philosophy, epistemology, spirituality, psychology | The individual's unique angle |

The geometry is admittedly loose, but the logic is clear. Each layer is a dimension of capability, and expansion within each layer produces overlaps, which are connections. A problem placed at the center can be approached from any combination of market knowledge, durable skill, current tool and personal interest. The claim that one mind can now do what a company with departments once did is the same claim as Section 20.2's "as a one-person business you are every department." The ordering advice embedded in the experiment (start with a problem in the Eternal markets; prefer evergreen skills to modern ones; learn only one or two modern skills) is a practical consequence: the layers differ in durability, and the generalist should invest most in the most durable.

#### Become nobody: the polymath without labels

The second Renaissance thesis has an identity correlate, which Koe expresses in a formula that appears in 2023 and again in 2024. In August 2023: "Become nobody become label-less become everything": be a designer, writer, marketer, socializer, runner, bodybuilder, philosopher, scientist, psychologist and polymath who sustains obsessive curiosity. "Subscribing to one's skill ideology or identity limits your potential in every situation." The universe, he says, is a shape shifter in constant flow: oceans evaporate, condense into clouds, rain into puddles, and the water finds its way back. Nothing is permanent; your cells are different from what they were a few years ago. Your interests, mind and self are allowed to change. "Become the universe."

**Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md

In March 2024 he repeats the formula with a rationale. With all the experimentation, "we're learning so much we need to learn how to apply that and turn it into a creative income." "Become a nobody become everything become a designer writer marketer socializer Runner bodybuilder philosopher scientist psychologist and polymath who knows how to sustain the obsessive curiosity the universe rewards those who don't impose their own limits."

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

**Become nobody** is the identity version of "don't be a tool." A label (one skill, one ideology, one identity) is a self-imposed limit in the sense of Chapter 3: it filters what one perceives and what one allows oneself to try. To "become nobody" is not to have no self but to hold no single label as the self. The water cycle and cell renewal are metaphors for impermanence, used to argue that a fixed identity is unnatural; they are not offered as evidence. "The universe rewards those who don't impose their own limits" is a principle of Koe's, stated as conviction rather than demonstrated.

The second point of the same 2023 video introduces a term Koe uses only once, the **Curiosity Compass**, and the only definition available is the one in the passage. As a kid, people discourage you from "going through a phase." Koe had an emo phase in high school, a bodybuilder phase and a raver phase, and all of them shaped him, "because evolution is about transcending and including": you transcend a phase and pull its good parts up with you (lessons from the gym-bro phase, different music from the emo phase, interesting people met at raves). Nothing is wrong with experimentation; what is wrong is conforming to others' whims. Even within a phase, you should consistently check that you are thinking independently and not relinquishing autonomy in order to fit in. Prescriptions, roadmaps and long curricula are not bad, but they narrow the mind on a specific outcome; they are useful, but not "one and done," or you get trapped in a miserable life.

**Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md

"Transcend and include" is Ken Wilber's formula for development, which Chapter 4 and Chapter 38 discuss; here Koe applies it to interests. Each phase is a stage that is surpassed but whose useful parts are carried forward. This reframes the multi-interest person's history: what looks from outside like a string of abandoned enthusiasms is, on this view, a sequence of stages each of which left something behind. The same structure underlies the "failed attempts still transfer skills" principle of Section 20.2. The qualifier about conformity is important: the compass is curiosity, and a phase adopted to fit in rather than out of curiosity is a different thing.

The formula reaches its practical conclusion in a July 2023 video titled "Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People)": "This is really the way to become a **modern polymath** or a digital Renaissance Man: by exploring reality through your interests and distributing your discoveries through content and making a living doing what you love by creating something worth charging for. So flow with the direction of the universe and bask in the enjoyment that comes with it."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

The sentence contains the whole program of the book's middle parts in compressed form: explore through interests (Part VI), distribute discoveries through content (Parts VIII and IX), create something worth charging for (Parts X and XI). The modern polymath is defined not by the number of things known but by this cycle of exploration, distribution and creation.

#### The path to becoming future-proof

In September 2025 Koe gives the second Renaissance its most concise practical form. Becoming a creator was always possible, "but it's never been so accessible. We're in the second renaissance and it's happening on the internet," "a digital society where anyone can be the next Einstein or Shakespeare." The path has four steps: (1) "shift from consumer to creator"; (2) "solve your own problems"; (3) "distribute your solutions in the global town square," the internet; (4) attract people who share your vision. "Even with just a thousand true fans, you'll find the power to create a good life."

**Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md

**Complementary context:** "1,000 true fans" is the title of a 2008 essay by Kevin Kelly, who argued that a creator can make a living from about a thousand people willing to buy whatever they produce. In this passage Koe uses the idea without naming Kelly.

The four steps connect this chapter to the previous and the following ones. Step 2 is Chapter 19's "solve your own problems and sell the solution." Step 3 introduces the **global town square**, Koe's name for social media as the public place where an individual's value can be discovered (Chapter 25). Step 4 and the thousand true fans lead to Section 20.4, where the same arithmetic supports the claim that everyone can be a creator. The word **futureproof** names what the path is meant to produce: a person who knows what they want and can achieve it in the current world.

#### A personal motive: a second Renaissance of creators

The thesis also has a personal dimension. In October 2024 Koe says that a video had "re-sparked" his desire to help create "a second Renaissance of these creators." He does not want to be "the guiding light" of it, only a role model who shares his passions and what he learns through life, hoping to change the direction of some viewers' lives, as the early fitness YouTubers did for him.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

The model he names is significant: the creators who changed his own life were not institutions but individuals sharing what they had learned, which is exactly the "decentralized education system" he believes the second Renaissance consists of. His aspiration is to occupy that role for others, which is also the role he recommends to his reader.

### 20.4 Getting Paid to Play

#### Paid to be yourself

The three previous sections built a figure: a generalist who has an aim, stacks skills, accumulates specific knowledge and combines many interests into a perspective no one else has. This section asks what that figure is for. Koe's answer is an aspiration he returns to for years and always frames with the same borrowed sentence, from the philosopher Alan Watts: "sensible people get paid for doing what they enjoy doing."

In November 2024 he presents the sentence as the articulation of "the ultimate goal of being **paid to be yourself**," and argues that this goal is "more than possible now," thanks to what social media allows: learning, building a business and finding connections.

**Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md

**Complementary context:** Alan Watts (1915–1973) was a British-born writer and speaker who popularized Zen Buddhism, Taoism and Hindu philosophy for Western audiences. Many of his recorded lectures circulate online; among the most widely shared is a talk on the question "what would you do if money were no object?", which argues that it is absurd to spend one's life doing what one dislikes in order to go on living in order to go on doing what one dislikes. The material does not identify which lecture Koe's quotation comes from.

"Paid to be yourself" is the logical endpoint of Chapter 19 and of this chapter together. If the niche is the person (Chapter 19) and the person is a combination of interests and capabilities organized by an aim (this chapter), then the business that serves the niche is the person's life made available to others, and the income is payment for being who one is, as long as who one is creates value for others. The phrase should not be read as a promise of payment for self-expression without value; Section 20.4 ends with Koe's own reasons why it is not.

#### A portfolio of incomes: Zuby

How does a generalist get paid for several interests at once? In "The Age Of The Generalist" (May 2024), Koe describes a step he calls building a **portfolio of income sources**, and he takes his inspiration from Zuby, a Twitter personality who is a rapper, a fitness buff and a political commentator. Zuby once broke the women's world-record deadlift "by identifying as a woman," which got Joe Rogan to pick him up and kick-started a fairly general audience. He promotes his music, his fitness ebook and his public-speaking gigs. He is not boxed into one topic, which, Koe says, is what lets him stand out.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

**Complementary context:** The deadlift episode (2019) was a satirical stunt: Zuby posted a video in which he lifted weights exceeding the British women's record while declaring that he identified as a woman, as a comment on debates about transgender athletes. Koe mentions it only as the event that brought Zuby a broad audience.

The case illustrates the audience logic of Section 20.3: an audience attached to a person rather than a topic can be offered products in several domains, because what it buys is the person's perspective applied to each. The portfolio is the income version of the skill portfolio of Section 20.2: several sources aimed at one overall result, so that no single failure is catastrophic.

#### A case: the coffee-shop videographer

Koe's most concrete illustration comes from a conversation he recounts in June 2026. At a coffee shop, a viewer asked him what he should monetize. The man did videography for real-estate personal brands, had clients and could scale that service, but ideally wanted to do something more artistic: filmmaking. Koe asked whether he created content for his own brand. The answer was no.

Koe's advice had two parts. First, express the artistic side by using filmmaking and videography in his *own* videos, so they stand out: "That's a part of his unique edge." Second, teach what he is passionate about, videography and filmmaking, to personal brands, where he already has experience. This, Koe says, is "a glaring gap in the market": many personal brands do not know how to edit, and Koe receives emails every day from people who think recording video is a high barrier because they do not know which editing tool to use or do not want to learn. Teaching would attract videography and filmmaking clients by positioning him as an authority, and would build a broad enough audience to eventually create a product that sells "while he sleeps," without his having to fulfill a service.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

The case shows the whole chapter's logic in miniature. The man is, at the start, a specialist service provider (real-estate videography) whose real interest (artistic filmmaking) is an escape from his work. Koe's advice does not ask him to choose between them. It combines them: the artistic interest becomes the distinguishing quality of his own content; the service experience becomes the knowledge he teaches; the audience of personal brands is a market he already knows and whose problem (editing) he has already solved. The path then runs from service to education to product, the roadmap Part X will develop. And it illustrates Section 20.3's "the best way to manage multiple interests is to make them your work": the escapist interest becomes part of the work and stops being an escape.

#### Everyone is an entrepreneur

Behind the aspiration lies a thesis about the future of work, which Koe develops from 2022 onward and which he first labels explicitly as "a theory." In May 2022 he proposes that entrepreneurship is the future career path, the thing almost everyone will have to do to make some form of income. The chain of reasoning: the human psyche is wired for entrepreneurship because it is wired for survival; in modern times "modern day entrepreneurship is survival modern day survival is entrepreneurship." The analogy that carries the argument: "we used to hunt gather trade and now we hunt for let's say ideas we gather information we make connections and then we trade value through the internet."

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

The analogy maps each activity of the forager onto the creator's: hunting becomes hunting for ideas, gathering becomes gathering information, trading becomes making connections and exchanging value online. It continues Chapter 2's argument about the hunting psyche in a cubicle (Section 2.4): the same drives that the office frustrates are exercised by entrepreneurship. It should be read as what Koe calls it, a theory built on an analogy, not as an established result of evolutionary psychology.

In June 2023 he gives the thesis a historical premise. Centuries ago, individuals fulfilled a purpose within their tribe or community; they acquired skills or interests that fit the "marketplace" that was their community. In that sense, "everyone is an entrepreneur."

**Source:** Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md

A month later he states the principle in its definitive form: "Everyone is an entrepreneur because they have value to provide to someone else; some just choose to get paid for it." He adds a second formula: "Social media is the new society and personal brands are the people of that society." Young people saying they want to be YouTubers is not bad: "people feel the natural pull to do what they enjoy," and there will be a way for most of them to do it. "That's what evolution is all about": solving the problems that make life bad until we can do what we want. Not everyone will be a YouTuber, but people will work in the creator economy, as the face or freelancing for creators, with autonomy.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

**Everyone is an entrepreneur**, Koe's coinage, is a redefinition rather than a statistic. "Entrepreneur" here does not mean someone who has started a company; it means someone who has value to provide to others. Everyone has some, so everyone is an entrepreneur in this sense; the only difference is whether one chooses to put a price on it and put it in front of people. The redefinition moves the threshold of entrepreneurship from capital and risk to a decision. The qualification about YouTubers is also important: the claim is not that everyone will be a famous creator, but that the creator economy will employ many people in various roles, with more autonomy than the jobs it replaces.

#### The future of work is play

The thesis acquires its name in April 2023. "There are almost 7 billion people on this planet someday I hope there will be almost 7 billion companies. **The future of work is play** and everyone will be an entrepreneur." "Everyone is an entrepreneur but some just get paid for the value that they provide in a non-job setting." Everyone will be forced to create, contribute and deliver value to their community, "or else their way of survival will dry up." Artificial intelligence, automation and most things on the internet point to this reality. And the closing sentence, which governs how the forecast is meant to be used: "Even if that does not become the reality for most people it can for you."

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

The "seven billion companies" line is Naval Ravikant's idea, which Section 20.2 showed Koe adapting in 2022 as "amplifying your voice and your value." Here it appears in its economic form, as a hope rather than a prediction ("someday I hope"). The final sentence converts a social forecast into a personal option: whether or not the economy moves in this direction for everyone, the individual reader can move in it. This move, from "the world will" to "you can," is characteristic of Koe's treatment of his forecasts, and it is what allows him in 2026 to hold the second Renaissance as useful speculation (Section 20.3).

In July 2023 he gives the thesis numbers. The future of work is play; individuals can grow a one-person business with new technologies to perhaps one to five million dollars or more (a few, he says, have passed five million). This is new because technology (automation, systems) lets it scale; "nobody's really pushed the boundary past five, ten million as one person," perhaps with a small team or contractors, whom he still counts as one-person businesses because they are not employees. Most such businesses, he says, maintain profit margins of 90 to 95%, sometimes higher or lower. Society is changing to reflect this: creators and influencer marketing are prominent because the model is so profitable, and opportunities are created there while other jobs are automated.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The figures are Koe's estimates from his own experience and his observation of other creators; the source does not cite data for them. They are also time-bound. The ceiling he names in 2023 ("five, ten million as one person") rises in later statements: in 2024 he speaks of one-person businesses making ten million a year and mentions Sam Altman's bet on the first billion-dollar one-person company; in 2026 he reinterprets the pre-AI model as a "lifestyle business" that eventually required hiring a team. The evolution record classifies this as a change of emphasis tied to technological stages: each wave of tools raises what one person can do.

In December 2023 he gives the thesis its deepest justification, the one that explains the word "play." Through evolution and technology, "we have been removing labor work." To Koe this reflects the human drive to pursue creative work further, to advance "the expansion and unity of mind and consciousness." We want to do what we want when we want "in a positive direction," by pursuing interests, solving problems and building solutions "that create heaven on Earth." Never has this been more of an option for the majority, because the internet lets us create our ideal lives. This, he says, is the basis of the title "the future of work is play."

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md

This is the most philosophical version, and its claims are interpretive: the direction of evolution, the expansion of consciousness, "heaven on Earth." What it adds to the economic version is a reason why the removal of labor should lead to *play* rather than to idleness or despair. If labor is what humans do because they must, and creative work is what they do because they want to, then removing labor frees time and energy for the second kind. "Play" in Koe's sense is not frivolity; it is work chosen for its own sake, the same structure as the flow states of Chapter 10, where challenge and skill are matched and the activity becomes its own reward.

#### Objections and reasons: "not everyone can be a creator"

The obvious objection is that not everyone can be a creator. Koe answers it in June 2024 by zooming out. Everyone can be a creator, because our ancestors were creators or entrepreneurs, each serving a specific role in the tribe. The tribe has expanded to become digital and global: social media is "less about being an app on your phone and more about being a large tribe or community," and it is easier and more profitable to find your tribe, because you can fulfill a specific need with your product or service and attract people with the ideas you distribute. And you do not need a large tribe: "you don't need a million followers you need 500 maybe a thousand a thousand true fans that entire concept."

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

The answer has two parts, and the second is the more practical. The first restates the tribal premise of June 2023. The second lowers the threshold: if a creator needs only five hundred to a thousand true fans (Kevin Kelly's concept, unattributed here), then "creator" does not mean "celebrity," and the objection that the attention economy cannot support everyone loses much of its force. Chapter 25 treats the thousand true fans in detail.

In February 2025 Koe gives three reasons why everyone *should* become an entrepreneur, creator or business owner, in a video provocatively titled "The creator economy is dying thanks to AI." First, attention is on social media; previously it was on television, billboards, radio and newspapers, where the average person could not promote their work. Second, AI continues to replace lower-level jobs. Third, the top-paying jobs are reserved for the skill elite, the top 1% or 0.1%, "like getting into the NBA." So the path forward, according to what Naval "has been saying or hinting at" and what Koe has said many times, is for everyone to become an entrepreneur, creator or business owner. The signs he points to: remote-work and freelance statistics, and big companies outsourcing low-level work to AI (he cites no specific figures in the passage). The internet, he adds, is a **decentralized school system**: creators are teachers whose purpose is not to make you a useful worker, and the internet is a public town square where your value can be discovered. "Building an audience is the new resume and status symbol"; followers are less vanity and more social proof that people value your ideas.

**Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md

The three reasons correspond to three forces this chapter has described. The first is the shift of attention that makes distribution available to individuals (Part IX). The second is the automation of specialized labor (Section 20.1). The third is the polarization of employment: if the remaining good jobs go to an elite as selective as professional sports, then for most people the statistical argument of Section 20.3 applies again, and the realistic path is not excellence in one competitive dimension but a combination that creates its own market. The phrase "building an audience is the new resume" completes the picture: in a world where specialization no longer secures employment, the credential is replaced by public proof of value.

Koe's position on the future of employment has shifted over the years, and the shift bears on how strongly to read these reasons. In April 2023 he said that "we are on the horizon of complete removal" of labor work; in mid-2024 he said "I don't see employment going away," only decentralizing; and from 2025 he described the jobs of the future as "reserved for the elite, the top 1%, the NBA of jobs," with the entry level "going extinct." The evolution record classifies this as a move from the disappearance of employment to its transformation and elitization. The 2025 argument above belongs to the last phase.

#### Five observations from the creator journey

In April 2023 Koe summarizes three to four years of his own creator journey in five theses, which gather the strands of this section into one list:

1. "Entrepreneurship is the only path for long-term logical thinkers."
2. Humans have been removing labor work since the beginning of time, and "we are on the horizon of complete removal."
3. The creator economy is the new economy: "if you don't have a personal brand you lose — like the Black Mirror episode Nosedive but in a good way."
4. "Sensible people get paid for doing what they enjoy doing" ("shout out Alan Watts for being way ahead of his time").
5. "The future belongs to the self-improver, the person that takes responsibility for their life and becomes a fountainhead of value, and when done in public it's almost impossible not to get paid."

**Source:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md

**Complementary context:** "Nosedive" (2016) is an episode of the British anthology series *Black Mirror* depicting a society in which people rate every interaction and their aggregate score determines their access to housing, jobs and status.

The Nosedive comparison is double-edged, and Koe's "but in a good way" acknowledges it. The episode is a dystopia of reputation; Koe borrows its premise (social standing as a function of public reputation) while rejecting its tone. The fifth thesis introduces the **fountainhead of value**, his name for the person who becomes a source of value for others by improving themselves and documenting it; and its final clause ("almost impossible not to get paid") is the strongest form of the aspiration in this section.

These 2023 theses have a later echo that the reader should know. In January 2026 Koe asked himself, on camera, whether he was "just promoting this dogma," concluding that "the more I break it down, the more it seems like the one true way." The evolution record classifies this as self-criticism without a change of thesis: he acknowledges the objection that not everyone can do this and reformulates the claim as "anyone ambitious can." The material notes a tension with his own criticism of business ideologies (Chapter 19): the claim that becoming a creator is the one true way has the shape of the ideologies he warns against. He does not resolve this tension.

#### The art of living is getting paid to play

The aspiration returns to Alan Watts in its most condensed form. In May 2024 Koe uses the quotation to frame his audience: creatives who want to get paid for their interests, for whom the conventional path is not the smartest move. "As Alan Watts would say, sensible people get paid for doing what they enjoy doing."

**Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md

In September 2024, introducing the idea that money is a skill, he says that "we need to understand that **the art of living is getting paid to play**," and that this requires understanding the difference between a job, a career and a calling. He starts, again, with Watts: "sensible people get paid to do what they enjoy. I continue returning to that quote."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md

The formula "the art of living is getting paid to play" is Koe's own; the quotation it rests on is Watts's. The distinction between job, career and calling, which he announces at this point, is not developed in the passage itself; the money chapter (Chapter 34) takes up his broader view of money.

In March 2024 he connects the quotation to motivation and to business: "Business is how you Channel your interests skills and expertise to impact others at scale." And: "If you want to live a fulfilling life you need to leverage all five intrinsic drivers curiosity purpose passion autonomy and Mastery you need to create and have full control over the lifestyle you wish to live business is how you get there."

**Source:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md

The **five intrinsic drivers** are a list Koe adapts from Steven Kotler's work on flow (Chapter 10), though in this passage he presents them without attribution. The argument is that a job, which assigns the work, the schedule and the goal, can satisfy few of the drivers; autonomy in particular is structurally excluded. A business that channels one's own interests can satisfy all five. This is the motivational counterpart of the economic arguments above: getting paid to play is not only possible but necessary for fulfillment, because only self-directed work engages all the drivers at once.

The most personal statement of the aspiration is about Koe's own work. In November 2023 he asks why he is making the video he is making. "Because it's how I make money. I do this on a weekly basis because I get paid to do so," but "there are more reasons or whys behind that"; "I can very well stop doing this and go make money another way." "There's a good feedback loop here where I enjoy talking to you guys, you guys enjoy my content; it's as close to the Japanese concept of ikigai as it can be."

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md

**Complementary context:** *Ikigai* is a Japanese word usually translated as "reason for being." In its popular Western form it is drawn as the intersection of four circles: what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs and what you can be paid for. Koe names it only as "the Japanese concept."

The passage is a description of the goal achieved, and it specifies what "getting paid to play" means in practice. It is not that the work involves no effort; it is that the work is paid, enjoyed and valued by others at once, so that payment, enjoyment and contribution form a loop rather than a trade-off. And the freedom to stop ("I can very well stop doing this") is part of the condition: the play is chosen.

#### Do what you love: against the fashionable dismissal

The section ends with Koe's reply to a common piece of advice that would undercut everything above. In January 2023 he describes the result of a decade of obsessions as the **impossible achievement**: doing what he loves for a living. Most people, he says, claim or imply that this is impossible, or tell you not to focus on it. It is a trend to say "don't follow your passion, don't do what you love" for the sake of grabbing attention, while leading you toward something that eventually lets you do what you love, "so it's kind of contradictory." He "gets where they're coming from," but thinks it silly to write off doing what you love or a passionate life: "if you want to go all the way to the negative extreme it's doing what you hate and never living with a shred of passion in your life," which nobody wants. And you do not have to postpone those things until you reach some form of material success.

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The argument is a reductio. The anti-passion advice, taken to its extreme, recommends a life without passion, which no one would choose; so the advice must be a matter of sequencing (develop skill first, passion follows) rather than of principle. Koe accepts the kernel of the sequencing argument elsewhere: skills must be built, specific knowledge comes from years of immersion, interests clarify only once they become work. What he rejects is the conclusion that enjoyment should be deferred until after success. In his model, the enjoyment is part of the mechanism: specific knowledge "feels like play" (Section 20.2), and interests that are enjoyed are the ones sustained long enough to compound.

#### The limits of the aspiration

The material assigned to this section contains several qualifications that should be gathered, because together they define what "getting paid to play" does and does not claim.

First, it is a theory and a hope, not a forecast for everyone. Koe labels the entrepreneurship thesis "a theory" in 2022, says "someday I hope" of the seven billion companies, and adds "even if that does not become the reality for most people it can for you." The claim is about what is available to an individual, not about what will happen to a population.

Second, it is conditional on creating value. "Everyone is an entrepreneur because they have value to provide," "when done in public it's almost impossible not to get paid" for the person who "becomes a fountainhead of value." Payment follows value, not self-expression as such; the coffee-shop videographer is paid for solving the editing problem of personal brands, not for his love of filmmaking alone.

Third, it is conditional on consciousness and on overcoming programming. The second Renaissance belongs to "conscious and intentional individuals" (Section 20.3), and the "residue" of decades of schooling has to be cleaned up for a lifetime.

Fourth, its numbers are estimates that move with technology: the ceiling of the one-person business, the margins, the share of jobs automated. And fifth, Koe himself has asked whether the thesis has become a dogma, and has answered that it still seems to him the one true way, an answer the reader is free to weigh.

With these limits stated, the chapter's argument can be summarized as a chain. Specialization made humans efficient by making them machine-like; machines are now better at it; so the specialist's position is fragile. The human default is the tool-building generalist, defined by an aim rather than a skill, who accumulates capabilities by stacking them and accumulates specific knowledge by following curiosity deep enough. Multiple interests, once a liability under the industrial order, become an advantage in an era in which, as after the printing press, one person can learn and combine many domains. And the point of all of it is to get paid for being oneself: to make one's interests one's work, so that work becomes play. Part VIII begins with the skill through which that play is most often made public and paid: writing.

### Exercises

1. **Audit your attachments.** Following the distinction in Section 20.1 between being "attached to the skill" and "attached to the vision," write down how you would complete the sentence "I am a ___" in a professional context. Then imagine that a tool appears tomorrow that performs that capability better and more cheaply than you. Describe honestly how you would react. Now rewrite your self-description as a direction rather than a capability (what you are trying to bring about, not what you can do). What changes in how you would respond to the same tool? Where does Eriksen's "don't be a tool" apply to you, and where might a capability-based identity still be useful?

2. **Map your skill tree.** Reconstruct the sequence by which you acquired the skills you have now, in the order they were acquired. For each skill, note which earlier skill made it visible or possible (Koe's "you can't improve what you aren't aware of"). Include skills from abandoned projects and "failed" phases. Which of them transferred into what you do now? Then, from your current position, identify the one or two skills that your present aim makes visible as the next to unlock. Explain why you could not have seen them earlier.

3. **Locate yourself in the specialist–generalist debate.** Section 20.2 presents six formulations of Koe's position, from "become a generalist, not a specialist" (2022) to the Musashi strategist (2026). Choose the formulation you find most defensible and argue for it against the others. Then identify the one Koe statement in this chapter that you think is weakest or most rhetorical (for example, "specializing in only one skill is certain death," or "schools were created to enslave the brightest minds") and explain what evidence would be needed to support it. Is the apparent contradiction between Koe's radical and hybrid statements real, or mostly verbal, given his definition of the generalist?

4. **Design your cube.** Apply the thought experiment of Section 20.3 to yourself. List your current knowledge in each of the four layers: the Eternal markets (which of health, wealth, relationships and happiness you have solved problems in), evergreen skills, modern skills and personal interests. Where do the circles already overlap? Choose one specific problem that sits at an intersection of at least three layers and that you have experienced yourself. Describe how you would approach it differently from a specialist in any single one of those layers.

5. **Test the "top 25%" argument.** Koe claims that being the best at one thing is "close to statistically impossible," while being in the top 10 to 25% in a few areas is easy, and admits his numbers are invented. Construct an argument for and an argument against this claim, considering at least one field where combination does not seem to substitute for excellence (Koe's own example of top AI researchers is one). Under what conditions does the strategy of combining average-to-good skills fail? Under what conditions does it work best?

6. **Separate escape interests from directions.** List all your current interests. For each, ask whether you would pursue it if your work were already meaningful to you, or whether it functions mainly as relief from your work (Section 20.3's "most people pursue multiple interests as an escape from their work"). Which interests survive the test? Then apply the coffee-shop videographer case: identify one interest that is currently an escape and describe how it could become part of your work, either as the distinguishing quality of what you make or as knowledge you teach.

7. **Evaluate the second Renaissance as a hypothesis.** Koe moved from asserting that we are living through a second Renaissance to holding it as a pattern "without accepting it as truth," because "it's better than doing nothing." Reconstruct the printing-press argument step by step and identify the step you find weakest. Then consider his justification for holding an uncertain idea: when is it rational to act on a hypothesis you do not accept as true? When does that stance become dangerous? Relate your answer to Koe's own question in 2026 of whether "become a creator" has become a dogma.

8. **Write your "paid to play" account.** Using the coffee-shop case and Koe's ikigai loop as models, describe a version of your own work in which payment, enjoyment and value to others reinforce one another. Be specific about who would pay, for what problem, and why your combination of interests and experience would make you better placed to solve it than a specialist. Then list the conditions from "The limits of the aspiration" that you have not yet met, and decide which one is the binding constraint in your case.
