## Chapter 26 — Growing: Validated Content, Traffic and Networking

Chapter 25 argued, at the level of principle, why anyone who creates should build an audience: attention is power, distribution is freedom, and an audience is the form of leverage an individual can build alone and that accumulates as trust. This chapter turns from the why to the how. It asks what a person with zero followers actually does, week after week, to grow, and it gives Koe's answer in the form he converged on after years of reformulation: growth is a system with two levers, **validated content** and **networking**, and everything else is either a breakdown of those two levers or a distraction from them.

The chapter proceeds in six steps. Section 26.1 presents growth as a system rather than a lottery: the shift to the interest graph that changed what follower counts mean, the realistic timelines and benchmarks Koe offers, and the successive versions of his growth framework, from the four steps of 2023 to the "magic click" and the six daily tasks of 2024. Section 26.2 deals with the phase every creator passes through first, which Koe calls **beginner hell**, and with the psychological barrier that keeps most people from leaving it, impostor syndrome; his answer is that experience is gained by starting, and that the people you can help best are those one or two steps behind you. Section 26.3 explains the first lever, validated content: make noise, double down on signal, do what already works from your own perspective, sell what is already selling, and treat each post as a data point in an iterative pipeline that occasionally produces nonlinear jumps. Section 26.4 explains the half of the work most beginners ignore: getting eyes on your content. Writing is not building an audience; traffic mechanisms, other people's audiences, remixing, clippable moments and paid growth are the means by which writing reaches readers. Section 26.5 develops the second lever, networking, which Koe prefers to call "making friends": why growth is social, how tribes and masterminds form, what DMs are for, how the audience itself functions as an ecosystem, and the step-by-step "non-needy networking" process in its 2023 and 2026 versions. Section 26.6 closes with Koe's own growth as a case: the Photoshop art account that first showed him the mechanics, and the iterative path from one reader to more than a million.

The chapter depends on Chapter 24, where the post was introduced as a minimum viable product and the content ecosystem as the structure that turns one idea into many pieces; on Chapter 23, whose structures and hooks are what "persuasive content" means here; and on Chapter 25, whose distinction between built, borrowed and bought distribution reappears below as concrete tactics. It prepares Chapter 27, on the personal brand, which treats the identity and trust that this mechanical growth is meant to carry.

A word on chronology before starting. Koe has taught audience growth in many videos between 2021 and 2026, and his frameworks change name, number of steps and emphasis. The core does not change: write something worth reading, put it in front of people, learn from what they respond to, and make friends with others doing the same. Where the versions diverge (on volume, on following counts, on paid growth, on engagement groups, on how far "ahead" of a client one needs to be), the chapter presents each version with its date and the reason, when Koe gives one.

### 26.1 Growth as a System

#### The interest graph: why follower count stopped mattering

Any account of how to grow on social media in the mid-2020s has to begin with a structural change in the platforms themselves. Social media, Koe explains in an October 2024 video, switched from chronological, follow-based feeds to what is called an **interest graph**: TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube's algorithm and the "For You" page on X all show users what matches what they share, engage with or give attention to, rather than what the accounts they follow have published.

**Complementary context:** "Interest graph" is a term from the platform industry, contrasted with the "social graph" of earlier networks such as Facebook, where the feed was built mainly from the accounts and friends a user had chosen to follow. Koe uses the term in its common sense; it is not his coinage.

Koe treats this shift as both a blessing and a curse. The blessing is that "follower count doesn't really matter anymore." A small account can reach a large number of people, and with the skill of content writing and engagement a creator can consistently go viral, "though you shouldn't bank on it," and funnel that traffic to an email list. The curse, which he develops later in the same video, is the mirror image: you cannot guarantee virality every day, and you cannot even guarantee that your followers will see your content. Any given post may be shown entirely to new people who know nothing about you.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The same point was developed a few months later, in January 2025, in a conversation between Koe and John Hugh, co-founder of the creator platform Stan. Koe framed the question by mentioning the noise in the creator and AI space, the "for you pages," the interest graph and the move away from follower count. Hugh's answer was that "there's never been a better time to start," and he gave five reasons. First, people have begun to realize that "social media content is no longer social media, it's just the new marketing channel." Second, attention has moved from newspapers and legacy television to YouTube and TikTok; Hugh says he barely watches Netflix anymore because a YouTube thumbnail and title are simply better. Third, every small business owner therefore "needs to be where the user attention is." Fourth, on the interest graph "some of our most successful creators who make the most income have like 5,000 followers," because they are connected with their core niche: "you don't even need a large follower account now to be seen as credible, you just need to be in front of the right audience." Fifth, success rates on Stan are rising because audiences are maturing and there is "more social acceptance around buying things online from Instagram or from YouTube." His advice is to position yourself "in favor of that tailwind," with the caveat that legacy channels may one day have "alpha" (an untapped advantage) again.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

These are Hugh's arguments, offered from the vantage point of a company whose data covers many creators; Koe sets up the question and agrees with the conclusion. Together the two passages mark a real change in Koe's own position. Earlier, he had treated large follower counts as the measure of leverage: in 2022 he spoke of the scale of 500,000+ followers as leverage and mocked those who said gaining followers did not matter, and in 2023 he observed that people do not follow accounts with few followers, while at 100,000 "they follow you without questioning." After 2024 the emphasis moves to trust and to the email list as the only real measure of an audience. Koe never fully abandons follower numbers (he keeps using them as benchmarks, as the next subsection shows), and in 2026 he summarizes the reconciliation in two phrases: "likes ain't cash," but "it's not binary." The reason he gives for the shift is the platform change itself. The older view returns in Section 26.2, where it explains why the first months of an account are so hard.

#### Realistic timelines and benchmarks

Koe offers numbers for what growth should look like, and they are useful both as targets and as a check on unrealistic expectations. They also vary with the year and the context, and it is better to present them side by side than to pretend they form a single rule.

In September 2023 he says that owned distribution compounds: "you can get to 10 to 20,000 followers in a year, 50 to 100,000 in two years and millions after four to five years." The condition is decisive: "that is if you know how to iterate on your best content or even write content in the first place." Two months later he calls 100,000 followers in a year an "extremely good," nearly perfect result.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md; Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

In July 2024 he gives a monthly benchmark in a sharper tone: "if you weren't gaining 1,500 to 3,000 followers a month in under 30 minutes a day of work you're doing something wrong" and "you can do better." With that consistent growth, "over a year two years you can make a good amount of money, way more than you're used to." Annualized, this implies roughly 18,000 to 36,000 followers a year, somewhat above the 2023 figure.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

By March 2026 the metric itself has changed, consistently with the shift to the interest graph: Koe now speaks in views and impressions rather than followers. "If you become skilled at it, then you can get 10 to 50,000 views per YouTube video and you can also get 500,000 to a million impressions on social media per month." He adds, dryly, that "it will take some time to do that if you're not immediately good at the thing, shocker," and insists on the nature of the benchmark: "You can't just go to a school, get a degree in social media and immediately have 50,000 followers; this is based on real-world feedback; this is based on skill." Those impressions are enough, in his business arithmetic, to reach about 720 daily visitors to an offer, which he calls "a challenge for sure, but it's not too much to ask for." And he blocks the all-or-nothing reading: "even if you don't get [those numbers], it's not like you're making zero dollars if you actually do this stuff. Many people would be very happy with less than a million dollars per year."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

Against these targets, Koe sets an explicit warning about expectations, from October 2023: "You will not see insane results in less than a year, and even if you do it's wise to not think that you will. That expectation most of the time won't match with reality come time and it's not going to feel good, so don't hold that expectation." In the same video he frames the work of testing traffic mechanisms and iterating as a process of six to twelve months.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

| Date | Benchmark | Condition or caveat |
|---|---|---|
| 2023-09 | 10–20K followers in year one, 50–100K in two, millions in four to five | Only if you know how to iterate on your best content |
| 2023-10 | No "insane results" in under a year | Testing and iterating takes 6–12 months |
| 2023-11 | 100K in a year is "extremely good" | Near-perfect execution |
| 2024-07 | 1,500–3,000 followers a month in under 30 minutes a day | "Otherwise you're doing something wrong" |
| 2026-03 | 10–50K views per YouTube video; 500K–1M social impressions a month | "Based on skill"; less is not zero |
| 2026-05 | First effects of networking in two weeks to three months | Still compounding over 6–12 months (Section 26.5) |

Koe does not present these figures as contradictory, and they need not be read that way: they depend on skill, stage and platform. Two things are worth noting. The figures are his own estimates from his experience and that of students, not data from a study. And the most recent version, which gives a shorter horizon for the first visible effects of networking, coexists with the older warning against expecting much before a year; the first is about early signs, the second about substantial results.

#### The growth system, version one: four steps (2023)

Koe has summarized audience growth in several sequential frameworks. They are best read as successive compressions of one system, and the chapter is organized around the last of them. The earliest complete version comes from the October 2023 video "How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers," and consists of four steps that depend on one another.

Step one is to write persuasive content: content that captures attention, holds it and delivers value on it (the subject of Chapters 21–23). Step two is to get eyes on your content: "your content is meaningless unless you attempt and put effort into getting eyes on your content." Writing into the void is "okay... for practice, but eventually you have to just get out there." Step three is to iterate on your ideas with data, and Koe stresses the dependency: "you can only reach this step if you have eyes on your content," since without readers you have no way of knowing what performs best. Step four comes "down the road a bit": creating depth and span with your best ideas through long-form content. Koe's term **depth and span** refers to two complementary dimensions of trust: span is the breadth of short-form content that reaches many people; depth is the long-form content (threads, newsletters, videos) that lets a smaller number go deep with your ideas.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The point of numbering the steps is the dependency between them. Each step is impossible without the one before. A beginner who skips step two and keeps refining step one is trying to do step three without data; a creator who jumps to long form before finding which ideas resonate is building depth on untested ground. This is the same logic as the "tweet is the new MVP" of Chapter 24: short posts are cheap tests whose results decide what deserves expansion.

#### Version two: the magic click and the two levers of the business (2024)

In January 2024, in a video on internet business models, Koe compressed the system into a five-link chain he calls the **magic click**: you write content; you put effort into getting eyes on it, "a crucial step that everyone ignores no matter how much I tell them because this is where the work comes into play"; people who like your content follow you; you create a product; you market the product.

He then names two levers in the chain. The **main lever** is content: "if you can gain one follower you can gain 100,000." The **secondary lever** is marketing: "if you can make $1 you can make $100,000." What these sentences claim is not a forecast but a form of scalability: the hard part is the first instance, because once you know how to produce one result you can repeat the process. The specific figure fluctuates across sources: in another February 2024 passage he says one follower can become $1,000 and one dollar a million, apparently a slip, and in May 2024 he settles on "if you can make $1 you can make 1 million; if you can gain one follower you can gain 1 million." The idea is the same in every version. Koe frames the chain as "the new economy," enabled by technology from the last five years, and observes that most people still regard earning an income from what they enjoy as crazy.

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

The magic click extends the four steps beyond the audience to the business, which Chapter 28 will define minimally as audience plus product. It also repeats the diagnosis that organizes this whole chapter: the step everyone ignores is the second one. Writing is the part beginners are willing to do; getting eyes on the writing is "where the work comes into play."

#### Version three: six things, one to two hours a day (2024)

In October 2024, Koe restated audience building as six things to focus on "every morning," which "when done well should take no longer than 1 to 2 hours a day" and can therefore be done part time:

1. Testing ideas and structures on social media. A **structure** here means the shape of a post. Koe's instruction is to scroll through 10 to 30 posts and notice the forms: blocks of sentences, paragraphs, line breaks, long and short tweets, bullet points, threads. Then test your own ideas inside those shapes.
2. Having a **catalyst strategy** for new posts: a deliberate way of getting new posts in front of other audiences (developed in Section 26.4 under the name "idea catalysts").
3. Turning your best ideas into threads and newsletters.
4. Repurposing your writing to other platforms.
5. Taking advantage of **exponential events**, the sudden nonlinear jumps in growth that occasionally follow a post (Section 26.3).
6. Sending everyone to your newsletter.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The transcript shows that the numbering shifts in his walkthrough (newsletters become step three, exponential events step four, newsletter promotion step five), and repurposing is not developed separately there; Chapter 24 covers it. Compared with the four steps of 2023, the six things add two elements: the explicit reliance on exponential events, and the newsletter as the destination of all traffic, the owned distribution of Chapter 25.

#### Version four: two levers (2026)

The latest and most compressed form comes from a May 2026 video titled "How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits)." There Koe reduces growth to two levers, validated content and networking, and tells the viewer to "treat everything else as a distraction." Section 26.3 develops the first lever and Sections 26.4–26.5 the second, together with the traffic tactics that belong to both.

Read in sequence, the versions show a progressive compression rather than a change of doctrine. The 2023 four steps and their tactics (traffic mechanisms, remixing, paid growth) remain valid as a tactical breakdown of the 2026 levers: "persuasive content" plus "iterate with data" become validated content; "get eyes on it" becomes, increasingly, the relationships that make other people share your work. Koe does not explain why he compressed the system, beyond presenting the two levers as "levers that you pull" rather than something to be guessed at. Between the 2024 and 2026 versions there is also a 2025 version in seven steps for getting out of beginner hell, which Section 26.2 presents.

#### Where the hour goes

A system has to fit into a life. In a June 2024 video on what he calls the 365-hour year (one focused hour a day, developed in Part V), Koe explains how to use that single hour for audience building. At first, you split it: "you spend some of that time educating yourself, you spend some of that time actually doing the work" of writing and growing the audience. Step one is "getting the ball rolling," creating the system that grows an audience around what you want, and that takes time. Only later, once you have the hang of building an audience, do you use the hour to build a product to monetize it. Weekends are an accelerator: if you have twelve hours on weekends, use them; four to six hours will already speed things up.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

The sequence matters as much as the time budget. Audience before product is the order of the magic click, and it is also the order of Koe's own business, as Chapter 37 will show. Section 26.6 adds a complication: Koe himself did not always follow it.

### 26.2 Beginner Hell and Impostor Syndrome

#### What beginner hell is

Every growth system described in Section 26.1 assumes that the creator keeps going long enough for it to work. Most do not. Koe has a name for the phase in which they give up: **beginner hell**. It is his coinage, and he uses it from 2024 to 2026 with definitions that complement one another.

The plainest definition comes from May 2026: "Beginner hell is where you kind of you just post into the void and you don't really gain any followers." It happens, he explains, when your first serious endeavor is social media itself and you are learning the skills and the taste as you go. The mechanism that keeps people there is specific: "If you don't correct your mistakes or you don't pay attention to what's working and what's not, and you don't course correct and iterate, then you're never really going to have your breakout." That, he says, is why most creators quit, "and I don't blame them," because they cannot gain any traction. Later in the same video he adds the corollary: in beginner hell "you can't expect the algorithm to just launch you into the ether."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

A February 2025 video, "Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands," opens with the duration and the emotional texture of the phase: "3 to 6 months — that's how long most creators are trapped in beginner hell." It is "that dreadful place where you feel like you don't know what you're doing," where you question whether it is worth continuing and think about what to try next "just to do it all over again." Most people quit before things start to get good. Zero likes, zero followers and zero sales are, for Koe, "one of the most painful things in the world," because becoming a creator was supposed to be your ticket to the good life, and you begin to wonder if it was "a pipe dream that was sold to you by marketers and gurus." In the same video he widens the range: beginner hell "can last for 3 months or 30 years," the second figure being the case of the starving artist who never learns marketing or monetization.

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The two definitions describe the same phase from two sides. The 2026 version gives its cause (no iteration), the 2025 version its experience (dread, doubt, restarting) and its typical length. Elsewhere Koe applies the term to his own early years, in which he jumped between business models for almost five years without anything working; Section 26.6 returns to that.

#### Why the first followers are the hardest

Beginner hell is not only a matter of skill. It is also a structural disadvantage, and Koe described it precisely in October 2023: "Those first however many followers are the hardest. You don't have any social proof, you don't have any authority, you don't have any content built up and people just don't like to follow people that have a low follower count." His illustration is a profile visit. Someone lands on an account with 100 followers and, "even if it's the best content in the world," thinks "he probably hasn't been doing this long enough, maybe that was a one-off post... he's not that important." Someone visiting an account with 100,000 followers sees a post that may be terrible and thinks "he must be important," and follows "without even questioning it." The beginning is therefore "a heavy grind that you have to overcome with certain strategies."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

This is the 2023 view of follower count that Section 26.1 contrasted with the later "follower count doesn't really matter anymore." The two are less opposed than they look. The interest graph lowers the barrier to being seen; it does not remove the social proof that a large count still confers once someone visits a profile. What the beginner lacks, in either case, is not visibility alone but the accumulated evidence (followers, a bank of content, a recognizable name) that makes a stranger trust a new account. The "certain strategies" Koe mentions are the subject of the rest of this chapter.

#### The seven steps out (2025)

In the February 2025 video, Koe structures the way out of beginner hell as "seven tips, strategies and insights." The transcript's numbering is inconsistent (it mixes sub-steps and main steps, saying "number one" and "step number three" in ways that do not line up), so the reconstruction below is partly inferred from the flow of the video.

| Step | Name in the video | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Do what works for beginners | Solve more problems and pain points; cover the most important evergreen ideas in depth; filter your favorite accounts for their oldest content |
| 2 | Your content just isn't that good | Improve it with the Ten Commandments of Engagement (Chapter 23) |
| 3 | Start with training wheels | Structure, a swipe file, frameworks (Chapter 23) |
| 4 | Nobody knows who you are | Inject yourself into a tribe; non-needy networking; one strategic post a week (Section 26.5) |
| 5 | You need a product | Monetization from the start (Part X) |
| 6 | Build a world, not a funnel | Give context to all your content (Chapter 27) |
| 7 | Make noise and double down on signal | Experiment with ideas and keep what resonates (Section 26.3) |

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The table maps almost the entire book onto the problem of the beginner. Two observations follow. First, the seven steps contain the two levers of 2026 in embryo: steps 1, 2, 3 and 7 are about the quality and validation of content; step 4 is networking. Second, the advice to filter favorite accounts for their *oldest* content is a small but telling idea: the posts that a large creator published when they were small show what works for an account without social proof, which is exactly the beginner's situation. The term **building a world** (step 6) refers to giving context to everything you publish, so that each piece belongs to a recognizable universe of ideas rather than being a one-off hook or the top of a funnel; Chapter 27 develops it as the brand understood as an environment.

At the end of the video Koe adds an exercise. He asks viewers to answer, in the comments, "what was the one thing that kept you trapped or is keeping you trapped in beginner hell," because "once you become aware of that and make that public it's a lot easier to overcome." Anyone who says they have none, he adds, is "lying or you just don't have the knowledge to know better." The exercise applies a principle from Part I, awareness as the first step out of any trap, to the narrow case of a creator's sticking point, with the added element of making it public, which turns a private excuse into a stated problem.

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### You just need to be growing

A June 2025 video on personal brands supplies a consolation that is also a criterion. Koe calls the part of the brand devoted to reach the "growth pillar," because without growth there is no influx of traffic, "so you won't get paid." But the standard is modest: "You don't need to grow by thousands or millions of followers. You just need to be growing." And the way out of stagnation is already available: "The click with this growth pillar is that validated ideas... are already out there and it's up to you to find those, take those and make them your own and experiment until you see consistent growth."

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

**The click** is another of Koe's terms. In the 2025 video on beginner hell he uses it for the moment of insight in which you understand that growth is a skill and not luck, the level that follows beginner hell and which he jokingly calls "intermediate heaven." This passage is a bridge to Section 26.3: what moves a creator out of beginner hell is not more effort applied blindly but the deliberate use of ideas that have already been validated by the market.

#### Impostor syndrome: the barrier before the first post

Beginner hell is what happens to those who start. Many never start, and Koe's most frequent diagnosis of why is a set of beliefs he groups under impostor syndrome: "I don't have enough experience," "I'm not qualified," "my life is too boring." His answers to these beliefs are among his most stable positions, repeated from 2022 to 2026.

The first answer is an inversion. In a November 2022 list of lessons from his business, he argues that the ego performs "incredible mental gymnastics" to convince you of what you cannot do. Thinking you lack experience is a limiting belief, and it usually means you do not yet understand how to position yourself authentically even with zero experience. Tell someone to write online and list its "200 benefits," and the mind produces objections: "I'm not good at writing," "that's only for influencers," "I'm just freelancing before I build an audience." Koe finds the last one particularly silly. The same person would accept $500 to $1,000 from a client when they have no idea what they are doing, yet fears posting free information from a beginner's perspective ("I'm sharing what I learned"), which would let them gauge the viability of their idea for free through an audience and build leverage in the form of traffic, so that they would not even need outreach. His summary is the graphic he showed on screen: it is not "you need experience to start," it is "you gain experience by starting." People leave out the pieces of the picture that would lead to action; the ego closes the mind and lasers in on one objection, at the expense of the holistic picture and of creative thinking. "Don't let fear rule you and keep you the same."

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md

The same argument appears in a sharper form a month earlier. In an October 2022 video, Koe takes the example of someone who says: "I know a lot about web design and I'm trying to land my first client but I can't post about it online." If you are prepared to reach out to a stranger to try to make money from a freelance service, he replies, it makes no sense to be scared of posting free content. Free content should come first, "as a way of you distilling what you learn and organizing your thoughts" so that others benefit, and as a way of building authority to the point of landing a client.

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md

In January 2023 he states the converse: "if you're afraid to write content for free then you shouldn't even be thinking about working for a client." The argument "goes both ways." You do not have to "fake it till you make it," but you do have to start posting and delivering the value you are learning, the same value you will later deliver to clients in exchange for a lot of money. Given that, he says, it makes no sense that people do not start a personal brand.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

There is a tension worth stating here, which Koe himself does not resolve. He recommends publishing content before having results, but in his own trajectory he obtained results as a freelancer before he began to publish seriously. He presents this with humility ("I'm just a regular dude") rather than as a reason to reverse the advice. One reading is that his own path was the slower one, and that the advice describes what he would do now; but that is an interpretation, and the source does not give it.

#### Simple tests for being "qualified"

Against the abstract question "am I qualified?", Koe offers concrete tests. The best known, from October 2022 and repeated in a 2024 compilation, is the friend test: if you could go to a friend right now and teach them something you are studying, on social media, at school or anywhere, "you're more than experienced"; "you're already doing this... you just need to do it in public."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In July 2024 he extends it to family: "if you've helped your friends or family with any topic that you've ever learned about in your life you have enough experience." In January 2026 he applies it to the business itself, in answer to the belief that business is for talented or rich people: "if you've ever helped anyone with any of your interests in any way, then you are qualified to start a business. It doesn't need to go any further than that."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md; If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

In December 2024 he locates the source of the objection. People say "I've just been studying this in college, or in my free time online, I don't know if I'm qualified to teach it." What, he asks, do they think people online are doing? They are sharing what they know and find valuable so that others can benefit too. The disconnect is perceptual: "you are paying attention to the gap between you and other people online rather than realizing that they're a human too," "just a human that decides to speak well to a camera or write in a serious way." Koe uses **the Gap** in several senses across the corpus (the distance between where you are and where others are, which causes overwhelm; the distance between you and a desired object, which drives dopamine); here it is the distance one perceives between oneself and established creators, which hides the fact that they are ordinary people who decided to publish.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

#### Two blocks: "not enough experience" and "too boring"

In an October 2022 video, repeated nearly word for word in a February 2024 compilation, Koe names two specific blocks. He begins with his own analytics: most of his viewers are between 18 and 35 (with older outliers he applauds). The problem is that most of them do not see those years as experience they can leverage to help others. They have not self-reflected enough to see their own value, the problems they have overcome, and the fact that they hold information that can educate and help others. Block one: you think you do not have enough experience. Block two: you think your life is too boring, an effect of social media and its glamorous lifestyles. His answer to the second is one of his signature phrases: you do not need a glamorous lifestyle; "you need to be interested enough to make your interest interesting to other people."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

**Make your interest interesting** is Koe's own formula, and it links this section with Part VII: the niche is the person, and what makes a person worth following is not an extraordinary life but the ability to make what interests them compelling to others (the craft of Chapters 22–23). In the 2024 compilation he adds two practical reassurances. He recommends starting with writing (Twitter, a newsletter, a blog, even YouTube; in that very video he is reading his newsletter aloud), and he notes that you do not even need to show your face on the account.

#### Honesty and the market of extremes

In February 2024 Koe proposes a solution to impostor syndrome that is also a positioning strategy: "impostor syndrome is solved with honesty." Your marketing should be based "solely on where you are in your journey and exactly what you help others with." The creator industry, he notes, already has enough false and attention-grabbing promises, and that very excess creates an opportunity: "in a market of extremes, if you want to be outstanding all you have to do is be average."

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md

The **market of extremes** is his term for a market saturated with exaggerated promises, in which honesty about a modest position becomes a differentiator. The same video contains a practical rule for how honesty sounds when you do not yet have results, which he states more explicitly in July 2024: you are not writing "how I made a million dollars in 3 days" but "how I plan to make a million in 5 years"; not "how I lost 10 lbs in 2 months" but "here's my plan to lose 10 lbs in 2 months," "until you gain experience and then you can start talking about the results that you have."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

#### Stepping into the arena

The second family of arguments explains why the only way out of the experience problem is through publication. Koe calls this **stepping into the arena**: gaining experience by practicing your skills in a real-world setting and receiving feedback. "How do you think you gain experience... you don't gain experience without stepping into the arena." His analogy is the freelancer, who reaches out to clients with zero experience, "potentially in exchange for money," in order to gain experience; that is how any business starts. By comparison, writing a social media post costs nothing and promises nothing: "it's free content, you can do that without experience so that you can reap the benefits of articulating your thoughts in order to expose knowledge gaps, because that's the only way that you're going to learn faster." And: "You're not going to learn and gain experience by sitting and reading about things; you're going to gain experience by getting direct feedback and criticism on your work."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The argument connects directly with Chapter 14's "build to learn": writing in public is a learning instrument before it is a growth instrument, because articulating a thought exposes where it is incomplete. In November 2023 Koe states the principle as a warning: "your websites, designs, writing and other projects do not count until other people see them. You are delaying the time to feedback; without feedback you can't make it valuable; your first iteration will not be valuable." **Time to feedback** is his term for the interval between making something and showing it; hiding work lengthens it, and since the first version of anything is never good, every day of hiding is a day of not improving.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md

The oldest version of the argument, from October 2022, frames it in terms of value. It is "absolutely crucial" to put your value on what he calls the **public market**: turn it into internet content and post it, because you need data to know whether your ideas are truly valuable, or at least perceived as valuable. You can write like Alan Watts or a philosopher, but if you are not growing, getting engagement or making sales, the work is not sustainable or doable full time. "You need a balance of internal and external"; and you play the external game "from a place of I want to give value."

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The reference to Alan Watts is not a criticism of Watts but an example of an inward-facing writer whose model, without an audience mechanism, a beginner cannot afford. The phrase "balance of internal and external" anticipates the tension the chapter will meet in Section 26.3 between writing about what interests you and studying what works.

Finally, Koe names what he calls a "huge dissonance" in the objection "why would I do these things if I'm not being paid for it?": "you're not going to be able to get paid for it until you have enough practice from doing it without getting paid." Preferably the unpaid practice is something you like doing. If you plan to be writing every day in the future and getting paid for it, but you are not writing every day now and working your way up in income, "I don't know what you're doing."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

#### Whom to serve: one or two steps behind you

The positive side of the impostor argument is a theory of whom a beginner can help. Its earliest form in the corpus comes from Justin Welsh, in a December 2021 interview. Asked what an absolute beginner's first income stream should be, Welsh says there is something to decide first: "who do they like to spend time with? Because in my opinion, misery doesn't scale. So, if you're spending a lot of time talking to people that you hate talking to, that does not scale." The choice of audience precedes the choice of offer, because a one-person business is built on many hours of conversation with that audience. And the group you like spending time with "should be a group of people that are behind you in their journey." His example comes from sales: "If you're a sales manager talking to account executives, they've never managed people before. They're 2 years behind you in their journey and they want to learn how to become a sales manager." The prompt he offers is: "Where were you 2 years ago? Where were you 3 years ago? That's a group of people that hopefully you enjoy talking to."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

**Misery doesn't scale** is Welsh's phrase, not Koe's, and the "two to three years" is his figure. Koe adopts the logic and reformulates it as "steps." In October 2022: "you need to become valuable enough to sell a product or service to a person that is one to two steps behind you," or to sell something with a use case in other people's lives; you do not need to be the ultimate expert, only ahead of your buyer. In March 2023, in a sharper register: most people think they need the perfect customer avatar, the perfect system, curriculum or coaching structure, a fancy physical product with perfected supply chain management, or prior results, when in fact you need to work with someone in order to get results. The "harsh truth" is that "you need to know more than someone that is one step behind you." In another March 2023 video, he says that if you have done any self-development, you can help "someone that's right behind you."

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md; The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md; Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md

The same 2023 passage connects this to a definition of business. Business is solving problems for others in exchange for a valuable resource, today money, in the past barter and the exchange of goods: "products solve problems... that's what business is." The logic follows that the more problems you have solved, the more profitable your business can be. But that does not mean you lack the experience to help now. Koe recommends teaching from the start, because teaching helps you solve your own problems better, gives you more experience, "pulls minds together," and opens up more problems to solve, especially ones that are not directly personal to you.

#### Why the nearby teacher is better: the fat personal trainer

The most developed version of the argument is a metaphor from February 2024. Koe used to think that overweight personal trainers were silly. Peeling back the layers, he changed his mind. The average person would not want to be trained by an Olympic bodybuilder or a super-fit guy who is "probably neurotic about their health and unhealthy because of that." The gap between where they are and where they want to be is so large that anxiety and overwhelm make them quit early. With someone one or two steps ahead, they would stick with it longer, for two reasons: they relate more, and they understand that the other person is improving along with them. The relationship becomes less transactional, more like an accountability partner who is paid for the value exchange (one plays the business, the other the consumer). His conclusion is that students get better results from people one to two steps ahead, and he translates it into his own field: "It doesn't matter if you aren't a productivity guru with a billion dollar company; it does matter if you can focus for 2 hours and help someone who can only focus for 15 minutes."

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md

The July 2024 version adds weight-loss arithmetic: "If you've lost 10 lb you're probably going to be the better teacher than the person who is super jacked trying to help an overweight person lose weight, because the person who is just a few steps ahead of them, they can relate so much closer." With the bodybuilder, the beginner feels awkward and will not want to show up day after day: "they want a friend, they want connection, to be able to grow along with them."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

This is Koe's argument from experience and observation, not a cited finding from educational research; he offers it as a reason, and it is persuasive as such. It also links to the idea of "the Gap" above: the same distance that makes a beginner feel unqualified is what makes an expert a worse teacher for beginners.

#### How far ahead? The numbers vary

The distance Koe recommends is not constant. Justin Welsh speaks of two to three years (2021). Koe says "one to two steps behind" (2022, 2024), "one step behind" (2023, 2024), "someone that's right behind you" (2023), and in August 2024 "one to three steps behind." In March 2023, in a video on courses, he gives a different but related number: people do not learn best from someone ten steps ahead; "they learn best from someone that is two steps ahead of them," who has freshly solved the problem. That is why he usually creates his courses when he is two to three steps ahead of who he once was, right after he starts seeing results, and then iterates; he positions the product for someone two steps behind him at that point, so that it remains evergreen rather than being taught from the very high level he reaches later.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md

The two numbers can be reconciled, and the reconciliation is useful. "One step behind" is the *minimum* condition for being able to teach: you only need to know more than someone. "Two steps ahead" is the *ideal* distance from the student's point of view: close enough to relate, far enough to have a solution. The variation in figures depends on the context (content, a course, a service) and on the year, and Koe himself does not present it as a change of position. The reason he gives for proximity is constant: closeness makes identification easier and removes the anxiety of the gap.

#### Three things the productized did, and two more false beliefs

In December 2022 Koe summarizes the path of everyone who has "productized themselves," turned their skills and interests into a product. They did three things: they improved their quality of life through personal and professional development; they learned the necessary skills and interests along the way; and "they started posting online regardless of how basic they thought their information was."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md

The third item is the one the impostor objection blocks, and it is the only one that is public. Koe adds two more false beliefs elsewhere. The first concerns time. In a January 2025 conversation with Sahil Bloom, he says that "a lot of people got this false idea in their head that... in order to start working 40 to 80 hours a week on the thing that they want to build, they have to just start working 40 to 80 hours. When people don't have that time, they think that an hour is too short in the morning or 30 minutes is too short." What they need to realize "is that everyone starts that way. There are things you can do, and it's just up to you to find those things." This is the time-budget counterpart of the one-hour day described in Section 26.1.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

The second concerns other people's timelines. In March 2023 Koe describes a case he sees often: someone with a job, children and a spouse wants to start a business, sees people with few responsibilities flexing business success within six to twelve months, and adopts that expectation without considering their own context. They then build explanations for why they cannot do the same and how life is unfair because of their job, children or spouse. What they fail to face is reality: they did not take the opportunity earlier, and they cannot go back and change that. The sooner they accept reality and their specific situation and open their mind to the opportunities available, the sooner they perceive their situation through a more useful lens and act, even if progress takes much longer than for someone who started earlier. "The sooner you start the sooner you can suck less," and the sooner you see results, even if the time horizon is somewhat longer. Rather than adopting someone else's online goal, he advises zooming out, creating your own, and then looking for aligned mentors, education and advice for your specific situation, which "can't be prescribed or given to you."

**Source:** Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md

This case qualifies the benchmarks of Section 26.1. They describe what is possible for someone who can devote focused time; they are not a standard against which a person with very different constraints should measure their worth.

#### Uncertainty is signal

The last piece of Koe's answer to the beginner's fear comes from a September 2025 list of twelve rules for changing one's life. Rule nine is that **uncertainty is signal, not noise**. "You're supposed to feel lost. You're supposed to feel overwhelmed. You're supposed to feel like you have no idea what you're doing. Everyone feels that way." Did you expect all knowledge and skill to be deposited in your head the moment you started? "When you commit to excellence, you commit to a life of uncertainty because you commit to a life of learning. Your potential is determined by the amount of uncertainty you're willing to embrace." The most successful people "don't perceive uncertainty as something dangerous," because "all outsized gains lie in their ability to embrace, manage, and extend uncertainty." And the contrast: "The certain life is the least rewarding. A job is certain. Your paycheck reflects that."

**Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md

Placed at the end of this section, the rule reframes beginner hell itself. The feeling of not knowing what you are doing, which the 2025 video describes as the defining misery of the phase, is not evidence that you are on the wrong path; it is what being at the start of any learning curve feels like. What matters, as the next section shows, is whether that uncertainty is used to gather data or merely endured.

### 26.3 Validated Content

#### Two levers, and everything else is a distraction

The May 2026 video in which Koe compresses growth into two habits begins with the claim that organizes the rest of this chapter: "Two levers. That's what we need to know. Treat everything else as a distraction. Literally just focus on these two things." Lever one is "do what works from your own perspective," which he also calls **validated content**: "study what works and create your own opinion on it." Lever two is networking: making friends so that they share your content. The relationship between the two is practical. If the algorithm does not pick up your validated content, "which it probably won't, especially if you're a beginner," then "you have a manual way of making friends and having them share it to their audience so that it can at least get kick-started." And he insists on their nature: "these are not levers that you guess at. They're just levers that you pull."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Koe's term "validated content" has a family of variants across the corpus (validated ideas, validated structure, ideas "validated by the market"), which designate the same frame from 2022 to 2026: content, a topic or a structure that has already demonstrated that it works, redone from your own perspective. The phrase **do what works from your own perspective** appears only in this 2026 video, as the name of lever one.

The same video gives the levers a concrete time budget. "I would recommend prioritizing it. I would recommend time blocking it for 30 minutes on your calendar, so that you actually sit down and do it." The first thirty minutes "should be for researching, validating posts, and writing out ideas, and actually drafting the posts. And then another 30 minutes should be reaching out to people to get those posts shared." One hour a day, split evenly between the two levers, is the 2026 equivalent of the one to two hours of the 2024 "six things."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Koe adds a diagnosis of why beginners resist this. Introducing lever two ("you're going to hate this word"), he says that most beginners are "probably bringing consumer behavior over." They are used to scrolling and seeing posts do well, and they think "all I have to do is start creating posts and then one will do well and then I'll become famous." What they do not see is "that a creator treats it as a business because that's what it is at this point." He immediately qualifies the word: "a business doesn't have to be soulless. It can be very purposeful." For him, it has been "the most purposeful thing in my life" and led to drastic changes. And he defends selling: the thing sold, "preferably for a specific person, is valuable and it's worth paying the money and preferably it would be worth more than what you're charging." "It's called voluntary exchange."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The distinction between **consumer behavior** and creator behavior links this section to the consumer/creator opposition of Chapter 25. The consumer sees only finished results (the viral post) and infers that success is a lottery; the creator sees the process that produced them, research, iteration and distribution, and treats it as a craft.

#### Nobody is on social media to see something new

The first lever rests on an observation about why people consume content. A common beginner problem, Koe says, is "Oh, I don't have anything new to say." "As if people are on social media to see something new. They're not." Look at how you and he consume: "people are there to see what's already being talked about from a specific creator's opinion." This is the core of lever one, which "doesn't sound that great just yet, but I promise like this is the secret." And: "If you actually treat it as a priority, it will become a priority."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Later in the same video he restates the lever as a definition and a warning against complication: "The first one is just validated content. Study what works and create your own opinion on it. It is that simple. That's how you start practicing writing good content. Please do not over complicate that." And in the recap: "We learned about validated ideas. We learned how to find those. We learned that if you create your own opinion on them, you're setting yourself up to do really well."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The observation does two things at once. It dissolves the beginner's fear of having nothing original to say, which is a variant of the impostor syndrome of Section 26.2. And it moves the creator's contribution from the topic to the perspective: what a reader follows is not a subject but a person's opinion on a subject, which is the thesis of "you are the niche" in Part VII applied to individual posts.

#### From "write what interests you" to "research what works"

This position marks a shift of emphasis in Koe's teaching. In October 2022 he said "post the things that are interesting to you"; in February 2023, "write about whatever you want in a way that's interesting." In 2026 he says: "The best creators don't just post what's on their mind... Some do. I do sometimes. You should. But... that's not a strategy." The reason: "If you're treating social media as a way to pursue your life's work, then business is a part of that. Practicality, strategy, tactics." So "the best creators, most of the time, research what works and base what they're going to create about on that."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Koe does not present this as a retraction. He admits that he himself sometimes posts what is on his mind and says you *should* too. What changes is the criterion: one's own interest stops being sufficient on its own and becomes one side of a balance between art and business, informed by market research. The two positions can coexist according to the goal: expression for its own sake, or a body of work that also has to sustain an income. Elsewhere Koe describes this balance as the intersection of what performs and what excites you, and warns that artists who ignore the business side end up "starving."

#### The recommendation mechanism

The 2026 video gives a structural reason why validated content works, which Koe calls the **recommendation mechanism**: "That's literally how the algorithm works, especially YouTube. If you watch one video on a specific topic, YouTube is going to show you the same freaking topic from another creator." Therefore "if you create a video similar to one that has already done well, then you're going to be recommended to the people who viewed the one that did well." "That's YouTube growth in a nutshell. Every other course, all the other tactics are secondary. They're useful, but they're secondary." And he generalizes: "This is the single most fundamental aspect of social media growth on any platform."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

This is a significant development in Koe's view of the algorithm. In 2023 he recommended starting on platforms such as X, LinkedIn or Instagram, where growth can be controlled through networking, and avoiding TikTok or Shorts, where you depend on the algorithm; he spoke disparagingly of being a "slave to the algorithm." By 2026 the algorithm has become a mechanism to be exploited deliberately with validated content, though without abandoning the manual lever of networking, and in the same video he says that on Twitter the algorithm is "kind of non-existent," so that the manual lever matters more there. He gives no explicit reason for the change of emphasis. A plausible reading is that the two positions describe different kinds of platforms, recommendation-driven ones such as YouTube and "networkable" ones such as X, but that reconciliation is an inference.

His illustration is the case of Craig Perry, who, Koe says, "is just running my playbook": his YouTube bio is "literally reading my newsletters to a camera" (Koe notes that he too reads his newsletter as an outline to riff off, not word for word). Perry's first two videos ever got 230,000 and 144,000 views, "literally absurd... you do not see that for the first two videos." Did he get lucky? "Maybe." Would copying them one to one work? "Maybe. Maybe not." But "if you do this enough times, you will start to see a green streak of engagement and followers coming in." The first video was titled "How to remember everything you read": Koe has made almost the same title, so has Ali Abdaal, and a YouTube search shows ten to twenty versions. The second, "How to become dangerously self-educated," is fairly unique as a title but uses a validated structure ("how to become dangerously articulate," "dangerously confident"), "a rather new structure" that "still shows that it works."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The case distinguishes two things that can be validated: a **topic** (how to remember what you read) and a **structure** (the "how to become dangerously X" template). Either can be borrowed; what Perry adds is his own experience and opinion. The "green streak" is Koe's image for what accumulated repetitions produce: not one lucky hit but a sustained run of engagement.

#### Proven topics, your interests, and the reset period

How does one take a validated topic and make it one's own? Koe's 2026 example is the topic "how to get ahead of 99% of people." "Anyone can take the topic how to get ahead of 99% of people and just think, how would I get ahead of 99% of people based on my interests." It could be business ("start a business like this to get ahead of 99% of people"), finance ("budget like this"), or relationships ("how to get ahead of 99% of men," since young men "compete against each other in looks max all day and don't really get anywhere with it").

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

He adds a caution that complicates the idea of a "proven" topic: "It's a proven topic, at least for now, until it gets ran through." "You'll eventually learn that some topics that used to do well are kind of just dead now, or they've been exhausted to the point where they need a reset period." "How to get ahead of 99% of people" will "start to come back up in maybe a year, 2 years, and they'll flood the space, will be super viral for a second, and then it'll die down." The **reset period** is his term for the time an exhausted topic needs before it can go viral again. It turns validation into something dynamic: a topic's proof is valid for a time, and part of the research is noticing which topics are currently fresh, which are saturated and which are returning.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

#### Stealing like an artist: what can and cannot be taken

If lever one consists of doing what already works, the obvious objection is that it is copying. Koe answers it on several levels, and the answer has a history.

In the 2026 video he addresses the objection directly with Craig Perry. Are Perry's videos the same script as everyone else's? "No... He's pulling from his own experience, from his own perspective. It's his own opinion on how to do it." "Nobody has a novel idea. Novel ideas are extremely rare." He then draws a line. A topic or process such as "how to remember everything you read" is "a process. That's not something that you can claim." But "if it's... a unique category or a unique concept that you've created, then, yes, it is kind of copying if you take it." Semantics matter: Koe's own "4-Hour Workday" is a spin-off of Tim Ferriss's *The 4-Hour Workweek*, with his own framework, and if others took it, "I wouldn't be very mad because... I can't claim that." For tweets and reels, "nobody can claim the idea," but "the way that they worded it, the way that they structured it can be considered theirs." Copy-pasting with a few words changed makes you "look like an idiot," and Koe labels people who do it as those who "can't think originally." "But you can take the idea." His general rule: "Use your own judgment... if you're obviously just purely copying, then yeah, don't do that. But steal like an artist is a thing for a reason. It's because that's just how nature and reality works. You don't reinvent the wheel."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The line can be summarized in a table, which reconstructs the distinctions Koe makes in this passage.

| Can be taken | Cannot be taken (or must be credited) |
|---|---|
| Topics ("how to remember everything you read") | A unique concept or category someone coined |
| Processes and common methods | The exact wording of a post |
| The idea behind a tweet or reel | The specific way a post was structured, if copied wholesale |
| A validated structure or template | Direct "rips" with a few words changed |

**Complementary context:** "Steal like an artist" is the title of a 2012 book by Austin Kleon; Koe uses the phrase without naming the author. The 4-Hour Workweek is Tim Ferriss's 2007 book; elsewhere in the corpus Koe says he never read it and misread its title as "4-hour workday," which became his own concept (Part V).

In August 2026 he states the underlying philosophy more provocatively: "Nothing is original, nothing is authentic. It honestly peeves me when people get in little arguments on social media of like 'Oh, you copied my idea.' But you're just looking through the lens of specificity." He builds a rhetorical chain: Did they copy your niche? You write about the same topics. Did they copy how you walk? "You both walk, you're both human, you both speak the English language. Did you come up with that all on your own? Did you come up with any of the words that you speak? Did you create the clothes that you wear? Have you actually audited the beliefs in your head? How original and authentic are you actually? Why do you treat this as some form of high value that you can stand on to virtue signal?" Then the limit: "I'm not saying that plagiarism is good... but people take it way too seriously... people are allowed to have similar ideas... if it's a direct rip, then yeah, credit the person. But if it's just an inspired idea, welcome to how the world works."

**Source:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md

The **lens of specificity** is Koe's name for the error of looking at a resemblance so closely that one forgets that everything is inherited: language, gestures, topics, beliefs. This is an opinion, stated with some irritation, and it should be read alongside the line he draws above; Koe does not defend plagiarism, and he asks for credit when the borrowing is direct.

The same month he offers a third-party image for the same idea, the bees of Seneca. "A metaphor from Seneca was to gather pollen from many flowers and then digest it into your own honey. That's his way of saying steal like an artist." He jokes that the author of *Steal Like an Artist* may have stolen it from Seneca. And he draws the practical consequence: "This is the antidote to writer's block. You don't have ideas for your writing content or videos because you don't have a repository of ideas to pull from." He adds that "every single thing I've said in this video is from other articles, videos, social posts, random ideas, and highlights that I've saved before."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

**Complementary context:** The image comes from Seneca's *Letters to Lucilius* (Letter 84), where he advises imitating bees, which gather from many flowers and transform what they gather into honey, as a model for reading and writing. Koe uses the metaphor as Seneca's version of the same principle, not as a scholarly reading of the letter.

The metaphor connects lever one with the note-taking and idea-capture practices of Chapter 16 and Chapter 22: validated content depends on a repository of saved posts, highlights and ideas, digested into one's own perspective.

This position has evolved. In 2022 Koe said that "people need to be reminded more than they need something new" and that all content is your version of what others say; at the end of that year he called stealing ideas valid but "overused," recommending synthesis instead, with the warning that copying without perspective does not last. In 2023 he said he had changed the saying to "people need to be reminded with something new." In 2025 he returned to the original saying to defend repetition, on the grounds that new audiences have not seen earlier content and that repetition conditions; and in 2026 validated content becomes "the secret," while the demand for an original perspective grows stronger ("nobody needs another person repackaging common sense"). The change is one of emphasis: stealing moves from a tolerated practice with a risk of commoditization to a central principle, always paired with a perspective of one's own. What is stolen is the topic and the structure, never the wording or coined concepts.

#### How to research what works

Studying what works is itself a method. In August 2026 Koe describes it: "If you're treating this as something that you actually want to do well at, you need a strategy. You need to study what other people in the market are doing... understand what works, and... let that inform your strategy." Use a research tool (he mentions Eden, among others); scroll the discover feed to see what is working; scroll social media and save posts "so that you have them ready when it's time to write." "You curate lists of creators, and you study them. You don't just blindly scroll." And then: "Once you have enough research, you begin to strategize how can you wedge yourself into the market by doing what works, but by doing also what is unique."

**Source:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md

The formula "what works plus what is unique" is the two halves of lever one stated as a strategy. The distinction between studying and "blindly scrolling" is the consumer/creator distinction applied to the feed itself: the same activity, done with a purpose and a swipe file, becomes research.

#### Make noise and find signal

Validated content tells you to begin from what already works. But what works *for you*, with your perspective and your audience, can only be discovered by testing. Koe's name for that process is **make noise and find signal** (2024), later **make noise, double down on signal** (2025). It is his own formula, and it is also his answer to the question of the niche.

The May 2024 version appears as a step in a video on generalists. Most creators struggle because they do not know what to write about. First, just start writing: nobody is watching when you have zero followers. Second, if friends follow you, maybe it is time they learned who you really are and possibly joined you; if they do not like it, maybe they do not really care about you, and it is better to find that out now. Third, write about all your interests: "make a lot of noise and let your audience decide what they want to hear more of from you." A few poorly performing posts are simply data. And posting good content is only one aspect of growth: "80% of your social growth comes from actually growing on social media," that is, from a growth strategy and the skill of social media, which is the subject of Section 26.4.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The February 2025 version, step seven of the way out of beginner hell, adds the balance between art and business. You can write about whatever you want; you do not have to be "a slave to the algorithm" if you understand what you are doing and have escaped beginner hell. Make room for both: templated "framework" ideas based on your offer, and ideas you simply like writing about. "Make a lot of noise": write about any idea your heart desires; pay attention to the ideas people resonate with (slightly more engagement, more DMs, more followers); and incorporate them as a consistent part of your content strategy. The conclusion is a rejection of niche selection in its usual form: "You don't need to choose a niche, you need to experiment with ideas until people tell you what niche best suits your relationship with your audience."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### The counterpoint: the "in wedge"

Koe's guests did not all agree. In the January 2025 conversation with the founders of Stan, John Hugh offered what he called a polarizing take: when starting out, do not be as broad as possible. His analogy is memes. Think of all the memes and TikToks you have sent to friends: you do not remember the creators' faces, and of hundreds you might remember one or two "with a subconscious recall." Therefore, "they don't know who you are and they won't remember and they don't care who you are until you consistently show up in a really consistent way." The more specific you are early on, as "the beekeeping guy," "the crochet person," or "a personal trainer for moms over 40 who want more time back after their 9-to-5," the more likely that "if you show up three times you'll be remembered." His examples are real: beekeeping and crochet creators on Stan who make tens of thousands of dollars. His recommendation is to "find an in wedge and differentiate maniacally at that specificity and then expand over time and allow yourself to be more of a full person over time."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Koe's response, in the same conversation, is notable for not choosing a winner. He says, "at the risk of potentially discouraging action," that polarizing topics show that two successful people can do different things and both get results. He agrees that "you are the niche." What he did himself was "one focus area but I make a lot of noise on the side." On Twitter you can post a lot, so perhaps once a week he would post specifically on web design, and talk about self-improvement and mindset here and there, which gave him "wider data points where I can notice more anomalies between different topics and then start to merge them." His reasoning was that web design was what he charged for, "but it's not what I want to do in the future," so he did not want to go too deep into it. His recommendation follows: if you know what to wedge into, do it; for "the hyper polymaths that just don't know, just make a lot of noise, see what works and then stick with one thing for a bit if it does work."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Two more guest voices complete the picture. Hugh himself, asked by Koe what product to make first and what to do if one does not know which interests to pursue, says the second question comes first, "because you can't start monetizing until you actually have a clear understanding of who you are in your value proposition." If you are "a polymath and you have 15 different things, try all of them to a varying degree and you'll see what hits both from a market perspective but also an internal alignment perspective." That identity "can change at any time but initially you've got to do that work." His co-founder Vitali adds a criterion for choosing: approaching niche selection as "here's a range of topics I can talk about, I just want to invest in them next year to create content and digital products" "sounds like work, and anything that sounds like work tends to not work out in the long run." So there is "tremendous weight into finding something that you feel playful about and doubling down into that."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Two years earlier, in March 2023, Dickie Bush had proposed a version that combines both. On Twitter especially, "you can talk about a very specific thing and become known for that thing," and "that's a pretty good strategy in the beginning of just talking about what you're learning, what you're interested in." But alignment with people is discovered when they hear you talk about everything you are interested in, for example in long-form conversations where he revealed his fitness journey. Narrow, in his formulation, is for being known; broad is for finding your people. In the same conversation Koe warned against suppressing off-niche ideas.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md

| Approach | Who proposes it | Starting point | Later |
|---|---|---|---|
| Make noise, double down on signal | Koe (2024–2025) | Write on all interests; let the audience decide | Incorporate what resonates consistently |
| One focus area plus noise | Koe's own practice (described 2025) | One monetizable topic weekly, plus other interests | Merge topics where anomalies appear |
| In wedge, differentiate maniacally | John Hugh (2025) | Hyper-specific identity, remembered after three appearances | Expand into "a full person" |
| Specific to be known, broad to find your people | Dickie Bush (2023) | One specific thing on Twitter | Reveal other interests in long form |
| Try all fifteen interests | John Hugh (2025) | Test each against market and internal alignment | Settle on a value proposition (revisable) |
| Choose what feels playful | Vitali (2025) | Exclude what "sounds like work" | Double down on it |

The tension here is real, and Koe leaves it open. His public position after 2023 is that you should not choose a niche; his operational advice often includes an initial focus, and in late 2024 and 2026 he speaks of "one main skill or interest" to start from, or "one skill or interest you plan to monetize" plus two complementary ones. The most coherent reading, which his own practice supports, is that an initial focus serves monetization and early recognizability, while breadth serves the brand and the long term. Part VII treats the question of the niche itself; here the point is narrower: whichever path one takes, the method of discovery is the same, testing ideas and keeping what the data confirms.

#### Every post is a data point

The empirical core of validated content is iteration on data, and Koe has described its mechanics in many versions. The earliest, from November 2022, already contains the logic. You grow by writing basic, beginner-level content that works, and "it's not just writing something you think is basic: the idea has to be validated by the market." On social media some posts get 10 likes and some get 1,000; take the messages that get 1,000 and write about them more. Over time engagement increases, and you have "a consistent cycle of evergreen content" in your creative process. Then you can sprinkle in other things.

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md

In February 2023 he states the corollary for those who have too little data: if after two weeks you do not have enough data or feedback, you have to post even if it is bad, because "even bad posts, they're data points; good posts, they're data points." Take both and "dance between the two" until all your content reaches new levels, stages or baselines of development, an "evolutionary psychology lens approach to business," as he puts it loosely. Koe's term **data points** names this: every post, good or bad, is information for the next.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The October 2023 version turns iteration into a monthly routine. "With time, month after month, this is going to take 6 months to a year, as all good things." "Every month review your highest performing content: just go scroll through, see which does the best, copy paste the link to another browser... and then start to rewrite them from different angles: make some short, make some long, make some lists, make some the big idea, make a better hook, make a more clear big problem, make a certain actionable list." This is how "you gradually sustain an increase in engagement" and "increase your baseline of the persuasiveness and impact of your writing." And it has a commercial consequence: "it's not about just writing whatever you want, it's about iterating on the best ideas that your audience actually likes," which "points to a product that you should create with that specific angle." You can create any product, "but you need the angle that performs best in order to catch attention and actually sell it."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The **baseline** is the term that recurs in these passages: the ordinary level of performance of one's content, which iteration is meant to raise. The **angle** is the specific framing of an idea; rewriting a winning post from new angles both raises the baseline and reveals which framing an eventual product should use.

The May 2024 version extends iteration into the construction of assets. Review post performance every week to see which ideas did better; look for better writing or more engagement, and save both. Make the most of winners: turn them into longer-form content (a thread, a carousel, a newsletter, a video), rewrite the post from multiple angles, and bake the topic into your writing routine and brand. If a topic keeps doing well, establish authority with a **digital asset**: create a free product, pin a thread or carousel to your profile, and note it as a starting point for a paid digital product. The reason is subtle. A free product contains almost everything you know about the topic, so you can point people to it instead of repeating yourself and boxing yourself into a niche; you are then free to talk about other interests and bake the topic in here and there. His example is a fitness creator with a nutrition program, who no longer needs to talk about nutrition much: he bakes it in so that people become interested in the product or the free guide.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

This is a practical answer to the niche tension above. Validation could trap a creator in their one winning topic; the digital asset lets the topic keep working in the background while the creator makes noise elsewhere.

#### You cannot skip the reps

Iteration requires volume, and Koe insists that a new platform does not exempt anyone from it. In July 2023, when Threads launched, people thought "it's my time to shine." There is slightly more opportunity on a new platform, he conceded, "but you're still at ground zero in terms of your idea generation." Had you written 10,000 tweets, you would grow much faster on Threads; you cannot skip "those 10,000 iterations." "You must post, you need data, you need to refine your ideas." He himself could grow on Instagram from Twitter only because he had written some 20,000 tweets, of which about 100 were the best; he turned those into 100 Instagram posts, and they all "popped off." His rule of thumb: "under every 100 good posts there's a thousand bad posts, and if you don't put in those reps you're [screwed]."

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The case shows validated content working across platforms: the 100 best tweets were already validated, so their translation into another format was a test with very high odds. It also explains why the advice to "branch out" to other platforms (Chapter 25) is conditioned on having validated ideas.

#### Volume or scarcity?

On how much to post, Koe's advice has changed, and the change is best stated plainly. In 2022 and 2023 he recommended three tweets a day; in February 2023, posting even when the result is bad if two weeks have passed without data; in July 2023, one to three posts a day to "get the failures out of the way"; in 2024, two to three short posts a day and one or two long ones a week, and in a September 2024 sixty-day plan, three to five short posts a day, more than the one or two he "normally" advises. He explains that one or two is what he does now, "it's not what I did, I wrote more," and that "ideas beget ideas."

By July 2026 his position is different. More content than one to two hours a day is not needed. Alex Hormozi and others want you to push more and more volume, "but everyone's doing that." For a competitive advantage, do something different: tone it down, increase quality, make things "impossible to ignore." "Add a bit of scarcity to your brand so people aren't just overloaded with it." People then wait for you to post, and when they see something from you they are not used to it: "it's a dopamine hit for them when that's not happening for any other creator." Elsewhere in 2026 he speaks of "one piece of content a week," broken down into the rest of the ecosystem.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

**Scarcity** here is a common word that Koe uses in a specific sense: lowering the volume of publication so that each piece is anticipated. It is distinct from scarcity in pricing. The two positions can be reconciled by stage, and that is the reading the material supports: volume is for the beginner who needs data and validation; scarcity is for someone who already has an audience and a reputation for quality. Koe's own reason for the 2026 view, however, is competitive rather than developmental: if everyone pushes volume, the advantage lies in doing something different.

#### Show up and iterate: the success pipeline

The guests in Koe's 2025 conversations describe the iterative process in their own words, and their versions add something to his. John Hugh, asked for the big-picture steps from "I have this skill set or interest" to followers and income, answered that "it's literally never been easier" and that what is required is "almost just a mindset shift." He supported this with Stan's figures: 60,000 customers who have collectively made close to $250 million, many with stories like his own (an immigrant raised by a single mother, without connections, who started creating content while doom-scrolling TikTok during Covid and asking himself "what value do I have to give"). His conclusion: "it's permissionless now; we have some sort of value to give." The implication is that the barrier is internal, emotion and insecurity, not external access.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

**Permissionless** is Hugh's word here; Chapter 25 discusses the related idea of "permissionless leverage." Hugh then described what he calls "the written formula... literally all someone needs to do," and "the success pipeline that we see for all of our successful customers": "every day show up and iterate." Its components, in order: figure out what content to make (passion crossed with skill); every single day "show up and push yourself to post"; and treat each result as learning: "don't take it personally, use it as a learning however it performs." The steps are "really easy," he argued, and "we actually are the ones who make it harder on ourselves because of the emotion, the insecurity."

He went further, offering a guarantee: "if you sit there and you post for an entire year every single day, like, I guarantee you will be successful, cuz you will start to see the patterns." The mechanism is pattern recognition. Out of, say, 30 posts, 25 or 27 will "flop and get crickets" (and you will be embarrassed), but one or two will do well, and "you just double down on whatever that is." He cited himself, Koe and "everyone that we see" as testaments.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

A few days later, in February 2025, Koe qualified this from his side: consistency alone is not the answer; "you aren't making progress because you aren't doing the right things." The two positions are less a contradiction than a refinement. Both agree on iterating on data and doubling down on what works. Hugh's "guarantee" assumes that the creator actually reads the patterns; Koe's qualification points out that daily posting without reading them is just repetition. Consistency, in the combined view, is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.

Two guest stories from Koe's interviews illustrate the pipeline from the inside. Justin Welsh, in December 2021, described the moment he committed to writing: "One day I wrote something, and it went berserk," getting "three or four million impressions." Two signals converged: "A, I like this. I like to write. And B, like it seems to be working." From then on he stopped video and audio (except podcasts) and "started writing every single day. And my goal was to be a better writer." He argued that the platform was secondary: "LinkedIn is just, you're just typing into a box and hitting send. It doesn't matter if it's LinkedIn or Twitter." What mattered was "the practice of writing every day and shipping and publishing," which taught him "how to create as much attention for myself as possible." At the time of recording he had written every day for about three years.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

Welsh's story is a small instance of "double down on signal": one anomaly, combined with enjoyment, decided his medium. Sahil Bloom's origin, told to Koe in January 2025, is an instance of value as the criterion. Koe noted that Bloom "doesn't sell anything" and asked what drove him at first. Bloom had a high-paying finance job; Covid hit; there was no commuting, less work, no travel; he was bored and had a Twitter account. "All this crazy stuff was happening in the world of finance and business and all my friends were texting me because I was their finance friend." So he tried "explaining these things in simple terms." A few posts went viral: "Maybe I'm creating value for people. I'll keep doing this." He has "always... gotten a lot of energy from feeling like I had value to give others." He is candid that this is not pure altruism: "I like money... I've just always been able to ground that in the desire to create value for others. I have this suspicion that if I can create a whole lot of value for tons of people out in the world, that I'll do just fine financially." He was not planning to quit: "There was no chance I was going to leave a job to go tweet." His book was "the first thing that I'm... selling," after giving "everything away for free for... four straight years."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

Bloom's story also connects with the friend test of Section 26.2: he began by doing in public what he was already doing in private, explaining finance to friends.

#### Nonlinear growth and exponential events

Iteration is linear; its results are not. Koe's term for the jumps is **exponential event**. In the February 2025 video he shows the follower graph of a Twitter user named Jackson, flat or even negative and then "boom, out of nowhere, just a bunch." "That is what I call an exponential event," which "we want to make happen and then replicate over and over again." In the same passage he states his credentials for teaching it: more than 3.8 million followers across platforms, "probably close to 4 million," and over 30,000 people taught.

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

In October 2024 he describes the mechanism in two videos. In one, he generalizes the idea beyond social media: "I call these exponential events, it's the nonlinear progress that you make." In the gym, for example, disciplined periods of diet and training build the most muscle even in year ten. On social media, you try different angles, ideas and experiments instead of trapping yourself in one niche doing the same thing over and over, which he calls Einstein's definition of insanity. You notice one that does well, "an anomaly," and you double down on it. It works for a while (his examples: when Instagram Reels arrived, carousels, different topics); eventually it dies because "the market catches on." You maintain the gain by incorporating it into your brand, and you keep experimenting until the next exponential event. That, he says, is how he grew by 200,000 YouTube subscribers when he first started doing well, and how he reached 1.2 million on Instagram.

**Source:** It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md

**Complementary context:** The saying "insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results" is popularly attributed to Einstein, but the attribution is doubtful; Koe himself uses it with varying degrees of confidence across the corpus.

In the other October 2024 video, this is step "four" (in his numbering) of the six things of Section 26.1, and he calls it extremely important: audience growth is nonlinear; you flatline with little progress for an extended period, then something works and you get viral growth all at once. On Twitter, perhaps one in ten or one in twenty posts brought many more followers; he repurposed and incorporated those to grow consistently. The pattern is slow or no growth, a big jump, repeat, until you integrate what works into your brand, those things come up more often, and you reach a baseline of monthly followers. Then you keep experimenting: "if you don't experiment you're never going to find out what works." He adds a reason to "be the niche": if you choose a niche that is too narrow, one you are not, exponential events are much less likely.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

He then gives three cases from his own growth. On YouTube, growth was slow until his podcast with Justin Welsh on the one-person business did extremely well; he talked more about the one-person business, more videos went viral, viewers flooded into other recommended videos, and he gained 200,000 to 400,000 followers in a short time. On Instagram, two trends came from experimenting. First, carousels did well; he had his tribe share individual slides to their stories on different days (the first slide one day, a middle slide two days later), which "would almost guarantee" the explore page, and he reached two hundred-some thousand followers. Second, animated reels: the first ten or so did extremely well, and "I went to 1.2 million followers in like the course of one or two months."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The figures for his YouTube jump differ between the two videos (200,000 in one, 200,000 to 400,000 in the other); they are his recollections, not audited data. The cases are worth noting for what they show about the two levers working together. The YouTube jump combines validated content (the one-person business topic) with the recommendation mechanism; the carousel jump combines a format trend with networking (the tribe sharing slides on a schedule, which Section 26.5 develops). Koe's term **anomaly**, a post that performs far above the rest, often defined as at least twice the usual engagement, is the signal that exponential events begin from.

#### Sell what's already selling

Validated content has a commercial twin, and Koe treats them as the same principle applied to two objects: content and product. In January 2025 he made the connection explicitly in a metaphor: "Saying the same thing isn't bad because the best advice in business is to sell what's already selling, but with your own twist. So you stick your hand in the river that's already flowing. In business you stick your hand in the niche or the industry that's already making money. You're not going to somewhere where the money isn't and trying to attract it to you. You go where the money is or where the attention is, like on social media, and you plant your own unique flag there with what already works, but the key is to talk about those things with a novel perspective."

**Source:** You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md

The image (a river already flowing, a flag planted in it) applies equally to attention and to money. The principle itself, **sell what's already selling**, is one Koe has stated since 2022, and he calls it "the best single piece of counterintuitive advice I've ever received in my career." The fullest version is the story of 2 Hour Writer, told in December 2022 and repeated almost word for word in a February 2024 compilation. Koe created the course because he knew how profitable the writing niche was in his corner of Twitter: people were "banking like 2–3 million a year" running a cohort on basic writing, not promising money, nothing fancy, just "learn how to write," with good, compelling sales-page copy. He writes every day, so he got in; on Black Friday he made $130,000 in sales in three days. To create it, he went through the competitors' funnels, emails and landing pages and took inspiration from their headlines, the burning problems they discussed, their offer and curriculum structure, the guarantee, the benefits, the FAQs, and the positioning (for example, not being "how to make money online with writing"). That, he stresses, is inspiration, "clearly not just copy and paste": he has his own way and has to articulate it, but borrows the structure and "the why behind why it works."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The Black Friday figure varies across sources: $130,000 in three days in these passages, $139,000 over three to four days in another passage of the same 2024 compilation. It is probably the same event recalled differently; the units do not settle it.

In October 2023 he gives the principle as a remedy for a common failure. People often create a product and see zero results. His fix: "study the people that you already follow and that you know [are] selling the product or service and that it's actually selling; create it, take it, make it better under your own name. Sell what's already selling, stick your hand in the money that's already flowing... don't try to be clever, don't try to create something new, at least for the first product; just create what you know is going to work."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

In September 2023 he stated the same thing more broadly: "You can really monetize anything nowadays." Think of something you would actually want to monetize (not a joke like "dog poop"), look it up, and someone is already selling it: "study them, replicate their business model, do it under your name." "There's a reason you follow 10 to 20 people talking about the same thing online... why can't you be one of them?" If you are not making money, he says, there are three reasons: you have not developed a skill set; you have not created the product; or "you haven't stuck that product in the flowing water of money that's already there."

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

#### Buyers buy again

In December 2023 Koe adds the argument from the buyer's side. One route to a first product is a product or service you would buy, use and benefit from. "A business principle that most people glance over is to sell what's already selling. You don't try to create new markets just yet; you stick your hands in the ones that are already flowing." He suggests questions: what products have you bought recently, which did you use daily, what courses gave you your own results? "Buyers buy again, buyers buy again, buyers buy again. You're a buyer. What have you bought? Sell it, because someone else will buy it. Don't fall for the silly belief that the market is saturated."

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

At the end of that month he called this "the only shortcut in business": "Create a product that you would buy, use and benefit from." You belong to a niche and have bought products in it; if you understand business, you should "skip 50% of the trial and error and sell what you've bought but better." You know it sells, and your personal experience and interests set it apart. The resulting stack is skills, traffic (an audience you built as a creator), and a validated product.

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md

**Buyers buy again** is a principle Koe uses in several versions (buyers of a course move up to one-to-one services; someone who buys one course on a topic will buy more on the same topic). Here it means that your own purchases are evidence of a market. The idea of saturation is answered in Chapter 27.

#### Mother Nature and blue oceans

The negative side of the principle is a warning against originality pursued for its own sake. In February 2024 Koe treats it as "business 101": sell what is already selling, especially when starting out. It is not wise to chase **blue oceans**, opportunities that seem to have no competition, because "there's a reason for that: it's because people don't want it, it's because money isn't flowing there, and you think you're smarter than Mother Nature."

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md

**Complementary context:** "Blue ocean" comes from W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne's *Blue Ocean Strategy* (2005), which advises creating uncontested market space instead of competing in crowded "red oceans." Koe uses the term in its common business sense and argues against it for beginners; he does not engage with the book.

**Mother Nature** is Koe's term for what the market, that is human nature, wants, as opposed to what the creator's ego wants. In November 2023 he puts it as a rebuke: "people don't want what your ego wants, they want what Mother Nature wants, and who are you to act like you know what that should be?" Market demand reflects human nature, not the creator's preferences.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md

#### The consumer's eyes and the creator's eyes, and the limits of copying

Koe adds a perceptual point in September 2024. If you need help designing something to start testing with content, "research what other people in the topics that you like talking about are doing, and you can see exactly how they're making money. It's right in front of you; you're just looking at them as a consumer. So ask yourself: could you create the same thing but better?" His own example shows that "better" can mean ambitious: with Cortex, his software, "we're taking on a massive task of looking at the entire second brain space and creating something better"; "I just like aiming big; we may fail massively."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md

And the principle is not limited to digital products. In May 2022 he said that you can sell anything that is already selling: open a farm and have your followers go to the grocery store, or make and sell refrigerators. Media, the front end, is how you capture attention and potentially sell something of value.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

The principle coexists in Koe's corpus with apparently opposite advice about products. "You can't take someone else's planner, put your name on it and sell it" (2023); if you copy someone exactly, "you can't teach it" (2023); in 2025, "we don't want to take what's free and make it paid. We want to create something new." Koe's own reconciliation works by layers: what is replicated is the market, the marketing and "the why behind why it works" (headlines, problems, offer structure); what is created is the system, the unique mechanism that makes the product one's own. The Cortex example sits at the meeting point: same market, a better product. Chapter 33 develops the product side; here the point is that the logic is the same as that of validated content. In both cases one starts where attention or money already flows and adds a perspective.

### 26.4 Getting Eyes on Your Content

#### The one and only principle

Section 26.1 noted that in every version of Koe's growth system there is one step that "everyone ignores": getting eyes on your content. This section is about that step. Koe's strongest statement of it comes from the October 2023 video on building an audience from zero, where he locates the moment he learned it in his early digital art account (Section 26.6): "this is when I started to understand how to actually grow." His "beautiful images... meant absolutely nothing on their own. I couldn't just post and hope that our Lord and savior the algorithm would save me. I had to get my content in front of people. I had to get my content shared by people with larger audiences than me." And then the generalization: "When you zoom out, this is the one and only principle for social media growth," whether the channel is a post, an X/Twitter Space or being a guest on someone's podcast: "people, people, people, eyeballs, eyeballs, eyeballs."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The ironic phrase **our Lord and savior the algorithm**, which Koe uses from 2024 to 2025 as well, names the beginner's faith that the platform will make good writing famous on its own. **Get eyes on your content** is his name for the principle that opposes it: content has to be put in front of people deliberately.

#### Writing is not building an audience

The principle has a corollary that Koe repeats in many forms between 2022 and 2025: writing content and building an audience are different activities. In January 2024 he put it as a "slight difference" with large consequences: "don't necessarily just write content, instead you need to build an audience." Writing is necessary, but most people stop there; they do not put effort into getting their content shared, making their name known in the space, or making friends who help them grow. He mentions his own higher-ticket program, Cortex University, as one network where serious creators can find each other, but adds that you do not have to pay: you can form and find communities online.

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

A week later he gave it a ratio: "your writing means nothing if nobody sees it." The beginner trap is writing all day in the hope that "our Lord and savior the algorithm will make them an overnight success." "Writing is 20% of the equation, traffic is 80%." Traffic to your profile and writing comes through replies, networking and shares.

**Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md

The ratio echoes the May 2024 claim in Section 26.3 that "80% of your social growth comes from actually growing on social media." These are rhetorical proportions, not measurements; what they express is a correction of emphasis for an audience of writers who over-invest in the writing. In October 2023 Koe explains why he, a writing teacher, teaches social media at all: "that's how you share your writing." It angers him that people neglect it: "there's writing and there's people. Your writing will not get seen without people, and people think they're so introverted that they can't get in front of people. You have to use the strategies that get your writing in front of people." Introversion, in this view, is not an excuse (Section 26.5 returns to it).

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The fullest diagnosis is from February 2025, step four of the way out of beginner hell: "nobody knows who you are." You have good content, hard-hitting ideas, you write daily, and you gain a few followers a day at most. You are "waiting for your lord and savior the algorithm," and even if it would help you, you should act as though it would not, because growth is in your control. "Content is only one small piece of the puzzle when it comes to growth." The bigger part has three components: the number of people who know and like you, who are more likely to share and engage; the number of people who see your post, of whom "almost nobody will as a beginner"; and "the quality of friendships you make with those on the rise, because they'll pull you up as they grow."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The third component is the bridge to networking: the people who will matter are not only the large accounts of today but peers who are growing at the same time.

#### Branding and eyes: the two parts of platform growth

The earliest version of this framework, from August 2022, divides platform growth into two parts. The first is **branding**: what makes a good, compelling profile and what makes people want to follow you; "all of this is human nature and human behavior, it's psychology, it's marketing." The second is getting eyes on the profile: "you can have the best profile and best content out there, but if you don't know how to get eyeballs on your profile then you're not going to grow." And the most effective way to get eyes is people sharing your content with their audience: a retweet, a story share, a Facebook share, a YouTube share, people sharing your newsletter.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md

The same video, reproduced almost word for word in a February 2024 compilation, begins with a warning about ego. Too many people think they are too good to learn social media growth strategies, when growth strategy is "literally the differentiator between whether you do what you love for a living or not." It is not just a matter of posting good content: "if you aren't growing, one, your content probably sucks." Good content is a given. The trap is the "crazy philosopher" who says: "I write things so clever, but nobody likes it, it's a problem with them." Koe's reply: "No dude, you have no idea what people want and you have no idea what an impactful message consists of." "Set your ego aside, because it's not just posting good content."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The weight of the profile in this framework has changed over time. In August 2022 branding is half of growth; in January 2023 Koe asks "does your profile look like it deserves a million followers?" and devotes a day to the profile picture. By September 2024 he says "they're not following the profile, they're following the content," and that the profile should not block you, though he keeps the million-follower test; in June 2025: "forget about your bio and banner for now." His reason is that many creatives get stuck on the bio because it is the first thing courses teach, while trust is created by the quality of ideas over time. The profile matters when optimizing, not when starting.

#### The algorithm is human psychology

Koe's position on the algorithm has a constant underneath the changes noted in Section 26.3. In December 2023 he lists "eyes and effort" as one of the elements of content and says that most creators make the massive mistake of caring about the algorithm and the latest trends, without understanding that the algorithm is based on "human understanding, psychology and mechanics." The principles are a desired outcome, a burning problem and a clear solution or system. Nail those and people will share: "if they don't share it then you aren't as good of a writer as you think you are," and you need more data, testing and experimentation. Creators realize that the algorithm shows content but "don't realize they can control the amount of people that see their content." So you put effort into growth, especially as a beginner: reply to larger accounts so that their audience sees your replies and follows you; network with creators to help each other; potentially pay for help.

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

In October 2024 he extends the point. Content creators see social media and the algorithm as "some formulaic thing" to study and dissect. That is true to an extent, but "you're missing that all of that is shaped by human nature." Going deeper not only lets you understand it better and get better results; it "opens up this world of depth" that lets you find meaning in what most people see as superficial, which, he adds, is true of many areas of life.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

The two claims ("the algorithm is psychology" and "you can control how many people see your content") together explain why Koe can both disparage reliance on the algorithm and, in 2026, call the recommendation mechanism the most fundamental aspect of growth. The algorithm is a proxy for what people want; content that serves human nature is favored by it, and manual effort can supply the first audience it needs to detect that.

#### Leverage people who already have the audience

The strategic consequence is stated most directly in November 2023: "The way you grow on social media is by leveraging people that already have the audience." There are two ways to grow: be a slave to the algorithm, or find, network with and potentially pay the people who have the audience you want. "That's what you're doing with ads anyways": telling Facebook "target this audience, I'll give you money." So, "bypass that, pay less, go to the people that have your audience, have them share your content," and you attract followers who actually want to read your content and whom you can turn into customers over time; "they don't just disappear after the ad disappears." He grounds it in his theory of identity: "you can sell anything to anyone when you create that person, because identities are created through information, AKA content." **Information creates identity** is a concept from Part II applied here to the audience: the content a person consumes shapes who they become, and they attribute their results to the source. Koe dismisses the critics of this approach bluntly: "it doesn't work for them because their brand and content strategy suck."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

**Slave to the algorithm** is Koe's coinage for this dependence. In October 2023 he gives it a second sense: someone who does not understand traffic mechanisms and therefore must always chase the tactic of the day. The two senses converge on one point: whoever does not control how their content reaches people is controlled by whatever changes on the platform.

In August 2024 he illustrates the alternative with arithmetic. You have zero followers and write a post; someone with 100,000 followers shares it; now your audience for that post approaches 100,000, and further reposts and story shares can push it to hundreds of thousands. "This is a skill." "This isn't relying on the algorithm, I'm not a fan of that"; what he favors is building a network, learning to persuade, and offering value you already have to someone else so that they exchange it for their audience. If he reposted one of his viewers to a million people, that person would get his impressions plus everyone in his audience who shared it, and could then promote their newsletter or product to that traffic. He ends with a question for the reader: when your content does not take off, are you blaming the algorithm, or meeting new people with audiences so that you are in control of your growth?

**Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md

#### Other people's audiences

The 2026 version of the argument begins from where followers come from. In beginner hell, especially on platforms like Twitter "where the algorithm is kind of non-existent," you cannot expect the algorithm to launch you. Koe started there, "so I knew that my growth had to take manual effort," and he calls himself "blessed" for it, because "if the algorithm isn't working in my favor, I still have a lever I can pull." His breakdown is almost syllogistic. "You want followers. Followers come from people seeing your post on their phone and then clicking your profile and being interested enough to follow you." "If the algorithm is like the lottery... how can we manually get people to see your posts? How can we start rolling the snowball" until it becomes an avalanche? "Where is the source of followers or people on social media? And it's in other people's audiences." On social media you can only follow, like, repost or comment (bookmarks and the rest "don't matter"): "you engage and you follow. So the followers that you want lie [in] following someone else."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

**Other people's audiences** is Koe's name for this source. The February 2025 version adds the conclusion about what an audience really is. With 500 followers, if someone with 500,000 reposts or story-shares you, "your audience is no longer 500, your audience is 500 and 500,000," with some drop-off. That is how people make tens of thousands of dollars with small audiences: they "generate traffic manually through networking." Beginners think they are limited to their follower count and the algorithm; experts know that they can reach their own followers and their network's followers. They write a post they know will bring followers or sales and have ten to fifteen people share it. And this is not done daily but strategically: "they build up goodwill and act right when the time is right."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Generate traffic manually** names this practice, and the word "goodwill" is important. Shares from a network are not an unlimited resource; they are a relational currency built by giving and spent at the right moment. That is why networking (Section 26.5) is the precondition of manual traffic.

#### The network effect

Koe has a back-of-the-envelope calculation for the potential of this indirect reach. In January 2024: if you have 1,000 followers, each with 200 to 500 followers, each of whom in turn has 200 to 500 followers, you have an indirect network of 40 million to 250 million people who could spread your name ("yes, that math is correct"), if all of them retweeted and shared. Adjust for engagement rate, content quality and whether people even log in, and a "very generous" figure of 10,000 people a month who somewhat know who you are and what you do remains; multiply that by six to twelve months of effort, and "I find it extremely hard to fail in this game."

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

The calculation is an illustration of the shape of the opportunity, not a forecast; Koe himself discounts it immediately. Its point is that a follower count understates reach, because each follower is a node in a further network. **Network effect** is a common term that Koe uses in this sense of "your audience is not just your audience."

He also turns the comparison around, in August 2023: the difference between a 500-follower account and a 500,000-follower account is not only the followers but "the connections that they have." The large account has probably met 50 to 100 high-level people you will never meet in real life, has access to many behind-the-scenes opportunities consumers do not see, texts with people, and has a wide network with diverse skill sets "that you can never fail once you reach a certain point." This is an opinion from observation, and it explains why Koe treats growth and networking as inseparable: the audience is the visible part of a network.

**Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md

#### Traffic mechanisms

The October 2023 video breaks the work of getting eyes into **traffic mechanisms**, Koe's term for the ways people get eyes on content on each platform. Understanding them, together with human nature, lets you "pick and choose and create a strategy that works best"; "test them all and see what works." "Don't fall for the trap of social media gurus telling you one is any better than the other because the algorithm changed"; "the hot topic of the day is always the algorithm." His own example: "I don't even have to post reels anymore... because I understand human nature... what's going to capture or hold and generate attention. I don't need the algorithm to share my content when I'm in direct control of it." Otherwise you are "a slave to the algorithm, always having to study the latest and greatest tactics and never being able to develop a system or feel to your brand." His Instagram, he says, has "an obvious feel," while accounts that post memes, then a carousel, then a video show that they are "hopping on the bandwagon."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

He names four mechanisms, with the platform features they correspond to.

| Mechanism | What it is | What people miss | Koe's rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Replies | Commenting under others' posts | Boring posts don't get replies read; boring replies don't get clicks; small accounts give few impressions | Reply to bigger accounts with interesting content; talk about yourself; "spam replies" over months |
| Quote posts | Reposting with commentary (X quote; Instagram story share with tag) | A bland "this is great" gives no reason to repost or click | Make it targeted; with huge accounts it is a lottery |
| Reposts | X repost, YouTube or Facebook share, Instagram share to story | People repost what they want their audience to see them agree with | Check before posting whether and why people would share it |
| Manual DMs | Direct messages | Slow and difficult; corporate tone kills them | Network in general; don't be a "corporate robot" |

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

Replies are "the most obvious one... low hanging fruit and you have to do it." People read comments, and if yours captures, holds and delivers value on attention, they click your profile and follow. Koe names three things people miss. First, "boring content doesn't make people read the replies": a tweet can get huge numbers of likes while only 100 people open the replies, "because there's nothing sparking controversy or conversation," so choose posts that generate discussion. Second, "boring replies don't make people click on your profile. Stop repeating what was said in the original post. Talk about yourself, tell people how you've experienced the same thing in your life. Talking about yourself leads to the most profile clicks." Third, "small accounts get fewer impressions overall": keep networking with accounts your size, but "be sure you are replying to the bigger accounts with interesting content."

Replies also compound. "Not only does replies help you increase your followers, just spam replies, go reply to everyone. You need to get your name in people's mouths... known over the course of months." "This isn't a one-day thing... this is compounding nature, where I see someone in the replies multiple times over the course of a month and I'm like, oh, there's that guy again... maybe I should DM him, maybe I should follow him... oh, he's replying to this other guy that I really like too." Creators who spend time in their replies start to notice you, and "things just get better when you reply with a personal story so people trust you more."

The advice to talk about yourself in replies sits in some tension with what Koe's guests said in 2025. In the Stan interview, Vitali argued that "me me me" content fails, and John Hugh spoke of moving past the "me me me" of survival mode (Section 26.5). The tension is between sources more than a correction by Koe, and the two can be reconciled by function: in a reply, a personal story shows shared experience and gives a reason to click; as a content strategy, constant self-focus loses the audience. In 2026 Koe himself clarifies that filtering ideas through your story does not mean talking about yourself all the time but translating ideas.

Quote posts mean reposting someone's content with your commentary (on X, a quote; on Instagram, sharing someone's post to your story with commentary and a tag). You write something that captures, holds and delivers value, the creator may see it and repost it, and traffic follows. The problem is "you think you can just post something and be like, oh, this is great": there is no reason for them to repost it, and even if they do, to show their audience the validation, "other people aren't going to click on your profile, it's boring." "Again, talk about yourself." And this mechanism "is more like a numbers game and luck": if you quote someone with 500,000 followers, they probably will not see it, and if they do "it's just by dumb luck." "If you're going to do some quote posts, make sure it's targeted and actually worth your time."

Reposts depend on why people share. "Your content has to be good enough and sharable enough for people to share to their feed." Koe asks for "self-awareness here": "when I repost something it's usually because I want my audience to see what it is that I agree with or resonate with"; "someone's not going to retweet you if they don't want their friends to see what they like." So make content relatable, attention-catching, and "something they want to identify with or be known for." He gives a checklist "before hitting post": "Will people share it? Why should they share it? Do I identify with anything I'm saying? Is it polarizing enough to spark discussion and get comments and boost engagement further?"

The fourth mechanism is "just manual DMs. This one is more difficult and slow, but I highly recommend networking in general," and it is also a way to get reposts. "DMs work best when you're not a corporate robot like most people are on LinkedIn." His anti-example is a parody: "hey, great to connect, I love your stuff, we should have a virtual coffee and talk about the complexity of Zapier automations to enhance the overall experience of your loyal email subscribers so that you can build an incredible digital presence." Done well, "you can make friends online to the point where they're happy to just share whatever you ask them to share." **Corporate speak** (or the corporate robot) is Koe's term for the impersonal register that makes such messages fail.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The mechanisms are meant to be tested over the six to twelve months mentioned in Section 26.1. Koe frames the replies tactic in particular as a beginner's job. In August 2023 he warned that what he does now will not look the same for an absolute beginner. On social media you will need to **reply like a madman** to get eyes on your account; practice writing content that gets shared and brings followers; build a product or service that solves your own problems; and then write longer-form content to build trust and make sales. "I write 30 minutes every morning" makes no sense without a product. Think big picture: dedicate time to one part, automate it, then shift time to the next, until you have a system that sustains everything.

**Source:** Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md

This resolves an apparent contradiction. In November 2022 Koe said he spent less than thirty minutes a day on social media; in 2023 he tells beginners to "reply like a madman" and "spam replies." The two refer to different stages: his current system rests on years of automated and accumulated work, while the beginner's has to be built one part at a time.

#### Earlier tactics: four ways to get shares (2022) and the community pages

Before the traffic mechanisms of 2023, the August 2022 video (and its 2024 reproduction) listed four ways to get shares. The first is networking with similar accounts: Koe is "not a huge fan of engagement groups," but he is a fan of making friends, a group of three to five close online friends who grow together, sharing strategies and each other's posts, "because your offline friends probably aren't doing this, so you need a new friend group." The second is commenting on big accounts' posts: they have a large audience, and a good comment that delivers value and catches attention (where, he notes, you also practice your writing) gets people to click your profile, and if the profile is good, they follow. The third is making your newsletter, or a long YouTube video, so good that it gains traction and people share it with their friends, which builds loyal followers from the start. The fourth, "a less taboo way than you think," is paying for shares: not paid ads (Facebook ads, Instagram or Twitter boosts) but what he calls influencer marketing, paying someone to share your content with their audience, so that those who like it follow. "I don't like shout-outs, I like just pay someone for shares."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In May 2023 he restated it as step six of a writing business: learn to network and grow on the platform. He points to his video on the non-needy networking process, underviewed but "one of the most... actionable and beneficial things you can do." "You're not going to win... the social media game if you don't understand the rules and mechanics." The common error he sees among students of his program Modern Mastery is "I've been writing content and I'm not getting any followers"; but "if you just wrote content and got followers then everyone would be a content creator." You need both good content and eyes on it, and "that is done manually at the start; you do not rely on the algorithm; you network with people and get them to share your content." His list of tactics: readers sharing your writing because it is good; networking with accounts of similar size and growing together; commenting on larger accounts; making your newsletter "so good that people can't help but talk about it"; and joining a community of people doing the same thing. It is not either/or: only good content plus hope will not work, and only networking with poor content will not work either. "If there's a hole or a problem in your brand, fix it."

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

The comment tactic has an earlier source in the corpus. In December 2021, Justin Welsh described "two really simple ways" to get initial traction on LinkedIn, the first being "being underneath large accounts." Because LinkedIn, in his words, "is run by Microsoft and therefore antiquated and doesn't roll out very good features," there was no notification bell for someone's posts. So: find three to five favorite thought leaders; message them, "when do you post? I don't want to miss it"; whenever they post, "get there first." "But get there first with value... add something meaningful underneath their posts." His claim: "You can grow from 1,000 followers to 10,000 followers just doing that." On the show he gave his own posting time (7:15 a.m. Central), and Koe joked that listeners should expect more engagement then; Koe contrasted the Twitter version, commenting under other people's posts or tagging someone in a value post so they retweet it.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

**Get there first with value** is Welsh's phrase; the tactic is his, and Koe adopts it as a variant of the reply mechanism.

Koe's own first experience of leveraging other audiences came from Instagram, and he has told it in two versions. In June 2022: posting his photo edits, he grew quickly through community pages that only posted photographers' work, picked from hashtags and tags, and reposted it to pages of 300,000 to a million followers; if they tagged him, he gained followers. He focused on high-quality images and edits and on getting them in front of people, and that gave him his first 2,000 or so followers. In October 2023 he adds the logic of those pages. In digital art (and still today in business niches, for example Instagram pages of "business mindset" quotes), curation accounts shared other people's art and grew; "since they were choosing the best art to post, they grew faster because they didn't really have to create art; they had a lot more leverage to build an audience," though "that doesn't necessarily equal monetization unless the artists are paying them." Artists could get featured by using the account's specific hashtag (his example is an account called A Game of Tones), by consistently commenting on the account, or by networking: DMing them, using "the non-needy networking process."

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md; How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The curation account is a pure case of the principle that the source of followers is other people's audiences, and also of the observation that reach without a product does not equal income.

#### Free work as a connection tactic

A June 2022 tactic belongs here as an early form of "lead with value." Start with free work: posting content, a call, or making something. If you want to sell social media banners at about $100 each, make one for someone you want to connect with: "hey, I made this for you, man, it's yours for free." They respond happily, you connect, you have given value, and if they have a large audience they may shout you out or retweet you, which grows your audience and can bring potential work.

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md

#### Idea catalysts (2024)

In the October 2024 version of the system, the work of getting eyes takes the name **idea catalyst** (or catalyst strategy). Not all posts go viral, and "there's still such a thing as beginner hell": you have to get over a hump of followers, and you need a way to test whether your ideas are good. The idea catalyst is the means of getting your posts in front of other audiences. He names three.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The first is **pay to play**. Ads pay Facebook or Google to show your post to their audience; Koe would rather pay an individual with a highly engaged audience with whom he wants a relationship. Most people with an audience offer growth services or promotion spots. Being shared by a larger account for pay is "on par with paid ads and paid sponsorships," like sponsoring a podcast or newsletter, except that you are not asking anyone to buy; you are testing whether content does well and attracts followers. The best way is to DM accounts with more than 20,000 followers and ask whether they help with growth or offer promotion. Big brands and very large accounts often do not check DMs (they are not spending eight to twelve hours a day on 300 messages) and charge a lot, because audiences are valuable; some charge $10,000 for ten shares. That, he notes, shows that an audience is valuable even without a product. If you will not invest, fine, but "I'd question whether you're serious about building your life's work": you will spend $40 on a dinner but not on traffic that could return ten times as much with a product. The advantage over paid ads is that you keep part of the audience (follows, newsletter sign-ups) and drive traffic to your product. Those who dismiss it as inauthentic or ineffective "don't know what you're doing." Then the qualification: he has not done it in three years and avoids teaching it, though he did it when escaping freelancing; "you don't need to do that."

The second is building a tribe, which Koe says to do whether or not you pay. It is not an engagement pod into which you are tossed with people you do not care about; "you're trying to inject yourself into a social media tribe." Find accounts around your size, add them to a list or bookmark their profiles, and reply to their content daily with your own insights, stories and anecdotes, not regurgitation or "great post," so as to start real conversations. If you are not naturally social, research social dynamics and how to hold conversations, online included. Move to DMs; ask what they are doing to grow ("hey dude, I'm trying to grow just the same as you"); they will likely share your work as a friend; then propose the arrangement: "if I send you a thread you can send me one back and we'll both share it." With four to six people you have a group: send an idea, they share it, and you have more viral potential, traffic and followers. Consistency then reveals "levers that you can pull for audience growth." **Inject yourself in a tribe** is Koe's term for this practice; Section 26.5 develops the tribe at length.

The third is exchanging non-monetary value. If you have a big Instagram and someone else has a big Twitter: "I'll share you on Instagram if you share me on Twitter." Or offer a service or product to a larger account: "I'll create a landing page for you or write an email for you if you give me 5 to 10 retweets or shares." "Social capital and trading goods is just as viable as trading money for these things." **Social capital**, in Koe's sense, is an audience used as a currency for non-monetary exchange: shares, services, introductions.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

| Catalyst (2024) | Mechanism | Condition or caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Pay to play | Pay individuals with engaged audiences for shares | Accounts over 20K; content must be good; Koe no longer does it and says "you don't need to" |
| Building a tribe | Daily replies to peers, then DMs, then a 4–6 person share group | Real conversation, not "great post"; not an engagement pod |
| Non-monetary exchange | Cross-platform share swaps; services for retweets | Requires having something of value to trade |

#### The strategic post

The February 2025 video turns the tribe into a weekly routine. "If you aren't trying to get one of your best posts shared every single week by someone on social media, then you will not grow," and even if you would, act as though you would not. The process: have conversations every week; research the timeline (not scroll and consume it); reply, keep and start conversations in DMs; from those conversations, collect content ideas, of which there will be many; write a post, a thread (which usually brings more followers), a carousel or a reel; and send it to the people with whom you discussed the topic: "hey, threw this together, thought you'd like it." You can mention them in it so that they benefit and gain followers too, which makes them more likely to repost.

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The **strategic post** is Koe's name for this weekly piece designed to be shared by someone you have been talking to. It joins the two levers in a single act: the conversation produces a validated idea (the person you talked to found it interesting), and the same conversation produces the person who will share it.

#### Spread without effort: being mentioned, and the shared launch

Two cases show what happens when other people's audiences work in your favor without a deliberate request. In November 2023 Koe told of gaining 50,000 YouTube subscribers in about two weeks with no idea where they came from, since his videos were not performing better than usual. Talking to people, he learned: "yeah dude, you're just being mentioned everywhere," in other people's videos and quotes. "These bigger accounts that are mentioning me are just giving me more authority because I help them." In that video he uses the case to illustrate the "spread without effort" factor of what he calls time under attention, the cumulative attention a body of work receives.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The other case is deliberate. In February 2025 Koe described Sahil Bloom's launch of *The 5 Types of Wealth*. Even with over a million followers, Bloom understood that "his audience is only a small portion of the people that he has access to." He worked day and night, not only on content but on getting others to share: he sent the book to people, marked specific spots in chapters, and said "if you could share that would be awesome." Koe posted photos of the pages on Twitter, the post went almost viral, so they told others to do the same, and "it's just taken over Twitter." Do it on a small scale with a Twitter thread, Koe says, and you get a ton of impressions; "if you can't turn a ton of impressions into a replacement for your salary, I don't know what you're doing."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The first case is the result of helping others for years; the second is the deliberate use of a network at the right moment, "acting right when the time is right," as the previous subsection put it.

#### Remixing: borrowing the authority of bigger creators

The 2023 video adds three "strategies of control" over traffic: going from short to long form, remixing, and paid growth. The first is covered in Chapter 24. **Remixing** (or "stealing the traffic") is Koe's metaphor for borrowing the authority and audience of larger creators: "DJs and producers grow by remixing popular songs so they can steal the traffic from that popular song." His example is a remix that went viral "because he was banking off of the back of Taylor Swift's authority"; the transcript is uncertain about the details ("how Crank That remixed... I forget what song by Taylor Swift"), so the specific example should not be relied on. The pattern is clear, and he finds it everywhere: YouTube videos about a popular person take that person's traffic; podcasts invite big guests to grow their audience; and in his own digital art, he would remix an artist's image, post it, tag the artist, and when they shared it, "I leverage their audience and grow," through a repost or quote-post. "The same is true for writers."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

He gives four remix tactics for writers. First, "quote them in a thread or newsletter, use their ideas in your longer post to illustrate a point, then DM them the post saying that you mentioned them"; they may repost without being asked. Second, "commentate on their content," like the YouTube reaction videos that ride a larger creator's traffic. Third, "get people on a podcast, because people just love getting on podcasts": they share it with their audience, and the podcast is also a networking tool to meet and befriend bigger people "and ask them for something in the future," which, he says, "is what I did when I had a guest podcast two years ago." Fourth, "talk about their ideas and credit them," for example in a post on "the best idea I've seen this week," explaining how it affected you and crediting the author, who may share or comment. The tactics combine long and short form; in a newsletter, for instance, you are "slowly helping other people spread their name so they'll help you spread your name."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

Remixing is the traffic counterpart of "steal like an artist" in Section 26.3: in both, one borrows openly and credits, and the borrowed authority becomes a reason for the source to share.

#### Clippable moments

A December 2025 passage applies the same logic to appearing on other people's podcasts, the third remix tactic seen from the guest's side. Studying how guests answer, Koe noticed that "the best speakers don't answer the question that the podcast host asks directly. They don't say 'Um, well, good question. Let me think about this for a bit.' Instead, they just speak their best idea, the best idea that relates to that question, with confidence, and then they expand on it with a few supporting points." The benefits form a chain: it keeps the listener engaged, so the podcast does better, so more podcasts want you, so you have more success. And it creates what he calls a **clippable moment**, a confident, strong answer that can become a viral clip; if the idea has already gone viral, that is, been validated, the clip will go viral again, and success compounds.

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

His example is hypothetical, and he presents it as such ("he would probably respond"). Asked "What's the greatest skill that someone can learn right now?", Alex Hormozi could say "learn sales or offer creation," "but he understands that there are levels to this game." So he would probably answer with his own second most viral tweet: "The single greatest skill you can develop is the ability to stay in a great mood in the absence of things to be in a great mood about." It "has nothing to do with actual skill acquisition that's on people's mind," so it is also a pattern interrupt, and it sets up "a very interesting conversation on a topic that he's probably very fresh on and can talk about non-stop." The tweet has 105,000 likes; clipped, it "is going to pay back tenfold, because it's going to go viral again."

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The clippable moment shows the two levers at work in speech: the guest reuses validated content (an idea that has already proven itself) and borrows the host's audience (the network).

#### Paid growth

The third strategy of control, **paid growth**, is the one on which Koe's position has shifted most, and he introduces it in October 2023 with "this is where it gets very touchy." People who are "super authentic and moral and noble," with no social media experience, brag about not using paid growth. He has observed them for years: "four years down the road, they've either not grown at all or lost followers," becoming "bitter complainers."

He first says what he does *not* mean. Not running ads to gain followers, "the worst thing you can do," after which people lose hundreds of followers a day. Not Instagram shoutout pages ("I started an Amazon FBA company and grew to a million dollars in 5 seconds... follow account, follow account"), which "does not work" for loyal followers; it might produce "churn and burn" clients, but no nurturing and no trust. "Just don't do anything that requires something or someone else to tell people to follow you." **Churn and burn** is his term for growth through shoutouts and ads that brings followers without loyalty or trust.

What he does mean is "a truly authentic post, one that you would normally write, and using a bit of money to get it in front of other people's eyes." "Good content is obviously a prerequisite"; he tried it at the beginning and it worked "because my content was good." The mechanism: you pay someone you otherwise could not reach (they offer it as a service), you network with them, and they repost your post with no ad, no sponsored tag and no "follow him." People who like it like it, those who want to follow do, you nurture them, and eventually some can buy. "To me that's 10 times more authentic than literally any other kind of paid growth," and better than ads relying on "sleazy direct response principles."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

Why pay at all? Because "reality is harsh: large accounts are busy and probably won't do this, AKA they won't share you unless you pay them, because they need a filter." With "hundreds of DMs a day, they're not going to spend 24 hours a day responding"; they "have other things to do that led to those DMs." "You need to offer payment for them to pay attention to you. If you don't like that, don't start a business; if you don't start a business, enjoy the other side, which is 10 times more corrupt. This isn't unfair, it's just reality." The argument concerns very large, busy accounts; the free non-needy networking of Section 26.5 is aimed at peers and friends.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The options are simple: ask them to repost you ("just as simple as reposting or sharing to the story, again no caption, no asking to follow you"), or ask them to reply to you, "if you think replies are better... for however much money instead." There is a platform condition: "this really only works on a platform like Twitter or X; on Instagram the replies don't boost you on the other person's feed; on X, if someone replies to you, their followers still see." The process: "DM them, ask them if they help with growing your brand, take the conversation further and see what they offer... figure it out, test it, waste a bit of money." And his reply to the objection: "if you think it's fine for advertisers to promote a product randomly in your face, then I don't see why you shouldn't think it's fine for creators to accept money to share content" that you will probably like, "without you asking to buy anything"; shared content is less positioned to sell than an ad.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

He closes the 2023 discussion by defending the whole set of tactics against the charge of manipulation. "You either learn to play or you don't." "None of this, ever, in my entire social media career, has seemed as dark or inauthentic as people make it seem"; videos that frame "what influencers do" as "the most evil thing in the world" are wrong; "it's just reality, it's a great thing." It "changed my life," "changed who I'm building a business with," and "almost everything in my life over the past 4 years would not have been possible without any of this," including the lives of people he has helped. "If you don't have the authority of someone who already has all of the eyes, if you're just starting out and you're using social media as a beginner to build your authority, this is what you're going to have to do."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

By May 2026 his emphasis has changed. "This is why brands pay so much for creators to post about their product, because they have an audience." Brands use podcast sponsorships, creator partnerships, Facebook and Google ads, "maybe run ChatGPT ads now," and as a creator "you're practically marketing yourself." "You could technically pay people to share your posts, and that works to some extent if you do it the correct way, but let's not bank on that." To those turned off by it: "that's a very viable strategy... if you have the money to do it, go ahead. I do not care." Calling it cheating "doesn't make sense; they don't have a business mindset," because "people are only going to follow you if your content is good." If someone shares your post and people do not like it, the money is wasted, "the same thing as putting money into Facebook ads and people not buying your product because your product sucks." The conclusion reverses the emphasis without reversing the judgment: "It doesn't really matter if you pay or not, because you're only going to grow if you're good, if you're skilled." And the alternative: "if you're smart, you can get in front of other people's audiences for free."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

|---|---|
| 2022-08 | "A less taboo way than you think"; prefers paid shares to shoutouts |
| 2023-10 | Recommended: "10 times more authentic" than other paid growth; large accounts need a filter; "this is what you're going to have to do" |
| 2024-10 | Valid ("pay to play"), but he has not done it in three years, avoids teaching it, "you don't need to do that" |

The change is one of emphasis: paid growth moves from a recommended strategy to an optional, secondary one. The reason Koe gives in 2026 is that paying does not substitute for quality. The two positions can coexist by context: paying applies to large, busy accounts that need a filter; free networking applies to peers and friends. That is where the next section begins.

### 26.5 Networking: Making Friends

#### Growth is networking

The second lever is the one Koe expects his audience to resist. Introducing it in May 2026, he says: "you're going to hate this word." The word is networking, and he has never liked it himself. In February 2023, in a video devoted to the subject, he says he prefers another name, "I like making friends is a better way to put it," and immediately ranks it: "networking is the pillar of my success." If you ask any creator or one-person business owner what the most important part of their success is, he adds, "they're going to tell you it's networking."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

**Making friends** is therefore Koe's own name for the second lever, used from 2023 to 2026. A month earlier, in January 2023, describing the growth strategy taught in his writing cohort, he had already reduced growth to it: "in reality it's just networking, but how to network properly, build relationships, get new opportunities, whether it be a job opportunity, freelance clients or just meeting great people that'll help you grow."

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

The 2026 video states the same claim in its simplest form: "All of it just boils down to making friends. And I know that sounds simple, again, because it is. But most people don't know how to make friends. Most people in today's world are antisocial. You would think that more people being on social media would be more social, but they're not." On social media, he notes, talking to people happens in DMs or replies, one to one in the DMs.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The observation that social media users are not social has a 2023 counterpart that explains why. Most people approach social media with the mindset "I want to be educated and entertained." "It doesn't even cross their mind that they could reach out to this person and become friends with them," specifically with people "within a similar follower range as them," and then grow together, offering value in front of "potentially 4.9 billion people." Koe adds a judgment about the field: if you are in "this creator game or this one-person business game" and you are not on social media, "you've already lost," whether you use paid ads or a personal brand, and he recommends the brand.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

This is the consumer/creator distinction of Section 26.3 seen from the social side. The consumer uses the platform to be educated and entertained; the creator uses it to meet people. In the same 2023 video Koe says, of anyone who wants to be a creator, solopreneur or one-person business: "you're there to meet people."

#### The vote of approval

Why do relationships produce reach? Vitali, co-founder of Stan, gave the mechanism in the January 2025 conversation. It applies at least to LinkedIn and X, he said, while Instagram may work differently: "if someone gives you their vote of approval, which is like a comment, like any form of engagement, then you're effectively getting exposed to their audience." If your content is "authentically good," more people look at you and decide whether to follow or move on. So, besides creating ("content is bread and butter"), there is other work: figure out "who are the people that you associate with," "learn to engage and be present around their content," for example commenting as soon as they post, and "find your group and grow with it."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

**Vote of approval** and "find your group" are Vitali's phrases. Koe followed them in the same conversation with his own early experience, saying that building a tribe is "the one thing that a lot of creators miss out on," and what keeps them from initial success. They think "content creator, write content, that's all I do," and watch small fluctuations in likes and views, but miss the question "who is a part of my greater circle, and am I known to these people, are they going to share me, are they going to support me." On early Twitter, he says, "I wasn't replying to people just to reply to people, I was replying to them to form this tight-knit tribe of people that could support me; we could share strategies."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

#### Injecting yourself into a tribe

In February 2025 Koe develops the tribe into a method and attacks a common piece of advice in the process. "Reply to large accounts" is "half-baked." What you actually do has three parts. First, write quality replies, not "great post" or ChatGPT restatements, so that people have a reason to visit your profile and follow; the Ten Commandments of Engagement (Chapter 23) apply to all writing, replies included. Second, become well known to a tribe. To break into, say, the startup or tech circle, reply to ten great accounts there "by adding value and personal experience." Their consumers get used to your face and follow "after they see enough, not after my first reply." People unconsciously map who is friends with whom in order to determine who is important in a network, and you have to become part of those connections consistently, which takes time. Third, replies are **seeding connections**: the more you reply and DM within a niche group, the easier it becomes to DM its members or have them DM you; they engage more and "feed you opportunities."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The observation about mapping who is friends with whom is an interesting addition to the reply mechanism of Section 26.4. Replies do not only expose you to an audience; they place you visibly in a social graph, and being seen repeatedly in conversation with recognized people lends you some of their status. **Inject yourself in a tribe** names the whole practice.

#### The mastermind: from engagement groups to tribes

The tribe has a history in Koe's thinking that runs through a term he once disliked: **engagement groups**, groups of creators who agree to engage with or share each other's posts. His position on them changed, and the change is worth following step by step.

In August 2022, as Section 26.4 noted, he was "not a huge fan of engagement groups" but was a fan of a group of three to five close online friends who share strategies and posts. By October 2023 he had reframed them. "Harsh truth: every creator you know is in a group chat with other creators." They talk strategy, have fun building businesses, share and help each other grow, and "use the traffic mechanisms to create a strategy they can bake into their routine": some comment on each other's posts every morning, others repost what is shared in the group. "Most of all, they form a tribe and start to identify as a part of a group"; you can see "tribes of 5 to 10 creators that all talk to each other online." People call these engagement groups; Koe calls them "a mastermind, because that is a pattern in reality: shared minds work better than a singular mind." He attributes the concept to Napoleon Hill, with a hedge ("I believe, I think that was him"): "a shared mind working towards a singular goal." It need not be "superficial transactions"; you can meet "lifelong friends." And if a group is nothing but "you share this post, I'll share this post" and you do not care about anyone in it, "leave, go and form a new one... iterate on everything in business."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

**Complementary context:** Napoleon Hill introduced the "Master Mind" in *Think and Grow Rich* (1937) as the coordination of knowledge and effort between two or more people working toward a definite purpose. Koe's attribution is correct, though he states it tentatively.

In March 2023 he had already used the same concept in a different direction, applying it to the audience rather than to peers. Napoleon Hill's mastermind, he explained, is in essence a group of two or more people with a shared purpose who, working together, can actualize that purpose "10 times faster than they would be able to alone." His adaptation: you build a community of people with the same purpose; what you attract them to is your vision or purpose for the future; and by combining minds you help others reach it much faster. Your audience is the community you serve, and in the creator economy each member will in turn have their own community if they lean into their entrepreneurial nature. You distill your knowledge, expertise, experience and skills for them, to help them reach the goals you set for them: you become an educator. In May 2024 he made the mastermind a step toward self-confidence (below).

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md

In September 2024 his tone toward critics of engagement groups hardened. Critics who say "don't pay for growth, don't join an engagement group" often say, in the same breath, "if you want to meet smaller accounts, comment on my post," turning their own post into an **engagement farm**, knowing that the small accounts get nothing from it. "Take matters into your own hands, make friends, call it an engagement group if you want, share each other's stuff," like working on homework with classmates, building projects together, business partners, or a marketing person who helps you grow. In the same 60-day plan he adds that "the algorithm probably isn't going to make you an overnight success. Most people don't realize that social media growth comes from manual effort and skill," and that once you realize this, growth is no longer "a game of luck for popular people." You do not need a large audience, he says, but something like a thousand true fans (the transcript reads "a thousand true"), and even small audiences make $100,000, $500,000 or a million a year. Traffic without the algorithm comes from replying to big and small accounts, DMing people to make friends and grow together, and writing threads that tag people so that they have a reason to repost.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Complementary context:** "1,000 True Fans" is an essay by Kevin Kelly (2008), arguing that a creator can make a living from roughly a thousand people who will buy everything they make. Koe uses the idea without naming its author in this passage.

In October 2024 he went further: engagement groups have become "the weird child of the creator economy," seen as bad. Zoom out. In school you worked with friends on homework and strategized to achieve a goal; in business you find partners, reach out to your network for advice, and help each other grow. That is what a "quote unquote engagement group" is. His analogy: going into a Call of Duty lobby, arguing with the other team, communicating and helping your own team do better. It does not have to be shallow. "A requirement for your success, not only online but in any area of your life, is to make friends that you work with to grow whatever it is that you're doing." "The lone wolf mentality doesn't work in a social setting." The **Lone Wolf mentality** is his term for the belief that one can grow alone.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

In the same video he adds a qualification that keeps the tactic from becoming cynical. Another creator behavior is commenting under large accounts not because one likes the ideas but to get those accounts' followers to follow. Koe says such things "aren't bad," but that for almost everything discussed "we're missing the other half of the perspective... the meaningful part."

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

And in the October 2024 description of the tribe as an idea catalyst (Section 26.4), he insists that "it's not an engagement pod" into which you are tossed with people you do not care about, but a tribe.

#### Introversion, social skills and the group chat

The lever that Koe calls "the pillar" of his success is the one most beginners feel least equipped for. He is direct about it. In October 2023: "Yes, you're going to have to DM people, yes, you're going to have to develop some social skills, yes, you're going to have to disidentify with your misinterpretation of what an introvert is, so that you can actually achieve something great in your future." He apologizes for getting heated: after three or four years, "I've seen every objection in the book," and closed-minded people "swear off anything that you may think is [in]authentic because your mind is so closed and narrow."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The verb "disidentify" comes from Koe's theory of identity (Part II): a self-image such as "I'm an introvert" can function as an identity that is defended, and the work is to stop treating it as a fixed fact. His own story is the evidence he offers. In February 2023 he opens the networking video by saying he is "an introvert at heart." In college he was never the first to engage in any social or classroom situation; even the friend group he still has formed because they "stumbled upon each other in the dorms" and the others approached him ("hey, you guys want to go walk to lunch?"). Speaking in front of the class or going to networking meetups to meet new people "turned me off in every single way." All he wanted was to sit in his dorm room, play video games and work on the side businesses he was pursuing. He frames this as his "before" state: before he realized that being introverted is not the same as being antisocial.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

In February 2025 he gives the skill its rank: it is "a shame that this has to be taught," since social media has made us socially awkward and social skills were never taught in school, but "social media social skills are arguably the most important skill you can learn to succeed there." People fear sending a DM as much as saying hi to a stranger on the street (when, he asks, did you last do that?), and both are "as simple as finding a shared interest to form the connection around."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

In December 2024 he offers a metaphor that lowers the threshold. Do it with writing, so that you do not need video editing, audio editing or graphic design; anyone can write a post and share an impactful idea, just as you text your friends daily. If you do not have self-improvement-minded friends, get on social media to find them; it is not all toxic, and there are people in comment sections you can befriend and build a business with. Act as if you were texting or DMing those friends: "think of social media as a big internet group chat, but those group chats are different niches." The self-improvement space, in his sense, umbrellas everything valuable, including business, but not memes, entertainment or influencer content such as showing off one's body; even fitness people can simply write and educate.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The **big internet group chat** is Koe's image for the platform as a set of conversations among friends divided by niche, and it connects writing with networking: a post is a message to the group, a reply is joining the conversation, a DM is stepping aside to talk one to one.

#### Give, give, give: neediness is sensed

The adjective in "non-needy networking" has a source in an earlier lesson. In June 2022, the sixth of his lessons from seven failed businesses was "give, give, give." You will not make it if you *need* things, whether money, validation or anything external. If you think about money the entire time, and are dependent on and afraid of losing money or a client, you do worse work, and "people are going to sense your neediness." So get into the habit from the start of giving free value: DM people with "hey man, I saw you were working on this, here's a few tips I wrote up," or "here, I built a landing page for you," or offer them a call and consult them for free.

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md

**Neediness** here is a common word with a specific sense: the perceptible dependence on an outcome that makes a person less attractive to work with and makes their work worse. The phrase "give, give, give" appears in Koe's corpus with this meaning; John Hugh uses a similar phrase in 2025 for a content philosophy of solving someone's problem first.

#### Building an audience like launching a product

In May 2026 Koe compares the start of networking to a product launch. "When you launch a product, that's practically what you're doing in the first place to kick it off the ground": reaching out to see whether people like it, whether they will promote it (for money or not), or use it. "If they do use it, boom, you have a user. You think one user isn't a lot, but if you compound over time, and then word of mouth kicks in, and then you start growing organically. The same thing works for building an audience."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The analogy makes networking the equivalent of the manual early-user acquisition of a startup, and it implies the same expectation: the first gains are small and slow, and compounding comes later.

#### DMs: how you get in front of people without an audience

Before the step-by-step process, Koe's 2023 video establishes why direct messages matter and how not to use them. Everyone starts without an audience, and "DMs are how you get in front of people and you start making connections." Freelancers understand this for landing clients, but the average person trying to grow on social media does not. He has seen people land job offers through DMs, so the point applies to anyone advancing a career, not only to business; and he has seen tweets from accounts with few followers go viral because their authors were friends with a large account that shared them. People fall into two camps: those who do not understand "the sheer power and network effect of DMing people on a consistent basis," and those who do not understand that "you can get large accounts to share your post if you are strategic enough."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

Then the rules. "From here on out, never ever... send a DM" that starts and ends with "hi" or "hey, how are you"; that asks "can you follow me back"; that is a straight sales pitch with no prior connection; or that is a long block of text that takes too much time to read, especially if the sentences are not spaced out. You must approach DMs tactically, above all with big accounts that receive ten or more DMs an hour.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

He explains why from the receiving side. He gets ten or more DMs every hour on almost every platform and physically cannot reply to all of them. Replying is not one message back: usually there is a question, he answers it, and "the DMs compound," so if he started answering, the number would go from ten or more to forty or more an hour. People with large followings are "just skimming through DMs to see if they're missing anything important," so your DM works "like an email subject line or a hook for a piece of content or a headline for an article": you must capture attention with what they can see in the preview and be strategic about what you say.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

The analogy to subject lines and hooks links networking back to the craft of Chapter 23: a DM is a piece of writing that has to win attention in a preview, under the same conditions as a post in a feed.

The habit he recommends is daily. "You should be reaching out to people every single day." If you want to be a creator, solopreneur or one-person business, you are in the space to learn, to produce and to meet people: "that's exactly it, you're there to meet people." If you like someone's post, DM them and tell them; if you like someone's reply, like it, engage, and say "hey man, I love that reply on the post." "It's so easy to just send a DM to thank people for what they're doing online."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

He also gives DMs an economic frame, in October 2023. DMs can be used for lead generation, "but obviously there's diminishing returns": "your effort is much better spent when you're selling a high-ticket service when you're doing cold outreach, because that's a lot of time invested and the payoff is a lot better." Selling a $2,500 service with a decent number of DMs beats sending the same DMs to sell a $150 product, which in turn beats sending them for "one impression on my post," "if I treat my post as my offer in the DMs, because technically a post is an offer... and the conversion of that offer is engagement."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

**A post is an offer** is Koe's coinage here: a post asks the reader for attention and engagement in exchange for value, and engagement is its conversion. The frame explains why DMs used purely to get impressions are a poor investment and why the networking process aims at relationships rather than individual shares: a relationship makes many future shares possible for the cost of one effort.

Finally, in May 2026, a warning about patience: "I know that DMing other people is just like this non-tangible strategy. Like, it just doesn't result in an immediate direct outcome as feedback, but I promise if you do this, like, this is the greatest advantage you can have."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

#### A smart social game: the Dickie Bush case

Koe's favorite illustration of a DM done well is one he received. In 2021 Dickie Bush, who already had many followers, reached out after Koe posted about his fitness progress, specifically about switching back to a high-carb diet to see its effect on mental clarity. Bush asked questions, shared what he was doing, and the conversation flowed naturally. The mechanism, as Koe describes it, is that Bush took what Koe had written publicly and "acted as if I was talking to him," continuing the conversation in the DMs by linking the tweet and writing his thoughts. Five days later he sent Koe a thread: "threw this together after a bit of reflection, appreciate you bringing the positive energy around here." Koe, who had a decent following, reposted it, and Bush knew he would. "He's just playing a smart social game, and there's nothing wrong with that; that's what we're all doing when we're making friends."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The **smart social game** is Koe's name for this pattern: continue in the DMs a conversation someone started in public, and then offer them something they will want to share. The case contains the whole non-needy process in compressed form: genuine interest in what the other person posted, a conversation, value (the thread was about a shared topic), and an implicit ask, delivered without neediness.

#### The digital world has no barriers

Why is networking so central now? Koe's answer is historical. In February 2023 he shows a graphic illustrating "the evolution of networking"; the transcript does not let us reconstruct it beyond what he narrates. Twenty years ago, to get opportunity you needed college, a network, location, a profession and status; today "all you really need is the internet." In the past the only way to the top was your network, and your network was limited by your physical location or by the prestigious college you attended and by climbing the ladder. Introverts "didn't stand a chance," and Koe says he himself would have been out of a job. Today you can DM a celebrity, and if you are a good persuasive writer, which is a skill, "there is a string of words that you can say that will get them to respond." He repeats the phrase at the end of the video as "such an important thing": **the digital world has no barriers**; we do not live in the world of ten years ago.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

In May 2024 he makes the same point in a step toward self-confidence: making new friends online, the mastermind. "There is a concept called a mastermind: multiple minds working together to achieve a shared goal." In the past you needed local connections or a prestigious local school; "now you have access to anyone at the click of a button. Your success is no longer determined by your socioeconomic status and physical location; it is determined by who you are connected to and the opportunities they can pass off." How to find those connections? Have a goal; attract people by starting a business with a mission to achieve that goal; and get in front of well-connected people by writing in public. The link to confidence is that if you do not push beyond your limits, fail and overcome, you will not become confident; "a goal, business and writing, in that order, are how you continue to repeat this process to infinity." "There are no limits except for the ones you place on yourself"; in a job, "someone is in charge of the limits you can reach." If you do not hop "on the evolution train with technology, AI and the internet," they will make you obsolete. You make friends online "by just showing up and talking to people," as at a party; online you increase your social proof with your brand and ideas, offline your status with cars, looks and material acquisitions, "and it's obvious which one leads to a better life."

**Source:** It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md

The order "goal, business, writing" is worth noting: networking, in this version, is not a technique pursued for its own sake but the result of having a direction (Part IV) that attracts people who share it. The same video tells what writing has done for his own reach: "after four years of writing, I can message almost anyone online and get a response from them. I mean, Gordon Ramsay follows me." He has many followers, he says, "because I write on social media": Instagram, Twitter, the YouTube Community page, "the newsletters that get turned into YouTube videos, it's all writing." With the social proof of his ideas, he can DM almost anyone; he jokes that he could probably DM John Cena tomorrow and get a response (the transcript is garbled at this point).

#### The goal is actual friends

The 2026 video states the purpose of the lever plainly. "The goal here is to make actual friends, because what do friends do? They support each other, and they're usually working toward the same goal. They have a similar value set." With five people you are in regular communication with, you write a great piece and send it: "Hey, I put a lot of time into this... I think your audience will resonate with it. Can you share it?" It takes time to get there, "but it really does become that simple once you actually have friends," because sharing costs them no effort. There is a condition: "if the content sucks, then they're not going to want to do that. So, go back to step one," that is, to validated content. And there is an obligation: "you're there to return the favor." When he launched his software Eden, he asked friends whether they liked it, asked them to promote the launch, and told them: "Anytime that you launch a product or anytime you need something shared, please do not hesitate to send it to me, so I can return the favor. Value exchange."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

**Value exchange** is the principle that makes the system non-exploitative in Koe's view: the shares flow in both directions, among people who would help each other anyway.

#### Perception goes two ways

One more personal story completes the social side of the lever. In August 2023 Koe tells how, at his apartment complex, he walks with focus ("if I'm going outside to go on a walk, I'm going outside to go on a walk"), beelining; people said hi, he answered "oh hey, what's up" and kept walking. Later, neighbors told him he seemed very intimidating, so focused that they did not want to say anything, though in his own mind he is happy to stop and chat. He draws two lessons. First, see beyond how people present themselves, and act regardless of how the other person will respond, knowing that most people respond positively. Second, you must also present yourself in a welcoming way, or with the vibe you want: how you look, going to the gym, clothes that fit all shape perception; you do not want to look "like a total creep that doesn't have any control over their life"; be someone people want to talk to.

**Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md

**Perception goes two ways** is the name he gives this lesson. Applied online, it explains both halves of the networking problem: the beginner who hesitates to DM a large account is misreading the other person's presentation, and the account that writes like a "corporate robot" is presenting itself in a way that discourages contact.

#### The audience as an ecosystem

John Hugh's contribution to the 2025 conversation gives networking a broader frame, and it extends from peers to the audience itself. What stops creators, he argued, is a **mindset and awareness gap**, and he described closing it as "the journey of a human being," like "climbing up Maslow's hierarchy of needs." First we are in silos, "me, me, me," because "I literally need to put food on the table." Then, with some success, you notice people leaving kind comments (and a couple of mean ones), you feel grateful, you want them "to feel seen" ("that's just relationship building in general"), and you discover other humans who care about the same things, which brings a "giddy, almost childhood joy." The next level of awareness, which he admits can be "utilitarian," is that "a more effective business is one where more people are more bought into what you're doing": you realize "this whole Instagram app is actually an ecosystem" in which creators can work together toward a mission. And "every follower isn't worth the same." The conclusion: "you're not just sending your content into the void, there are real human beings on the other end," and the more they feel "seen and heard" and part of something greater, the more effective you are, "purely rationally."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

**Complementary context:** Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs (1943) orders human needs from physiological survival through safety, belonging and esteem to self-actualization. Hugh uses it loosely as an analogy for the stages of a creator's attention, from survival to community.

His illustration of "every follower isn't worth the same" is personal: some creators have people who would "die for" them, and he would for Emma Chamberlain because "she's built such a great community and built such a great brand," but he is also subscribed to "like 17 other people that I can't even name off the top of my head, because they're not as personally relevant to me."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

These are Hugh's views, and they connect to Koe's own adaptation of Napoleon Hill's mastermind to the audience (above). In both, the audience is not a number but a community with a shared purpose, and the creator's role is to serve it. Hugh's account of moving past "me, me, me" also sits in some tension with Koe's 2023 advice to talk about yourself in replies (Section 26.4); as noted there, the two can be reconciled by distinguishing the personal story as a bridge of identification from self-focus as a content strategy.

#### The non-needy networking process (2023)

Koe's main contribution to the second lever is a repeatable process he calls **non-needy networking**: a sequence for befriending other creators in which value and relationship come before any request. He presented it in full in February 2023 as "the seven steps to non-needy networking." It began as a guide in his earlier course Digital Economics, was repurposed into 2 Hour Writer to help students get eyes on their writing, and was put into Modern Mastery; he calls it "foundational for anyone trying to grow a business or just improve their career." Its purpose, "peeled back," is to get your name "in front of people's eyes and in their mouths." He clarifies that the order is variable, "these are kind of just the principles," rooted in the psychology of influence and persuasion, and he recommends "the book Influence by Robert Cialdini" for anyone who wants to understand them better, invoking "the law of reciprocity" in the compliment step. He does not elaborate on Cialdini's content beyond that. The video does not define "non-needy" explicitly; the meaning follows from the sequence: the ask comes only at the end, after a relationship exists.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

**Complementary context:** Robert Cialdini's *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (1984) describes reciprocity as the tendency to feel obliged to return a favor or gift. Koe adapts it as the engine of the process: praise and help create a felt obligation to give back.

Before the steps, he corrects a misconception about outreach. Even freelancers think they are in a constant cycle of "I need to reach out to this guy, and then the conversation ends if they never respond." It is not so. If you reach out to 100 people, are kind, have value to offer and walk through the process, then even those who cannot help you directly will have you "top of mind for the next person that comes to them and asks if they know anyone" who can help with web design, email marketing, fitness or whatever you do. Outreach compounds through referrals.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

**Step 1: find somebody you want to DM.** The emphasis is on *want*, and it matters for paid work too. Many freelancers choose niches they "don't give a [expletive] about," and end up hating reaching out because they hate the people, hate working with them, hate the work, and "build themselves into another miserable nine to five." So reach out to people who inspire you, whom you would want to work or strategize with, or with whom you see potential for mutual benefit. This is Justin Welsh's "misery doesn't scale" (Section 26.2) applied to networking. Where to find them: you presumably follow at least one person you want to connect with or learn from. Go to that person's following list (somewhere between zero and a thousand accounts), spend time going through it, find like-minded accounts or people you would want to connect with, follow them, start replying and engaging, and eventually walk them through the DM process.

Koe adds a heuristic about reach in this step: when starting out you "have to work your way up the ladder." If you have zero followers and DM someone with 100,000, and they look at your profile, they will think (it is "a bit superficial," but real) that there is nothing you can offer: "this guy hasn't put in any time at all." Once you have an audience and leverage, you can reach anyone: 100,000 to a million followers is social proof that you have been in the game for a while, offer value and have "proven" yourself, "like a badge." With about a million followers, he gets a response "from almost anyone." He acknowledges that "there's a lot more nuance" than one video can explain.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

The ladder is consistent with the 2023 argument for paid growth (Section 26.4): very large accounts need a filter, and for a beginner either payment or a gradual climb through peers is the way to reach them.

**Step 2: send an inspired compliment.** The first message is an **inspired compliment**: find a piece of their content or work that truly inspires you, that you like or that gave you an insight, and send it to them explaining what you liked. "People love praise," and through the law of reciprocity, if you give value ("praise is a form of value"), they feel obliged to give something in return. His example DM, pretending he himself had written a tweet on managing emotions: "What's up Dan, this tweet hit me hard, I've been going through it the past couple of days and this instantly gave me some relief, thank you," plus the link to the post. If they do not respond, reach out again in the same way about something else you liked, "and keep doing that until they respond."

**Step 3: show genuine interest.** Showing interest is "communication 101": "showing interest makes you interesting." Ask them about their goals, what they are building, what they do for work. This gives you the opportunity to give value even if you do not yet have any to give. If they respond with a generic "thank you so much," go to their profile, find what they are working on or a goal they have, and work from that. His scripts: if you find the project, "What are your next plans for Modern Mastery? It's been crazy seeing it grow, I'm curious what you've got in store"; if you cannot find it, "Do you have anything that you're building right now? With that kind of content you must have something bigger in the works."

**Step 4: lead with value.** "This is the part that trips most people up, because most people don't think they have value to give." The options: see where you can help; send actionable tips if you personally have them; send resources (videos, articles) that may help them build what they are building in line with their goal; ask more questions to understand specifically what they are building so that you can actually help. If you have no value to give, send a resource, connect them with someone in your network, or simply continue a good conversation and show interest in helping.

**Step 5 (optional): get on a call.** Optional but highly recommended, especially for introverts as practice. A face-to-face connection is "priceless" online, where nobody knows your mannerisms or how you talk outside a scripted reel or post; it helps you "mesh on that personal level." When he was starting out, Koe got on Zoom calls "left and right, even if I didn't feel like it." He recalls being very anxious before calling someone with about 50,000 followers, then realizing that "every single person behind a high following count is human too." People think calls are weird only because they have never done it; it is more normal than you think if you ask "hey, you want to get on a call? I can help out with what you're building, or we can just talk shop." An alternative is to move the conversation to Telegram or WhatsApp and send voice messages, which makes it more personal. (In the same passage he warns viewers about scammers impersonating him in his YouTube replies with a WhatsApp number and his profile picture.)

**Step 6: follow up with value.** After some time has passed, after a call, or after a conversation has fizzled, follow up with value to strengthen or maintain the connection. Keep their goals top of mind: when you come across something interesting while consuming content, reading books, watching YouTube or building things, think of sending it to them. Koe has sent content systems and Notion templates he built to friends in his network ("hey man, I built this out, maybe it'll help with XYZ"). His script, to be adapted to your own voice rather than copy-pasted: "I remember you telling me about your plans for [project or goal]. I found this today and thought you'd find it helpful."

**Step 7: follow up with an ask.** By now you have built "a pretty damn solid connection without asking for anything yet." Understand that this is a long game: if you only want a quick payment, retweet or share, you may get it, but "it's not sustainable," and you are not building a network you can rely on. Possible asks: invite them to join a mastermind group; send them one of your posts you put a lot of time into, which they may share without being asked; or ask specific questions you have "without paying for consulting." Many people miss this, he says: they shoot a message straight to a busy person expecting a thought-out answer, with no relationship, and some big accounts respond with a link to a consulting call that costs "like a thousand bucks," which nobody pays. If you network and show that you are "a cool person and a real person," you can usually ask questions and get answers "because they like you."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

He adds a tactic for using connections for audience growth: write something "that they would think about retweeting," for example a tweet on a topic you learned from them, or an Instagram post tagging them because they inspired part of it. Then send it: "yo, just wrote this up and thought you'd enjoy it, it was inspired by our previous conversation." If they are tagged, the benefit is mutual: if they share it and it gets traction, it looks good for them too.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

Finally, he answers the objection "why would I do these things if I'm not being paid for it?" with the "huge dissonance" already seen in Section 26.2: you cannot be paid for something until you have practiced it unpaid. A June 2023 passage summarizes the spirit of the process in a few lines. The best conversation starter is "hey dude, looks like you're building something cool, mind if I ask more about it? What's this website, content, interest?" Ask questions: "you become interesting by being interested in other people." Do not be robotic; text the way you would text a friend; people want authenticity. His editor, Devin, gets DMs from community members with "the most washed down" pitches, and people can see through them. Just say: "hey, what's up man, I want to get to know you, what are you working towards, what's this thing you're building, is there any way I can help?" Be a genuine human being.

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

#### The non-needy networking process (2026)

In May 2026 Koe presented the process again, with renamed steps and new emphases. "If you don't have that friend group yet, I call this process... non-needy networking. And so, if you do this right, this is how you get someone like a 50,000-follower account to share your post, and now your post is in front of 50,000 people, and you didn't rely on the algorithm. And that's how you grow, if you turn this into a repeatable process." The steps are: find someone to DM; send simple praise; show interest in them; show you are useful; get on a call (optional, recommended); continue sending resources or checking in; ask. "You don't need to go through all seven steps... It can just come naturally in the conversation." And he is explicit about the role of the other person's audience: leveraging it "isn't the goal of the friendship, but that's a benefit of it."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

**Step 1** is now framed as curating a group. "You shouldn't just reach out to anyone and everyone. This should be like a small curated group of people that you're going to strategize and grow with over the course of 6 to 12 months, or probably even longer." Finding them is casual: "you scroll; if you see an account that you like, make an effort to DM them. Make an effort to engage. Make an effort to be seen by them and... to show that you're supportive." Or go to a profile you like (even Koe's), open their following list, find people similar to them or to you, and check their content. "There isn't, like, an exact way to just pinpoint exactly who to talk to." It "should be pretty quick": "That guy sounds cool. I'm going to DM him."

**Step 2** is now **simple praise** (formally optional, though "you have to learn how to make friends"). Look at their content, website or what they are creating, and find something you resonate with. "The simplest thing you can do here is to just send a single sentence." His example, replying to someone who posted that most people quit social media too early: "Damn, that's true. I actually quit like four times, and now I'm back at it. How long have you been a creator?" "Wow, so difficult to start a conversation," he says ironically. You could ask ChatGPT how to start a conversation, "I wouldn't recommend it, but if you're that far gone socially, maybe try it." His analogy here is the way girls compliment each other's shoes: "Oh my god, girl, where did you get those?", and then they are getting lunch, and then they are best friends.

He then develops a longer analogy for the register of these messages. "Avoid corporate speak like the plague... You're not here to connect with someone on LinkedIn. You're here to just, like, text them like a friend." Picture the gym. You see someone in a shirt like yours: "Oh, dude, nice shirt," and a connection forms. Next day, "what's up, man," and a fist bump. Again a fist bump, and "I didn't get your name." Then "what's your split? What are you training today?" "Arms." "What are you training on Saturday?" "Chest." "We should hit that together... It's a date." "It's literally the same exact thing... you're just doing it in the replies and the DMs as opposed to in the gym. And rather than getting in a lift together, you support them in some way, by sharing their content."

**Step 3**, show interest, means drilling "into something that is important to them. And for another creator, it's usually what they're building." Ask "what are you building?", or "I see that you're building Eden. How's that going? What's the next feature you're going to drop? I actually looked into it. It looks super cool." Or refer to a specific article from a year back on their Substack: "this part really resonated with me. Where did you come up with that?" "It doesn't even matter," he adds; what matters is the genuine interest.

**Step 4** is now **show you're useful**, and here Koe makes his most important refinement. "This is really where the power comes into play," and it trips people up most "because they don't think that they have any value to provide." But "you personally don't need any value to provide. Like, you just need to be someone who can connect the other person with some form of value." That can be a resource or a person ("dude, I think you should reach out to this guy... you guys would hit it off"). What he likes doing, and receiving, is knowing what someone is working on and sending relevant things: if someone is building software and he watches a video on how AI will disrupt tech, he sends it: "you might find this interesting, I know you're building XYZ." They may watch it, or simply "take mental note... he thought of me outside of our DMs." "That's when the law of reciprocity kicks in and they feel the need to return the favor. If you give something, someone else is going to feel the need to give something in return."

**Steps 5 and 6** are the call and the **faint connection**. The call is the online version of getting coffee or lunch with a gym acquaintance: "Yo, we should catch up on a call sometime and talk about business." You come out with "a lot deeper connection." His number of calls as a beginner "was absurd," because "people just like to speak with other people [who] have related interests, because they're not going to find those people in their everyday life": few people around him or from school share his interests, "so you kind of have to build a tribe of those people." Then "continue sending more resources or just check in," a video, an article, a tweet: "Oh, dude, this is exactly what we were talking about the other day." "You just kind of maintain faint connection while providing value to them." Or create something for them: people have scraped all of Koe's YouTube videos and had AI collect his patterns and concepts, which he appreciated (though he keeps such a note himself: "you reverse engineer your own stuff").

**Step 7**, the ask, "can come naturally in conversation." The options are to invite them to a group chat with others you have been talking to, "a really powerful group chat where you can share strategies"; to ask questions in their area of expertise ("free answers... without paying for consulting or coaching"); or, if you plan to leverage their audience (a benefit, not the goal), to send a post "that you actually put a lot of time into and that's good," and ask them to share it; "if it's not good, they're not going to want to share it." Conversations also generate content: a problem or idea comes up, you write about it and send it: "inspired by a previous conversation... if you hit it with a share, I'll hit you back at some point." And his favorite approach, which he calls **brutal honesty**: "Hey dude, I'm really trying to grow. If you could share this, that would be awesome. If not, absolutely no worries at all. I'm not expecting anything."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The 2026 video ends the process with a timeline and a threshold. "Is this all going to happen overnight? ... Probably not. It's going to happen over the course of 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 3 months. But it will compound." "These aren't levers that you guess at. They're just levers that you pull. It's just an action that you do and you get better at. It's a skill." And the threshold: "Not that you're going to DM 500 people, but if you DM'd five people a day for 30 days or 60 days, it's a lot of freaking people. And if one isn't kind enough or close enough of a friend to share one of your posts, then you're doing something wrong."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

#### Comparing the two versions

| Step | 2023 version | 2026 version | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Find somebody you want to DM; mine a following list; work up the ladder | Find someone to DM: a small curated group to grow with for 6–12 months | Emphasis on a stable peer group |
| 2 | Inspired compliment (praise as value; reciprocity) | Simple praise: one sentence ending in a question | Simpler, conversational |
| 3 | Show genuine interest ("showing interest makes you interesting") | Show interest, usually in what they are building | Essentially the same |
| 4 | Lead with value (tips, resources, introductions) | Show you're useful: you need not *have* value, only connect them to it | Lowers the bar; reciprocity made explicit |
| 5 | Call (optional): everyone behind a large count is human | Call (optional): deeper connection; build a tribe of people with related interests | Same |
| 6 | Follow up with value, keeping their goals top of mind | Faint connection: keep sending resources or checking in | Renamed |
| 7 | Follow up with an ask: mastermind invite, a post, questions; a long game | Ask: group chat, questions, a good post; "brutal honesty" | Adds Koe's preferred tone |
| Pace | Reach out every day; thank people | Five DMs a day for 30–60 days; effects in 2 weeks to 3 months | Adds measurable action |

The changes are renamings and refinements rather than reversals. Two are substantive. The 2026 version lowers the barrier of step four, so that a beginner who has nothing to offer can still be useful by connecting people with resources or with each other. And it shifts the target from "working up the ladder" toward much larger accounts to a curated group of peers who grow together, with the 2023 ladder still valid for accounts far larger than one's own (where, as Section 26.4 showed, Koe's 2023 answer was paid growth as a filter). Koe does not explain these changes; they are consistent with the general movement of the 2026 video toward free, peer-based growth.

### 26.6 The Author's Growth as a Case

The previous sections presented Koe's growth system as he teaches it. This last section reads the system backward from his own history, as he has told it in pieces between 2022 and 2026. The pieces do not form a single narrative in the corpus, and some of the figures differ between tellings; they are arranged here in chronological order of the events, with the differences noted. Chapter 37 treats Koe's trajectory as a whole; this section keeps to his growth as an audience builder, because it is the case from which most of the principles above were abstracted.

#### First attempt: the photographer who spammed hashtags

"My first attempt at social media was as a photographer. I would post images on Instagram and just spam hashtags and hope and pray that I would someday gain 100,000 followers." He thought his images were at least decent compared with those of others who were growing, but "I posted my images, I used the hashtags, I did what people told me to do, and I gained no followers," "so obviously there was a problem there."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

This is beginner hell in its pure form (Section 26.2): posting into the void, following generic advice, with no mechanism for getting eyes on the content.

#### The Photoshop art account: first lesson in the power of social media

"As shiny object syndrome goes," Koe became more interested in editing photos than in taking them, and binge-watched Lightroom and Photoshop tutorials. **Shiny object syndrome** is a common phrase that Koe uses with a sense of his own: jumping from interest to interest, which he sometimes defends as a source of stacked skills and sometimes blames for his failures; here it is simply the mechanism of a change of direction. Around 2017 he found an Instagram creator, Visuals of Julius, who posted surreal images: "eye candy," galaxies, mountains and clouds, clearly art but fairly realistic, scroll-stopping. He wanted to do that. He borrowed Adderall from his roommate and sat in his room blending stock images with his own, "6, 7, 8 hours in my room forgetting to eat, obsessed with what I was creating."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

What changed this time was the method of distribution. In an April 2023 retelling he calls the digital art account one of his "favorite failed businesses," and the first time he realized the power of social media for business, though he did not realize at the time that he was building one. He emulated how larger accounts grew: tagging community pages that share digital art, creating the best composites he could so that they would be shared, and remixing photographers' images in the hope that they would share his content. He compares it to mentioning a large account in a Twitter thread so that they retweet it, or tagging someone on Instagram whose idea inspired you so that they share it to their story: "boom, you have eyes on your content."

**Source:** Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md

Section 26.4 described the mechanism of the curation pages and the remixing; here is the result. He was consistent "for around 2 months," and "if I remember correctly," his posts were shared by many small accounts, "but maybe 10 to 20 times on big massive accounts that had 100K to 500K followers," all digital art pages. "In my first two months, by doing that and getting eyes on my content, I gained around 2,500 followers." In the June 2022 telling, community pages of 300,000 to a million followers gave him his first 2,000 or so followers.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md; 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md

The contrast between the photographer and the digital artist is the contrast between the first and second steps of the 2023 growth system. The quality of the work may have improved, but what made the difference, in Koe's account, was getting the work shared by people with larger audiences. This is the experience behind "the one and only principle of social media growth."

#### Why the art account failed

"At 2,500 followers I burnt out and quit, because it took so long to create daily Photoshop composites, and I just didn't really have a vision for the account, and I didn't know marketing and sales, I didn't know how to create a product." In the 2023 retelling he explains the failure in terms of value: he loved to create but had no idea how to monetize; his art "was valuable, but it wasn't valuable enough to demand payment," because others did not perceive it as that valuable; and he felt he had to learn a more profitable skill to freelance with, keeping art as a hobby.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md; Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md

The diagnosis lists three missing elements: vision, marketing and sales, and a product. These correspond to Part IV, Chapter 32 and Chapter 33 of this book, and to the last two links of the magic click (Section 26.1): the account had content and distribution but no product and no marketing.

He also describes, in hindsight, what he would have done differently. "Of course I was an idiot." Knowing what he knows now, he says, he would probably still be doing Photoshop art (though he now loves writing more and believes "the universe conspired in my favor"). If he went back, he would, first, keep growing and "realize my audience as a traffic source." Second, he would create a product or service to send that traffic to, courses and coaching: he had bought a course from Visuals of Julius to learn the craft, and it never registered that he could create his own, for example showing how he made his favorite piece, promoted to a growing audience, which would likely have produced a sustainable income. Third, he would learn copywriting, marketing and offer creation to position the product. Fourth, he would partner with personal development creators to visualize their best ideas and become an authority in that space. "In other words, I'd do everything that we talk about in the one-person business model series."

**Source:** Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md; How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The fourth item is an application of remixing and networking: partnering with creators in another field would have borrowed their audiences while giving them something of value. The second is an application of "sell what's already selling" (Section 26.3): he was himself a buyer of exactly the kind of product he could have sold. The case is, in Koe's own framing, a retrospective exercise, and its value for the reader is that it shows how the principles of this chapter would apply to a field (visual art) quite different from his later one.

#### Twitter: consistent growth out of the gate

After freelancing in web design (the part of his trajectory that Chapter 37 covers), Koe returned to Twitter, where, as Section 26.5 told, he lurked for six months before asking himself "why am I just consuming?" His description of the growth that followed, given in May 2026, is unspectacular by design: it "wasn't super fast all at once." About 100 followers a week for two months; then about 500 a week; then it "kind of stabilized around 2,000 followers a month." There were waves: "large explosions of follower growth when I had a tweet do really well," and troughs of 1,500 to 2,000 a month, "pretty stable." He calls it "consistent growth out of the gate."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The stabilized rate, 1,500 to 2,000 a month, is the source of the benchmark in Section 26.1 ("1,500 to 3,000 followers a month, or you're doing something wrong"), and the "large explosions" are the exponential events of Section 26.3.

How much work did it take? In May 2025 he answers the belief that one has to grind content to build an audience. Quality and consistent output give you a better chance of spreading. "I personally wrote three tweets a day over the course of two years," with occasional threads, and grew to around 100,000 followers (along with networking and other things). It was not a grind: he spent perhaps five to ten hours a week on it alongside freelancing, and it barely took time from client work. The life it enables, he adds, is one of walks, listening to books, filling your mind, making sense of ideas through your own experiences, and living experiences that add uniqueness "because I'm the niche," then distributing them for people to follow and look into your products or services, and growing by making friends with interesting people you cannot find locally. Freelancing was hard because he had limited time for clients, so building an audience was "the next logical step"; it evolved into something completely different.

**Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md

What did he write about? In February 2023 he says he wrote about whatever he wanted (self-improvement, his fitness journey, managing emotions, ideas from books he thought his audience would like), and not from the "scarcity mindset" of needing to make money from the audience immediately: "I wasn't in that scarcity mindset of, oh, I need to make money from my audience immediately." To promote his services, he included actionable web design tips here and there, approached broadly ("why you should learn web design").

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

This is the "one focus area plus a lot of noise on the side" that he described to John Hugh in 2025 (Section 26.3), told two years earlier. It also illustrates the tension, noted in Section 26.3, between writing about what interests you and researching what works: in his early Twitter period, by his own account, he did the former, combined with a small monetizable focus, while the iteration on what performed well (three tweets a day, tens of thousands of tweets) did the work of validation.

The timeline he gives varies. In October 2023: year one, about 10,000 followers; year two, 50,000; year three, 100,000. Then he used his "validated ideas" to post on Instagram, LinkedIn and YouTube, knowing they would get engagement because he had "so much data from writing on Twitter," and four years later he had about 2,800,000 followers across platforms, "a testament to exponential growth and consistency." The May 2025 telling puts 100,000 at two years rather than three; the units do not resolve the difference. The cross-platform expansion is the case behind the "you cannot skip the reps" argument of Section 26.3: 20,000 tweets yielded 100 validated ideas, which became 100 Instagram posts that all performed.

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

#### The discovery: one reader, one million

In November 2023 Koe describes a moment from this period as "a discovery that changed the direction of my life": "if I could gain one reader I could gain 1 million, and if I could gain one buyer I could gain 1,000." "2.8 million readers and 20,000 buyers later, my discovery held true." He adds that his creative problem solving has since evolved beyond a one-person business: he is building Cortex, which requires a team (the limit case of Chapter 37).

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

This is the personal origin of the "main lever" and "secondary lever" of the magic click in Section 26.1, where the numbers vary ("one follower, 100,000"; "one dollar, a million"). The ratio here (a million readers per thousand buyers) is more modest on the commercial side, and it is the version backed by his own reported numbers. As with all such figures in the corpus, they are his statements and not audited data.

#### Always building something

A July 2023 passage explains why his engagement held up when others' collapsed. People, including his Twitter clients, told him their engagement was falling and asked how he kept such good engagement. His answer was that he had been "practicing what I preach for years": long-form newsletters, YouTube videos, podcasts, "creating eight to ten lead magnets," products, iterating and evolving them, a community, a service. "I'm always building something literally every single quarter"; "the first hour of every morning I was building a product or service, and I was trying to sell it."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

The habit connects the one-hour day of Section 26.1 with the fourth step of the 2023 system, depth through long form. In Koe's account, engagement on short-form content is sustained not by short-form tactics alone but by the depth behind it: products, lead magnets and long-form pieces that give the audience a reason to keep paying attention.

The same video gives a picture of the result as what he calls **digital real estate**. "My brand is a city within social media, and it's a community of millions of people." His Power Planner, a free planner born of his own productivity problem, has a niche audience of 70,000 downloaders around productivity ("this is digital real estate I am occupying"); his newsletter, a niche audience of 120,000 subscribers around depth, philosophy and business; his products, a niche audience of more than 15,000 students around writing, branding and business. By contrast, people who niche down their brand "maybe get to a hundred thousand total followers after three years"; his Power Planner alone is a more niche audience than that and has 70,000 people. "This is how you remove the boundaries around who you are online in the Digital Society and build whatever the [__] you want with time."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

The argument is the practical form of the digital asset of Section 26.3. Each validated topic becomes a free or paid asset that holds a niche audience, so that the brand as a whole can remain broad. Koe's term "digital real estate" has several senses across the corpus; here it means the niche audiences he occupies within his "city" on social media. Chapter 27 develops the brand as a world in this sense.

#### Iteration volume

In October 2024 Koe states the number of iterations behind the growth. The fourth need of a creator, he says, is experimentation, iteration and persistence, because "you never land on the one specific thing." He has launched ten products and ten free downloads, made a few hundred YouTube videos, more than 50,000 tweets, "I think like 1,200 Instagram posts," and "thousands upon thousands of iterations on my content." You do not post one tweet and become famous, or make one product and become rich. "The first thing of anything that you do is going to suck, and the longer you delay that, the longer you're delaying actually creating something good." The most powerful mindset, he says, is "just put the thing out, just publish the thing, get the feedback and then improve." Publishing is "the start of the journey," not the end.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

The passage restates, from Koe's own record, the "time to feedback" principle of Section 26.2 and the "under every 100 good posts there's a thousand bad posts" of Section 26.3.

#### The video that grew from notes

In the same October 2024 video, Koe gives an example of an exponential event and its origin. One of his most popular videos, which led to 200,000 subscribers "in just a few months," was "The One-Person Business Model: How to Productize Yourself." "One-person business" was a concept from his Core Notes, his note-taking system; "how to productize yourself" was a process from those notes. He pulled the two blocks together: "one-person business sounds cool, how to productize yourself, put them together in a title, fill out the content of the video with the notes."

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

The case joins three threads of this chapter. The video is a combination of validated ideas from his own repository (Seneca's bees, applied to one's own notes); it produced an exponential event of the kind described in Section 26.3; and its subject, productizing yourself, is the theme of Part X. Section 26.3 also recorded that his YouTube jump followed the podcast with Justin Welsh on the one-person business; the two accounts point to the same topic as the catalyst, and the specific subscriber figures differ between tellings.

#### When growth stalls

The case would be incomplete without the troughs. In February 2025 Koe describes a YouTube slump: he normally got 150,000 to 200,000 views per video, and then, for about four videos in a row, he got 50,000 to 60,000. It was a combination, he says, of ideas "just not being very good" and of how social media goes. It is demotivating, and "that's where most people fall off," saying "the YouTube algorithm changed." His answer: "No dude, you just need to iterate."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The slump is a small instance of the whole system. Even at scale, the data are noisy, and the response is the same as in beginner hell: treat the poor results as data points, adjust, and continue. The alternative explanation, the algorithm, is the one Koe has rejected throughout this chapter as a way of placing the cause outside one's control.

#### What the case shows, and where it diverges from the advice

Read together, the episodes trace the system of this chapter in the order Koe discovered it. Posting without distribution produced nothing (the photographer). Distribution through other people's audiences produced growth but no business (the art account). Consistent output on a writing platform, with a monetizable focus plus noise, produced steady growth, punctuated by exponential events (Twitter). Validated ideas carried across platforms multiplied the audience. Relationships turned into friends, partners and shares (Section 26.5). And continuous building of products and long-form assets turned the audience into a business and sustained its engagement.

Two divergences between the case and the advice deserve to be stated. First, as noted in Section 26.2, Koe advises beginners to publish before they have results, but his own sequence was the reverse: he obtained results as a freelancer before he began to publish seriously on Twitter. He does not explain the discrepancy. Second, his current practice (under thirty minutes a day on social media in 2022; scarcity and one piece a week in 2026) differs sharply from what he asks of beginners ("reply like a madman"; three tweets a day). Here he does explain: the stage is different, and what he does now rests on years of the work he asks beginners to do. The reader who uses his case as a model should therefore model the sequence of stages, not his present routine.

6. 7. Identify a third possible divergence, or a condition under which one of the chapter's principles (for example, "you're only going to grow if you're good," or "the recommendation mechanism is the most fundamental aspect of growth") would stop applying.
