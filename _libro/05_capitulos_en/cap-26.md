## Chapter 26 — Growing: Validated Content, Traffic and Networking

Chapter 25 argued, at the level of principle, why anyone who creates should build an audience: attention is power, distribution is freedom, and an audience is the form of leverage an individual can build alone and that accumulates as trust. This chapter turns from the why to the how. It asks what a person with zero followers actually does, week after week, to grow, and it gives Koe's answer in the form he converged on after years of reformulation: growth is a system with two levers, **validated content** and **networking**, and everything else is either a breakdown of those two levers or a distraction from them.

The chapter proceeds in six steps. Section 26.1 presents growth as a system rather than a lottery: the shift to the interest graph that changed what follower counts mean, the realistic timelines and benchmarks Koe offers, and the successive versions of his growth framework, from the four steps of 2023 to the "magic click" and the six daily tasks of 2024. Section 26.2 deals with the phase every creator passes through first, which Koe calls **beginner hell**, and with the psychological barrier that keeps most people from leaving it, impostor syndrome; his answer is that experience is gained by starting, and that the people you can help best are those one or two steps behind you. Section 26.3 explains the first lever, validated content: make noise, double down on signal, do what already works from your own perspective, sell what is already selling, and treat each post as a data point in an iterative pipeline that occasionally produces nonlinear jumps. Section 26.4 explains the half of the work most beginners ignore: getting eyes on your content. Writing is not building an audience; traffic mechanisms, other people's audiences, remixing, clippable moments and paid growth are the means by which writing reaches readers. Section 26.5 develops the second lever, networking, which Koe prefers to call "making friends": why growth is social, how tribes and masterminds form, what DMs are for, how the audience itself functions as an ecosystem, and the step-by-step "non-needy networking" process in its 2023 and 2026 versions. Section 26.6 closes with Koe's own growth as a case: the Photoshop art account that first showed him the mechanics, and the iterative path from one reader to more than a million.

The chapter depends on Chapter 24, where the post was introduced as a minimum viable product and the content ecosystem as the structure that turns one idea into many pieces; on Chapter 23, whose structures and hooks are what "persuasive content" means here; and on Chapter 25, whose distinction between built, borrowed and bought distribution reappears below as concrete tactics. It prepares Chapter 27, on the personal brand, which treats the identity and trust that this mechanical growth is meant to carry.

A word on chronology before starting. Koe has taught audience growth in many videos between 2021 and 2026, and his frameworks change name, number of steps and emphasis. The core does not change: write something worth reading, put it in front of people, learn from what they respond to, and make friends with others doing the same. Where the versions diverge (on volume, on following counts, on paid growth, on engagement groups, on how far "ahead" of a client one needs to be), the chapter presents each version with its date and the reason, when Koe gives one.

### 26.1 Growth as a System

#### The interest graph: why follower count stopped mattering

Any account of how to grow on social media in the mid-2020s has to begin with a structural change in the platforms themselves. Social media, Koe explains in an October 2024 video, switched from chronological, follow-based feeds to what is called an **interest graph**: TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube's algorithm and the "For You" page on X all show users what matches what they share, engage with or give attention to, rather than what the accounts they follow have published.

**Complementary context:** "Interest graph" is a term from the platform industry, contrasted with the "social graph" of earlier networks such as Facebook, where the feed was built mainly from the accounts and friends a user had chosen to follow. Koe uses the term in its common sense; it is not his coinage.

Koe treats this shift as both a blessing and a curse. The blessing is that "follower count doesn't really matter anymore." A small account can reach a large number of people, and with the skill of content writing and engagement a creator can consistently go viral, "though you shouldn't bank on it," and funnel that traffic to an email list. The curse, which he develops later in the same video, is the mirror image: you cannot guarantee virality every day, and you cannot even guarantee that your followers will see your content. Any given post may be shown entirely to new people who know nothing about you.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The same point was developed a few months later, in January 2025, in a conversation between Koe and John Hugh, co-founder of the creator platform Stan. Koe framed the question by mentioning the noise in the creator and AI space, the "for you pages," the interest graph and the move away from follower count. Hugh's answer was that "there's never been a better time to start," and he gave five reasons. First, people have begun to realize that "social media content is no longer social media, it's just the new marketing channel." Second, attention has moved from newspapers and legacy television to YouTube and TikTok; Hugh says he barely watches Netflix anymore because a YouTube thumbnail and title are simply better. Third, every small business owner therefore "needs to be where the user attention is." Fourth, on the interest graph "some of our most successful creators who make the most income have like 5,000 followers," because they are connected with their core niche: "you don't even need a large follower account now to be seen as credible, you just need to be in front of the right audience." Fifth, success rates on Stan are rising because audiences are maturing and there is "more social acceptance around buying things online from Instagram or from YouTube." His advice is to position yourself "in favor of that tailwind," with the caveat that legacy channels may one day have "alpha" (an untapped advantage) again.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

These are Hugh's arguments, offered from the vantage point of a company whose data covers many creators; Koe sets up the question and agrees with the conclusion. Together the two passages mark a real change in Koe's own position. Earlier, he had treated large follower counts as the measure of leverage: in 2022 he spoke of the scale of 500,000+ followers as leverage and mocked those who said gaining followers did not matter, and in 2023 he observed that people do not follow accounts with few followers, while at 100,000 "they follow you without questioning." After 2024 the emphasis moves to trust and to the email list as the only real measure of an audience. Koe never fully abandons follower numbers (he keeps using them as benchmarks, as the next subsection shows), and in 2026 he summarizes the reconciliation in two phrases: "likes ain't cash," but "it's not binary." The reason he gives for the shift is the platform change itself. The older view returns in Section 26.2, where it explains why the first months of an account are so hard.

#### Realistic timelines and benchmarks

Koe offers numbers for what growth should look like, and they are useful both as targets and as a check on unrealistic expectations. They also vary with the year and the context, and it is better to present them side by side than to pretend they form a single rule.

In September 2023 he says that owned distribution compounds: "you can get to 10 to 20,000 followers in a year, 50 to 100,000 in two years and millions after four to five years." The condition is decisive: "that is if you know how to iterate on your best content or even write content in the first place." Two months later he calls 100,000 followers in a year an "extremely good," nearly perfect result.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

In July 2024 he gives a monthly benchmark in a sharper tone: "if you weren't gaining 1,500 to 3,000 followers a month in under 30 minutes a day of work you're doing something wrong" and "you can do better." With that consistent growth, "over a year two years you can make a good amount of money, way more than you're used to." Annualized, this implies roughly 18,000 to 36,000 followers a year, somewhat above the 2023 figure.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

By March 2026 the metric itself has changed, consistently with the shift to the interest graph: Koe now speaks in views and impressions rather than followers. "If you become skilled at it, then you can get 10 to 50,000 views per YouTube video and you can also get 500,000 to a million impressions on social media per month." He adds, dryly, that "it will take some time to do that if you're not immediately good at the thing, shocker," and insists on the nature of the benchmark: "You can't just go to a school, get a degree in social media and immediately have 50,000 followers; this is based on real-world feedback; this is based on skill." Those impressions are enough, in his business arithmetic, to reach about 720 daily visitors to an offer, which he calls "a challenge for sure, but it's not too much to ask for." And he blocks the all-or-nothing reading: "even if you don't get [those numbers], it's not like you're making zero dollars if you actually do this stuff. Many people would be very happy with less than a million dollars per year."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

Against these targets, Koe sets an explicit warning about expectations, from October 2023: "You will not see insane results in less than a year, and even if you do it's wise to not think that you will. That expectation most of the time won't match with reality come time and it's not going to feel good, so don't hold that expectation." In the same video he frames the work of testing traffic mechanisms and iterating as a process of six to twelve months.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don't Tell You).md

| Date | Benchmark | Condition or caveat |
|---|---|---|
| 2023-09 | 10–20K followers in year one, 50–100K in two, millions in four to five | Only if you know how to iterate on your best content |
| 2023-10 | No "insane results" in under a year | Testing and iterating takes 6–12 months |
| 2023-11 | 100K in a year is "extremely good" | Near-perfect execution |
| 2024-07 | 1,500–3,000 followers a month in under 30 minutes a day | "Otherwise you're doing something wrong" |
| 2026-03 | 10–50K views per YouTube video; 500K–1M social impressions a month | "Based on skill"; less is not zero |
| 2026-05 | First effects of networking in two weeks to three months | Still compounding over 6–12 months (Section 26.5) |

Koe does not present these figures as contradictory, and they need not be read that way: they depend on skill, stage and platform. Two things are worth noting. The figures are his own estimates from his experience and that of students, not data from a study. And the most recent version, which gives a shorter horizon for the first visible effects of networking, coexists with the older warning against expecting much before a year; the first is about early signs, the second about substantial results.

#### The growth system, version one: four steps (2023)

Koe has summarized audience growth in several sequential frameworks. They are best read as successive compressions of one system, and the chapter is organized around the last of them. The earliest complete version comes from the October 2023 video "How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers," and consists of four steps that depend on one another.

Step one is to write persuasive content: content that captures attention, holds it and delivers value on it (the subject of Chapters 21–23). Step two is to get eyes on your content: "your content is meaningless unless you attempt and put effort into getting eyes on your content." Writing into the void is "okay... for practice, but eventually you have to just get out there." Step three is to iterate on your ideas with data, and Koe stresses the dependency: "you can only reach this step if you have eyes on your content," since without readers you have no way of knowing what performs best. Step four comes "down the road a bit": creating depth and span with your best ideas through long-form content. Koe's term **depth and span** refers to two complementary dimensions of trust: span is the breadth of short-form content that reaches many people; depth is the long-form content (threads, newsletters, videos) that lets a smaller number go deep with your ideas.

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don't Tell You).md

The point of numbering the steps is the dependency between them. Each step is impossible without the one before. A beginner who skips step two and keeps refining step one is trying to do step three without data; a creator who jumps to long form before finding which ideas resonate is building depth on untested ground. This is the same logic as the "tweet is the new MVP" of Chapter 24: short posts are cheap tests whose results decide what deserves expansion.

#### Version two: the magic click and the two levers of the business (2024)

In January 2024, in a video on internet business models, Koe compressed the system into a five-link chain he calls the **magic click**: you write content; you put effort into getting eyes on it, "a crucial step that everyone ignores no matter how much I tell them because this is where the work comes into play"; people who like your content follow you; you create a product; you market the product.

He then names two levers in the chain. The **main lever** is content: "if you can gain one follower you can gain 100,000." The **secondary lever** is marketing: "if you can make $1 you can make $100,000." What these sentences claim is not a forecast but a form of scalability: the hard part is the first instance, because once you know how to produce one result you can repeat the process. The specific figure fluctuates across sources: in another February 2024 passage he says one follower can become $1,000 and one dollar a million, apparently a slip, and in May 2024 he settles on "if you can make $1 you can make 1 million; if you can gain one follower you can gain 1 million." The idea is the same in every version. Koe frames the chain as "the new economy," enabled by technology from the last five years, and observes that most people still regard earning an income from what they enjoy as crazy.

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

The magic click extends the four steps beyond the audience to the business, which Chapter 28 will define minimally as audience plus product. It also repeats the diagnosis that organizes this whole chapter: the step everyone ignores is the second one. Writing is the part beginners are willing to do; getting eyes on the writing is "where the work comes into play."

#### Version three: six things, one to two hours a day (2024)

In October 2024, Koe restated audience building as six things to focus on "every morning," which "when done well should take no longer than 1 to 2 hours a day" and can therefore be done part time:

1. Testing ideas and structures on social media. A **structure** here means the shape of a post. Koe's instruction is to scroll through 10 to 30 posts and notice the forms: blocks of sentences, paragraphs, line breaks, long and short tweets, bullet points, threads. Then test your own ideas inside those shapes.
2. Having a **catalyst strategy** for new posts: a deliberate way of getting new posts in front of other audiences (developed in Section 26.4 under the name "idea catalysts").
3. Turning your best ideas into threads and newsletters.
4. Repurposing your writing to other platforms.
5. Taking advantage of **exponential events**, the sudden nonlinear jumps in growth that occasionally follow a post (Section 26.3).
6. Sending everyone to your newsletter.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The transcript shows that the numbering shifts in his walkthrough (newsletters become step three, exponential events step four, newsletter promotion step five), and repurposing is not developed separately there; Chapter 24 covers it. Compared with the four steps of 2023, the six things add two elements: the explicit reliance on exponential events, and the newsletter as the destination of all traffic, the owned distribution of Chapter 25.

#### Version four: two levers (2026)

The latest and most compressed form comes from a May 2026 video titled "How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits)." There Koe reduces growth to two levers, validated content and networking, and tells the viewer to "treat everything else as a distraction." Section 26.3 develops the first lever and Sections 26.4–26.5 the second, together with the traffic tactics that belong to both.

Read in sequence, the versions show a progressive compression rather than a change of doctrine. The 2023 four steps and their tactics (traffic mechanisms, remixing, paid growth) remain valid as a tactical breakdown of the 2026 levers: "persuasive content" plus "iterate with data" become validated content; "get eyes on it" becomes, increasingly, the relationships that make other people share your work. Koe does not explain why he compressed the system, beyond presenting the two levers as "levers that you pull" rather than something to be guessed at. Between the 2024 and 2026 versions there is also a 2025 version in seven steps for getting out of beginner hell, which Section 26.2 presents.

#### Where the hour goes

A system has to fit into a life. In a June 2024 video on what he calls the 365-hour year (one focused hour a day, developed in Part V), Koe explains how to use that single hour for audience building. At first, you split it: "you spend some of that time educating yourself, you spend some of that time actually doing the work" of writing and growing the audience. Step one is "getting the ball rolling," creating the system that grows an audience around what you want, and that takes time. Only later, once you have the hang of building an audience, do you use the hour to build a product to monetize it. Weekends are an accelerator: if you have twelve hours on weekends, use them; four to six hours will already speed things up.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

The sequence matters as much as the time budget. Audience before product is the order of the magic click, and it is also the order of Koe's own business, as Chapter 37 will show. Section 26.6 adds a complication: Koe himself did not always follow it.

### 26.2 Beginner Hell and Impostor Syndrome

#### What beginner hell is

Every growth system described in Section 26.1 assumes that the creator keeps going long enough for it to work. Most do not. Koe has a name for the phase in which they give up: **beginner hell**. It is his coinage, and he uses it from 2024 to 2026 with definitions that complement one another.

The plainest definition comes from May 2026: "Beginner hell is where you kind of you just post into the void and you don't really gain any followers." It happens, he explains, when your first serious endeavor is social media itself and you are learning the skills and the taste as you go. The mechanism that keeps people there is specific: "If you don't correct your mistakes or you don't pay attention to what's working and what's not, and you don't course correct and iterate, then you're never really going to have your breakout." That, he says, is why most creators quit, "and I don't blame them," because they cannot gain any traction. Later in the same video he adds the corollary: in beginner hell "you can't expect the algorithm to just launch you into the ether."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

A February 2025 video, "Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands," opens with the duration and the emotional texture of the phase: "3 to 6 months — that's how long most creators are trapped in beginner hell." It is "that dreadful place where you feel like you don't know what you're doing," where you question whether it is worth continuing and think about what to try next "just to do it all over again." Most people quit before things start to get good. Zero likes, zero followers and zero sales are, for Koe, "one of the most painful things in the world," because becoming a creator was supposed to be your ticket to the good life, and you begin to wonder if it was "a pipe dream that was sold to you by marketers and gurus." In the same video he widens the range: beginner hell "can last for 3 months or 30 years," the second figure being the case of the starving artist who never learns marketing or monetization.

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The two definitions describe the same phase from two sides. The 2026 version gives its cause (no iteration), the 2025 version its experience (dread, doubt, restarting) and its typical length. Elsewhere Koe applies the term to his own early years, in which he jumped between business models for almost five years without anything working; Section 26.6 returns to that.

#### Why the first followers are the hardest

Beginner hell is not only a matter of skill. It is also a structural disadvantage, and Koe described it precisely in October 2023: "Those first however many followers are the hardest. You don't have any social proof, you don't have any authority, you don't have any content built up and people just don't like to follow people that have a low follower count." His illustration is a profile visit. Someone lands on an account with 100 followers and, "even if it's the best content in the world," thinks "he probably hasn't been doing this long enough, maybe that was a one-off post... he's not that important." Someone visiting an account with 100,000 followers sees a post that may be terrible and thinks "he must be important," and follows "without even questioning it." The beginning is therefore "a heavy grind that you have to overcome with certain strategies."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

This is the 2023 view of follower count that Section 26.1 contrasted with the later "follower count doesn't really matter anymore." The two are less opposed than they look. The interest graph lowers the barrier to being seen; it does not remove the social proof that a large count still confers once someone visits a profile. What the beginner lacks, in either case, is not visibility alone but the accumulated evidence (followers, a bank of content, a recognizable name) that makes a stranger trust a new account. The "certain strategies" Koe mentions are the subject of the rest of this chapter.

#### The seven steps out (2025)

In the February 2025 video, Koe structures the way out of beginner hell as "seven tips, strategies and insights." The transcript's numbering is inconsistent (it mixes sub-steps and main steps, saying "number one" and "step number three" in ways that do not line up), so the reconstruction below is partly inferred from the flow of the video.

| Step | Name in the video | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Do what works for beginners | Solve more problems and pain points; cover the most important evergreen ideas in depth; filter your favorite accounts for their oldest content |
| 2 | Your content just isn't that good | Improve it with the Ten Commandments of Engagement (Chapter 23) |
| 3 | Start with training wheels | Structure, a swipe file, frameworks (Chapter 23) |
| 4 | Nobody knows who you are | Inject yourself into a tribe; non-needy networking; one strategic post a week (Section 26.5) |
| 5 | You need a product | Monetization from the start (Part X) |
| 6 | Build a world, not a funnel | Give context to all your content (Chapter 27) |
| 7 | Make noise and double down on signal | Experiment with ideas and keep what resonates (Section 26.3) |

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The table maps almost the entire book onto the problem of the beginner. Two observations follow. First, the seven steps contain the two levers of 2026 in embryo: steps 1, 2, 3 and 7 are about the quality and validation of content; step 4 is networking. Second, the advice to filter favorite accounts for their *oldest* content is a small but telling idea: the posts that a large creator published when they were small show what works for an account without social proof, which is exactly the beginner's situation. The term **building a world** (step 6) refers to giving context to everything you publish, so that each piece belongs to a recognizable universe of ideas rather than being a one-off hook or the top of a funnel; Chapter 27 develops it as the brand understood as an environment.

At the end of the video Koe adds an exercise. He asks viewers to answer, in the comments, "what was the one thing that kept you trapped or is keeping you trapped in beginner hell," because "once you become aware of that and make that public it's a lot easier to overcome." Anyone who says they have none, he adds, is "lying or you just don't have the knowledge to know better." The exercise applies a principle from Part I, awareness as the first step out of any trap, to the narrow case of a creator's sticking point, with the added element of making it public, which turns a private excuse into a stated problem.

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### You just need to be growing

A June 2025 video on personal brands supplies a consolation that is also a criterion. Koe calls the part of the brand devoted to reach the "growth pillar," because without growth there is no influx of traffic, "so you won't get paid." But the standard is modest: "You don't need to grow by thousands or millions of followers. You just need to be growing." And the way out of stagnation is already available: "The click with this growth pillar is that validated ideas... are already out there and it's up to you to find those, take those and make them your own and experiment until you see consistent growth."

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

**The click** is another of Koe's terms. In the 2025 video on beginner hell he uses it for the moment of insight in which you understand that growth is a skill and not luck, the level that follows beginner hell and which he jokingly calls "intermediate heaven." This passage is a bridge to Section 26.3: what moves a creator out of beginner hell is not more effort applied blindly but the deliberate use of ideas that have already been validated by the market.

#### Impostor syndrome: the barrier before the first post

Beginner hell is what happens to those who start. Many never start, and Koe's most frequent diagnosis of why is a set of beliefs he groups under impostor syndrome: "I don't have enough experience," "I'm not qualified," "my life is too boring." His answers to these beliefs are among his most stable positions, repeated from 2022 to 2026.

The first answer is an inversion. In a November 2022 list of lessons from his business, he argues that the ego performs "incredible mental gymnastics" to convince you of what you cannot do. Thinking you lack experience is a limiting belief, and it usually means you do not yet understand how to position yourself authentically even with zero experience. Tell someone to write online and list its "200 benefits," and the mind produces objections: "I'm not good at writing," "that's only for influencers," "I'm just freelancing before I build an audience." Koe finds the last one particularly silly. The same person would accept $500 to $1,000 from a client when they have no idea what they are doing, yet fears posting free information from a beginner's perspective ("I'm sharing what I learned"), which would let them gauge the viability of their idea for free through an audience and build leverage in the form of traffic, so that they would not even need outreach. His summary is the graphic he showed on screen: it is not "you need experience to start," it is "you gain experience by starting." People leave out the pieces of the picture that would lead to action; the ego closes the mind and lasers in on one objection, at the expense of the holistic picture and of creative thinking. "Don't let fear rule you and keep you the same."

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md

The same argument appears in a sharper form a month earlier. In an October 2022 video, Koe takes the example of someone who says: "I know a lot about web design and I'm trying to land my first client but I can't post about it online." If you are prepared to reach out to a stranger to try to make money from a freelance service, he replies, it makes no sense to be scared of posting free content. Free content should come first, "as a way of you distilling what you learn and organizing your thoughts" so that others benefit, and as a way of building authority to the point of landing a client.

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md

In January 2023 he states the converse: "if you're afraid to write content for free then you shouldn't even be thinking about working for a client." The argument "goes both ways." You do not have to "fake it till you make it," but you do have to start posting and delivering the value you are learning, the same value you will later deliver to clients in exchange for a lot of money. Given that, he says, it makes no sense that people do not start a personal brand.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

There is a tension worth stating here, which Koe himself does not resolve. He recommends publishing content before having results, but in his own trajectory he obtained results as a freelancer before he began to publish seriously. He presents this with humility ("I'm just a regular dude") rather than as a reason to reverse the advice. One reading is that his own path was the slower one, and that the advice describes what he would do now; but that is an interpretation, and the source does not give it.

#### Simple tests for being "qualified"

Against the abstract question "am I qualified?", Koe offers concrete tests. The best known, from October 2022 and repeated in a 2024 compilation, is the friend test: if you could go to a friend right now and teach them something you are studying, on social media, at school or anywhere, "you're more than experienced"; "you're already doing this... you just need to do it in public."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In July 2024 he extends it to family: "if you've helped your friends or family with any topic that you've ever learned about in your life you have enough experience." In January 2026 he applies it to the business itself, in answer to the belief that business is for talented or rich people: "if you've ever helped anyone with any of your interests in any way, then you are qualified to start a business. It doesn't need to go any further than that."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md; If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

In December 2024 he locates the source of the objection. People say "I've just been studying this in college, or in my free time online, I don't know if I'm qualified to teach it." What, he asks, do they think people online are doing? They are sharing what they know and find valuable so that others can benefit too. The disconnect is perceptual: "you are paying attention to the gap between you and other people online rather than realizing that they're a human too," "just a human that decides to speak well to a camera or write in a serious way." Koe uses **the Gap** in several senses across the corpus (the distance between where you are and where others are, which causes overwhelm; the distance between you and a desired object, which drives dopamine); here it is the distance one perceives between oneself and established creators, which hides the fact that they are ordinary people who decided to publish.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

#### Two blocks: "not enough experience" and "too boring"

In an October 2022 video, repeated nearly word for word in a February 2024 compilation, Koe names two specific blocks. He begins with his own analytics: most of his viewers are between 18 and 35 (with older outliers he applauds). The problem is that most of them do not see those years as experience they can leverage to help others. They have not self-reflected enough to see their own value, the problems they have overcome, and the fact that they hold information that can educate and help others. Block one: you think you do not have enough experience. Block two: you think your life is too boring, an effect of social media and its glamorous lifestyles. His answer to the second is one of his signature phrases: you do not need a glamorous lifestyle; "you need to be interested enough to make your interest interesting to other people."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

**Make your interest interesting** is Koe's own formula, and it links this section with Part VII: the niche is the person, and what makes a person worth following is not an extraordinary life but the ability to make what interests them compelling to others (the craft of Chapters 22–23). In the 2024 compilation he adds two practical reassurances. He recommends starting with writing (Twitter, a newsletter, a blog, even YouTube; in that very video he is reading his newsletter aloud), and he notes that you do not even need to show your face on the account.

#### Honesty and the market of extremes

In February 2024 Koe proposes a solution to impostor syndrome that is also a positioning strategy: "impostor syndrome is solved with honesty." Your marketing should be based "solely on where you are in your journey and exactly what you help others with." The creator industry, he notes, already has enough false and attention-grabbing promises, and that very excess creates an opportunity: "in a market of extremes, if you want to be outstanding all you have to do is be average."

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md

The **market of extremes** is his term for a market saturated with exaggerated promises, in which honesty about a modest position becomes a differentiator. The same video contains a practical rule for how honesty sounds when you do not yet have results, which he states more explicitly in July 2024: you are not writing "how I made a million dollars in 3 days" but "how I plan to make a million in 5 years"; not "how I lost 10 lbs in 2 months" but "here's my plan to lose 10 lbs in 2 months," "until you gain experience and then you can start talking about the results that you have."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

#### Stepping into the arena

The second family of arguments explains why the only way out of the experience problem is through publication. Koe calls this **stepping into the arena**: gaining experience by practicing your skills in a real-world setting and receiving feedback. "How do you think you gain experience... you don't gain experience without stepping into the arena." His analogy is the freelancer, who reaches out to clients with zero experience, "potentially in exchange for money," in order to gain experience; that is how any business starts. By comparison, writing a social media post costs nothing and promises nothing: "it's free content, you can do that without experience so that you can reap the benefits of articulating your thoughts in order to expose knowledge gaps, because that's the only way that you're going to learn faster." And: "You're not going to learn and gain experience by sitting and reading about things; you're going to gain experience by getting direct feedback and criticism on your work."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The argument connects directly with Chapter 14's "build to learn": writing in public is a learning instrument before it is a growth instrument, because articulating a thought exposes where it is incomplete. In November 2023 Koe states the principle as a warning: "your websites, designs, writing and other projects do not count until other people see them. You are delaying the time to feedback; without feedback you can't make it valuable; your first iteration will not be valuable." **Time to feedback** is his term for the interval between making something and showing it; hiding work lengthens it, and since the first version of anything is never good, every day of hiding is a day of not improving.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md

The oldest version of the argument, from October 2022, frames it in terms of value. It is "absolutely crucial" to put your value on what he calls the **public market**: turn it into internet content and post it, because you need data to know whether your ideas are truly valuable, or at least perceived as valuable. You can write like Alan Watts or a philosopher, but if you are not growing, getting engagement or making sales, the work is not sustainable or doable full time. "You need a balance of internal and external"; and you play the external game "from a place of I want to give value."

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The reference to Alan Watts is not a criticism of Watts but an example of an inward-facing writer whose model, without an audience mechanism, a beginner cannot afford. The phrase "balance of internal and external" anticipates the tension the chapter will meet in Section 26.3 between writing about what interests you and studying what works.

Finally, Koe names what he calls a "huge dissonance" in the objection "why would I do these things if I'm not being paid for it?": "you're not going to be able to get paid for it until you have enough practice from doing it without getting paid." Preferably the unpaid practice is something you like doing. If you plan to be writing every day in the future and getting paid for it, but you are not writing every day now and working your way up in income, "I don't know what you're doing."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

#### Whom to serve: one or two steps behind you

The positive side of the impostor argument is a theory of whom a beginner can help. Its earliest form in the corpus comes from Justin Welsh, in a December 2021 interview. Asked what an absolute beginner's first income stream should be, Welsh says there is something to decide first: "who do they like to spend time with? Because in my opinion, misery doesn't scale. So, if you're spending a lot of time talking to people that you hate talking to, that does not scale." The choice of audience precedes the choice of offer, because a one-person business is built on many hours of conversation with that audience. And the group you like spending time with "should be a group of people that are behind you in their journey." His example comes from sales: "If you're a sales manager talking to account executives, they've never managed people before. They're 2 years behind you in their journey and they want to learn how to become a sales manager." The prompt he offers is: "Where were you 2 years ago? Where were you 3 years ago? That's a group of people that hopefully you enjoy talking to."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

**Misery doesn't scale** is Welsh's phrase, not Koe's, and the "two to three years" is his figure. Koe adopts the logic and reformulates it as "steps." In October 2022: "you need to become valuable enough to sell a product or service to a person that is one to two steps behind you," or to sell something with a use case in other people's lives; you do not need to be the ultimate expert, only ahead of your buyer. In March 2023, in a sharper register: most people think they need the perfect customer avatar, the perfect system, curriculum or coaching structure, a fancy physical product with perfected supply chain management, or prior results, when in fact you need to work with someone in order to get results. The "harsh truth" is that "you need to know more than someone that is one step behind you." In another March 2023 video, he says that if you have done any self-development, you can help "someone that's right behind you."

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md; The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md; Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md

The same 2023 passage connects this to a definition of business. Business is solving problems for others in exchange for a valuable resource, today money, in the past barter and the exchange of goods: "products solve problems... that's what business is." The logic follows that the more problems you have solved, the more profitable your business can be. But that does not mean you lack the experience to help now. Koe recommends teaching from the start, because teaching helps you solve your own problems better, gives you more experience, "pulls minds together," and opens up more problems to solve, especially ones that are not directly personal to you.

#### Why the nearby teacher is better: the fat personal trainer

The most developed version of the argument is a metaphor from February 2024. Koe used to think that overweight personal trainers were silly. Peeling back the layers, he changed his mind. The average person would not want to be trained by an Olympic bodybuilder or a super-fit guy who is "probably neurotic about their health and unhealthy because of that." The gap between where they are and where they want to be is so large that anxiety and overwhelm make them quit early. With someone one or two steps ahead, they would stick with it longer, for two reasons: they relate more, and they understand that the other person is improving along with them. The relationship becomes less transactional, more like an accountability partner who is paid for the value exchange (one plays the business, the other the consumer). His conclusion is that students get better results from people one to two steps ahead, and he translates it into his own field: "It doesn't matter if you aren't a productivity guru with a billion dollar company; it does matter if you can focus for 2 hours and help someone who can only focus for 15 minutes."

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md

The July 2024 version adds weight-loss arithmetic: "If you've lost 10 lb you're probably going to be the better teacher than the person who is super jacked trying to help an overweight person lose weight, because the person who is just a few steps ahead of them, they can relate so much closer." With the bodybuilder, the beginner feels awkward and will not want to show up day after day: "they want a friend, they want connection, to be able to grow along with them."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

This is Koe's argument from experience and observation, not a cited finding from educational research; he offers it as a reason, and it is persuasive as such. It also links to the idea of "the Gap" above: the same distance that makes a beginner feel unqualified is what makes an expert a worse teacher for beginners.

#### How far ahead? The numbers vary

The distance Koe recommends is not constant. Justin Welsh speaks of two to three years (2021). Koe says "one to two steps behind" (2022, 2024), "one step behind" (2023, 2024), "someone that's right behind you" (2023), and in August 2024 "one to three steps behind." In March 2023, in a video on courses, he gives a different but related number: people do not learn best from someone ten steps ahead; "they learn best from someone that is two steps ahead of them," who has freshly solved the problem. That is why he usually creates his courses when he is two to three steps ahead of who he once was, right after he starts seeing results, and then iterates; he positions the product for someone two steps behind him at that point, so that it remains evergreen rather than being taught from the very high level he reaches later.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md

The two numbers can be reconciled, and the reconciliation is useful. "One step behind" is the *minimum* condition for being able to teach: you only need to know more than someone. "Two steps ahead" is the *ideal* distance from the student's point of view: close enough to relate, far enough to have a solution. The variation in figures depends on the context (content, a course, a service) and on the year, and Koe himself does not present it as a change of position. The reason he gives for proximity is constant: closeness makes identification easier and removes the anxiety of the gap.

#### Three things the productized did, and two more false beliefs

In December 2022 Koe summarizes the path of everyone who has "productized themselves," turned their skills and interests into a product. They did three things: they improved their quality of life through personal and professional development; they learned the necessary skills and interests along the way; and "they started posting online regardless of how basic they thought their information was."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md

The third item is the one the impostor objection blocks, and it is the only one that is public. Koe adds two more false beliefs elsewhere. The first concerns time. In a January 2025 conversation with Sahil Bloom, he says that "a lot of people got this false idea in their head that... in order to start working 40 to 80 hours a week on the thing that they want to build, they have to just start working 40 to 80 hours. When people don't have that time, they think that an hour is too short in the morning or 30 minutes is too short." What they need to realize "is that everyone starts that way. There are things you can do, and it's just up to you to find those things." This is the time-budget counterpart of the one-hour day described in Section 26.1.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

The second concerns other people's timelines. In March 2023 Koe describes a case he sees often: someone with a job, children and a spouse wants to start a business, sees people with few responsibilities flexing business success within six to twelve months, and adopts that expectation without considering their own context. They then build explanations for why they cannot do the same and how life is unfair because of their job, children or spouse. What they fail to face is reality: they did not take the opportunity earlier, and they cannot go back and change that. The sooner they accept reality and their specific situation and open their mind to the opportunities available, the sooner they perceive their situation through a more useful lens and act, even if progress takes much longer than for someone who started earlier. "The sooner you start the sooner you can suck less," and the sooner you see results, even if the time horizon is somewhat longer. Rather than adopting someone else's online goal, he advises zooming out, creating your own, and then looking for aligned mentors, education and advice for your specific situation, which "can't be prescribed or given to you."

**Source:** Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md

This case qualifies the benchmarks of Section 26.1. They describe what is possible for someone who can devote focused time; they are not a standard against which a person with very different constraints should measure their worth.

#### Uncertainty is signal

The last piece of Koe's answer to the beginner's fear comes from a September 2025 list of twelve rules for changing one's life. Rule nine is that **uncertainty is signal, not noise**. "You're supposed to feel lost. You're supposed to feel overwhelmed. You're supposed to feel like you have no idea what you're doing. Everyone feels that way." Did you expect all knowledge and skill to be deposited in your head the moment you started? "When you commit to excellence, you commit to a life of uncertainty because you commit to a life of learning. Your potential is determined by the amount of uncertainty you're willing to embrace." The most successful people "don't perceive uncertainty as something dangerous," because "all outsized gains lie in their ability to embrace, manage, and extend uncertainty." And the contrast: "The certain life is the least rewarding. A job is certain. Your paycheck reflects that."

**Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md

Placed at the end of this section, the rule reframes beginner hell itself. The feeling of not knowing what you are doing, which the 2025 video describes as the defining misery of the phase, is not evidence that you are on the wrong path; it is what being at the start of any learning curve feels like. What matters, as the next section shows, is whether that uncertainty is used to gather data or merely endured.

### 26.3 Validated Content

#### Two levers, and everything else is a distraction

The May 2026 video in which Koe compresses growth into two habits begins with the claim that organizes the rest of this chapter: "Two levers. That's what we need to know. Treat everything else as a distraction. Literally just focus on these two things." Lever one is "do what works from your own perspective," which he also calls **validated content**: "study what works and create your own opinion on it." Lever two is networking: making friends so that they share your content. The relationship between the two is practical. If the algorithm does not pick up your validated content, "which it probably won't, especially if you're a beginner," then "you have a manual way of making friends and having them share it to their audience so that it can at least get kick-started." And he insists on their nature: "these are not levers that you guess at. They're just levers that you pull."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Koe's term "validated content" has a family of variants across the corpus (validated ideas, validated structure, ideas "validated by the market"), which designate the same frame from 2022 to 2026: content, a topic or a structure that has already demonstrated that it works, redone from your own perspective. The phrase **do what works from your own perspective** appears only in this 2026 video, as the name of lever one.

The same video gives the levers a concrete time budget. "I would recommend prioritizing it. I would recommend time blocking it for 30 minutes on your calendar, so that you actually sit down and do it." The first thirty minutes "should be for researching, validating posts, and writing out ideas, and actually drafting the posts. And then another 30 minutes should be reaching out to people to get those posts shared." One hour a day, split evenly between the two levers, is the 2026 equivalent of the one to two hours of the 2024 "six things."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Koe adds a diagnosis of why beginners resist this. Introducing lever two ("you're going to hate this word"), he says that most beginners are "probably bringing consumer behavior over." They are used to scrolling and seeing posts do well, and they think "all I have to do is start creating posts and then one will do well and then I'll become famous." What they do not see is "that a creator treats it as a business because that's what it is at this point." He immediately qualifies the word: "a business doesn't have to be soulless. It can be very purposeful." For him, it has been "the most purposeful thing in my life" and led to drastic changes. And he defends selling: the thing sold, "preferably for a specific person, is valuable and it's worth paying the money and preferably it would be worth more than what you're charging." "It's called voluntary exchange."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The distinction between **consumer behavior** and creator behavior links this section to the consumer/creator opposition of Chapter 25. The consumer sees only finished results (the viral post) and infers that success is a lottery; the creator sees the process that produced them, research, iteration and distribution, and treats it as a craft.

#### Nobody is on social media to see something new

The first lever rests on an observation about why people consume content. A common beginner problem, Koe says, is "Oh, I don't have anything new to say." "As if people are on social media to see something new. They're not." Look at how you and he consume: "people are there to see what's already being talked about from a specific creator's opinion." This is the core of lever one, which "doesn't sound that great just yet, but I promise like this is the secret." And: "If you actually treat it as a priority, it will become a priority."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Later in the same video he restates the lever as a definition and a warning against complication: "The first one is just validated content. Study what works and create your own opinion on it. It is that simple. That's how you start practicing writing good content. Please do not over complicate that." And in the recap: "We learned about validated ideas. We learned how to find those. We learned that if you create your own opinion on them, you're setting yourself up to do really well."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The observation does two things at once. It dissolves the beginner's fear of having nothing original to say, which is a variant of the impostor syndrome of Section 26.2. And it moves the creator's contribution from the topic to the perspective: what a reader follows is not a subject but a person's opinion on a subject, which is the thesis of "you are the niche" in Part VII applied to individual posts.

#### From "write what interests you" to "research what works"

This position marks a shift of emphasis in Koe's teaching. In October 2022 he said "post the things that are interesting to you"; in February 2023, "write about whatever you want in a way that's interesting." In 2026 he says: "The best creators don't just post what's on their mind... Some do. I do sometimes. You should. But... that's not a strategy." The reason: "If you're treating social media as a way to pursue your life's work, then business is a part of that. Practicality, strategy, tactics." So "the best creators, most of the time, research what works and base what they're going to create about on that."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Koe does not present this as a retraction. He admits that he himself sometimes posts what is on his mind and says you *should* too. What changes is the criterion: one's own interest stops being sufficient on its own and becomes one side of a balance between art and business, informed by market research. The two positions can coexist according to the goal: expression for its own sake, or a body of work that also has to sustain an income. Elsewhere Koe describes this balance as the intersection of what performs and what excites you, and warns that artists who ignore the business side end up "starving."

#### The recommendation mechanism

The 2026 video gives a structural reason why validated content works, which Koe calls the **recommendation mechanism**: "That's literally how the algorithm works, especially YouTube. If you watch one video on a specific topic, YouTube is going to show you the same freaking topic from another creator." Therefore "if you create a video similar to one that has already done well, then you're going to be recommended to the people who viewed the one that did well." "That's YouTube growth in a nutshell. Every other course, all the other tactics are secondary. They're useful, but they're secondary." And he generalizes: "This is the single most fundamental aspect of social media growth on any platform."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

This is a significant development in Koe's view of the algorithm. In 2023 he recommended starting on platforms such as X, LinkedIn or Instagram, where growth can be controlled through networking, and avoiding TikTok or Shorts, where you depend on the algorithm; he spoke disparagingly of being a "slave to the algorithm." By 2026 the algorithm has become a mechanism to be exploited deliberately with validated content, though without abandoning the manual lever of networking, and in the same video he says that on Twitter the algorithm is "kind of non-existent," so that the manual lever matters more there. He gives no explicit reason for the change of emphasis. A plausible reading is that the two positions describe different kinds of platforms, recommendation-driven ones such as YouTube and "networkable" ones such as X, but that reconciliation is an inference.

His illustration is the case of Craig Perry, who, Koe says, "is just running my playbook": his YouTube bio is "literally reading my newsletters to a camera" (Koe notes that he too reads his newsletter as an outline to riff off, not word for word). Perry's first two videos ever got 230,000 and 144,000 views, "literally absurd... you do not see that for the first two videos." Did he get lucky? "Maybe." Would copying them one to one work? "Maybe. Maybe not." But "if you do this enough times, you will start to see a green streak of engagement and followers coming in." The first video was titled "How to remember everything you read": Koe has made almost the same title, so has Ali Abdaal, and a YouTube search shows ten to twenty versions. The second, "How to become dangerously self-educated," is fairly unique as a title but uses a validated structure ("how to become dangerously articulate," "dangerously confident"), "a rather new structure" that "still shows that it works."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The case distinguishes two things that can be validated: a **topic** (how to remember what you read) and a **structure** (the "how to become dangerously X" template). Either can be borrowed; what Perry adds is his own experience and opinion. The "green streak" is Koe's image for what accumulated repetitions produce: not one lucky hit but a sustained run of engagement.

#### Proven topics, your interests, and the reset period

How does one take a validated topic and make it one's own? Koe's 2026 example is the topic "how to get ahead of 99% of people." "Anyone can take the topic how to get ahead of 99% of people and just think, how would I get ahead of 99% of people based on my interests." It could be business ("start a business like this to get ahead of 99% of people"), finance ("budget like this"), or relationships ("how to get ahead of 99% of men," since young men "compete against each other in looks max all day and don't really get anywhere with it").

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

He adds a caution that complicates the idea of a "proven" topic: "It's a proven topic, at least for now, until it gets ran through." "You'll eventually learn that some topics that used to do well are kind of just dead now, or they've been exhausted to the point where they need a reset period." "How to get ahead of 99% of people" will "start to come back up in maybe a year, 2 years, and they'll flood the space, will be super viral for a second, and then it'll die down." The **reset period** is his term for the time an exhausted topic needs before it can go viral again. It turns validation into something dynamic: a topic's proof is valid for a time, and part of the research is noticing which topics are currently fresh, which are saturated and which are returning.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

#### Stealing like an artist: what can and cannot be taken

If lever one consists of doing what already works, the obvious objection is that it is copying. Koe answers it on several levels, and the answer has a history.

In the 2026 video he addresses the objection directly with Craig Perry. Are Perry's videos the same script as everyone else's? "No... He's pulling from his own experience, from his own perspective. It's his own opinion on how to do it." "Nobody has a novel idea. Novel ideas are extremely rare." He then draws a line. A topic or process such as "how to remember everything you read" is "a process. That's not something that you can claim." But "if it's... a unique category or a unique concept that you've created, then, yes, it is kind of copying if you take it." Semantics matter: Koe's own "4-Hour Workday" is a spin-off of Tim Ferriss's *The 4-Hour Workweek*, with his own framework, and if others took it, "I wouldn't be very mad because... I can't claim that." For tweets and reels, "nobody can claim the idea," but "the way that they worded it, the way that they structured it can be considered theirs." Copy-pasting with a few words changed makes you "look like an idiot," and Koe labels people who do it as those who "can't think originally." "But you can take the idea." His general rule: "Use your own judgment... if you're obviously just purely copying, then yeah, don't do that. But steal like an artist is a thing for a reason. It's because that's just how nature and reality works. You don't reinvent the wheel."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

The line can be summarized in a table, which reconstructs the distinctions Koe makes in this passage.

| Can be taken | Cannot be taken (or must be credited) |
|---|---|
| Topics ("how to remember everything you read") | A unique concept or category someone coined |
| Processes and common methods | The exact wording of a post |
| The idea behind a tweet or reel | The specific way a post was structured, if copied wholesale |
| A validated structure or template | Direct "rips" with a few words changed |

**Complementary context:** "Steal like an artist" is the title of a 2012 book by Austin Kleon; Koe uses the phrase without naming the author. The 4-Hour Workweek is Tim Ferriss's 2007 book; elsewhere in the corpus Koe says he never read it and misread its title as "4-hour workday," which became his own concept (Part V).

In August 2026 he states the underlying philosophy more provocatively: "Nothing is original, nothing is authentic. It honestly peeves me when people get in little arguments on social media of like 'Oh, you copied my idea.' But you're just looking through the lens of specificity." He builds a rhetorical chain: Did they copy your niche? You write about the same topics. Did they copy how you walk? "You both walk, you're both human, you both speak the English language. Did you come up with that all on your own? Did you come up with any of the words that you speak? Did you create the clothes that you wear? Have you actually audited the beliefs in your head? How original and authentic are you actually? Why do you treat this as some form of high value that you can stand on to virtue signal?" Then the limit: "I'm not saying that plagiarism is good... but people take it way too seriously... people are allowed to have similar ideas... if it's a direct rip, then yeah, credit the person. But if it's just an inspired idea, welcome to how the world works."

**Source:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md

The **lens of specificity** is Koe's name for the error of looking at a resemblance so closely that one forgets that everything is inherited: language, gestures, topics, beliefs. This is an opinion, stated with some irritation, and it should be read alongside the line he draws above; Koe does not defend plagiarism, and he asks for credit when the borrowing is direct.

The same month he offers a third-party image for the same idea, the bees of Seneca. "A metaphor from Seneca was to gather pollen from many flowers and then digest it into your own honey. That's his way of saying steal like an artist." He jokes that the author of *Steal Like an Artist* may have stolen it from Seneca. And he draws the practical consequence: "This is the antidote to writer's block. You don't have ideas for your writing content or videos because you don't have a repository of ideas to pull from." He adds that "every single thing I've said in this video is from other articles, videos, social posts, random ideas, and highlights that I've saved before."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

**Complementary context:** The image comes from Seneca's *Letters to Lucilius* (Letter 84), where he advises imitating bees, which gather from many flowers and transform what they gather into honey, as a model for reading and writing. Koe uses the metaphor as Seneca's version of the same principle, not as a scholarly reading of the letter.

The metaphor connects lever one with the note-taking and idea-capture practices of Chapter 16 and Chapter 22: validated content depends on a repository of saved posts, highlights and ideas, digested into one's own perspective.

This position has evolved. In 2022 Koe said that "people need to be reminded more than they need something new" and that all content is your version of what others say; at the end of that year he called stealing ideas valid but "overused," recommending synthesis instead, with the warning that copying without perspective does not last. In 2023 he said he had changed the saying to "people need to be reminded with something new." In 2025 he returned to the original saying to defend repetition, on the grounds that new audiences have not seen earlier content and that repetition conditions; and in 2026 validated content becomes "the secret," while the demand for an original perspective grows stronger ("nobody needs another person repackaging common sense"). The change is one of emphasis: stealing moves from a tolerated practice with a risk of commoditization to a central principle, always paired with a perspective of one's own. What is stolen is the topic and the structure, never the wording or coined concepts.

#### How to research what works

Studying what works is itself a method. In August 2026 Koe describes it: "If you're treating this as something that you actually want to do well at, you need a strategy. You need to study what other people in the market are doing... understand what works, and... let that inform your strategy." Use a research tool (he mentions Eden, among others); scroll the discover feed to see what is working; scroll social media and save posts "so that you have them ready when it's time to write." "You curate lists of creators, and you study them. You don't just blindly scroll." And then: "Once you have enough research, you begin to strategize how can you wedge yourself into the market by doing what works, but by doing also what is unique."

**Source:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md

The formula "what works plus what is unique" is the two halves of lever one stated as a strategy. The distinction between studying and "blindly scrolling" is the consumer/creator distinction applied to the feed itself: the same activity, done with a purpose and a swipe file, becomes research.

#### Make noise and find signal

Validated content tells you to begin from what already works. But what works *for you*, with your perspective and your audience, can only be discovered by testing. Koe's name for that process is **make noise and find signal** (2024), later **make noise, double down on signal** (2025). It is his own formula, and it is also his answer to the question of the niche.

The May 2024 version appears as a step in a video on generalists. Most creators struggle because they do not know what to write about. First, just start writing: nobody is watching when you have zero followers. Second, if friends follow you, maybe it is time they learned who you really are and possibly joined you; if they do not like it, maybe they do not really care about you, and it is better to find that out now. Third, write about all your interests: "make a lot of noise and let your audience decide what they want to hear more of from you." A few poorly performing posts are simply data. And posting good content is only one aspect of growth: "80% of your social growth comes from actually growing on social media," that is, from a growth strategy and the skill of social media, which is the subject of Section 26.4.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The February 2025 version, step seven of the way out of beginner hell, adds the balance between art and business. You can write about whatever you want; you do not have to be "a slave to the algorithm" if you understand what you are doing and have escaped beginner hell. Make room for both: templated "framework" ideas based on your offer, and ideas you simply like writing about. "Make a lot of noise": write about any idea your heart desires; pay attention to the ideas people resonate with (slightly more engagement, more DMs, more followers); and incorporate them as a consistent part of your content strategy. The conclusion is a rejection of niche selection in its usual form: "You don't need to choose a niche, you need to experiment with ideas until people tell you what niche best suits your relationship with your audience."

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

#### The counterpoint: the "in wedge"

Koe's guests did not all agree. In the January 2025 conversation with the founders of Stan, John Hugh offered what he called a polarizing take: when starting out, do not be as broad as possible. His analogy is memes. Think of all the memes and TikToks you have sent to friends: you do not remember the creators' faces, and of hundreds you might remember one or two "with a subconscious recall." Therefore, "they don't know who you are and they won't remember and they don't care who you are until you consistently show up in a really consistent way." The more specific you are early on, as "the beekeeping guy," "the crochet person," or "a personal trainer for moms over 40 who want more time back after their 9-to-5," the more likely that "if you show up three times you'll be remembered." His examples are real: beekeeping and crochet creators on Stan who make tens of thousands of dollars. His recommendation is to "find an in wedge and differentiate maniacally at that specificity and then expand over time and allow yourself to be more of a full person over time."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Koe's response, in the same conversation, is notable for not choosing a winner. He says, "at the risk of potentially discouraging action," that polarizing topics show that two successful people can do different things and both get results. He agrees that "you are the niche." What he did himself was "one focus area but I make a lot of noise on the side." On Twitter you can post a lot, so perhaps once a week he would post specifically on web design, and talk about self-improvement and mindset here and there, which gave him "wider data points where I can notice more anomalies between different topics and then start to merge them." His reasoning was that web design was what he charged for, "but it's not what I want to do in the future," so he did not want to go too deep into it. His recommendation follows: if you know what to wedge into, do it; for "the hyper polymaths that just don't know, just make a lot of noise, see what works and then stick with one thing for a bit if it does work."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Two more guest voices complete the picture. Hugh himself, asked by Koe what product to make first and what to do if one does not know which interests to pursue, says the second question comes first, "because you can't start monetizing until you actually have a clear understanding of who you are in your value proposition." If you are "a polymath and you have 15 different things, try all of them to a varying degree and you'll see what hits both from a market perspective but also an internal alignment perspective." That identity "can change at any time but initially you've got to do that work." His co-founder Vitali adds a criterion for choosing: approaching niche selection as "here's a range of topics I can talk about, I just want to invest in them next year to create content and digital products" "sounds like work, and anything that sounds like work tends to not work out in the long run." So there is "tremendous weight into finding something that you feel playful about and doubling down into that."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Two years earlier, in March 2023, Dickie Bush had proposed a version that combines both. On Twitter especially, "you can talk about a very specific thing and become known for that thing," and "that's a pretty good strategy in the beginning of just talking about what you're learning, what you're interested in." But alignment with people is discovered when they hear you talk about everything you are interested in, for example in long-form conversations where he revealed his fitness journey. Narrow, in his formulation, is for being known; broad is for finding your people. In the same conversation Koe warned against suppressing off-niche ideas.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md

| Approach | Who proposes it | Starting point | Later |
|---|---|---|---|
| Make noise, double down on signal | Koe (2024–2025) | Write on all interests; let the audience decide | Incorporate what resonates consistently |
| One focus area plus noise | Koe's own practice (described 2025) | One monetizable topic weekly, plus other interests | Merge topics where anomalies appear |
| In wedge, differentiate maniacally | John Hugh (2025) | Hyper-specific identity, remembered after three appearances | Expand into "a full person" |
| Specific to be known, broad to find your people | Dickie Bush (2023) | One specific thing on Twitter | Reveal other interests in long form |
| Try all fifteen interests | John Hugh (2025) | Test each against market and internal alignment | Settle on a value proposition (revisable) |
| Choose what feels playful | Vitali (2025) | Exclude what "sounds like work" | Double down on it |

The tension here is real, and Koe leaves it open. His public position after 2023 is that you should not choose a niche; his operational advice often includes an initial focus, and in late 2024 and 2026 he speaks of "one main skill or interest" to start from, or "one skill or interest you plan to monetize" plus two complementary ones. The most coherent reading, which his own practice supports, is that an initial focus serves monetization and early recognizability, while breadth serves the brand and the long term. Part VII treats the question of the niche itself; here the point is narrower: whichever path one takes, the method of discovery is the same, testing ideas and keeping what the data confirms.

#### Every post is a data point

The empirical core of validated content is iteration on data, and Koe has described its mechanics in many versions. The earliest, from November 2022, already contains the logic. You grow by writing basic, beginner-level content that works, and "it's not just writing something you think is basic: the idea has to be validated by the market." On social media some posts get 10 likes and some get 1,000; take the messages that get 1,000 and write about them more. Over time engagement increases, and you have "a consistent cycle of evergreen content" in your creative process. Then you can sprinkle in other things.

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md

In February 2023 he states the corollary for those who have too little data: if after two weeks you do not have enough data or feedback, you have to post even if it is bad, because "even bad posts, they're data points; good posts, they're data points." Take both and "dance between the two" until all your content reaches new levels, stages or baselines of development, an "evolutionary psychology lens approach to business," as he puts it loosely. Koe's term **data points** names this: every post, good or bad, is information for the next.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The October 2023 version turns iteration into a monthly routine. "With time, month after month, this is going to take 6 months to a year, as all good things." "Every month review your highest performing content: just go scroll through, see which does the best, copy paste the link to another browser... and then start to rewrite them from different angles: make some short, make some long, make some lists, make some the big idea, make a better hook, make a more clear big problem, make a certain actionable list." This is how "you gradually sustain an increase in engagement" and "increase your baseline of the persuasiveness and impact of your writing." And it has a commercial consequence: "it's not about just writing whatever you want, it's about iterating on the best ideas that your audience actually likes," which "points to a product that you should create with that specific angle." You can create any product, "but you need the angle that performs best in order to catch attention and actually sell it."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md

The **baseline** is the term that recurs in these passages: the ordinary level of performance of one's content, which iteration is meant to raise. The **angle** is the specific framing of an idea; rewriting a winning post from new angles both raises the baseline and reveals which framing an eventual product should use.

The May 2024 version extends iteration into the construction of assets. Review post performance every week to see which ideas did better; look for better writing or more engagement, and save both. Make the most of winners: turn them into longer-form content (a thread, a carousel, a newsletter, a video), rewrite the post from multiple angles, and bake the topic into your writing routine and brand. If a topic keeps doing well, establish authority with a **digital asset**: create a free product, pin a thread or carousel to your profile, and note it as a starting point for a paid digital product. The reason is subtle. A free product contains almost everything you know about the topic, so you can point people to it instead of repeating yourself and boxing yourself into a niche; you are then free to talk about other interests and bake the topic in here and there. His example is a fitness creator with a nutrition program, who no longer needs to talk about nutrition much: he bakes it in so that people become interested in the product or the free guide.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

This is a practical answer to the niche tension above. Validation could trap a creator in their one winning topic; the digital asset lets the topic keep working in the background while the creator makes noise elsewhere.

#### You cannot skip the reps

Iteration requires volume, and Koe insists that a new platform does not exempt anyone from it. In July 2023, when Threads launched, people thought "it's my time to shine." There is slightly more opportunity on a new platform, he conceded, "but you're still at ground zero in terms of your idea generation." Had you written 10,000 tweets, you would grow much faster on Threads; you cannot skip "those 10,000 iterations." "You must post, you need data, you need to refine your ideas." He himself could grow on Instagram from Twitter only because he had written some 20,000 tweets, of which about 100 were the best; he turned those into 100 Instagram posts, and they all "popped off." His rule of thumb: "under every 100 good posts there's a thousand bad posts, and if you don't put in those reps you're [screwed]."

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The case shows validated content working across platforms: the 100 best tweets were already validated, so their translation into another format was a test with very high odds. It also explains why the advice to "branch out" to other platforms (Chapter 25) is conditioned on having validated ideas.

#### Volume or scarcity?

On how much to post, Koe's advice has changed, and the change is best stated plainly. In 2022 and 2023 he recommended three tweets a day; in February 2023, posting even when the result is bad if two weeks have passed without data; in July 2023, one to three posts a day to "get the failures out of the way"; in 2024, two to three short posts a day and one or two long ones a week, and in a September 2024 sixty-day plan, three to five short posts a day, more than the one or two he "normally" advises. He explains that one or two is what he does now, "it's not what I did, I wrote more," and that "ideas beget ideas."

By July 2026 his position is different. More content than one to two hours a day is not needed. Alex Hormozi and others want you to push more and more volume, "but everyone's doing that." For a competitive advantage, do something different: tone it down, increase quality, make things "impossible to ignore." "Add a bit of scarcity to your brand so people aren't just overloaded with it." People then wait for you to post, and when they see something from you they are not used to it: "it's a dopamine hit for them when that's not happening for any other creator." Elsewhere in 2026 he speaks of "one piece of content a week," broken down into the rest of the ecosystem.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

**Scarcity** here is a common word that Koe uses in a specific sense: lowering the volume of publication so that each piece is anticipated. It is distinct from scarcity in pricing. The two positions can be reconciled by stage, and that is the reading the material supports: volume is for the beginner who needs data and validation; scarcity is for someone who already has an audience and a reputation for quality. Koe's own reason for the 2026 view, however, is competitive rather than developmental: if everyone pushes volume, the advantage lies in doing something different.

#### Show up and iterate: the success pipeline

The guests in Koe's 2025 conversations describe the iterative process in their own words, and their versions add something to his. John Hugh, asked for the big-picture steps from "I have this skill set or interest" to followers and income, answered that "it's literally never been easier" and that what is required is "almost just a mindset shift." He supported this with Stan's figures: 60,000 customers who have collectively made close to $250 million, many with stories like his own (an immigrant raised by a single mother, without connections, who started creating content while doom-scrolling TikTok during Covid and asking himself "what value do I have to give"). His conclusion: "it's permissionless now; we have some sort of value to give." The implication is that the barrier is internal, emotion and insecurity, not external access.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

**Permissionless** is Hugh's word here; Chapter 25 discusses the related idea of "permissionless leverage." Hugh then described what he calls "the written formula... literally all someone needs to do," and "the success pipeline that we see for all of our successful customers": "every day show up and iterate." Its components, in order: figure out what content to make (passion crossed with skill); every single day "show up and push yourself to post"; and treat each result as learning: "don't take it personally, use it as a learning however it performs." The steps are "really easy," he argued, and "we actually are the ones who make it harder on ourselves because of the emotion, the insecurity."

He went further, offering a guarantee: "if you sit there and you post for an entire year every single day, like, I guarantee you will be successful, cuz you will start to see the patterns." The mechanism is pattern recognition. Out of, say, 30 posts, 25 or 27 will "flop and get crickets" (and you will be embarrassed), but one or two will do well, and "you just double down on whatever that is." He cited himself, Koe and "everyone that we see" as testaments.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

A few days later, in February 2025, Koe qualified this from his side: consistency alone is not the answer; "you aren't making progress because you aren't doing the right things." The two positions are less a contradiction than a refinement. Both agree on iterating on data and doubling down on what works. Hugh's "guarantee" assumes that the creator actually reads the patterns; Koe's qualification points out that daily posting without reading them is just repetition. Consistency, in the combined view, is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.

Two guest stories from Koe's interviews illustrate the pipeline from the inside. Justin Welsh, in December 2021, described the moment he committed to writing: "One day I wrote something, and it went berserk," getting "three or four million impressions." Two signals converged: "A, I like this. I like to write. And B, like it seems to be working." From then on he stopped video and audio (except podcasts) and "started writing every single day. And my goal was to be a better writer." He argued that the platform was secondary: "LinkedIn is just, you're just typing into a box and hitting send. It doesn't matter if it's LinkedIn or Twitter." What mattered was "the practice of writing every day and shipping and publishing," which taught him "how to create as much attention for myself as possible." At the time of recording he had written every day for about three years.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

Welsh's story is a small instance of "double down on signal": one anomaly, combined with enjoyment, decided his medium. Sahil Bloom's origin, told to Koe in January 2025, is an instance of value as the criterion. Koe noted that Bloom "doesn't sell anything" and asked what drove him at first. Bloom had a high-paying finance job; Covid hit; there was no commuting, less work, no travel; he was bored and had a Twitter account. "All this crazy stuff was happening in the world of finance and business and all my friends were texting me because I was their finance friend." So he tried "explaining these things in simple terms." A few posts went viral: "Maybe I'm creating value for people. I'll keep doing this." He has "always... gotten a lot of energy from feeling like I had value to give others." He is candid that this is not pure altruism: "I like money... I've just always been able to ground that in the desire to create value for others. I have this suspicion that if I can create a whole lot of value for tons of people out in the world, that I'll do just fine financially." He was not planning to quit: "There was no chance I was going to leave a job to go tweet." His book was "the first thing that I'm... selling," after giving "everything away for free for... four straight years."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md

Bloom's story also connects with the friend test of Section 26.2: he began by doing in public what he was already doing in private, explaining finance to friends.

#### Nonlinear growth and exponential events

Iteration is linear; its results are not. Koe's term for the jumps is **exponential event**. In the February 2025 video he shows the follower graph of a Twitter user named Jackson, flat or even negative and then "boom, out of nowhere, just a bunch." "That is what I call an exponential event," which "we want to make happen and then replicate over and over again." In the same passage he states his credentials for teaching it: more than 3.8 million followers across platforms, "probably close to 4 million," and over 30,000 people taught.

**Source:** Don’t Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

In October 2024 he describes the mechanism in two videos. In one, he generalizes the idea beyond social media: "I call these exponential events, it's the nonlinear progress that you make." In the gym, for example, disciplined periods of diet and training build the most muscle even in year ten. On social media, you try different angles, ideas and experiments instead of trapping yourself in one niche doing the same thing over and over, which he calls Einstein's definition of insanity. You notice one that does well, "an anomaly," and you double down on it. It works for a while (his examples: when Instagram Reels arrived, carousels, different topics); eventually it dies because "the market catches on." You maintain the gain by incorporating it into your brand, and you keep experimenting until the next exponential event. That, he says, is how he grew by 200,000 YouTube subscribers when he first started doing well, and how he reached 1.2 million on Instagram.

**Source:** It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md

**Complementary context:** The saying "insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results" is popularly attributed to Einstein, but the attribution is doubtful; Koe himself uses it with varying degrees of confidence across the corpus.

In the other October 2024 video, this is step "four" (in his numbering) of the six things of Section 26.1, and he calls it extremely important: audience growth is nonlinear; you flatline with little progress for an extended period, then something works and you get viral growth all at once. On Twitter, perhaps one in ten or one in twenty posts brought many more followers; he repurposed and incorporated those to grow consistently. The pattern is slow or no growth, a big jump, repeat, until you integrate what works into your brand, those things come up more often, and you reach a baseline of monthly followers. Then you keep experimenting: "if you don't experiment you're never going to find out what works." He adds a reason to "be the niche": if you choose a niche that is too narrow, one you are not, exponential events are much less likely.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

He then gives three cases from his own growth. On YouTube, growth was slow until his podcast with Justin Welsh on the one-person business did extremely well; he talked more about the one-person business, more videos went viral, viewers flooded into other recommended videos, and he gained 200,000 to 400,000 followers in a short time. On Instagram, two trends came from experimenting. First, carousels did well; he had his tribe share individual slides to their stories on different days (the first slide one day, a middle slide two days later), which "would almost guarantee" the explore page, and he reached two hundred-some thousand followers. Second, animated reels: the first ten or so did extremely well, and "I went to 1.2 million followers in like the course of one or two months."

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The figures for his YouTube jump differ between the two videos (200,000 in one, 200,000 to 400,000 in the other); they are his recollections, not audited data. The cases are worth noting for what they show about the two levers working together. The YouTube jump combines validated content (the one-person business topic) with the recommendation mechanism; the carousel jump combines a format trend with networking (the tribe sharing slides on a schedule, which Section 26.5 develops). Koe's term **anomaly**, a post that performs far above the rest, often defined as at least twice the usual engagement, is the signal that exponential events begin from.

#### Sell what's already selling

Validated content has a commercial twin, and Koe treats them as the same principle applied to two objects: content and product. In January 2025 he made the connection explicitly in a metaphor: "Saying the same thing isn't bad because the best advice in business is to sell what's already selling, but with your own twist. So you stick your hand in the river that's already flowing. In business you stick your hand in the niche or the industry that's already making money. You're not going to somewhere where the money isn't and trying to attract it to you. You go where the money is or where the attention is, like on social media, and you plant your own unique flag there with what already works, but the key is to talk about those things with a novel perspective."

**Source:** You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md

The image (a river already flowing, a flag planted in it) applies equally to attention and to money. The principle itself, **sell what's already selling**, is one Koe has stated since 2022, and he calls it "the best single piece of counterintuitive advice I've ever received in my career." The fullest version is the story of 2 Hour Writer, told in December 2022 and repeated almost word for word in a February 2024 compilation. Koe created the course because he knew how profitable the writing niche was in his corner of Twitter: people were "banking like 2–3 million a year" running a cohort on basic writing, not promising money, nothing fancy, just "learn how to write," with good, compelling sales-page copy. He writes every day, so he got in; on Black Friday he made $130,000 in sales in three days. To create it, he went through the competitors' funnels, emails and landing pages and took inspiration from their headlines, the burning problems they discussed, their offer and curriculum structure, the guarantee, the benefits, the FAQs, and the positioning (for example, not being "how to make money online with writing"). That, he stresses, is inspiration, "clearly not just copy and paste": he has his own way and has to articulate it, but borrows the structure and "the why behind why it works."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The Black Friday figure varies across sources: $130,000 in three days in these passages, $139,000 over three to four days in another passage of the same 2024 compilation. It is probably the same event recalled differently; the units do not settle it.

In October 2023 he gives the principle as a remedy for a common failure. People often create a product and see zero results. His fix: "study the people that you already follow and that you know [are] selling the product or service and that it's actually selling; create it, take it, make it better under your own name. Sell what's already selling, stick your hand in the money that's already flowing... don't try to be clever, don't try to create something new, at least for the first product; just create what you know is going to work."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

In September 2023 he stated the same thing more broadly: "You can really monetize anything nowadays." Think of something you would actually want to monetize (not a joke like "dog poop"), look it up, and someone is already selling it: "study them, replicate their business model, do it under your name." "There's a reason you follow 10 to 20 people talking about the same thing online... why can't you be one of them?" If you are not making money, he says, there are three reasons: you have not developed a skill set; you have not created the product; or "you haven't stuck that product in the flowing water of money that's already there."

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

#### Buyers buy again

In December 2023 Koe adds the argument from the buyer's side. One route to a first product is a product or service you would buy, use and benefit from. "A business principle that most people glance over is to sell what's already selling. You don't try to create new markets just yet; you stick your hands in the ones that are already flowing." He suggests questions: what products have you bought recently, which did you use daily, what courses gave you your own results? "Buyers buy again, buyers buy again, buyers buy again. You're a buyer. What have you bought? Sell it, because someone else will buy it. Don't fall for the silly belief that the market is saturated."

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

At the end of that month he called this "the only shortcut in business": "Create a product that you would buy, use and benefit from." You belong to a niche and have bought products in it; if you understand business, you should "skip 50% of the trial and error and sell what you've bought but better." You know it sells, and your personal experience and interests set it apart. The resulting stack is skills, traffic (an audience you built as a creator), and a validated product.

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md

**Buyers buy again** is a principle Koe uses in several versions (buyers of a course move up to one-to-one services; someone who buys one course on a topic will buy more on the same topic). Here it means that your own purchases are evidence of a market. The idea of saturation is answered in Chapter 27.

#### Mother Nature and blue oceans

The negative side of the principle is a warning against originality pursued for its own sake. In February 2024 Koe treats it as "business 101": sell what is already selling, especially when starting out. It is not wise to chase **blue oceans**, opportunities that seem to have no competition, because "there's a reason for that: it's because people don't want it, it's because money isn't flowing there, and you think you're smarter than Mother Nature."

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md

**Complementary context:** "Blue ocean" comes from W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne's *Blue Ocean Strategy* (2005), which advises creating uncontested market space instead of competing in crowded "red oceans." Koe uses the term in its common business sense and argues against it for beginners; he does not engage with the book.

**Mother Nature** is Koe's term for what the market, that is human nature, wants, as opposed to what the creator's ego wants. In November 2023 he puts it as a rebuke: "people don't want what your ego wants, they want what Mother Nature wants, and who are you to act like you know what that should be?" Market demand reflects human nature, not the creator's preferences.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md

#### The consumer's eyes and the creator's eyes, and the limits of copying

Koe adds a perceptual point in September 2024. If you need help designing something to start testing with content, "research what other people in the topics that you like talking about are doing, and you can see exactly how they're making money. It's right in front of you; you're just looking at them as a consumer. So ask yourself: could you create the same thing but better?" His own example shows that "better" can mean ambitious: with Cortex, his software, "we're taking on a massive task of looking at the entire second brain space and creating something better"; "I just like aiming big; we may fail massively."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md

And the principle is not limited to digital products. In May 2022 he said that you can sell anything that is already selling: open a farm and have your followers go to the grocery store, or make and sell refrigerators. Media, the front end, is how you capture attention and potentially sell something of value.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

The principle coexists in Koe's corpus with apparently opposite advice about products. "You can't take someone else's planner, put your name on it and sell it" (2023); if you copy someone exactly, "you can't teach it" (2023); in 2025, "we don't want to take what's free and make it paid. We want to create something new." Koe's own reconciliation works by layers: what is replicated is the market, the marketing and "the why behind why it works" (headlines, problems, offer structure); what is created is the system, the unique mechanism that makes the product one's own. The Cortex example sits at the meeting point: same market, a better product. Chapter 33 develops the product side; here the point is that the logic is the same as that of validated content. In both cases one starts where attention or money already flows and adds a perspective.
