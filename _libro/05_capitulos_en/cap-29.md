## Chapter 29 — How to Start: Entry Routes, the Minimum Viable, and the First Client

Chapter 28 gave the one-person business its definition and its philosophy: a business is, at minimum, an audience and a product; the product is the path you walked to solve your own problems, packaged for the people one or two steps behind you; and growing the business and growing the person are the same activity seen from two sides. That chapter answered *what* the business is and *why* Dan Koe treats it as a life's work. This chapter answers the question that follows immediately for anyone who agrees: where do I begin, if I have no audience, no product, no track record and perhaps no clear idea of what I want?

Koe's answer has four layers, and the chapter follows them in order. Section 29.1 maps the *entry routes*: the two (sometimes three) ways a person with zero experience can start, which Koe calls skill-based and development-based, and the seven digital career paths he lays out as a progression of income streams. Section 29.2 isolates a pattern that runs through his whole system, borrowed from the start-up concept of the minimum viable product: the *minimum viable vision*, the *minimum viable goal*, and the first offer, which over four years he called the *minimum viable offer*, then the *micro offer*, then the *micro service*. Section 29.3 explains why he tells beginners to start with a service and real clients, why he insists on building an audience at the same time, and how his own career (from a $300 website for a mattress company to brand advisor for creators) illustrates the sequence. Section 29.4 gathers the attitude that makes any of this possible: the *permission to suck*, the idea he takes from Devon Eriksen and extends into a general doctrine of shipping the bad first version, failing in public and treating the first business as a prototype. Section 29.5 closes with the concrete plan Koe gave for the first $1,000 (the *60-day action plan*) and his criteria for deciding what to build.

Two cautions frame the whole chapter. First, almost every number here (prices, client counts, message volumes, timelines) is a rule of thumb from Koe's experience or from the creators he talks to, stated in a particular video for a particular audience; several of them change from year to year, and the chapter records those changes rather than choosing one. Second, the chapter is about *starting*. The ceiling of client work, the step from service to product, the roadmap by stages and the realistic timelines to large incomes belong to Chapter 30, which this chapter prepares.

### 29.1 Entry Routes: Skill-Based, Development-Based, and the Seven Career Paths

#### The human progression pattern

Koe's claim about who should start a one-person business is unusually broad. In a 2024 guide he says he believes the model "is the best route for 95+% of people who want to start a business," and his reason is not that it is easy or lucrative but that "it follows a human progression pattern, you evolve with time." The *human progression pattern* (his term) describes how people actually acquire something worth selling: they begin with no experience, acquire either a skill or a degree of personal development, and then educate others in what they acquired. The business does not ask the person to become someone else first; it grows at the same rate the person grows. This is the business-side expression of the thesis of Chapter 28, "personal growth equals business growth": if the business tracks the person's evolution, then every stage of the person's development is already a stage of the business.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

From that pattern Koe derives the two starting points that require "zero experience." He first stated them in October 2022: "it's either skill based or development based." The *skill-based* start means learning a marketable skill such as design, photography, email marketing, copywriting, SEO or web design; commercially, "this is like starting a freelancing business." The *development-based* start means self-development, "which is where a coaching business comes in." Both lead to the same second movement: you educate people on what you learned so that they buy your product or service. A writer teaches writing, gives writing tips and writes articles about writing; "a mindset guy" gives mindset tips and teaches people how to strengthen their minds. Development-based work, he adds, falls under the *Eternal markets*, the domains of human problems that never disappear (developed below).

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md

The two routes are not presented as equal in speed. The skill-based route "complements self-development so you can make money faster," because a skill can be sold as a high-ticket service: once you are good, "a high-ticket freelancing offer" can sell for $2,000–$3,000, sold either through your audience or through direct cold outreach. The development route is slower to monetize but, as the next pages show, it is the one Koe identifies with. The figure of $2,000–$3,000 is a 2022 observation that he repeats verbatim in the 2024 compilation; it describes what a competent freelancer can charge, not what a beginner charges on day one (Section 29.2 gives the beginner's prices).

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

#### Path one: learn a skill, sell a skill, teach a skill

Within weeks of naming two routes, Koe expanded them into three paths, and the first is the one most beginners hear about everywhere. He describes it as "the common advice," which he quotes as "learn a skill, sell a skill, teach a skill" (sometimes "learn, teach, sell"). This formula is not his; he adopts it as the standard skill-based route and then qualifies it. His verdict is double. On one hand, "I like that that works, you can make money." On the other, "that also makes you one-dimensional and if your entire brand or business is based around that then you don't really have a way of pivoting out in the future and then it gets boring and then you trap yourself in a new 9-to-5 and you can't get out of client work."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The objection is structural, and Koe gives three parts of it across his versions. First, a single skill is *one-dimensional* and *automatable*: you sell one thing, and that one thing can be replaced. Second, without anything beyond the skill you become "the freelancer with the same income who built themselves into a new 9 to 5," always "on the verge of losing every client," with "no backup or leverage like an audience" to pivot and follow evolving curiosities. The phrase *new 9-to-5* (also "second nine-to-five" and "new digital nine to fives" in other years) is one of Koe's recurring coinages: a client business that reproduces the logic of employment, with a ceiling on income and no real freedom, because each client is in effect a boss assigning projects. Third, path one "usually only pays attention to wealth," while human life has more domains than money.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

He does not tell anyone to avoid it. "If you do start with it, make it so you can evolve beyond it. Do not pigeonhole yourself into one niche." The practical instruction is to build a personal brand and a larger, more diverse audience alongside the skill. That brand also makes the skill easier to sell: clients "see your personality," and "you're going to land the client over another person if you have an audience... because they know you're legit." He is honest that at the beginning the brand will not do the selling for you; you will "usually still need the same monetization tactics as without an audience," which means the manual route of direct messages.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

In the 2023 "2.0" version of the model, path one is described in more operational detail. You learn a modern skill that creators or brands use (email marketing, funnel design, content writing), create content around it "to flex knowledge," and accept that this content "won't gain many followers"; your profile will function "more like a resume." Because you lack an audience or leverage to "sell a product in your sleep," you charge higher prices for freelance or consulting work. For any of it to work, he adds, you must learn the *evergreen skills* underneath the marketable ones: writing, speaking, marketing and sales (he refers viewers to his video on the "1 million dollar offer stack").

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

By January 2026 his description of path one has hardened. It is "the typical way": pick a marketable skill, create a product or service around it, talk about it online, make money. "The limitation here is the limitation of being a specialist." People niche down "because they were told it was most profitable, so now profit is your main interest," and the result is described in the vocabulary of Part I's critique of the default path: "productivity is still your god and you build yourself into a second nine-to-five working on things you don't care about for people you don't care about." The objection has moved from a practical risk (losing clients, getting bored) to an existential one (rebuilding the job you left).

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

#### Path two: the business of you

The second path is the one Koe calls his own: "the second path is development based and that's what I'm all about." It is for people interested in "human potential, self-improvement, psychology, metaphysics, philosophy, spirituality," and it is "based around the four Eternal markets which are health, wealth, relationships and happiness."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The *Eternal markets* are a term Koe uses across his business teaching; Chapter 19 presented them as the universal niche. Here he explains why they are *evergreen*. The problems in them "will never go away," because every *underdeveloped person* has unsolved problems and needs help "from someone of a like-minded personality." He immediately defuses the phrase: an underdeveloped person is someone "new to the space or new to improving their mind, body, business, spirit," with no negative connotation intended. The markets are evergreen not necessarily for any one individual (though people go through cycles and need help throughout life) but because "there's people born every day." He also anticipates the objection that AI will answer these needs: AI can help, but "the individual needs to change their behavior," and that is done best by learning from "a human who they can connect with." Against this, path one "only targets the wealth domain, so it's one-dimensional by default."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The count of Eternal markets is not stable in the corpus. In 2022 Koe lists health, wealth and relationships and then "adds" happiness; at one point happiness is a "bonus" tied to self-transcendence; in the micro offer framework of September 2024 (Section 29.2) he says big problems "should fall under the health, wealth and relationships eternal markets," three; two weeks later and in the 2024 compilation he lists four again. He never explains the variation. A plausible reading, consistent with how he uses the lists, is that he names three when he is looking for urgent, sellable problems and four when he is framing purpose and transcendence; the reader should treat the list as a working map, not a fixed taxonomy.

The content of path two is a three-part formula that Koe repeats almost word for word in 2022 and 2024. In the 2024 version: "With path two you quite literally pursue your own goals in life, [that's your] brand; solve your problems as you are pursuing those goals, [that's your] content or writing; and create a system to help others do the same, which is your product or service. This is how you be yourself, improve yourself and profit off of yourself. This is how you turn yourself into the business."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The formula maps the three pillars of Chapter 28 (brand, content, product) onto three activities of a single life:

| Activity in your life | Pillar of the business | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Pursuing your own goals | Brand | A public character with a direction others can follow |
| Solving the problems that block those goals, by researching your curiosities | Content | Information, lessons and value passed down as you learn |
| Reflecting on how you did it and turning it into steps | Product or service | A system that gets others to the same goal faster |

Koe gives the name *the business of you* to this path, and "be yourself, improve yourself and profit off of yourself" is his summary of it. The 2022 version adds the reflective step that produces the product. You self-reflect and look back: "How would I have done this better? How did I do this and what results did I have? How can I help others step by step get there faster?" "That step-by-step system is your offer or your product." He then lifts the formula to its largest frame. Helping people "actualize and transcend faster" advances "human evolution," and this is why he calls business "a vessel for self-actualization and spirituality." In the same 2022 passage he says that in the Eternal markets people pursue lofty goals with many problems in the way, and he names those problems "the profitable burning problems."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In the 2023 "2.0" model the second path is renamed: "become a value creator within your interests." The *value creator* is Koe's term (developed in Chapters 18 and 35) for someone who researches, synthesizes and teaches within their interests rather than entertaining or performing. The source passage relied on a graphic shown on screen that the transcript does not let us reconstruct, so what this version adds beyond the rename is not recoverable in detail.

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

The 2026 description of path two is the most developed. It is the path of creators like Koe "who aren't pinned to a niche" ("In one video, I talk about productivity. In this video... business"), producing "a synthesis of ideas with one overarching topic." These creators usually focus on one of the four Eternal markets, "where all human problems tend to fall, so that's where all human value tends to fall," or on all of them, bundled under *self-actualization*. "Most of the value creators online right now, they all have one niche. It's just a massive niche. It's self-actualization. What makes it unique is how you reach self-actualization or even get close to it. You don't even have to get there. It's just your aim." Because everyone pursuing self-actualization takes "infinitely unique paths," you document your journey and "take notes that are disguised as ideas." The phrase *notes disguised as ideas* is Koe's description of this kind of content: the creator is not inventing topics but publishing the record of their own learning in a form others can use. The three-part formula reappears unchanged: these creators pursue their own goals (brand), teach what they learn (content) and help others achieve the goal faster (product). By contrast, the skill-based path offers "very little path."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

This connects directly to "you are the niche" (Chapter 19): the development path is the business form of the niche of one. It also connects to Chapter 9's notion of the life's work, since the product of path two is literally the map of a life's project.

#### Path three: both, and why the one-person business forces you to be a generalist

The third path is the combination of the first two, and in every version Koe calls it his preference ("very much my speed," "Dan's ideal, more long-term"). In 2022 his argument was almost logistical: path three is "a given when starting," because building on social media forces you to learn skills. To grow at all you must design a profile picture and banner, write a bio and write posts. Each of those is a sellable service: you could become a Twitter ghostwriter, an Instagram post creator, a video editor or a banner designer. So you can monetize "nearly from the start" while getting real results on your own business, which means your portfolio consists of results rather than practice pieces. His example of testing a skill before selling it: build a landing page for an affiliate product to test your copywriting, and then sell landing pages to other creators for $1,000–$2,000.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md

The 2023 version adds a filter for which skills to learn: a personal brand forces you to learn most modern skills (landing pages, funnels, emails, content, profile design, graphics), and "if it doesn't help your brand grow it won't help others and it isn't worth learning." Your own brand is the test bench for every skill you will later sell. Once your brand gets results, you can help others for money, and what you sell "will usually be based on your interests." His worked example is bodybuilding: you get results for yourself, create a training program or offer health coaching, build your landing page, profile and content, and then decide what you like most among the things you had to learn (marketing, design or health itself). You are "practicing selling the health interest because you have results in that domain."

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

The fullest argument comes in July 2024. "Path three, which is very much my speed, is both... because the beautiful thing about a one person business is that you are forced to be a generalist: you need to learn any and all of the skills that make a business a success." *Forced to be a generalist* links this section to Chapter 20's defense of the generalist. Koe lists four benefits:

1. You learn skills that let you help other businesses for money. Building your audience means designing your own profile picture (graphic design), writing your own emails and your own content: "that's three skills alone right there."
2. You can practice marketing and sales without your own product by selling an affiliate product. His example is the affiliate program for Cortex, his writing app: "start talking about writing, help us sell it, and through that experience create your own... if you want to create an app and compete with us, try, we encourage you to." You can also build your own landing page for the offer.
3. Once you have results in the new domain, you can help other businesses and "work your way up the ladder in terms of how much you charge."
4. You acquire soft skills, which he calls *CEO-level traits*: "you need to acquire CEO level traits really quick, you need to learn to say no, you need to learn to systemize your work days into two to four to six hours a day," and to understand productivity. These traits "can then be packaged up": "you don't have to sell anything related to making money, you just sell the traits that allow you to live the lifestyle that you live," for example as teachings in psychology or productivity.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The fourth benefit matters more than it looks. It means that even the operational side of running a business (time management, saying no, systems) becomes development-based material. This is where the two paths merge, and Koe states the merger directly. Learning skills to build your business covers the wealth domain; the development domain enters because "once you're building the audience you're talking about your interests, you're talking about health... money, potentially wealth... relationships... happiness, so psychology, metaphysics, meaning, purpose." "All of those things are how you're building the audience and you're sharing what you learn along the way, so this is where they merge: you literally just build your own business without expectation of making money and you set yourself up to make so much money." "Just by becoming a creator you develop the interest and skill set that allow you to be future proof and hyper profitable."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

"Without expectation of making money" should be read with the rest of Koe's teaching, not as a promise. Elsewhere (Section 29.3 and Chapter 30) he is explicit that money in the first months usually comes from manual selling. The sentence describes an orientation: the business is built from interests and the record of one's own development, and the income follows from the skills that building it requires. "Future proof" and "hyper profitable" are his framing, not a measured outcome.

#### From three paths to one that contains the other

Koe's map of entry routes changed over four years, and the change is a refinement rather than a reversal.

| Date | Version | Status of the skill-based route |
|---|---|---|
| Oct 2022 | Two zero-experience routes: skill-based and development-based | A faster way to make money, complementary to development |
| Oct 2022 (repeated 2024) | Three paths: skill, development, both; path three is the ideal | A good start, but one-dimensional and a risk of a "new 9-to-5" |
| Jun 2023 | Three paths 2.0: learn/teach/sell a skill; value creator within your interests; both | A start whose profile works as a resume; evergreen skills needed underneath |
| Jul 2024 | Three monetization paths; path three because you are "forced to be a generalist" | It works, but "do not pigeonhole yourself into one niche" |
| Jan 2026 | Two paths again; the skill-based path is "encapsulated" in the development path | A specialist's "second nine-to-five"; still a stepping stone |

The 2026 version reorganizes the map into a hierarchy. "When you take this path [the development path]... the skill-based path is encapsulated in it. It's a natural part of it because you're building your own business." To build your own business you must learn marketing, sales, brand, content and the strategies, "and you just become inherently valuable when you can do that because you're learning the thing that actually gets results. Not all business owners do this. Go and talk to a small business and ask if they understand any of this. They don't." So the development path makes you valuable enough to be paid, perhaps not yet from your multiple interests themselves, "but it can be a stepping stone."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The change can be read in two ways, and both are supported. Read as a simplification, path three never disappeared; it became the definition of path two, because whoever builds a business from their development necessarily acquires the skills of path one. Read as a change of judgment, Koe became harsher toward the specialist over time: in 2022 the risk was boredom and fragility, in 2026 it is a life of work "for people you don't care about." He gives no explicit reason for the shift; the implicit one is the critique of the specialist that runs through Chapter 20. What remains constant across every version is the practical advice: whichever path you start on, build so that you can evolve beyond it, and build an audience while you do.

#### The seven digital career paths

In January 2024 Koe gave a different kind of map, organized not by where you start but by how an audience can be monetized. He calls them "seven digital career paths or seven income streams," while also saying they are "pretty much five different kinds of products or services"; the mismatch between seven and five is in the source and he does not resolve it. The point of the list is "a diversified income through the audience you build," and he treats the first paths as a progression (first work for someone else, then start a service business), while paths five to seven are not recommended for beginners but are worth knowing as options to work toward.

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

**1. Work with another creator or brand.** Most people see only two options, getting a conventional job or building their own business from scratch, and both are good, but there is a third: work for a creator or brand to gain the experience to build your own. "Your profile is your digital resume; your content is how you attract opportunities and gigs." (*Digital resume* is a variant of the term Koe more often calls the *public resume*, the profile and content that work as a curriculum vitae.) The more people who know what you do, the more may hire you. The skills required are the ones a creator or brand needs and the ones you already practice by building an audience: social media growth, content writing, email copywriting, funnel building, landing pages, brand design, lead generation or marketing. His learning rule: "Pick one, learn it, implement it, pick the next, learn it, implement it, then write about it, give advice, teach." Some creators may reach out; others you will have to contact; nothing is guaranteed, but you increase your chances by growing your audience and reaching out to more creators, and you will have to learn these skills along the way anyway.

**2. Start a service business** (tutoring, freelancing, coaching or consulting, agency). You can go straight here if you have a skill that has gotten you results, "anything that has solved the problem in your life or helped you achieve a goal," preferably in the health, wealth and relationships markets. "Fitness bro, good to go; mindset bro, good to go," though mindset is harder to frame and position; a business, marketing or skill person (web design, graphic design, productivity) is also good to go. Or, if you worked for a creator, you package what you did for them into a service, since the only reason you worked for them is that it got them results. Services are good starters because a client can pay from $1,000 on the low end to $50,000 on the extremely high end, and Koe tells beginners to aim for $1,000–$5,000 rather than selling a $50–$100 product. The reason is explicit: "You start a service business because your audience isn't large enough to monetize via other avenues." At $2,000 a month per client, two to four clients a month is a decent income.

**3. Affiliate marketing.** Koe is "not a huge fan," but it has pros and cons. You get an affiliate link from a creator, software, product or service and earn a commission on sales. His hypothetical: if he offered an affiliate program for his course 2 Hour Writer (he does not: "it's just a pain"), you could build content and a brand around writing, plug the link and earn 50% per sale. The advantage is that you need no product, experience or expertise yet: you write about the product based on what you learn from it, build an audience around that, monetize along the way, and later create your own product, already knowing how to sell it and already having an interested audience. Two conditions: affiliate only for products you believe in and will promote consistently or include in a course (his example is the Tweet Hunter link in 2 Hour Writer, the tool he used to schedule content when he had no such software of his own), and "the point is to not get stuck in this phase and build your own thing."

**4. Digital products** (courses, cohorts or memberships, "for simplicity"). These earn well once you have a large enough audience and the ability to get results, but Koe prescribes a progression rather than a jump: monetize with a service and grow your audience; get results with the service and grow your audience; turn what you offer in the service into a digital product and grow your audience; get product buyers and upsell them on your service and grow your audience; "then do whatever you want," including quitting client work for more free time and to "desaturate the market for beginners." *Desaturate the market* is his term for what happens when someone who has evolved leaves an offer behind: room opens for newcomers. He uses himself as the example. He is still a creator with a large audience, but he no longer occupies the web design market; he sells a book, some courses will phase out, he will write more books and is building software. And against the fear of saturation (taken up fully in Chapter 27): "if you think saturation is such a big deal, it's usually because you're in an echo chamber of people that only sell that thing."

**5. Sponsorships and ad revenue.** YouTube, newsletter and podcast sponsorships can be good money, but they require a large engaged audience, "and if you have one, why not build a digital product and keep all the profit?" His example is MrBeast, who could not accept a certain sponsorship because the company could not afford his prices, since he makes more from his own products. If you know something will sell to your audience, create it yourself rather than giving away promotional space; with both sponsors and products you would be promoting for the entire video. "If you really want to be sponsored by someone and you love their company, just build your own competitor and take over." The same applies to YouTube ad revenue: stop caring about it, because "if you're focused on it, you aren't building a business."

**6. Physical products**, only once you have an audience. Koe sold a planner. A physical product that complements what you do is an option, less profitable than digital products "but fun," and it still brings money.

**7. Software or service scaling.** You can build software based on the systems you created in your products, or scale a service business (freelancing, tutoring, consulting) with a team into an agency. This path is "highly dependent on the lifestyle you want." Koe "took the step off the deep end" by building software, which could take over much of his life, whereas the year before, with the 4-hour workday and the one-person business, "you can make a million to 5 million a year and live like a rich person doing that." Whether to push further depends on personality, and "you sure as hell don't have to."

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

| Path | Requires an audience? | Koe's stance |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Work for a creator or brand | Helpful (profile as digital resume) | A legitimate first step to gain experience |
| 2. Service business | No | The default start; aim for $1,000–$5,000 clients |
| 3. Affiliate marketing | Builds one | Acceptable, but "don't get stuck in this phase" |
| 4. Digital products | Yes | Reached through the service → product progression |
| 5. Sponsorships and ad revenue | Yes, large | Discouraged: build your own product instead |
| 6. Physical products | Yes | An option if it complements your work |
| 7. Software or service scaling | Yes | Depends on the lifestyle you want; optional |

Two observations help place this list. First, paths 1 to 4 are a ladder that the rest of this chapter climbs: 29.3 develops path 2 and the step toward path 4, and Chapter 30 takes path 4 further. Second, path 7 is where the one-person business reaches its edge. Koe's own decision to build software is the case Chapter 37 examines as the move "beyond the one-person business."

#### A middle path: work for a creator, then build your own

Path one of the seven deserves a closer look because it answers an objection many readers have: that they cannot afford the risk of starting from zero. In a December 2023 video Koe presents working for a creator as a middle path between a traditional job and entrepreneurship. You can take either path, but entrepreneurship is "an eventual step if you want full control over life and income." Rather than a traditional job "that doesn't live up to its promises of security," "you can create your own security by working for a creator," learning the skills that make you "an irreplaceable asset in any creator business," and then end up creating your own so that you control your direction. The *irreplaceable asset* is the person who has mastered the skills any creator business needs; it is a neighbor of the "irreplaceable individual" of Chapter 35.

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md

In an August 2023 video he spells out the progression he would follow himself:

1. Learn the skills all brands need: marketing, sales, branding, content, design, social media.
2. Apply for jobs or work for a creator to get real-world experience and earn, either as a freelancer or as an employee. This means reaching out and talking to people online ("oh no").
3. Dedicate "one hour minimum each day to building your own thing": write content, iterate until it grows, promote your offer as the audience grows.
4. Start transitioning into doing your own thing.
5. Expand your *product stack* (the set of offers at different levels) to serve more people in your audience.

This makes it "near impossible to not secure your future." He adds an observation about why the audience matters more than marketing skill: with subpar marketing skills, a good audience can still compensate, because people trust you, like your thoughts and buy, so that marketing and sales are "like a little boost." This compensation is not possible with paid ads, because writing the ad is itself marketing and sales.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md

The progression connects back to the "one hour a day" of Chapter 12 (and the 365 hours of the highest-leverage task mentioned in Chapter 28) and forward to Chapter 30's roadmap. It also illustrates the general logic of the entry routes: every route starts by acquiring a skill or a result in the real world, keeps the audience growing in parallel, and treats the first source of income as a stage rather than a destination.

### 29.2 The Minimum Viable Pattern: Vision, Goal, Offer

#### A borrowed idea applied to a life

The *minimum viable product* (MVP) is a start-up concept: release the smallest version of a product that can be used, so that real use tells you what to build next. Koe did not invent it and never claims to; what he does is generalize it. He applies the same logic to the vision of a life, to the first goal, to the first offer and, eventually, to "everything." The relevant passages are explicit about the borrowing: "you need a minimum viable vision like a minimum viable product in business"; "there's minimum viable product but an offer is like all encompassing"; "a vision is like a product."

**Complementary context:** The minimum viable product became a standard term of start-up practice through the "lean start-up" movement of the late 2000s and early 2010s, associated especially with Eric Ries. Its core claim is that learning what customers want requires putting something in front of them early, because plans made in isolation rest on untested assumptions. Koe uses the idea without citing that literature; his adaptations are his own.

The pattern has one premise, which Koe states in many forms and which Section 29.4 develops fully: *you can't iterate on something that doesn't exist*. A plan in your head, an imagined product or an unwritten vision cannot be corrected, because there is nothing for reality to push against. The first version therefore does not need to be good; it needs to exist. This section applies that premise to three objects in the order a beginner meets them: the vision (what life you are aiming at), the goal (what you put energy into now) and the offer (what you sell first). Koe himself ties them together under the phrase *minimum viable everything*, examined in Section 29.4.

#### The minimum viable vision

The *minimum viable vision* first appears in January 2023 as "Commandment 2" (vision and identity) of Koe's 4-Hour Workday framework, and he repeated the passage in a 2024 compilation. "You need a vision for your future, and the way that I like to paint this is that you need a minimum viable vision, like a minimum viable product in business. You just need to get it out so that you can make connections when you have certain experiences and are able to associate ideas with that and make it better with time." The exercise is to write, as specifically and in as much detail as possible:

- who you want to be;
- what you want to do for work;
- where you want to live;
- what your ideal day looks like;
- how you will contribute to humanity;
- what people you will surround yourself with (your network and social circle).

"Feel free to let the stream of consciousness keep going." The purpose is practical: to set a foundation for the goals that a daily productivity system works toward. Without the vision, the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12 has nothing to aim its four hours at.

**Source:** The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md

The mechanism in that passage deserves attention. The written vision is not a fixed target but a *receiver*: once it exists, experiences "connect" to it and ideas associate with it, and those associations improve it. Koe gives the same mechanism a memorable name in July 2024. "If you don't have a vision you're lost, kind of by definition; you can't create outcomes, so you're doomed to the mechanical living of determined outcomes." (*Mechanical living* is his term for a life that runs on outcomes others determined.) "Every decision you make in any domain of your life must be filtered through your vision": that brings meaning to actions and minimizes distractions. Write down everything you want, every detail, but it "doesn't happen overnight": "think of it as a minimum viable vision." Write one thing in a note, on paper or in a to-do list and add over time. Those *figments of consciousness*, as he calls the fragments, "culminate in your mind and self-correct your behavior," letting you spot problems, goals and opportunities you would otherwise miss.

**Source:** Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md

This is the model of perception from Chapters 3 and 6 applied to the vision: the mind registers what fits its frame, so even a fragmentary vision begins to filter what you notice. The phrase "self-correct your behavior" describes the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6, where a goal held in mind steers action by detecting the gap between where you are and where you aim.

How long should the exercise take? Koe gives different answers, and they are best read as describing different things. In April 2024: "Spend 30 minutes creating a minimum viable vision" for whatever you are creating, starting with your life's vision, and "come back often to add, subtract, remove"; "you won't get it right the first time around and you probably never will, and that's not the point." In July 2023, by contrast, he says the vision cannot come in 10–20 minutes ("literally impossible") and "culminates, expands and develops over years and years and years of experiencing life, failing, succeeding, and having data points from which to iterate on your vision." In September 2025 he went further, saying that rejecting the average life "isn't a 30-minute exercise... this is a lifelong thing." The positions are compatible if the thirty minutes produce the first version and the years produce the mature one, which is exactly the MVP logic: a quick release followed by indefinite iteration.

**Source:** Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md; A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md

Koe pushes the product analogy further in May 2024. Write down exactly what you want, "don't miss a detail, but realize that this is an iterative process." The vision is "like a project that you build infinitely, that you iterate on infinitely": you keep "adding to it, refining it, subtracting, changing the features." "A vision is your life project personified," he says, and then corrects himself: "personified" wasn't the right word, "but I think you get the point." The point is that the vision is to a life what a software project is to a company, a thing with features that are added and removed as use reveals what works. The term *life project* links the vision to the project of Chapter 9.

**Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md

In January 2024, in a protocol for resetting a life in seven days, the questions become broader and the rhythm daily. Write in a planner or notebook: What do you want your life to look like? Your mind? Your body? Your house? Your day? Your week? Write everything down, come back tomorrow, then the next day, and refine. It does not need to be perfect; you need "a starting point to frame your decision-making," so that you can begin the "I don't want this, I want this" process until things become clearer. He adds a limit: you will never figure it out 100%, perhaps a few select things, but not everything life is composed of. In March 2024 he states the reason directly: "treat this as a minimum viable vision to start, meaning that you don't have to worry about it being perfect or complete. You can add to it over time because you're not going to think of everything you want now. You have to experience life to be able to have the idea of what you want in the future."

**Source:** How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md; If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

The same March 2024 video shows where the vision sits in Koe's larger sequence (Chapter 7). Step one is the *anti-vision*, the picture of the life you refuse to live. "Step number two is to identify solutions with a vision, because now when you create your anti-vision you have a plethora of problems that you can solve: pick one, solve it, move on to the next." Here the vision is defined less as a destination than as the generator of problems to solve, and Koe attaches to it one of his central claims about meaning: "Meaning is found not in the achievement of a goal but in solving the endless string of problems that pull you deeper into the nature of reality." To strengthen the desire to solve those problems, "create a vision right out the opposite of what your anti-vision was."

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

The "opposite of the anti-vision" is also his answer to the person whose vision is weak. In 2023: "Your vision is imaginary"; it should culminate over time. "Write out the opposite of your anti-vision because your vision may not be strong yet." The anti-vision is easier to produce because its materials are observable (the lives around you that you do not want), while the vision is a projection into a future that has not happened. Inverting the first gives a provisional version of the second.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md

Two more resources for building the first version come from different years. In January 2023 Koe suggests mining past positive experiences for sensory detail. When you went on vacation and thought "I would love to live in this location," you can reflect back and recover the same feelings: how the air felt, the sun on your skin, the palm trees, the interior design of the hotel. The specific things you like and dislike, collected over time, help construct a vision you can work toward. Again, it need not be perfect at the start: if you know you want to live in a remote tropical location, you can start working toward it, building a life that allows it, and spotting the problems in the way.

**Source:** How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md

In December 2025 the exercise acquires an identity component, in a protocol for "fixing your life in one day." "A vision is like a product": it starts unclear and, with time, practice and experience, becomes better and more polished. Three questions:

1. Forget practicality. If you could snap your fingers and live a different life in three years (what you actually want, not what is realistic), what does an average Tuesday look like, described in the same detail as before?
2. What would you have to believe about yourself for that life to feel natural rather than forced? Write the identity statement "I'm the type of person who..."
3. What is the one thing you would do this week if you were already that person?

**Source:** How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md

The evolution is instructive. The 2023 list asks *what* you want (work, place, day, contribution, people); the 2025 protocol adds *who* you would have to be (the belief that makes the life natural) and *what* you would do this week. It joins the vision to the identity model of Part II (Chapter 4: all change is identity change) and to immediate action. The "average Tuesday" is a deliberate choice: an ordinary day reveals the structure of a life better than a peak moment.

The strongest objection to all of this is the one Koe hears most: "I don't know what I want." His March 2025 answer is that the objection describes a mind "in this little box" that "isn't trying to open the box": "I'm not interested in anything," "I don't know what I want, don't want, want to try" are reactions to small thoughts that are never followed further. A vision starts like school or a job: it "sucks" at first and feels inauthentic, but you need something on paper to improve. His example comes from designing screens and features for Cortex: they are "just theory" until people use them, and then "most of the time it doesn't work" and you know how to improve it. "That's called iteration. You can't iterate on something that doesn't exist or that's stuck in your mind." Then life shows you "I don't want this, I do want this," and you refine and slowly course-correct.

**Source:** How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md

In December 2023 he connects the same principle to stress. The vision will change, "that's actually a good thing, this is how you refine and iterate with time, but you can't iterate on something that doesn't exist." Without a written vision or direction you cannot course-correct, and you will not make meaningful progress toward a goal *you* chose. You can progress all day toward goals other people assign you, but then you wonder "why do I feel stressed, why do I feel overwhelmed": because the goal was assigned and you accepted it without question. The minimum viable vision is thus the practical antidote to the *assigned goals* diagnosed in Part I.

**Source:** You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md

Koe's position on whether a person can genuinely not know what they want moved over the years. In 2022–2023 he admitted the lack of clarity and offered workarounds ("if you don't know what you want, understand what you don't want"); in 2024 he read the claim as evasion ("you're lying to yourself"); in 2025 he said "you will never know what you want. It's in the future... It's imaginary." Koe does not reconcile the three positions himself; in the corpus they stand as an unresolved contradiction, and each is stated with conviction in its year. The reading that follows is this chapter's, not his. The minimum viable vision fits all three versions: it is a way of acting before clarity exists, whether the lack of clarity is a temporary gap, a self-deception or a permanent condition of a future that has not happened yet. One way to hold them together, again not Koe's own, is that you do know a general direction but cannot know the details: the indulgent version serves the beginner, the harsh one the person who has experience and is avoiding the work.

#### The minimum viable goal

The vision describes a life; the goal is what you put energy into now. Here too Koe applies the minimum viable logic, and he does so because he sees goal-setting as the point where most people stall. In October 2022 he calls step one, "set a meaningful goal," the step that "trips most people up." He supports it loosely, citing "any statistic about 99% of people not following through with their New Year's resolutions" (the figure is given without a source and should be read as rhetoric), and "way too many people not having goals in general, not having something that they're stretching towards, not having something that they can put energy towards."

**Source:** How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md

His May 2024 answer is to lower the bar for the first goal. "The first step to getting out of this rut, or just taking back control of your life, is one: just having a goal." If you say you do not know, he assumes "that you have other people that you aspire to be like... don't tell me that you don't know what you want to be or what goal you have, you're lying to yourself, just sit back and think." The goal "doesn't even have to be that accurate at first, it just has to be a minimum viable goal, something that you can at least start to put effort and energy into." The *minimum viable goal* is therefore an imprecise first goal whose only requirement is that it can absorb effort.

**Source:** My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md

Why is an inaccurate goal enough? Because, in Koe's account, the feelings people wait for before choosing a goal are produced by pursuing one. "Putting effort and energy into a goal is the only time when motivation and passion will start to build. You don't just become motivated or become passionate: it comes at the inception of a goal, and investing energy into the goal, actually moving toward the goal, is where those things come into play." This is the thesis of Chapter 8 ("passion is created") restated as a reason to start before you feel ready.

**Source:** My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md

Two months earlier he had formulated the mechanism behind it. "Remember, goals don't start out magnetic. You probably don't care about the goal. You must invest energy into the goal until you feel as if you are wasting resources for not achieving it. Your goal must frame your perspective for enough time to register reasons to continue pursuing it." The phrase *goals don't start out magnetic* names the asymmetry: attraction to a goal is the result of investment, not its precondition. The last sentence is again the perception model of Part III: a goal held long enough begins to *frame* perception, and only then does the mind register the reasons that sustain it.

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md

The June 2023 "2.0" business video gives the fullest version of the mechanism and links it to business directly. Many people say "I don't have any interests," because school "force-fed" them what to be interested in. You generate interest in a topic by investing energy into a goal, so that you feel you would waste the investment if you did not achieve it: you need pressure, the pull, something on the line. "You need mental currency, AKA focus, invested in a goal so that you feel pulled to actually achieve it." *Mental currency* is his name for focus treated as a resource that, once spent on a goal, makes the goal valuable to you. He then distinguishes *true goals*, "based on the Eternal markets" (health, wealth, relationships, happiness) "in pursuit of self-actualization," from *cheap desires*, "fabricated wants" projected onto you by others. The interests and skills learned along the way are how interest is generated: pursuing health, you find through trial and error your own path through nutrition, a diet and a form of training (bodybuilding, running, cycling). Later in the same video he restates it: you have to start and invest energy in the goal, then you feel you are wasting it if you do not complete it, and that is "the only way" to expose yourself to experience and find "the whys" that keep you going.

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md

In December 2023 he names the bias that makes this work. When leaving your job for a business is a potent goal and you have invested some energy in it, "the sunken cost fallacy comes into play, where I've invested this much time, I might as well just try to achieve the goal." His concrete sign of the change is a person's search history: Google searches move from "best careers to go into in 2024" to "best business models to start in 2024," and you become more compelled to seek out resources. (In the same passage he recommends his own courses, 2 Hour Writer and Digital Economics, for starting what he calls a "synthesizer business," describing their principles as evergreen; this is self-promotion, not evidence.)

**Source:** You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md

The use of the sunk cost fallacy is deliberate and slightly provocative. **Complementary context:** In behavioral economics, the sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to continue an endeavor because of resources already spent, even when future costs outweigh future benefits; it is normally presented as an error. Koe inverts its valuation: for a goal you have chosen, the same bias becomes a commitment device. He does not discuss when the inversion fails (a goal that should be abandoned); Chapter 8's treatment of anti-goals and Chapter 15's treatment of errors as feedback supply the counterweight.

There is a tension in the corpus that this section should not hide. In the same March 2024 video in which goals "don't start out magnetic," Koe also says "stop focusing on the goal," because "a problem creates a goal" and without a felt problem the goal has no gravity. In 2022 the first step was to set a meaningful goal; in March 2024 it was to become aware of the problem; in May 2024 it was "just have a goal" again, because a mind focused only on the problem digs itself deeper. The positions can be reconciled by audience (problem awareness for someone who perceives no problem; a minimum viable goal for someone who needs to move), but Koe does not reconcile them himself.

#### The minimum viable offer

The same logic, applied to what you sell first, produced the most practical of Koe's coinages. In October 2022, answering the question "what do I sell?", he names it: "There's minimum viable product, but an offer is like all-encompassing," covering products and services, so he calls it the *minimum viable offer* (MVO). It has two forms:

1. "A single freelance skill that you sell for $500 to $1,000," such as web design or email marketing. "Not a crazy offer"; "you'll never charge $5,000 for it," but "you have to start somewhere."
2. "A single interest consulting service where you sell a pack of four calls for $500 to $1,000."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

Why start here rather than with a course or an e-book? Because you can monetize immediately. Selling happens in direct messages, or through a Twitter thread or post that others retweet, after which you plug the service; if it goes viral, "a million views on your offer": "it's traffic and it's offers," the minimal definition of business from Chapter 28. And because the offer builds the next product. You teach people, get them results, "note the commonalities between what you're talking about on each call, and you package it up" into a course, an e-book or a cohort. You can then take on less client work as your audience grows and gain free time. The MVO is therefore not a business model of its own but the first rung of one.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md

Already in 2022 Koe states a preference between the two forms, in a passage that reveals his own taste as a buyer. "There's a stigma where people think they must do the work and can't just teach." But "as a creator, me, I hate paying for work. I'd rather pay you to get on a call with me and consult me" on how to do a specific thing, skill or not; for health, he wants to be taught. So if you are starting an education business, "don't do done-for-you"; offer consulting calls or a digital product in health, fitness, performance, mindset, life or business coaching, or in deeper sub-markets ("bodybuilding coaching," "neural hacking consulting," as transcribed). You find what to teach by diving into your *domain of mastery* (his term for the structured map of your interests), "the crevice of reality" that composes the interest you want to explore. He is surprised that *digital tutoring offers* are not more common: teaching a creator over four calls how to design a great website, write compelling emails or run Facebook ads. He considers these "much better and more scalable than a freelance offer."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The earliest version of the MVO in the corpus (June 2022) adds the sales process and the ladder it belongs to. As you build skill and an audience, you can charge (people, he notes, have "a scarcity mindset around money"). Help those "one to two steps behind you" with marketable skills (branding, persuasion, copywriting) or another interest (leadership, communication, web design). Start with an MVO of roughly $500–$1,000; talk to people and pitch in DMs. The process: reach out, connect, ask what their problems are, see how you can help. Being interested in their problems is "the foundation of value." Offer to help for free at first to see if you get results; then create a pack of four consulting calls, a form of online mentorship: "Hey, I'll sell you four calls for a thousand bucks." Then iterate: helping others expands your awareness of what gets results, which lets you craft a replicable process to package as a pricier consulting or freelance offer, a digital product, or a physical product (he used to sell a planner).

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md

In the same video Koe describes the whole trajectory as "working your way up the ladder." Start posting online. Hit roadblocks, which are problems others face too. Write about the problem, about overcoming it, or about what you are learning in order to overcome it. Develop confidence giving that advice or system, which attracts people you can help. (If you already have expertise, talk about it in an engaging way.) Eventually package a system and sell it. The ladder runs "from free to paid": MVO (a basic one-skill freelance service or four consulting calls) → get better → a better solution → charge more → productize your creative system and sell it to your audience. The engine is the principle of Chapter 28: "you solve your own problems or someone else's problems."

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md

In March 2023 he adds a constraint on what the MVO should be built from: a single skill or interest. "Don't go learning 8 billion. Learn one." Charging "big bucks" takes time, experience and *multidisciplinary skill acquisition* while building in the real world, "actually working with customers, not reading about it." So you immediately turn the one skill or interest into a freelancing, coaching, consulting or (his preferred word that year) *tutoring* offer.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

In July 2023 the MVO appears as step 5 of a small business hierarchy. To start, build a smaller structure: content, a newsletter, a lead magnet, a service; later add a product, coaching, an event. The reason the service comes right after the lead magnet: "you need a next step or a monetizable way to help the lead magnet downloaders that want more." Ask "what's the next logical step after the lead magnet they download," and how you will help them with "a minimum viable freelance or coaching offer," a single-skill freelance service (web design) or single-interest coaching (health coaching), charging $500 to $2,000. Then slowly evolve the hierarchy by raising prices, gaining experience, systemizing your process and getting results. Three or four clients can replace your income, let you go full time and develop your brand further.

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

In July 2024 Koe presents the MVO as "just a different way of me spinning the minimum viable product, but I can make it more specific and applicable to you," and now recommends the teaching form outright: (1) "a single skill freelance service that you can sell for 500 to 1,000" (web design, email marketing, landing page copywriting) or (2) "a single interest or skill consulting, coaching or tutorial service where you can sell a pack of four calls for $500 to $1,000." Start with the second, because buyers at this level "like to learn things themselves, they usually don't have the money... they don't want to buy a website, they want to learn how to build a website." The evolution: once better at the skill, consult larger companies, sell the freelance service, or "eventually create a digital product so you're higher leverage" that "sells while you sleep because you have an audience that can fuel it." The service route is used "because you don't have an audience yet."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The same passage explains how to sell it and why teaching beats doing. "You can start monetizing immediately. You don't need a landing page, you don't need anything really. You just need to DM people, ask about their goals, ask what they're interested in, shift the conversation over to what it is that you sell, get on a call with them, sell them, or just sell them in the DM" ("Hey, I have this program, maybe you'd benefit from it, do you want to hear more about it?"). He declines to go further ("this isn't a video on landing the client"). The argument for teaching is about what each form leaves behind. With a freelance service "you don't really build a curriculum or productize along the way," but "if you're teaching someone, you can build a curriculum based on what you're learning and the feedback that you get, and then you can slowly turn that into a product," reduce the time you spend, or increase effectiveness, still four calls but charging more "because you have week-by-week curriculum and action steps," "and then eventually you just turn that into a course that can sell on its own until people want to work with you one-on-one."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The argument is the crux of Koe's preference: teaching produces an asset (a curriculum) as a by-product of earning, while doing produces only the deliverable. This is why the MVO belongs in a chapter about the minimum viable: it is a product prototype that pays for itself.

#### Skill versus interest, and the tutoring offer

The March 2023 video in which Koe called it a "tutoring offer" contains his most detailed account of offer types and of how to structure the first one. He begins with a distinction. *Skill* examples: email marketing, web design, copywriting, Facebook ads, brand design, "anything from my $1 million skill stack video." *Interest* examples: health and fitness, performance and productivity, personal development and spirituality, relationships or dating. "Interests are skills too," but the distinction shows how each maps to an offer. A skill maps to freelancing, and the mapping is "fairly obvious": web design becomes a website or landing page; email marketing becomes writing emails or setting up sequences; "you're going to do what you've learned." An interest maps to coaching or consulting: you help people with their health, productivity or performance and walk them through it. *Tutoring* applies to skills that are taught rather than done.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

| What you have | Default offer | What you do | Koe's preference for a beginner |
|---|---|---|---|
| A skill (web design, email, copy) | Freelancing | Do the work for the client | Acceptable, but builds no curriculum |
| A skill | Tutoring | Teach the skill one-on-one | Preferred: more fulfilling, more clients, productizable |
| An interest (health, productivity, relationships) | Coaching or consulting | Guide the client to implement it | Natural for the development path |

The *tutoring offer* is "teaching a skill you self-educated in": "think of it like you're walking them through a course one on one," for example how to write an email newsletter, edit YouTube videos, write social content, or do web or graphic design. Koe prefers it to freelancing for three reasons: you do not do the work for them; it is more fulfilling; and you can take more clients and "scale a bit faster." It also lets you target a more beginner level, which opens the pool of people who can use the service. He gives a pointed example from the market of the time: instead of "flooding the market" like short-form video agencies, people could teach a creator over four calls how to make short-form content themselves, "but you have to be somewhat good."

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

The structure of the offer has three parts:

1. **A curriculum from point A to point B.** Point A is the problem, point B the goal, and the steps fill the gap. Write it in a notebook. What is the promise? (For example, one fully edited YouTube video.) What is the problem? (They lack the skill and cannot break into the online space.) How would you teach it? His YouTube editing example: call 1, an overview of the editing software; call 2, how to cut and structure the video; call 3, color grading and B-roll; call 4, exporting and uploading. "So that's four calls, boom."
2. **Weekly calls** walking them through the curriculum "like a live tutorial," answering questions at the end. Four calls, once a week, because the format lets you charge more: instead of $100–$200 per consulting call, $1,000 up front for the pack of four. That means fewer clients, less work, and more focus left for building leverage (an audience) so that you can *wean off* the manual work later.
3. **Weekly action items and text access.** At the end of each call give action steps aligned with a desired end result for the whole offer (a polished YouTube video): what do they deliver to you each week? Give text access via Telegram, WhatsApp, phone or a private Discord channel for questions and feedback.

Other examples he gives: graphic design tutoring with the outcome of one polished social post or Instagram carousel, and a small coding bootcamp whose outcome is a finished HTML and CSS web page.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

Why tutoring before a course? Koe gives three reasons. You can charge more, and raise prices to $2,500 or more after a few clients (variable; it is a progression). You do not yet have an audience or traffic to bring in consistent course sales. And "you don't know what gets results yet. Even if you think you do, you don't." Working one-on-one, you refine the method as you go. Clients come through direct outreach or other strategies, "not banking on the audience." This *manual work up front* is a beginner's strategy, done before the intermediate and advanced stages, and you wean off it as the audience grows. Finally, it is easier to make one $1,000 sale than 100 sales of a cheap product; you have more control over your income, which he calls "incredibly liberating."

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

The same video explains what tutoring leads to. Its results "transfer into a course or digital product much more easily," so you build an *offer stack* (a set of offers at different prices) faster. You teach someone, get them results, have them create a project you can show ("my students have created these projects"), then turn the curriculum into a course. That opens room for more people to afford your products. And then, by the principle Koe cites as "buyers buy again" (people who have already bought tend to buy again; he states it as a known marketing principle without attributing it), course buyers sign up for one-on-one work to be walked through and held accountable, which increases income faster.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

In December 2024 Koe adds the validation argument for the four-call format. You can charge a decent amount (the transcript gives a garbled figure, "$752,000"; context suggests something in the range of $750–$1,000, but the exact number cannot be recovered). And if people pay, you know that a more fleshed-out coaching program, course, digital product or even software could work. His own example: his course 2 Hour Writer turned into Cortex, his writing software. "It's just a good way of validating whether or not people want the product or service" before investing time in a website, a landing page and the rest.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

#### The micro offer

In September 2024 Koe renamed the first offer and narrowed it. "A micro offer is a service where you don't need anything built yet." What you need: a social media profile as your *public resume*; content that displays your authority by illustrating problems and giving solutions ("AKA giving value"); the ability to message people who like, comment, share or DM you about your posts; and a questionnaire for people to fill out if you do not message them first, so that they can show interest. "A micro offer is a way of testing what you should turn into a full-fledged product or service before you spend time doing so." The micro offer is thus explicitly a test, the MVP logic stated without the acronym.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The structure is fixed: "Offer a pack of four weekly one-to-one sessions for $1,000." Koe justifies each element:

- **The price.** $1,000 is solid. If people pay it, others will pay $3,000–$10,000 for a more fleshed-out offer, depending on the audience; entrepreneurs and business owners often "don't flinch" at high prices; and $1,000 is something most people can afford if they really want to.
- **The duration.** Four weekly calls are enough time to get results.
- **The mode.** Only a few people benefit from you doing the work for them (freelancing), but "anyone can benefit from you teaching them over a call."
- **The purpose.** After selling a few, you can identify what should go into a full product or service.

He adds advice on naming. You can call it coaching or consulting, but "I would actually stay away from calling yourself a coach or a consultant or a freelancer. Just offer the desired outcome and solve the pain point." Not "I'm coach Dan, I have a coaching offer," but "I solve this problem to get this solution," which is "much more compelling and a lot less scammy."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The rest of the framework tells you what to put into the four calls, in steps.

**Step 1: identify a big problem.** Identify a big problem or pain point you solve, related to your main topics. "It doesn't really matter what the topic is right now, it matters what problem are you going to solve." Dig into personal experience: where you were perhaps five years ago, your mindset, the problem you faced, compared with now and why now is better (this is "you are the niche" of Chapter 19 turned into an offer). Then find which problem resonates by three methods: (1) write content about different problems and see which gets the most engagement; (2) research YouTube videos on the topic with many views, watch them and identify the problem they target, because "all content targets a problem even if they don't state the problem," and you can infer it from the goal or from what the video teaches; (3) follow accounts on the topic and see which problem-related posts do best.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The problem must then be positioned. "Big problems should fall under the health, wealth and relationships eternal markets. Your job is to target one of them and position your interest around it." (Note that in this passage the Eternal markets are three, without happiness.) His examples show how an interest is attached to a market and then amplified. *Productivity*: a big problem is not having the time or energy to make your spouse feel loved (relationships) and to make progress in your career (wealth). Dig deeper: the relationship grows stale and boring, causing stress and little problems that stack into "mental chaos." *Programming or writing*: the problem is not having a skill set that lets you make more money; "you slowly get prodded along by the school system into a career that isn't relevant to your skill stack," and the past saps your energy and ties you down with responsibilities. All of this material can also be used for content.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Step 2: illustrate a desired lifestyle.** What is the opposite of the problem, the goal they want, what does their life look like after they reach it? "Write down three aspects of their ideal day." For productivity: waking up and doing work you enjoy; no longer feeling tired and groggy in the afternoon, so you can get work done; sleeping well knowing you do not have a list of spillover tasks causing stress. You can map out more. Notice that this is the minimum viable vision of the previous pages, written for the client: the offer sells a vision and protects against an anti-vision.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Step 3: create your unique process.** Your *unique process* (his term for your own system for getting someone from problem to outcome) is used in the micro offer and later in newsletters, landing pages, courses and coaching. Over the four calls you go through it step by step, with teaching, actionable homework, worksheets and projects. For 2 Hour Writer, the unique process is "the three-point content ecosystem" (short posts, long posts, newsletter; see Chapter 24). How to build it: (1) write out every step someone needs to go from the big problem (where they are now) to the desired lifestyle; (2) look over the steps and polish them into a system you believe will work. His analogy is planning your week. You write your morning routine and afternoon schedule, move things around ("this could be better"), and as you live them each week you notice problems ("I don't like doing that at that time"), exchange things, and slowly improve the weekly system until it is right for you. "That's how you're creating a unique process over time." "You need your own unique process or system that you can continue improving."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Step 4: package it.** Split the steps across the four sessions so they are manageable. As a bonus, create a project for the client that guides them through the steps: a productivity planner, a fitness program, writing a newsletter, programming a small app, or a template. "That's your entire offer: the problem you solve, the desired outcome, and unique process to bridge the gap." As you work with people, focus on improving the offer and collecting testimonials.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The three components (problem, desired outcome, unique process) are a compressed version of the structure of value that Part XI develops: a starting state, a desired state and a mechanism between them. Chapter 33 returns to them when the micro offer becomes a product.

#### The micro service

Three months later, in December 2024, Koe renamed the offer once more. "I called it the micro offer, but in reality it's a micro service." The problem he wanted to address was psychological: freelancers, coaches and consultants think they need everything in place before selling, a landing page, a logo, a program, a system, teachings. "Wrong. You literally just need to be able to teach your skill or interest." The micro offer of the earlier video was a pack of four calls for $1,000, and that remains the form.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The *micro service* is described in the simplest terms Koe ever used for it. Break out four calls on paper, call one to call four, one a week: "a month of helping someone." Think of where they are now, where you can help them be in your topic, and the exact steps to get there; organize those steps as talking points per call. After each call give a worksheet (he uses Cortex for this) with questions, homework or a project to build. They fill it out; on the next call you review it, go over their pain points, then teach the next talking points. Repeat over four calls, more or fewer depending on how much there is to teach. "That's worth $1,000. Fitness trainers do that all the time." The comparison with a personal trainer is the argument: the format is not exotic; it is how one-to-one teaching has always been sold.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

Selling the micro service needs no landing page either, only the ability to DM people and to attract leads through a newsletter. At the end of each email, ask readers to reply if they want to work with you on that one thing; talk to them there; send them the link to the product on Stan (the creator storefront Koe uses). Or, on social media, write a post and add a comment: "if you want help with this, DM me." You will filter people and it takes time, but you need only five people to make $5,000. If you message 100–200 people a month, five is not many; it will feel demotivating to land five out of 100–200, "but that's just how business is, it's called conversion rate." Once you build an audience and people buy from promotions without manual DMs, you are "in a better spot," which is the reason to build an audience. But you do not need a large audience to do this.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

Koe illustrates the main obstacle with a member of his Cortex premium community: a systems analyst and programmer of more than ten years who felt lost about monetization. Having learned about business from the self-improvement and online business space, he felt he could only sell social media management, ghostwriting or productivity coaching, the offers he saw that space selling. Koe suggested he teach basic coding or systems analysis as a way for people to acquire a skill that breaks them into new career opportunities. The man's answer: "Well, I just didn't think of it." The lesson Koe draws is "don't overcomplicate": you probably already have a skill or interest that helped you get a job, advance your career, or improve your health, mental health or relationships, and you only need to teach it. The case shows how the environment narrows what a person can register as possible, the mechanism Chapter 28 described with "registered new opportunities."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

A concrete starting example comes from an August 2023 video in which Koe imagines starting over from zero. "I could create a service that teaches people how to build landing pages that sell, so I could charge a thousand to twenty-five hundred for a four-call bootcamp, depending on how I want to structure that offer." He names three target groups: "new businesses that have a full website, because that isn't even close to an optimal way to sell a product or service"; "students who want to learn a profitable skill outside of employment"; and "creators that want the first step in monetizing their brand outside of platform revenue." His daily routine would be simple: "I would spend three hours in the morning DMing people and writing marketing content online." And a caveat about replicability: people will try to copy this, "which I recommend you do," but "at this point, me starting over again, I have 10 years of experience; I know the little nuances of all of this stuff." The example is useful precisely because he marks its limit: the structure transfers; the ten years of judgment do not.

**Source:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md

The first offer changed names twice and changed emphasis once; its form and price range stayed remarkably stable.

What the table shows above all is that the *minimum viable* in Koe's usage is not a fixed product but a stance: the smallest thing that someone will pay for and that teaches you what to build next.

### 29.3 Service First: Why the First Money Comes From Clients

#### Why beginners start with a service

The minimum viable offer of the previous section is a service, and that is not an accident. Across 2023–2026 Koe repeatedly tells beginners to start with clients, and his reasons begin with his own situation when he started. "When I first started out in business," freelancing felt like "the only option." "I didn't feel authoritative enough to start a personal brand, and I didn't have enough money to buy product for an e-commerce store," and he "did not even understand what the term consulting meant." He tried SEO, web design, "all of the above." Agency and freelance work, he observes, are marketed to beginners "for good reason," and for him the choice was "a no-brainer."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

In the same April 2023 video he lists the three reasons:

1. **The education is free.** With the internet you can learn almost any skill. He qualifies this immediately: learning marketing, sales and offer creation, and how to package a skill into a specific business model "that college can't teach you to run," is a different matter, and that is where courses come in.
2. **You do not need traffic.** You do not have to invest time or money in building traffic (SEO to a website, an audience or following). Building an audience helps "a lot" and he highly recommends doing it from the start, "but you don't need it": you can learn outreach strategies, cold email and LinkedIn prospecting to land clients.
3. **You need only three to four clients a month.** "You're not selling a product, so you don't need to make like a hundred sales a month" to earn an income. Improve your skill set, iterate on your service, land three to four clients, raise your prices, "and boom, you're making more money than ninety percent of the population."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The second reason is the decisive one and is restated in March 2026 in a sharper form. "If you go the client route, I recommend doing that as a beginner." The *client route* (Koe's 2026 term, paired with a "product route") means starting with a service sold to clients. He admits that charging high prices "feels weird" and calls it a limiting belief he had too. When he worked at a web design agency, "I was making like 60,000 dollars a year," "still living with four other people, paying a very low rent," and "still felt like I had money problems even as a single person." The arithmetic, however, favors the service: "it's much easier to sell a 1,000 to 5,000 dollar service to one person than it is to sell 100 to 200 Substack subscriptions to 100 to 200 people, because where are you going to find those people and how are you going to convince them? That's what the social media audience is for, or other traffic mechanisms."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

The argument is a comparison of two bottlenecks. A product with a low price needs many buyers, and many buyers need traffic, which a beginner does not have. A service with a high price needs few buyers, and few buyers can be found by hand. The service is therefore the business model that fits the beginner's one abundant resource, which (as Koe says elsewhere in the same discussions) is "labor and time."

The prices that can be charged this way grew during the years Koe was teaching. In February 2023 he notes that in the past a freelancer or agency owner could not simply DM a client, walk them through *strategized messaging* (his term for a sequence of direct messages that opens a prospect to a service) and get paid $2,500, $5,000 or $10,000. Now they can, and "some people are even charging sixty thousand dollars for services through the DMs," which he calls "insane" because most people "would kill for sixty thousand dollars." The figure is an observation about people he knows, not a benchmark for beginners.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

In the same video he lays out what "it's very clear people should be doing" now, contrasted with the old path of school, good grades, a prestigious college, climbing a department's ladder and being extroverted enough to form connections so that opportunities pass to you rather than "the next guy":

1. Upskill yourself "a little bit for three to six months" to create a freelancing, consulting or coaching service.
2. Start reaching out to people, because the digital world has no barriers.
3. Pull in an income by charging low, "500 to a thousand."
4. Get results and improve your skill stack.
5. Charge more.
6. Learn how to create a system that solves a specific problem within a specific niche.
7. Charge even more, to get even better results.
8. By then, if you have been growing an audience, productize.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

The sequence anticipates the whole roadmap of Chapter 30 in compressed form: the skill becomes a service, the service becomes a system for a niche, and the system becomes a product.

#### The four kinds of service

In January 2024, when presenting the service business as the second of the seven career paths, Koe distinguishes four types:

| Type | What you do | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| Tutoring | Teach your skill one-on-one (weekly calls on web design, graphic design, email marketing) | Similar to coaching, but the content is a skill |
| Freelancing | Do the work for the client with your skill (e.g., web design) | A good starting point, but "you won't last long selling something that simple" unless you learn to create a better offer |
| Coaching or consulting | Help people implement your skill or system: you do it *with* them and guide them | Not done for them, as in freelancing |
| Agency | A freelancing business with employees or contractors delivering the service | "If you don't have employees or contractors, you aren't an agency, you're a freelancer" |

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

The categories correspond to the general offer types developed in Chapter 33 (done for you, done with you, do it yourself): freelancing and agency are done for you; coaching and consulting are done with you; tutoring sits between done with you and the do-it-yourself course it will eventually become. The remark about agencies is a correction of a common inflation of titles: what defines an agency is that others deliver the work.

#### Never only outreach: build the audience alongside

The most consistent qualification in Koe's advice on services is that the service must never be the whole strategy. In April 2023, right after calling freelancing "an incredible option for beginners," he says that if he went back he would tell people to start posting online from the beginning: providing value, attracting clients that way, showing they are an authority and building leverage through a personal brand and a following. The reason: so that you "aren't left with nothing when you inevitably decide, hey, I'm done with this freelancing stuff, where do I go next?" If you have only been doing outreach to clients, he says bluntly, you are stuck with no asset.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The two statements (you do not need an audience to freelance; not building one is a strategic mistake) are not a contradiction. The first is about what is required to start earning; the second is about what is required to have a next step. In 2024 he restates it as a rule drawn "from experience and failures": always start posting content, because many people who start freelancing or coaching get stuck in a "manual-labor cycle" of cold emails and cold DMs, with no brand educating people and building trust and authority without doing it by hand each time. Build an audience along the way; once you reach a certain level of traffic, brand loyalty and authority, "clients start coming to you"; as the audience grows, productize your service, reduce time on client work and sell a digital product, or, in self-development spaces, even a physical product.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In 2026 he gives three reasons for building an audience even as a beginner who sells services. It "is also going to prove that you know what you're talking about when you reach out to clients; they're probably going to look at your social media profiles." It is leverage, "so why wouldn't you do that?" And "once you're writing content, once you're actually growing," you can "transition out of client work into something like a product that can sell while you sleep, so to say, based on the traffic that you generate." He ends with a confession that summarizes his attitude to services: "I hated client work, but it was a necessary step for me."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

There is a tension between this advice and Koe's own history, and he never addresses it. He recommends posting content before having results, but he himself got results freelancing *before* he began publishing (as the story below shows, he started writing on Twitter because cold outreach had exhausted him). The most charitable reading is that the advice is the lesson of that history: he is telling beginners to do from the start what he wished he had done earlier. The corpus, however, does not state this; he presents his path with humility ("I'm just a regular dude") and leaves the gap open.

#### From service to product: the sequences

Koe repeats the order service → audience → product in many versions. They differ in detail but not in direction, and together they show the logic of the whole progression.

The earliest version is part of the January 2023 "4-Hour Workday" video and was repeated in a 2024 compilation. When starting out (freelancing, starting an agency, learning a skill), it is "very wise to start a service-based business first," something "where you are in direct control of the outreach," not reliant on an audience or paid ads to drive traffic to a product. The steps:

1. Have a skill and create a valuable offer.
2. Iterate on it over time until you can charge more and more.
3. Reach out to people and land clients.
4. Perhaps build an audience along the way, which then fuels client acquisition.
5. Once the audience is large enough, productize and systemize "so that you can still maintain a four-hour workday."

The fifth step is what keeps the four-hour constraint of Chapter 12 alive: a service that grows without being productized consumes more and more hours. His own case is the illustration: he took on fewer clients, productized his service and sold that product to the audience he had built, which required much less work.

**Source:** The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md

The emphasis on "direct control of the outreach" is the key idea of service-first. With a product you depend on traffic you do not control; with a service, the number of people you contact is a decision you make each day.

In April 2023 he recaps what he calls the monetization method he has "spoken about a billion times": (1) study your interests; (2) start a service business; (3) grow an audience; (4) work for free; (5) develop your process; (6) increase prices; (7) repeat steps five and six; (8) productize your process; (9) scale your audience, "and your income scales with it as you evolve." The ordering is not strictly chronological (working for free comes after starting a service business because it is a tactic within it, used to get the first results and testimonials).

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

A week earlier he had given a sequence that inserts the cohort between service and course:

1. **Service business** (freelance, consulting, coaching or tutoring). Starting without a following, you have labor and time; you do direct outreach and charge a higher price to sustain an income "without banking on a nonexistent audience," delivering dedicated one-on-one results. "With a service you only need like two to three clients to make a pretty decent income instead of like 100, 200, 300 course sales." You refine the system from the one-on-one work.
2. **Cohort**, his favorite: still a higher price, since you are involved; you create a curriculum with weekly calls, group coaching at around $500, $1,000 or $1,500 for roughly eight weeks.
3. **Course**: you already have the curriculum, results and testimonials, and "a year later you have fifty thousand, a hundred thousand followers," more income with less work.
4. **Build whatever you want**: now you have leverage, experience and resources, instead of "shiny object syndrome for 10 years." His own plans at the time included a "Modern Mastery gym" in a year or two, and he had considered a focus supplement and a recovery supplement (make a productivity video, plug the supplement).

He closes with a definition of business that answers the discomfort many beginners feel about selling: "That's what business is: you provide something that makes people happy and they give you money. People that don't like sales don't like making other people happy."

**Source:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md

The gym and the supplements are plans he mentioned in 2023, not outcomes the corpus records; they illustrate the "build whatever you want" stage as a horizon.

The transition from calls to product has its own small method, stated in 2022: starting with an MVO lets you "flesh out a digital product with results": teach people, get them results, "note the commonalities between what you're talking about on each call, and you package it up" into a course, an e-book or a cohort. Then you switch to that product, take less client work as your audience grows and gain free time.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md

In December 2023 the same step appears as "earn more," the fourth step of his "Build, Teach, Earn" method. At this point you have a high-value skill stack, the ability to solve profitable problems, a growing readership with profitable problems, and experience building and selling: "one of the most optimal positions you can be in this digital renaissance or new golden age that is the creator economy," with the resources to be an independent thinker and earner who does not conform to "government agendas you hate" or report to someone for assigned work. To earn more, *productize your results*:

1. If you have helped people through freelancing or consulting, write the exact steps that got them results; if you have no steps, create them and refine them. "These steps are your unique mechanism." (The *unique mechanism* is a copywriting term Koe uses with several senses; here it means the specific process that produces your result.)
2. Write the desired outcome: "What is their vision for their future? You are selling a better life."
3. Write the worst outcome: "What is their anti-vision for the future? You're helping them avoid pain."

"That is your marketing strategy."

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

This passage closes a loop with Section 29.2: the vision and anti-vision that the beginner writes for their own life become, at the product stage, the vision and anti-vision written for the customer. The method of personal direction and the method of marketing are the same method.

| Source (date) | Sequence |
|---|---|
| 4-Hour Workday (Jan 2023) | Skill and offer → iterate and charge more → outreach and clients → audience along the way → productize and systemize |
| Non-Needy Networking (Feb 2023) | Upskill 3–6 months → outreach → charge $500–$1,000 → results → charge more → system for a niche → charge more → productize |
| Self Improvers (Apr 2023) | Service → cohort → course → build whatever you want |
| Micro Education (Apr 2023) | Study interests → service → audience → work for free → develop process → raise prices → repeat → productize → scale audience |
| Build Teach Earn (Dec 2023) | Results → exact steps (unique mechanism) → client's vision and anti-vision → marketing |

#### Justin Welsh: service before product

The clearest formulation of service-first in the corpus is not Koe's but Justin Welsh's, from a 2021 interview Koe conducted. Welsh is a solo creator-entrepreneur Koe admires and cites in later years (see Chapter 28 on the word "entrepreneur"). What follows is Welsh's own system, which Koe then adopted into his model.

Welsh describes how he does business: "I build everything with no-code and mostly service businesses that get productized eventually... Service business until it's good enough, I understand it well enough, I productize it, and then I move on and build another service business, I productize it. That is sort of how I do business." He is explicit that he is "not an engineer. I don't build SaaS products" ("although I may someday").

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

His reason for the order is epistemic: "I'm a big fan of service before product... because I think you learn so much for your product by first having a service business." His worked example has six steps:

1. Start a simple coaching business: "go find 20 account executives who want to be sales managers."
2. Make it "a no-brainer impulse buy price. Come spend an hour with me for 100 bucks." "You just made 2,000 bucks talking to 20 people."
3. Identify commonalities: "they all have five challenges, but what's the most common one? What's the biggest one? What's the most painful one?"
4. Solve that specific challenge with those 20 people until you see the common solution: "each time I do this one particular thing with all of these 20 people, it works."
5. "That becomes your product," for example "how to become a sales manager in 3 months at your job... go from point A to point B in 90 days."
6. Deliver that product to your audience.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

Then the service and the product begin to support each other. Once the product exists, "now you can raise your service rates. So, instead of $100 an hour, now you're 250 an hour." People are routed between the two: "Someone can't afford your rates, you point them to the product. Someone doesn't want to learn in the self-guided product, you point them to your rates. And that's just how you build your first two revenue streams." Then "repeat that... with another group of people you like helping. The potential is limitless." The end state: "you've got five products. They're all impulse buys" that you point people to from content, the email list or one-on-one requests, plus "five different service offerings... at hopefully a higher ticket price." Koe's comment on the whole sequence (who you want to work with → service business → productize what you learn) is that "almost everyone I've talked to has done" this, "but it's never been put that simply."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

At the end of Welsh's product flywheel there is one more layer: "an invitation to join my private community," priced as another impulse buy at $99 per quarter. "What I'm creating is my own personal Twitter, my own personal LinkedIn," so that as he creates more products he can promote them on Twitter, LinkedIn and his newsletter, but also "go to 2,000 people who I have as a captive audience who enjoy my stuff and drop a link in a Slack channel." It is "an easy way to continue to sell future products from people who are already excited." He frames the whole as "a diversified portfolio of income, if you will, and distribution." The *captive audience* in this sense is not a pejorative: it is an owned channel, independent of platform algorithms, made of people who have already chosen to pay. This is an advanced stage, well beyond starting, but it shows where the service-first logic leads when it is repeated.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

Koe's own path, told in the same interview, shows the adoption. He originally "went the freelancer route," which he still considers "extremely viable," but likes "the consulting route much much much more." He "realized that I was kind of a commodity, had to learn more skills, start with funnels, make it specific to a specific business. And then, once I had results there, I could start consulting people on it." He chose to consult "the people I liked being around, which were creators, other one-man businesses, because they're just fun to talk to," which is Welsh's question "who do you like to spend time with" applied to choosing clients. A personal note explains his taste for teaching: "I like to do things on my own rather than having someone do it for me. I would rather learn from them, pay them to teach me, and then do it myself from a better point of view." *Commodity* here has its ordinary economic sense applied to a person: a single-skill freelancer is interchangeable with every other freelancer selling the same skill.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

Koe adds a piece of his own to the service-and-product pairing in February 2023, which he calls a *multi-directional approach*. If he sells web design to someone, that person is a beginner in web design whether he does it for them or not and whatever the price: they do not know much about it. So broad content about why to learn web design attracts two kinds of people: those who want to learn it and those who may need it done for them. He can sell a product that teaches it and a service that does it. Building an audience "from this multi-directional approach" leaves "so much more room for leverage": when he pivoted out of client work, he had a base audience that let him keep using the same skill set to help them. The approach is the content-side counterpart of Welsh's routing rule: the same topic feeds both revenue streams, so neither has to be abandoned when the other changes.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

#### The limit of client work

Service-first has a ceiling, and Koe states it sharply. In October 2023, speaking about making $1 million a year as a digital writer, he says that without leverage and scalability you will not get there, "at least without hating your life." After about five years of becoming really good, you could land $25,000–$50,000 clients, two per month, but "that's a loser's game... that's hard and... not worth doing." And "if you don't like working a job, then you're not going to like client work, because you're not doing your own thing... you're doing projects that are still assigned to you by a client." The internet, resources and software now let you build and scale "without hiring an insane amount of employees: the software is now your employee." As you write and build, keep evolving your products.

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

*Loser's game* is Koe's name for high-ticket client work as a route to large incomes: possible, but exhausting and structurally capped. *The software is now your employee* names the alternative: leverage through tools and products instead of headcount. Chapter 30 develops this ceiling as "the new 9-to-5" and explains the step to productization; here it marks the limit of the advice in this section. Service is the right *start*, not the right *destination*.

Koe's evaluation of client work moved more than any other judgment in this chapter:

| Period | Evaluation of client work |
|---|---|
| Jan–Apr 2023 | Start with a service because you control outreach; "an incredible option for beginners," though outreach alone leaves you with nothing |
| Aug–Oct 2023 | Freelance and agency as "the new digital nine to fives"; high-ticket client work as "a loser's game" |
| Feb 2024 | Client work is the best option for one person without an audience, but skip the freelancer stage and go straight to coaching, consulting or tutoring, "holding the intention of evolution" (Chapter 12) |
| 2024 | "A new 9-to-5 called client work," "going from one boss to 10 bosses"; scaling a service with a team listed as career path 7 |
| Feb 2025 | Scaling client work with a team to $1M+ "is actually a very good option... many people do that with great success"; in the same month, freelance marketplaces and job boards are "known paths, that's no different from a job" (Section 30.1) |
| Mar 2026 | "If you go the client route, I recommend doing that as a beginner"; "I hated client work, but it was a necessary step" |

The February 2025 row is not a uniform turn toward acceptance. Within a single month Koe praises client work scaled with a team and dismisses work found on marketplaces as replaceable ("if someone can give you the work, if you aren't hunting for the work, if you aren't attracting the work, then it's replaceable"). The two judgments concern different arrangements, an agency that attracts its clients and a freelancer who waits for a platform to assign them, and he does not set them side by side himself. The reason he gives for the hardening is his own experience ("I didn't realize I was going from one boss to 10 bosses"); the reason for the return in 2026 is the arithmetic of the beginner (one $1,000–$5,000 sale versus 100–200 subscriptions). The positions are compatible if client work is judged by stage: good for starting without an audience, bad as a permanent arrangement, and in every version accompanied by the instruction to build an audience and evolve toward a product.

#### Koe's own beginning: from a $300 website to six figures

The service-first advice is a generalization of Koe's own start, and the corpus tells that story in pieces from several years. Read together, they give an unusually concrete picture of a first business.

Before freelancing paid, Koe was an underpaid beginner. Used to part-time jobs, "all I knew was making $400 to $600 a month," "living with seven other dudes and us trying to skimp on rent." He did not know a freelancer could make $10,000 a month, or that a creator could make $100,000, $500,000 or a million a month; it seemed "absurd to me." His conclusion is about belief: "You can only achieve that thing once you actually believe that it's possible." He argues that 99% of people fail to make money in the creator economy because "they just have such a bad relationship with money, and their mind matches their situation, and they haven't expanded their mind to something greater than that so they can grow into it." (The "99%" is his habitual rhetorical figure, not a statistic. The *relationship with money* is developed in Chapter 34 as money programming.)

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

Later he worked at a web design agency (the job at which, in the 2026 account above, he made about $60,000 a year while still living with four roommates). That job mattered for two reasons. At the agency he could observe marketing, sales, delivery and project tracking, which gave him "a foundation." And it was "a cushy job" with spare time, which he spent trying to land his own clients, "the key that I was missing."

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md; How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

His first web design client paid $300: a local mattress company that wanted a site to show its mattresses, "not even e-commerce." In a 2026 retelling he contrasts it with his girlfriend, who had just landed her first nutrition coaching client for $2,500. The $300 was nonetheless the moment it "clicked": if he could repeat, improve and iterate on what had made that money, he would gain more control over his lifestyle and future. "The antidote to employment is becoming unemployable." "It made me unemployable": a deep conviction that he would never accept a job again and would provide for his own survival. He adds that the money was only "the checkpoint," the milestone; it does not account for the identity change that led him to believe he could do it, and further. *Unemployable* is his word, in this sense, for a person for whom employment no longer makes sense.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

The way he got clients after that is the most concrete illustration in the corpus of *manual outreach*. He tried "everything under the sun": cold email, cold calls, cold DMs, networking in Facebook groups, paid Facebook and Google ads, walking into local businesses to talk to the manager, and writing down the phone numbers of service businesses from the graphics on their vehicles (pest control, glass replacement): "I would literally write it down or take a picture, then I would email them or call them later." He also got work from friends, family and referrals, because people knew he did SEO, web design and Facebook ads; he narrowed down to web design. Consistently he closed three to four deals of $1,500–$2,500. Over the months referrals kicked in, things flowed more smoothly, and he replaced his income and quit his job, nearing "the glorified $100,000 a year mark," but "I didn't just want to replace my income."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md; 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md

The step that changed his income was learning *offer creation*. "Web design itself, it's a very good thing to start out with, but... you can make it more powerful by learning offer creation." He tailored a service to service businesses (pest control, accounting firms, consultancies): instead of a full website, a simple funnel (a landing page with an opt-in, and back-end emails to follow up and get leads booked), with Facebook ads as an upsell to drive traffic. Charging $2,500–$5,000 for that *service funnel*, he made his first six figures as a freelancer. The lesson he draws in 2021 is the one quoted above: as a web designer he "was kind of a commodity"; specificity (a funnel for a specific kind of business) and a broader skill set took him out of the commodity market.

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md; Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

Social media came after. In a 2024 conversation with Devon Eriksen he summarizes: "I started off in terms of business as like a freelance web designer, and then when I got on social media... I was doing cold outreach before that to land clients, and local marketing. When I started to figure out my formula for social media stuff and really see the power of building an audience, there was like no turning back for me." The order matters for the tension noted above: the outreach came first, the audience second, and the audience is what made him stop doing outreach.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

#### Repositioning for creators: from web designer to brand advisor

The second half of Koe's early path is a repositioning: he kept essentially the same skills but changed whom he served and how. In February 2023 he tells it in one breath: he started as a freelance web designer, pivoted into funnels, understood marketing more, positioned himself as a marketing consultant, switched out of done-for-you work for service businesses, and then, once online and immersed in the creator economy, which he saw as "the future" and "the new economy," "I repositioned that same offer for creators." That is where it got "weird," he says, because he became a creator and personal brand himself, three years before that video.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

His reason for choosing creators was personal. Before Twitter he did web design and funnels for service businesses; he pivoted that offer into marketing consulting for creators because that is who he wanted to work with, the people he followed and looked up to: "It only made sense that I try and work with the people that inspire me." The service was "pretty much the same" (web design, funnels, copywriting), but he offered consulting rather than freelancing, because he was a creator, they were creators, and as one-person businesses "they want to do it on their own." Teaching them was more profitable than doing it for them. This is the tutoring argument of Section 29.2 in its original setting: the preference for teaching came from the buyers Koe chose to serve.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

A 2026 retelling adds why he started writing and what his clients actually needed. "When I first started on social media, I was a web designer. I started writing on Twitter because I was tired of doing cold outreach all day. It was frankly exhausting," and he knew his next step was building an audience to land clients. As his service and knowledge evolved, many people came to him "not because they wanted a website, but because they just didn't have clarity on their brand, content, product. They thought a website was going to fix everything," while the main funnel that would actually make them money "just wasn't there." He helped them, pivoted his offer, and for "a 2-year period" considered himself "a brand advisor or a personal brand advisor." The episode is a small case of the problem-first thinking of Chapter 17: the client's stated request (a website) was not the client's problem (no clarity on brand, content and product), and the offer moved to the real problem.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

Koe's description of himself has never settled. In June 2022 he says he does not even know his job title, because nothing he does falls under conventional career paths ("I forge my own way"), and then: "maybe like I label myself as a brand advisor right now." In the same video he describes his model: he puts content out to attract an audience and cultivate value, understands what people want and gives it to them; 80–95% of his content is free ("I would even say ninety-five percent"), and the remaining roughly 5%, still a vast amount, is paid: "creative solutions for navigating life and business." He attracts people with similar values, vision and goals and helps them through free or paid content. In June 2025 he says he was a "brand adviser for like a year or two," which he still considers himself; if he labeled himself as two things, "a writer, because I am an author, I write content, that's what I like doing," and "a brand adviser, where I help people set up their brands to do what they want in the unique way that I've discovered." Elsewhere in the corpus (2024 and July 2026) he says "I'm not a writer" and "I still don't call myself a writer." The contradiction is unresolved in the sources; it may depend on whether he means a professional identity or the stereotype of the literary writer.

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md; How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

The deeper pattern in this repositioning is a progression of skills, which Koe describes in August 2023 as a lesson about levels. Play at your level and get results there until the next level; "as your level increases you will fill your tool belt with skills that allow you to view the plane you are on," which is vaster than the one below. His first successful endeavor was freelance web design: a single skill anyone can learn, with lots of competition and a constant battle for higher prices. To do something more profitable, funnels for service businesses, he had to learn marketing, copywriting and the buyer's journey. Then marketing consulting and becoming a creator required combining everything he knew and adding content writing and the skills he teaches in his writing course, "because a creator is a full stack business." The *tool belt* (the skills accumulated by level) and the *full stack business* (the creator as a business that uses all of them) connect the entry routes of Section 29.1 to the generalist of Chapter 20: path three, "both," is this progression lived forward.

**Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md

| Level | What he sold | What the level required |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Freelance web design | A single, widely available skill |
| 2 | Funnels for service businesses ($2,500–$5,000) | Marketing, copywriting, the buyer's journey, offer creation |
| 3 | Marketing and brand consulting for creators | Combining the previous skills; teaching instead of doing |
| 4 | Creator with his own products | Content writing and every skill of a "full stack business" |

At each level he also built a product, and this is the step that turns service-first into a business. In October 2023 he explains that as a freelance web designer, to free his time and take less client work, he created two products in his first year as a creator while his audience grew: a freelancing product teaching the principles and basics that had helped him, and a web design course (websites and landing pages), because "if people don't have a skill to freelance with, then I need to give them a skill." In his second year he stopped freelance web design entirely: with more experience it "didn't make sense to continue doing something less profitable and more time consuming," and he moved into marketing consulting for online businesses, to whom he had direct access on social media. Then he repeated the cycle: he retired the old digital products and created better ones from the new service (marketing and brand consulting). "I wasn't stuck to it or attached to it, I just did something new."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

In July 2023 he states the principle behind it. "Here's the key point: with every level of development that you reach, you will build a tangible product to reflect that. This is how you educate your followers and build authority." After solving his own productivity he created a planner, the Power Planner, sold it, and later made it free. After getting results in web design and freelancing he "solidified my knowledge and results and experience within that domain by making it tangible": a web design course and a freelancing course. Doing so "only furthered my understanding... and allowed me to transcend it." After acquiring a skill stack, he created a consulting offer for branding as "a brand advisor," because his accumulated complexity let him do it well for creators, influencers and brands. The *tangible product* is thus both a source of income and a learning device: packaging a level is how you finish it and move past it.

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

This is the development-based path of Section 29.1 described from inside one life: each level of personal and professional development produced a service, the service produced a product, and the product was retired when the person outgrew it. Chapter 30 takes up the same progression as a roadmap by stages; Chapter 37 returns to Koe's trajectory as an integrated case.

### 29.4 Permission to Suck: Shipping the Imperfect

Everything in the previous sections assumes that a beginner will actually sell something before they feel qualified to. This section gathers the attitude that makes that possible. Its core is a phrase Koe heard from a guest, *permission to suck*, and a principle he states in a dozen forms, *you can't improve what doesn't exist*. From these he derives a set of practical rules: start monetizing before you feel ready, ship the first product knowing it will be bad, fail early and in public, launch immediately for data, and treat the first business as a prototype.

#### Devon Eriksen and the distance between who you are and who you want to be

The phrase comes from Devon Eriksen, the author of the novel *Theft of Fire*, in a December 2024 conversation with Koe. Eriksen's point is about shame. "You have to teach yourself not to be ashamed of the distance between who you are and what you can do and who you want to be and what you want to be able to do." He explains the mechanism: "a small amount of it can motivate you, but a lot of it will make you avoid the very kinds of tasks you should be seeking out." Hence the instruction: "you have to go do something and give yourself permission to suck at it." Later in the conversation he applies it to money and business: "you have to be a business that's just struggling to break even before you can be a business that makes a huge amount of money"; if you are so ashamed of being that struggling business, you will never get where you want to go.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The mechanism is precise. Shame about the gap between present and desired self is not simply unpleasant; at high doses it changes behavior in exactly the wrong direction, because the tasks that would close the gap are the ones that expose it. A beginner ashamed of being a beginner will avoid publishing, pitching and launching, which are the only activities that produce improvement. The permission is therefore not an indulgence but a removal of the obstacle to practice.

Eriksen illustrates it with his own first year as a professional author. "I just finished my first year as a professional author... I managed to break six figures, but I did not make as much as I made as a software engineer, and I have to remind myself not to be ashamed of that, because this is the first year." "If you don't give yourself permission to start out and suck at something, then you're never going to reach the point where you're good... you're never going to build that audience." "You have to give yourself permission to not be there yet." Koe's reaction places the anecdote in proportion: compared with the creators he talks to every day, a six-figure first year is "abnormal" and "absolutely incredible." The story therefore has a double edge. It shows that even an exceptional result can feel like failure when measured against a previous identity (the well-paid engineer), and that the remedy is to measure the first year as a first year.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

The idea is Eriksen's; Koe takes it up as his own the same month. In a December 2024 business video he applies it to the skills of the one-person business: if you never promote your product or service, how do you get better at marketing and sales? You have no data to improve from. The same holds for writing, business or any skill: you will suck at first, then get better, "but you don't get better unless you suck at first." "You have my full permission to suck." You do not technically need more than the advice in that video; you can figure out the rest by trial and error. Koe's adaptation shifts the emphasis from Eriksen's emotional mechanism (shame causes avoidance) to an epistemic one (without attempts there is no data).

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

#### Impostor syndrome: overcome by selling, not before

The most common form of the shame Eriksen describes, among Koe's audience, is impostor syndrome: "I don't want to teach other people because I feel like an impostor." Koe's answer in February 2024 is simple: "be honest, don't fabricate your experience, don't lie about what you know, teach from the lens you learned it." His example headline: "I read The Art of Focus in 23 hours, here are the life-changing lessons I learned on joining the new rich," which is honest about where you are in your learning journey. **Complementary context:** *The Art of Focus* is Koe's own book (2024), so the example also shows how a reader could write honestly about what they learned from it. It is like marketing a product: do not make false promises, do not promise something you have not done, speak from your experience. "People want to learn from people who are close to them." *Teach from the lens you learned it* is his name for this stance; it connects to the "one step behind" principle of Chapters 19 and 26, where the ideal customer is someone slightly behind you on the same path.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md

In June 2024 he adds that impostor syndrome is not overcome *before* selling but *by* selling. You need some skill, interest and knowledge, but you do not need to have sold a product before selling one; that is impossible. "I don't have results" makes no sense as an objection: how do freelancers get results? They work for free or cheaply until their skill can deliver a service worth a higher price. You are also not as skilled or successful as you think, precisely because you have not started selling; you are afraid to sell because of impostor syndrome and do not realize that "selling a product is how you overcome that imposter syndrome" and how you get results for others.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

The two answers fit together. Honesty removes the *ethical* ground of impostor syndrome (you are not claiming what you have not done); selling removes the *factual* ground (you acquire the results you lacked). Neither requires waiting.

#### You can't improve what doesn't exist

Behind the permission to suck stands the principle that recurs throughout this chapter. Its fullest statement comes in a May 2024 video on self-confidence, where publishing is step five. "To understand confidence we must understand stories. Stories equal transformation. You aren't confident because you're trapped in someone else's story": you cannot escape the low point, reach the climax and arrive at a happy resolution. "The path out of a low point in your life is trial and error, the essence of life itself." Your first attempt at writing, at launching a product, at making friends "will suck"; you fail, get back up and try again until it is right. On this path people can give you guidelines, but "it's up to you to do it"; in schools and jobs, by contrast, it is "do this exactly this way and you can be replaced." "The reason 99% of people are cripplingly unconfident is because they unconsciously think you just become confident without doing anything." "Confidence is improvement." "Publish your writing and be called an idiot, publish your product and collect the negative feedback, put yourself out there and let people expose your flaws"; only then can you know what to improve. "You can't improve what isn't published": *published* means "built in reality, not in your head." "Dying in your dark hole of comfort prevents you from both."

**Source:** It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md

The passage links three ideas from different parts of the book. The story as transformation (Chapter 23) supplies the frame: confidence is the felt result of having moved through a story's arc. Confidence as improvement redefines a feeling as a track record. And the definition of *published* as "built in reality" generalizes the principle beyond writing: anything that exists only in the mind (a product, a plan, a vision) cannot receive the feedback that improves it.

In June 2026 Koe applies the principle to the first post, as the step that marks "the beginning of the end of dependence on someone else for financial stability." "The final ingredient... is actually doing the thing," and it starts with one post. Take one post idea; make the hook attention-grabbing; word the body for impact (at this point he offers a prompt, packaged as a skill, that turns your answer into twelve angles and several draft variations, so that even the first post is drafted with AI in the loop); "embrace that the first iteration will suck and that you can't improve what doesn't exist." If you never post, you never improve. Posting puts you "in the flow of feedback, in the flow of trial and error." (*Flow of feedback* is his phrase for the state of continuous trial and correction that publishing opens.) When the post does badly, do not conclude "social media isn't for me" or "it's only for lucky people." Ask what did not work and why; compare it with a post that did well (hook structure, body structure). With the failure as a reference point, "that's when you learn the most": search YouTube or Google, or ask Claude, for a specific tactic (for instance, a way to write a hook), try it, and see whether it does better. If you are stuck "in the void," diagnose: is the problem how you are writing, how you are growing, or something else? (Both skills are needed.) "You just try and error and correct until you reach success."

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

The diagnostic step is the important refinement. Failure is useful only when it can be localized; a vague failure ("it didn't work") produces vague conclusions ("this isn't for me"), while a localized failure (the hook, the body, the distribution) produces a specific next experiment. This is the self-experimentation of Chapter 15 applied to content.

Koe describes the same loop as a writing process. In December 2025: "By writing, that's how you practice articulating your ideas from the start. It's like a first draft. And then you refine it, and then you practice speaking it, and then you get really good at it. And as a bonus, by posting your ideas in public, you get direct feedback in the form of engagement, so that you know which ideas are the most impactful. And building an audience from that also doesn't hurt." Writing is the first draft of thinking; publishing turns the draft into data. In March 2026 he describes his own loop as a pleasure rather than a method: "I love the process of capturing ideas, internalizing them, thinking about them, researching them, strengthening them, stress testing them, and then putting them out into the world to see if they help people, and then getting feedback so it refines it further." Writing in the morning is "my foundation": "If I don't write in the morning, my day kind of sucks. And I know that I shouldn't bank everything on being able to write good ideas in the morning, but that's just kind of how it is." And: "Isn't that just incredible, that you can talk about your interests and other people can find it interesting? And then, if you do the right things, you can turn that into a source of income."

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md; If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md

The loop has nine stages: capture, internalize, think, research, strengthen, stress-test, publish, receive feedback, refine. *Stress testing* (his term) is the step just before publishing in which an idea is tested against objections. The loop shows that "publish to get feedback" is not a substitute for thinking but its last stage. One exception should be noted. In the same March 2026 video Koe proposes a seven-day protocol for recovering lost creativity in which you do not write ideas down ("if it's important it will come back") and do not share what you create for 24 hours; he acknowledges that this is "antithetical to everything that I preach" and limits it explicitly to the protocol. The exception confirms the rule as his everyday practice.

The feedback loop requires one more capacity, which Koe in February 2023 calls "the biggest business hack": *self-awareness*. Beginners struggle to see the big picture of content creation, and the remedy is observation and awareness, "like becoming a mind reader." Read your content through your audience's eyes. Would they share it? Would you? Do you even like your own content, and if not, why are you posting it? (You will not like some of it; that is room for improvement.) If nobody would share, like or comment, and you have no strategy to get more eyes on it, "you can't just keep posting into the void." And, reading it, would you click the profile, read the bio and follow? If not, "where's the gap? Identify the gap and then close it."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

Self-awareness is what makes public feedback interpretable in advance: the creator simulates the audience's reaction before the audience gives it, and the gap between the two simulations (would they share it? would I?) becomes the next thing to fix. It is the awareness of Chapter 2 applied to one's own output.

#### Against the gatekeeper mindset

The principle has a direct consequence for money: you do not have to wait to monetize. In October 2022 (repeated in 2024) Koe attacks what he calls "the stupid gatekeeper mindset" that says you need a certain amount of experience before you can earn. The *gatekeeper mindset* is the internalized belief that someone or something must grant permission before you sell. His counterexample is how freelancers actually start:

1. They learn the fundamentals of a skill very quickly.
2. They start reaching out to people immediately to get real-world feedback.
3. They charge nothing or very little, because their skill is not yet good enough to charge $2,000–$5,000.
4. They gradually work their way up, step by step.

"The sooner you start selling something, not only the sooner you can make money, but the sooner you can start failing and you can start iterating."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The sentence reframes the purpose of the first sale. Its main value is not the money but the failure it makes possible, because failure is the input of iteration. This is why the freelancer's progression appears so often in this chapter: it is the existing, unglamorous proof that selling before mastery is the normal way skills become businesses.

#### The faucet: the first product is always bad

The image Koe uses for the first product comes from the same 2022 passage. "The first product is always going to be [crap]"; you need to get it out so you can make it better, because "you're never ever ever gonna have a great product the first time around." So put an offer out: build a digital product, sell a physical product (dropship if you have to), start a freelance or consulting service, put out content, and "start getting the [crap] out of the faucet so clear water can flow." The image is of a tap that has not been used for a while: the first water is dirty and must run before clean water comes. The metaphor carries the mechanism. The dirty water is not a failure of the tap; it is a necessary stage of using it, and the only way through it is to keep the water running.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

#### Minimum viable everything

In February 2024 Koe generalizes the minimum viable pattern of Section 29.2 into a single rule. "Your project isn't going to be perfect. You need a minimum viable product in business, you need a minimum viable vision in life, you need a minimum viable project in everything. You just get something out on paper. It doesn't have to be ready for you to start building it; you build it so you can identify what's not in it." The last clause states the function of the bad first version: it is a tool for discovering what is missing, which cannot be discovered by thinking about the finished version. His example is Cortex, the second-brain software he was building: "we've changed it completely from what it was originally planned to be. How do you think we figured out how it's going to be better? By building the bad version first, so you can improve it. You can't improve something that doesn't exist."

**Source:** Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md

*Minimum viable everything* (the corpus records it as Koe's adaptation of the MVP, from this passage's "minimum viable project in everything") names the unity of the pattern: vision, goal, offer, product and project are all handled the same way. It is also the point where Koe's adaptation of the MVP goes furthest from its source, since the original concept concerns products, while Koe applies it to a life.

#### Fail soon, and in a week

Two further rules fix the timing. In January 2024: it will take time to reach the stage of digital products, and you must be okay with that, but "build a product or service anyway so you can fail soon." If you do not fail, you have nothing to improve; if you put failures off, the failure is harder and you get more discouraged. Pursue and achieve your goals so that you have experience you can monetize. "Sitting around and doing nothing keeps you trapped selling for someone else." *Fail soon* adds a cost argument to the faucet image: a failure deferred is a failure enlarged, because more time, hope and identity have been invested in the untested version.

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

In June 2025, rather than listing the pros and cons of product types, Koe gives a metric: "Your first product should take no longer than a week to build, because you probably already have a product lying around." The second clause matters as much as the first: the material for a first product usually already exists (notes, a process you follow, a template, what you teach in your content), and the week is for packaging it, not for inventing it.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

#### Build products immediately, before an audience and before experience

A common belief among beginners is that a product should wait for an audience. Koe rejects it emphatically. In April 2023, when new creators tell him they do not want to sell a product yet and want to build their audience first, he answers: "you need to build... this is so [damn] important I will have to create an entire video on it, because it's the most important thing that I did in my life."

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

His argument for building immediately comes from experience, and he gave it in December 2022 and again in the 2024 compilation. Every product he built, even two that "failed miserably" and that he stopped selling a week after launch, gave him "ordered information" he could use elsewhere. He got "bad ideas out of my head... not bad as in like bad, but like bad as in unvalidated," non-resonant ideas, and could build on top of them: "it was like clearing my mind." The three information products from his first year that he did not see going anywhere still had value, so he packaged them into a new offer, Modern Mastery, which at the time had been running "two years strong." "So your first product... it's going to suck. Get it out, iterate, build on it."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

Two terms in this passage carry the argument. *Ordered information*: building a product forces scattered knowledge into a structure, and the structure survives even if the product does not sell. *Unvalidated*: Koe distinguishes a bad idea from an untested one; the first product is mostly a container of untested ideas, and building it is how they are tested and either kept or cleared. The Modern Mastery case shows that failed products are not lost; they are raw material.

In April 2023 he makes the same point about the course that made his reputation. People ask how he created Digital Economics; he says he does not even know how he wrote it, except that he had "so many previous failures out of the way." People assume those failures did not make money, "but they made me a full-time income plus." "You get the crap out, and it's not going to be crap at the time, people are gonna love it, and then you're gonna look back and you'll be like, oh, that was crap. That's called growth." He adds that without a product or service you are not growing as an entrepreneur: "you're not an entrepreneur because you don't have anything, you don't have a way of making money."

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

The remark "it's not going to be crap at the time" corrects a possible misreading of the faucet image. The first products are not bad in the eyes of their first buyers; they are bad in the eyes of their author's later self. "Bad" is a retrospective judgment that measures growth.

The same applies to experience. In a March 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, Koe says that many beginners do not think they can create a product, even with expertise, because they do not see how it fits the market. But even an absolute beginner with only a slight interest in productivity does not need to consult, coach, freelance or give advice to start a personal brand: "share what you're learning." A brand then develops a product for a problem in the market. His physical example: if you like productivity, "I can buy a planner and I can make my own without any expertise." "You can only know it's helpful if it helps you or if it helps the market... that's how you gain the experience, is by actually creating it and putting it out there, rather than waiting until you have the experience to create the same exact planner."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md

The planner example is not hypothetical for Koe: his Power Planner was exactly such a product (Section 29.3). The logic is the inversion of the usual order. The usual order says: gain experience, then create. Koe's says: create in order to gain experience, because the experience that matters (what helps people) can only be acquired by putting something in front of them.

In July 2023 he generalizes this into a rule for any area of progress: *turn personal projects into products*. Wherever you are making progress (a health brand documenting a journey, growing an audience, writing, marketing, spirituality) is a project you can package and sell. "This is how you start becoming an actual expert: by selling something, getting feedback on it and making it better." He acknowledges the obvious objection: scammers should be eradicated, but bad products in general will exist because of how hierarchies form. Good products come from people who stick it out and iterate; bad ones from people who took action (which is good) but did not stick it out because they took the feedback too seriously. So "don't let people gatekeep you": "you can't improve it before you put it on the market, because you don't have data." Study other people's products, brands and content for inspiration and structure; buy courses; be inside the creator economy you want to join. "If you want a job, then go pay for an overpriced degree; if you want a life of work, then pay a few bucks here and there for a course, a book."

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The distinction between those who quit and those who iterate is the real content of the argument about bad products. A bad product is not the end of a hierarchy but its bottom rung; it becomes a problem only when its maker stops there. The word "gatekeep" ties the passage back to the gatekeeper mindset: the permission others withhold is the same permission the beginner withholds from themselves.

#### Your first product will suck: fail in public

Koe's own first products, by his account, were terrible. In June 2024: your first product will suck. His did, but he did not give up. He thought his products were great when he made them; looking back they were "the worst products I've ever seen," which is why he did not make much money and people did not like them. "But I couldn't have created better if I didn't first create worse." Perfection is not possible; it may be demotivating to know that you must ship something poor first, but it has to happen. If you have some skill, you will more likely than not make money along the way. And digital products can be created, built, marketed and launched much faster than physical ones, at no cost to create.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

There is an apparent tension between this account and the previous one (the failed products "made me a full-time income plus"). Both are Koe's, from different years, and they can describe different products or different degrees of failure; the corpus does not reconcile them. What both share is the claim that the later, better product depended on the earlier, worse ones.

In February 2025 he adds the nuance that keeps the principle from becoming an excuse. "Of course a bad info product is bad. But what you don't understand is that everyone starts out bad." He is not telling anyone to put out a terrible product intentionally; "I'm telling you to put something out so you can fail in public and potentially get criticized," so that you improve it into a good product. *Fail in public* names the purpose: the failure must be visible to the people whose reaction can correct it.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

The 2024 compilation explains why building must happen in public. You must post, build, publish and launch publicly, because you need data to know whether the information is valuable. "Starving artists, it's in the name": they "like to create what they think is valuable and they get attached to their version of value," then wonder why they are not selling ("this painting is beautiful"). That is fine as a hobby, but if you do it full-time you have to "play a back and forth game with the market." Since the goal is to improve people's lives, if people are not buying or reading, or you cannot hold their attention, is it really valuable? If it is not getting results, iterate and pivot. The *starving artist* is a figure Koe uses in many senses (see Chapter 28); here it means the creator who substitutes their own judgment of value for the market's.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The test Koe proposes ("is it really valuable?") is a strong claim and should be read with its scope. It applies to work offered as a product or a living; Koe explicitly exempts work done as a hobby. It does not say that art the market ignores is worthless in every sense, only that a business cannot be built on a value no one else recognizes.

From this follows the timing rule: launch immediately. In April 2023 he says you do not have to wait two to three years, as with his writing product; he had products before it. "I recommend launching immediately, because you need data and feedback as to how to make your products better, and that can only be done when you put them in public." In February 2025 he is even more categorical: he is "a firm believer that you should launch a product now, ASAP, immediately," and he launched his first at 500 followers. A product makes trial and error *diagnosable*, which is what makes it tolerable and keeps you from quitting:

| Symptom | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| No clicks | "Your content sucks" |
| Clicks but no conversions | "Your landing page sucks" |
| No sales even after optimizing content and landing page | "Your product probably sucks" and needs improvement |

"You're supposed to start off in this embarrassing state, then iterate and improve." And because someone can pay you, instead of "5 cents from YouTube AdSense," it is easier to stick it out.

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md; Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The diagnostic table is the practical core of "launch for data." Without a product, a beginner who is not making money cannot tell which part of the system is failing; with one, each stage of the funnel (content, landing page, product) reports separately. Koe later qualified it: in June 2025 he said that a personal brand works as a traffic source and that with a trusted brand the optimization of the landing page matters little. The diagnostic sequence still holds as a starting heuristic; its middle step weighs less as trust grows.

A guest adds the social side of the same idea. John Hugh (also transcribed John Hu), co-founder of the creator platform Stan, tied pre-sales back to community in a January 2025 conversation. Perhaps because he grew up an only child, "we're so used to working in isolation, maybe it's our perfectionism, like I have to make it perfect and work on it for 3 months before anyone can see my baby." With the awareness that "there are people in my corner... that clearly want to hear something from me," the question becomes "how can I get them more involved in the process earlier"; "you end up being more successful, you accelerate your timelines." The point is Hugh's, not Koe's, though it fits Koe's argument: perfectionism and isolation are the private forms of the shame Eriksen described, and involving followers early is the public form of the permission to suck.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

The advice to launch immediately coexists in the corpus with other advice that seems to delay it: "we need traffic before we need an offer" (2023); create a "data-driven product" after six to twelve months; "become an authority" before a digital product. The most directly opposed element comes later: in December 2024, presenting the micro service, Koe ranks the micro product below it, "I would build the service first so you can make money faster," and treats the small product as an optional extra income stream that links to the service and builds trust for it (Chapter 32 develops this). Koe never retracts either side and does not reconcile them himself. The reconciliation that follows is this chapter's reading: they can be reconciled by distinguishing two products, a first product launched immediately in order to learn, and a later one built on data and iterated. Authority is built while selling something small, not before. The December 2024 ranking resists this reading more than the others, since it puts a service, not a product, at the start; it fits only if what is "launched immediately" is understood as the smallest sellable thing of either kind.

#### Iteration, not formula

In February 2025 Koe warns against reading any of his product advice as a recipe. None of the ideas a beginner brainstorms will be perfect the first time. "This whole product thing, it isn't formulaic." "All of business, content, product, brand, whatever, it's all iteration. If it were as simple as a list of steps... everyone would be a frickin' millionaire." "The people who followed the rules, then break the rules and create their own rules, are the ones who see success." His own rules, he says, are ones "I both created and taken and merged into one" in a way that worked for him. To refine your marketing, read books and watch videos on the topic, connect the ideas in your product document, talk to more people with the problem, write about it on social media to see what resonates, and merge all the data.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md

The three-stage formula (follow the rules, break them, create your own) is the business version of Chapter 17's "create your own philosophy" and of the advice-as-training-wheels of Chapter 23. It is also an honest description of Koe's own method: much of what this chapter presents is a synthesis of advice he took from others (the MVP, Justin Welsh, Devon Eriksen) and adapted through his experience.

#### The first business is a prototype

The widest application of the permission to suck concerns the first business as a whole. In May 2022 Koe says that your first business is "almost inevitably going to fail," because "there are just some things that books can't teach you." Advice from others will not make sense when you hear it, because "you haven't gotten to the place where it will make sense to you"; you glance over things because you have no idea what they mean. Understanding becomes available only after you reach the stage where it is relevant, through doing and failing.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

This is a claim about knowledge, not about luck. Some knowledge is stage-dependent: it can be heard before the stage but understood only during it. That is why reading cannot replace the first business, and why the first business is better understood as tuition than as a bet.

In September 2023 he uses a metaphor to make the claim less frightening. There are many moving pieces, and "it will take a year or so to actually feel like you're getting the hang of it." But this is not a special case: "every single thing you've done in life has been a process of trial and error." Being older, with psychological barriers built up, "doesn't mean you can't build a business like you learned to walk." Learning to walk "is arguably the hardest skill we've learned in our lives"; since then we have adopted psychological barriers and limiting beliefs that keep us from thinking anything is as easy as learning to walk. "I don't even remember learning to walk, but I can walk just fine." Ten years earlier he had no idea how to build a business; now, he says, he could "probably spin one up" and make however much he wanted in a year "if I did things right." The metaphor reframes failure as the normal texture of learning any complex skill, and it locates the difficulty of adult learning in the mind's barriers rather than in the skill itself (the theme of Chapter 15's "mind as body").

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

In 2025 Koe draws the logical conclusion about choosing a first business model: it barely matters. In July: if it is one of your first businesses, it does not really matter what kind, because what you do two or three years later will be completely different; you are simply starting a business and trying to make money, because you will not learn until you do. There is no world in which you watch 40 hours of YouTube, read ten books and build a million-dollar business in a year. Start, learn along the way, overcome roadblocks, pivot. The options include a personal brand, software, e-commerce through drop shipping, "whatever the flavor of the day" is (*flavor of the day*: whichever model is currently fashionable). His recommendation: do something popular on YouTube, because "if it is popular, that means that, yes, even though it's saturated, it works"; and keep a finger on the pulse of newer business models. In August: "Doesn't matter what business model you choose or which one you start with, because they all work. The first one is just a prototype. You try it. It's probably going to fail, but then you correct yourself next time. You choose a different one, and slowly you understand what you want to do, and then you do it."

**Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md; What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md

This is a visible change of position. In September 2024 Koe had singled out digital products and services as "what I think is just the best option," while also rejecting copy-paste business models as "the same thing as just going to school because your parents told you to"; in 2025 he becomes indifferent to the initial model. His reason is the one above: what you do in two or three years will be different, and only doing teaches. The two positions can coexist (the digital route as a sensible default for someone without capital; indifference as a defense against paralysis), but Koe does not present them together. Note also that the recommendation to pick "something popular on YouTube" sits in tension with his frequent warnings against following others' paths; the tension dissolves if the popular model is understood as a prototype for learning rather than as a life's work, which is exactly how he frames it.

The section can be summarized in Koe's own logic. The first version of everything (vision, goal, offer, product, business) will be bad; that is not an obstacle but the method, because only an existing version can be improved; and the beginner's real enemy is not the bad version but the shame, perfectionism or gatekeeping that prevents it from existing.

### 29.5 The 60-Day Plan and What to Build

#### The premise of the plan: only what it takes to start

The pieces of this chapter (an entry route, a minimum viable offer sold as a service, an audience built alongside, and the permission to do all of it badly at first) come together in one concrete program that Koe published in September 2024: "a 60-day action plan to making your first $1,000 as a one-person business." It is aimed at "creators or creative people with skills" who want to build their own business or a side hustle. He frames it against two alternatives he dislikes. "I don't like get-rich-quick [stuff]," so the plan is "the fastest, relatively, and what I believe to be just the most seamless way." And it is deliberately minimal: focus only on what it takes to start the business, then improve everything else once you are already making money and know what you will commit to. Advanced people, he adds, can use the same plan to build a new offer that makes much more because of their experience.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The video that contains the plan covers six topics: (1) why building an audience matters in a world of freelancers and agency owners; (2) what to talk about and what to sell; (3) the *micro skill stack*, two high-income skills (elsewhere Koe identifies them as writing and sales, which combine into persuasion; see Chapter 35); (4) the micro offer of Section 29.2, a way of landing clients without months of building; (5) "the only two ways to land clients"; and (6) the $1,000 challenge with daily steps. (The transcript sometimes reads "$11,000"; the context shows it is a transcription error for $1,000.) He insists that the parts depend on each other: "If you skip over one part, you're probably not going to see results." The DM script and the methods of client acquisition that the video presents belong to the systematized promotion of Chapter 32; this section reconstructs the steps of the challenge itself.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The premise ("only what it takes to start, improve the rest once money comes in") is the minimum viable pattern applied to the whole business. It is also the operational answer to the misplaced priorities Chapter 28 criticized (building a website, designing a logo or filing for an LLC instead of finding customers).

#### The steps

**Step 1: create a profile and forget it.** "No, changing your bio isn't going to help you magically make $10,000 a month." The profile is the public resume; it needs to exist, not to be perfected.

**Step 2: write three to five short posts a day.** This is more than Koe usually advises. He normally recommends one or two, "because that's what I do now; it's not what I did, I wrote more." One or two may seem easier, "but it's not, because *ideas beget ideas*": writing more gives you more ideas, more learning and the feeling of progress. (*Ideas beget ideas* is his phrase for the generative effect of volume: writing produces the material for more writing.) The practices that support the volume:

- Build a *swipe file* of other people's content you love and want to recreate (good tweets, posts, videos). The swipe file is a marketers' term Koe adopts for a collection of high-performing pieces to study and adapt.
- Jot down ideas while consuming podcasts, books or conversations.
- Write about struggles, solutions, how-to breakdowns and commentary on advice you disagree with, relating each to your offer. (Koe uses Cortex for this capture.)
- Every day or every other day, go through the comments and reposts on your posts and DM those people.
- Include a link and a call to action to your questionnaire, so that more interested people DM you.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The volume Koe prescribes has varied over the years: three tweets a day in 2022–2023; one to three posts a day to "get the failures out of the way" in 2023; two to three short posts daily and one to two long ones weekly in 2024; three to five a day in this plan; and by 2026 "one piece of content a week" broken down into the rest, together with the advice to "tone it down," raise quality and add scarcity to the brand. The variation is a change of emphasis by stage rather than a contradiction: volume serves the beginner who needs data and validation (and fits the faucet logic of Section 29.4), while scarcity serves someone who already has an audience. The 3–5 figure belongs to this specific 60-day sprint.

**Step 3: one or two long posts a week.** Write long posts "because the longer you hold attention, the more people trust you and the more value you can deliver" (the same reason, he says, that his own newsletters and videos are long). Newsletters are not part of this *micro creator workflow*; the full version, the three-point content ecosystem of short posts, long posts and newsletter, is what his 2 Hour Writer course teaches (see Chapter 24). For the long posts: take your best posts or ideas and expand them into a thread, a micro-article or a long post; use the extra space to give away extra secrets, and "try to include at least one thing that isn't common advice or knowledge"; plug your questionnaire at the bottom of each one. When promoting, use persuasion: "here's the problem, here's the solution, here's my unique offer, fill this out."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The short posts serve as tests; the long posts expand the ones that worked and convert attention into trust. This is the "short form attracts, long form converts" structure of Chapter 24, reduced to its smallest working version.

**Step 4** of the plan concerns getting eyes on the posts by manual effort (replying to other accounts, making friends by DM, tagging people); it belongs to the traffic mechanisms of Chapter 26 and is not repeated here.

**Step 5: map your micro offer.** You do not need a solidified product or service yet, only something (1) you are above average at and (2) that helps people solve a tangible problem. Koe then asks four questions. Do others want to learn it? Are they already learning it? Is there an industry around it? Do you follow people who make a living from it? If so, "great, you have everything you need to build a million-dollar company; that's not a joke." The questions test demand without any market research beyond what the beginner already sees every day.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The same step contains one of the most useful reframings in the chapter. "You are living through an active tutorial in how people make money." Look at the people you follow not through the lens of a consumer ("this information is great") but through the lens of "a researcher and someone who wants to build a business": everything they do is a tutorial. Koe does not create newsletters or videos just because he wants to: "they make money, they're a part of the business" (though he chose YouTube because he likes it). "You're watching money flow right now": every piece of content, landing page, email and book has money attached to it; "you aren't making money because you aren't creating and selling those things." All you need is a pack of four calls for $1,000 to teach something beneficial (fitness, a skill, nutrition, programming). Once you get results and know what people want, invest more in building a "real" business.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The *active tutorial* (with its companions, the *lens of a researcher* and *watching money flow*) is the business application of the shift from consumer to researcher in Chapter 16. The same content a consumer absorbs for its information, a researcher reads for its structure: what problem it targets, what it leads to, what is being sold. The beginner already has a free education in business models; what they lack is the lens that reveals it.

**Step 6: send 30 DMs a week for eight weeks.** "Send 30 DMs a week." It sounds high, Koe says, but it is "extremely low and borderline pathetic": freelancers and agency owners who send thousands of cold emails a month "would laugh in your face." "30 DMs multiplied by 8 weeks equals 210 DMs," he says (arithmetically it is 240; the slip is in the source, and the argument holds either way). You need fewer than 0.5% of those people to say yes to a $1,000 offer. And even if you land only one client, you have built a large network: direct connections with roughly 210 people; you have probably grown by a few hundred followers; around a thousand people understand your expertise, each of whom knows 50 to 500 more people who could hear about you. "The longer you stick with this, the more people say yes, the more your audience grows, and soon enough you'll have people popping out of the sky to work with you."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The arithmetic deserves a closer look, because it is the argument. At $1,000 per client, the target of the plan is a single sale. Against 240 messages, a single sale is a conversion rate of about 0.4%, which is why Koe can say that "less than 0.5%" is enough. The same logic appeared in the micro service (five clients from 100–200 messages a month: "it's called conversion rate"). Low conversion is not a sign of failure but the normal condition of selling, and the plan is designed so that the beginner's expected conversion is enough. The second argument (the network effect of even one client) is Koe's estimate, not a measurement; its point is that the plan's by-products (connections, followers, people who know what you do) have value independently of the first sale.

| Step | Action | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Create a profile and stop tweaking it | A public resume that exists |
| 2 | 3–5 short posts a day, swipe file, idea capture, DMs to engagers, questionnaire link | Volume for ideas, data and leads |
| 3 | 1–2 long posts a week with an uncommon secret and the questionnaire | Trust through held attention; conversion |
| 4 | Manual effort to get eyes on posts (Chapter 26) | Traffic without relying on the algorithm |
| 5 | Map the micro offer; read the market as an active tutorial | Something you are above average at that solves a tangible problem |
| 6 | 30 DMs a week for 8 weeks | One $1,000 client from under 0.5% conversion, plus a network |

The 60-day horizon is one of several in Koe's teaching, and they measure different milestones rather than contradicting each other: 60 days for the first $1,000; "a year or so" to get the hang of business (Section 29.4); one to three years from a problem to a productized offer; two to four years to a "hefty income"; three to five years to large figures. Chapter 30 gathers these timelines. The first $1,000 is the smallest of them, and its role in Koe's account is closer to the $300 mattress website of Section 29.3 than to an income: proof, to the person who earns it, that it can be done.

#### What to build: three founder reasons

The 60-day plan assumes the beginner already has a skill or interest to teach. For those who do not know what to build at all, Koe offers a short heuristic in March 2024: "If you don't know what to build, start with: (1) what you already use, but make it better; (2) a problem you've solved in your life; or (3) building what you want to see in the world."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

The week before, he presented the same three options as a pattern in the stories of successful founders. Excluding cases of *unconscious competence* (here meaning people born into success, whose path cannot be replicated), the most successful entrepreneurs share a theme: they built a product (1) to make something that helped them better, (2) to solve a problem in their own life, or (3) to build what they wanted to see in the world, "or even better, a combination of them all." The claim is an observation from stories Koe has read and heard, not a systematic study.

**Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md

His illustration is the founder of an eczema soap company. A woman with eczema could find nothing but "a boring white piece of soap that didn't smell like anything" that did not irritate her skin, and that soap stripped the soul out of relaxing in a spa-like shower. Wanting to bring life back into her relaxation, she studied horticulture and botanicals "for years on end" to create soap bars that smelled good, looked good and did not irritate her skin. Koe ties the case to the *value equation* from his previous video: it starts with a problem you face, which reveals a goal and sets up a potential process to be created; you learn the knowledge and skills to create a process that helps others reach the same goal.

**Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md

The case combines all three founder reasons: she improved something she already used (soap), solved a problem in her own life (eczema) and built what she wanted to see (a soap that made the shower a pleasure again). It also shows the chain problem → goal → process that runs through this whole chapter, from the development path ("solve your problems as you pursue your goals, create a system to help others do the same") to the micro offer (problem, desired outcome, unique process). Chapter 31 develops the value equation as a theory of value; here it serves as a criterion for choosing what to build.

In August 2024 Koe gives the same advice at the level of the business model. Choose a model that leverages the skills and unique advantages you already have, or the things you love and cannot pull yourself away from, or the things you study in your free time. That way you explore your interests, stack *second-order future-proof skills* like writing and persuasion (skills that underlie many others and that AI does not make obsolete; Chapter 35), and use modern marketable skills like email, graphic design and video to monetize those interests (he points to his courses as places to learn them). He ends with a definition that closes the circle with Section 29.1: being a "creator" is "a way of being that is currently channeled through the internet," until the best way to harness human capabilities evolves, and not "the myopic internet job" people make it seem.

**Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md

This definition is why the chapter's practical advice does not reduce to tactics. The routes, the minimum viable offer, the service and the 60-day plan are ways of channeling a way of being (solving one's own problems, learning in public, building what one wants to see) into a first source of income. Chapter 30 takes the next step: what happens when the service that started the business becomes a new 9-to-5, how it is productized, and what the stages of the roadmap look like from the first client to a business that no longer depends on the founder's hours.

### Exercises

1. **Diagnose your entry route.** Using Section 29.1, decide whether you are currently closer to the skill-based or the development-based path. List the skills you would be forced to learn if you built a personal brand around your main interest for one year. Which of them could you already sell, and to whom? What would make your current path "one-dimensional" in Koe's sense, and what would it take to build it "so you can evolve beyond it"?

2. **Evaluate a change of position.** Koe moved from three equivalent paths (2022) to a hierarchy in which the skill-based path is "encapsulated" in the development-based one (2026), and his judgment of client work moved from "an incredible option for beginners" to "a loser's game" and back to "a necessary step." Reconstruct the reasons he gives for each position. Which version would you give to a 22-year-old with no savings, and which to a 40-year-old with a specialized career? Defend your choice and identify the point at which each version would stop applying.

3. **Write a minimum viable vision and a minimum viable goal.** Spend thirty minutes on the 2025 protocol (an average Tuesday three years from now; the belief "I'm the type of person who..."; the one thing you would do this week). Then choose a minimum viable goal you can put energy into for the next sixty days. After two weeks, note whether the goal has become more "magnetic" and which reasons to continue you have begun to register. If it has not, decide whether the problem is the goal or the amount of energy invested.

4. **Design a micro service.** Following Section 29.2, identify a big problem you solved in your own life in the last five years and position it under one Eternal market. Write three aspects of the desired lifestyle on the other side of it, list every step between the two, and divide the steps into four weekly calls with a worksheet and a bonus project. Then write the one sentence that offers "the desired outcome and solves the pain point" without calling yourself a coach, consultant or freelancer.

5. **Question the arithmetic.** Koe argues that selling one $1,000–$5,000 service is easier than selling 100–200 subscriptions, and that 30 DMs a week for eight weeks need less than 0.5% conversion to produce a client. Identify the assumptions behind each argument (about price, trust, the buyer, the market, the seller's skill). Under what conditions would the product route be easier than the client route? What could make a 0.5% conversion rate unrealistic for a given person?

6. **Separate the useful failure from the useless one.** Take something you have avoided publishing or launching. Write down what shame, perfectionism or "gatekeeper" belief has kept it unpublished. Then apply the diagnostic logic of Section 29.4: if you released it and it failed, how would you localize the failure (content, landing page, product; hook, body, distribution)? Distinguish between a failure that would teach you something specific and one that would only discourage you, and decide what minimum version would produce the first kind.

7. **Test the permission to suck against its limits.** Koe insists that "everyone starts out bad" but also that he is not telling anyone to release a terrible product on purpose, and he criticizes the "starving artist" who ignores the market's judgment of value. Construct a case in which shipping early would harm customers or the creator's reputation more than it would teach. What distinguishes "failing in public" from selling something that does not work? Where would you draw the line, and does Koe's framework give you the tools to draw it?

8. **Read your feed as an active tutorial.** For one week, choose five creators or businesses you follow and read their content "through the lens of a researcher." For each, identify the problem their content targets, the product or service it leads to, and how the free content and the paid offer are connected. Then apply the three founder reasons of Section 29.5 to your own life: what do you already use that you could make better, what problem have you solved, and what do you want to see in the world? Which of your answers combines more than one reason?
