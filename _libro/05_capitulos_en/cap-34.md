## Chapter 34 — Money

Part XI explained what value is, how it is communicated and how it is packaged into a product. Chapter 31 defined value as the reversal of entropy, perceived through the identity and awareness of the buyer; Chapter 32 treated persuasion as the communication of that value; Chapter 33 showed how a product carries it from one person to another. All three chapters assumed something they never examined directly: that the exchange ends in money. This chapter examines it. What is money in Koe's account? Why does he insist that nobody, however spiritual, escapes it? Why do so many intelligent people fail to earn it, and why does he call that failure psychological rather than economic? Why does hard work not make anyone rich, and what does? And once money starts to come in, how much is enough?

The chapter answers in five steps. Section 34.1 builds the basic vocabulary: money comes from someone who decides to give it to you; all income is an exchange of value; money is deeply intertwined with modern survival; it is a unit of value, and also, in Koe's more expansive formulations, the lifeblood of society and a kind of spiritual energy. Section 34.2 turns to what Koe calls "money programming": the inherited beliefs that make people unable to make, keep or use money, the demonization of business, and the figure he calls the "noble broi." Section 34.3 dismantles "the delusion of hard work" and the "labor theory of value" and replaces them with leverage, creative income and the principle of monetizing the mind rather than time. Section 34.4 gathers his advice on getting rich: betting on yourself before investing, the ignorance tax, the "new rich," the three conditions for getting rich and the aspirational hourly rate. Section 34.5 closes with the counterweight: the spectrum of wealth, the "enough life," the indicators of financial growth, and the warning against materialism.

Two features of the sources shape the chapter. First, much of it comes from conversations with guests (Dickie Bush in 2023, Devon Eriksen in December 2024, Sahil Bloom in January 2025), and the chapter keeps their contributions distinct from Koe's own. Second, Koe's views on money move over time: from affirming that money is "spiritual" to criticizing those who "spiritualize" it, from an emphasis on earning "more than enough" to legitimizing the obsession with getting rich, from rejecting investment to an order of priorities. These changes are presented where they occur, without choosing a winner.

### 34.1 Money, Survival and the Exchange of Value

#### Where money comes from: someone decides to give it to you

Koe's most elementary question about money is not how it is created but how it reaches you. "Where does money come from? I'm not talking about how the government prints it; I'm talking about how does it get into your hands. The answer is that someone gives it to you." The rest of the argument follows from that sentence: "Whether it comes from a consumer or business, somebody makes the decision to hand you money because they see what you have to offer as valuable." If money is a decision made by another mind, then the first step toward earning it is not a tactic but an act of understanding. "So the first step to creating something worth paying for is to understand the psychology of one person," and "preferably yourself."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The phrase **the psychology of one person** compresses an idea that runs through Parts IX to XI. The person you understand best is yourself, and the product built from your own problems (Section 28.2) is built for a mind you have studied from the inside. The same choice reappears below, in the 2023 argument that you should target yourself as your persona because "you understand your identity best."

A business, in this account, is the structure that organizes that decision on a regular basis. In a 2024 guide to internet business models, Koe answers the question "how do you earn a creative income doing what you want?" with the shortest possible definition: start a business, which requires a product and customers. "Your job is to attract people to a product so they hand you money in exchange," and "the product that you give must have equal or greater value than the money that you receive." He anticipates an objection from people who find "selling a product for money" superficial. His reply turns the objection around: what product are you selling? Are you putting effort, energy and love into it, or "packaging up a pile of dog" and asking for money? The superficiality, if it exists, is in the product, not in the act of selling. "Business isn't a bad thing, get over that limiting belief."

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md (2024-01-21)

Koe's definitions of business in this period are deliberately deflationary. In a July 2024 video he says that "everything I'm talking about right now is a business model": the vision and goals he teaches are not only personal tools, because "your vision is your vision but it's also what you're attracting people towards and then that's what turns it into a business." Then he strips the word of its weight: "a business is just a legal structure to allow you to achieve your vision in public and make money by doing so." At the end of the same video he restates it with a historical contrast: "a business is just a legal structure that allows you to build a solution to a problem and get paid for it. Your ancestors didn't need the legal structure to make a living; they could simply trade goods and services directly." The legal structure is a modern wrapper around the oldest human activity. (The formulation is a slight variant of other definitions in the corpus, where business is to build what you want, solve valuable problems and profit from both; Chapter 28 is the home of those definitions.)

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md (2024-07-13)

The same deflation is aimed at artists. In a 2026 video on strategic thinking, Koe describes the downfall of "starving artists": they "want to practice their little art, but… won't merge it with business. They won't integrate the two. They think that by simply going about it in a business way just strips all the humanity and soul from it, when that's not anywhere close to the case." He diagnoses the error as black-and-white thinking: "You're letting other people's beliefs and polarizing opinions infect your mind." If your goal is success but your beliefs are "tainted," your actions will not serve that goal. He does not condemn art as a hobby: "If you just want to write as art, then go ahead and do it. But don't expect anyone to give you your attention just because you spent a lot of time writing, because time does not equate to value. It is perfectly okay to treat writing or art as a hobby. But if you're trying to do this as a form of meaningful work, then it is a business."

**Source:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md (2026-08-19)

The sentence "time does not equate to value" is the seed of Section 34.3. The starving artist and the hard-working employee make the same mistake from opposite ends: both believe that the hours they put in entitle them to something, and both are refuted by the fact that money is a decision made by someone else on the basis of perceived value.

#### Value for value: the oldest exchange

If business sounds alien, Koe proposes a reframing that removes the vocabulary of entrepreneurship altogether. In a July 2024 video on the future of work: if "building a business" or "becoming an entrepreneur" doesn't resonate, "just think of it as becoming valuable, packaging up that value and engaging in value exchange, because that's the one thing that's been around since the caveman days." The formula has three verbs (become valuable, package, exchange) and a claim of antiquity. He continues: "Giving and receiving, that's what it is, that's how you make an income. You have to give or contribute something to someone who determines your value outside of the labor theory of value."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md (2024-07-28)

Here Koe introduces a term that will become central in Section 34.3. He glosses the **labor theory of value** as "your work is worth this much per hour, you're going to get that at a job and it's going to get you stuck in there; it's not going to allow you to evolve and increase your income by whatever you see fit and how valuable you become." This is his own gloss, not the economists' definition: for him the phrase names the logic of payment by the hour. The contrast is between someone else setting the price of your time and the buyer deciding what your contribution is worth.

Value, in this frame, is not only money. In a January 2023 video on the "$1 million skill stack," Koe asks the viewer to "zoom out and go meta": with the skills that produce profit, you exchange value for value, and "value comes in the forms of money, time, social leverage, information or expertise; it's not just money." You trade the resources you have now (a skill, expertise, information others lack, or **social leverage**, such as a large following on one platform offered in exchange for help on another), and eventually you "trade your way to money." Then the direction can reverse: money can be traded to buy back other resources, such as time. The chain shows why a beginner without capital is not without assets. What a beginner lacks is the habit of seeing skills, information and attention as currencies. Koe ends the passage with a principle that governs all such trades: the exchange is with other humans, it is transactional, and "at the top of everything when interacting with other humans, human nature and psychology reigns supreme." This is the same priority of psychology that opened the section.

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md (2023-01-08)

Selling, then, is not a special activity reserved for salespeople. "Selling is synonymous with exchanging value," Koe says in an August 2023 video on high-income skills, or with selling what you have to offer and persuading someone it is valuable. The point extends beyond money: talking to a potential partner or a friend, "you're selling your ideas and worldview so that they want to be with you." He closes that video in the frame of self-reliance: he has only made you aware of what to study; "the rest is on you."

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

Because the exchange is mutual, it has an ethical structure built in. In the July 2024 video on the future of work, Koe describes marketing and sales as the way you understand your customer's mind and create a solution they benefit from: "you're not just creating a product to sell and steal money, it's a mutual exchange of value. Then you persuade, not force or deceive, as to why your option is potentially better. And if it's not a better option, that doesn't make it a scam; that makes it a product that you need to improve, or it's not going to survive." The definition of a scam is sharpened here. **Scam** is an ordinary word that Koe uses with two senses of his own. The first is a positive criterion: in his one-person business roadmap (December 2022, repeated in the February 2024 compilation), exploitation and manipulation for a non-mutual benefit is a scam, which he defines as promising something and not delivering it: "If you can't deliver and don't refund, that's a scam" (Section 23.5). The second is a diagnosis of the accuser: in August 2024 he says that people often call something a scam "because it doesn't benefit your life or you can't see the benefit for your life" (Section 32.6). The 2024 passage quoted above sits between the two. An inferior product honestly sold is not a fraud; it is a product the market will correct. What would make it a scam is the broken promise: failing to deliver what was sold, and refusing the refund. "That's the beautiful thing about this, is that you have to improve." The alternative he offers is deliberately unflattering: "if you're looking for the lazy way out, then yes, go work and do the replaceable task and get replaced and then get stuck in that cycle."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md (2024-07-28)

Koe makes the same point about vocabulary in his January 2025 conversation with Sahil Bloom. When he lists the skills worth learning, he says, "I say marketing and sales, because you need to know how to tell a story, and you need to get over the hump of thinking that marketing and sales are evil in some distorted lens where it's not about value exchange or offering something that will actually benefit people." Insisting on the full phrase "marketing and sales" is a small act of reprogramming. It refuses to let the second word be quietly dropped as the shameful half of the pair. Chapter 32 develops the ethics of persuasion at length (Section 32.2); here the point is narrower. If money is a decision someone makes on the basis of perceived value, then the skills that make value perceptible are part of the exchange, not a corruption of it.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

#### The golden rule: cultivate your value, then distribute it

Koe condenses the relation between personal development and money into what he calls the one **golden rule**. In a November 2023 video: "To become successful there's one golden rule: provide value to others, and this means two things." First, "you must cultivate your value: you must expand your mind, identify problems, acquire skills to solve them and make tangible progress in reality; you can't just sit around and learn theory all day; you must practice." Second, "you must distribute your value: you need a vessel, aka a business, to distribute a product or service that the market wants to buy; if they don't want it, it isn't as valuable as you think." He then names the two halves: "you need self-development and you need other development, personal growth and business growth." **Other development**, his coinage for the complement of self-development, is the development of other people through what you offer them. "Business is how you provide value to others at scale."

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

The second half contains a test that is easy to miss. "If they don't want it, it isn't as valuable as you think." Value cultivated but not distributed remains a private opinion about one's own worth. The market's willingness to buy is the check on it. This is the same logic as the starving artist passage, stated as a rule rather than a warning.

In the same video Koe explains why the movement from the first half to the second is natural: "Self-development is a gateway drug into entrepreneurship, because you realize that improving others is the next level of improving yourself." The phrase **gateway drug** has a history in the corpus. Dickie Bush used it in 2023 for fitness as the entry into total responsibility, and Koe for personal growth leading into business growth. Here it names the moment when someone who has improved themselves realizes that the improvement is transferable. That realization is the bridge between Parts II–VI of this book and Parts X–XII.

What, then, is the value being cultivated and distributed? In a March 2024 video Koe offers a definition: "Everyone tells you to become valuable but nobody tells you what value is. Value is the relationship between what you do, why you do it, how you do it and who benefits from that equation." The definition is relational. Value is not a property of an action or an object; it exists only in the relation between the action, its motive, its method and its beneficiary. He then breaks it into four parts:

| Part | Koe's definition |
|---|---|
| Problem | "A limitation or challenge that creates pain when unsolved" |
| Solution | "An impactful end result that allows the recipient to evolve beyond the problem" |
| Clarity | "A creative system that breeds knowledge, skill and awareness to bridge the gap between problem and solution" |
| People | "The amount of people that suffer from the problem, can benefit from the solution and are ready to receive the clarity to act" |

"That's what products solve, or that's what products earn money for: you're solving a problem with a solution and clarity in between, and you're distributing it to people." The four parts map onto the golden rule. Problem, solution and clarity are what you cultivate; people are where you distribute. The third element, clarity, is the most characteristic of Koe. The product is neither the problem nor the solution but the system that carries someone from one to the other, which is why Chapter 33 could claim that "systems are the new product." This four-part breakdown is one of several in the corpus; Chapter 31 presents others (the Value Equation and the "micronutrients" of value, Section 31.3).

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md (2024-03-10)

Koe also places the individual exchange inside a larger whole. In a July 2023 video against niching down: "You are a whole and a part of your network, which is a part of a community, which is a part of the creator economy, which is part of the internet and beyond." The vocabulary of wholes and parts comes from the holons of Chapter 38. The economic consequence is that the creator economy, like any society, is kept alive by circulation: "You must exchange value through money, which is the lifeblood of any society, in order for it to survive." To be part of it you must do three things: contribute value, receive money, and pay money. "Value exchange across the board." The third item matters. A creator who sells but never buys (courses, tools, help) takes from the circulation without returning to it. The same reasoning reappears in Section 34.4 as Dickie Bush's "ignorance tax."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md (2023-07-04)

#### Money and modern survival: the unignorability of money

The second foundation of Koe's account is a claim about the place of money in contemporary life. It opens his September 2024 video on money: "Most people struggle with money; that's a fact, because money is deeply intertwined with modern survival. It impacts every single area of your life." Then the formula: "Even if you weren't thinking about money, you're thinking about money." Health depends on money; "the sanity of your relationships depends on a baseline amount of money." He grants that people "can say that that's unfair and complain about how it shouldn't be that way," and declares: "I would heavily disagree." The text is ambiguous about what exactly he disagrees with: the complaint that it is unfair, or the hope that it could be otherwise. In context, the more likely sense is that complaining about the role of money does nothing to change it, and that the role itself is not a defect.

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The November 2023 video "People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions" develops the claim into a sustained argument about what Koe calls the **unignorability of money**. It proceeds in several steps.

The first step is a rhetorical question. "If you hate money, do you not hate your life? Everything around you right now, from the phone in your hands to the roads you drive on to the desk you sit at to the food that keeps you alive, are all products from a business that makes money for their contribution to the advancement of civilization. You are drowning in a world that was built by the drive of money stemming from survival." The argument is an appeal to the material environment. The objects that sustain the person who despises money exist because someone was paid to make them.

The second step addresses a familiar dismissal. "The best way to make money is teaching others how to make money" is a saying that some repeat as wisdom and others use as a put-down, for example in the comments under a video that gives decent money advice, as if it "dismantles the entire argument so that they don't have to face reality and go and make money for themselves." Koe's counter is that the saying is treated as an accusation only because people pretend money is minor, "when money rules people's lives; it is the one thing most of the time holding them back from reaching the next level of their personal development; it dictates almost every single action a person takes," including the actions of those who think they are the most spiritual.

The third step targets precisely those people. Koe describes someone who reads a spiritual book, adopts it as an identity, changes what they wear, and acts "like they can just reject the material world, all personal development, all material progress, and then defend everything they do with the spiritual lens." He concedes that "there's people that are truly spiritual," "more conscious and aware and big picture thinkers," so his target is the posture, not spirituality. "Money still dictates their actions, because money is deeply intertwined with modern survival." He gives three examples. "Why do you work 8 hours a day for 45 years? Because you need to pay the bills and support your family." "If you think that you don't care about money, then try not working; you will probably not survive very long." And the most pointed: "Why do you go off and live in the woods like a monk? Because you want to eliminate the need for money to pursue personal development." Even renunciation is defined by money. It is an attempt to escape its demands. And to think "this is what everyone should do is more foolish than leaving society to rot": "'all is one' until you actually have to take responsibility for the health of the world, so you choose to check out instead."

The fourth step returns to the saying and generalizes it. "Everything is teaching people how to make money; some are just more direct and honest about it than others." The clearest case is school: "Why do you go to school for 12 to 24 years of your life? To increase your earning capacity; that's why everyone's going." People choose a specific college "because it's going to help them make money in the future." Koe adds a nuance: school does not teach only money explicitly; it teaches "other interests and skills and just education that allows you to get to that point." But the question stands: "If you don't care about money, why does everything you do revolve around it?" The payoff is psychological, not moral: "When you make that conscious and you accept that that is how this world operates, you'll be much better off and you'll see a lot more progress in every area of your life."

The fifth step turns the argument on the viewer. "Why are you watching this? To get a new perspective, yes; to learn something new, yes"; it opens your mind to reduce limiting beliefs about money. "Why do you watch my lifestyle design videos or productivity videos or mindset videos? It's because it's going to make you a higher value individual that can make more money. Is that the only reason? No." But money is "one of the reasons behind" almost everything, and "if you look at Maslow's hierarchy of needs and you just read them, most of them require money nowadays." The **higher value individual** is his phrase for a person whom personal development has made capable of earning more. It is a deliberately unromantic description of what self-improvement content offers.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

**Complementary context:** Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs orders human needs from physiological and safety needs at the base, through belonging and esteem, to self-actualization (and, in Maslow's later work, self-transcendence). Koe uses it here only as a list to read through: his claim is that, in a modern economy, most of the levels have a price.

In July 2025, Koe turned the unignorability of money into a criterion of personal development. "I do not care how spiritual you are or how intellectual you are. If you have a poor psychological relationship with money, you are not as developed as you think you are." The reason is the same as in 2023: money is involved in almost every decision, so a poor relationship with it makes life "pretty terrible." He adds a historical note against nostalgia: we do not live in pre-industrial times, and we will not go back to living like our ancestors. The 2023 argument said that money rules even those who reject it; the 2025 version draws the consequence for self-assessment. A development that leaves money out is incomplete, however advanced it feels.

**Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md (2025-07-27)

Three further passages connect this diagnosis to practice. In a February 2025 video on multiple interests, Koe explains that the next problem after finding your interests is turning them into an income, "because whether you like it or not you need an income to survive in order to pursue your multiple interests full-time. So if you're not okay with making money, or you're hiding the desire to make money even though it's the lifeblood of society, then I can't help you any further." The sentence sets a precondition for his entire teaching. Someone who hides the desire for money cannot be helped by methods whose purpose is to earn it.

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md (2025-02-23)

In a June 2024 video, he addresses the suppression of that desire directly. People avoid thinking about money ("money's evil"), yet in today's world it is what makes it possible to improve the other areas of life. You can improve them without money, he concedes, but it is "much easier with money to a certain extent," and you do not need to go overboard. Then an analogy from the gym: people say "I don't want to go to the gym because I'll get too big," which never happens. The financial version is "I don't want to start a business because I'll make too much money and get addicted." His answer: "that's what we're all trying to do, dude, start the business, you're not going to make too much money." The fear of excess is a way of avoiding the attempt.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

And in a September 2024 video on writing frameworks, Koe ties money to the meaningful life itself. You want work you enjoy and a meaningful lifestyle, but to achieve them you need "a certain degree of power and status": people who pay you for your work, people who listen to what you say, and a sense of control over the lifestyle you create. "The people that have a bad relationship with money, good luck getting what you want." Not being okay with making money "is going to hurt your life so much until you finally shed that limiting belief." This passage is notable because it uses the words "power" and "status" without apology. Meaningful work, in Koe's account, does not float free of the social conditions that make it possible.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md (2024-09-08)

#### Money as a unit of value

If money is unignorable, what is it? Koe's most compact answer comes in a March 2024 video titled "Working Hard Won't Make You Rich." It is a chain of three definitions:

1. "Money is a unit of value."
2. "Value is a measure of how much people care about what you do."
3. "How valuable you are equals the magnitude of problems you solve, the results of the solutions you create and your ability to get people to care about your creation."

Read in order, the chain moves from money to people's attention and then to the individual's capacities. Money measures value; value measures caring; and caring is produced by three things you control: the size of the problems you take on, the results your solutions actually deliver, and your ability to make others care. The third component is where marketing, persuasion and writing enter. A large problem well solved still earns nothing if nobody is made to care. The chain ends with a consequence that Koe states bluntly: "if you aren't happy with how much you make, it might be time to take a brutally honest look at what you contribute to the world."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

The idea did not appear all at once. It converged over about two years, through several formulations that differ in emphasis but not in substance.

| Date | Formulation | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| June 2023 | "Money or currency is a neutral form of value" | Money as a leveller that replaced barter |
| November 2023 | "There has to be a currency" | Against the gifting society |
| March 2024 | "Money is a unit of value" | Money as a measure of caring |
| September 2024 | "Money is a tool to achieve your potential" | Money as an instrument of growth |
| December 2024 | "Money is a measure of [shit] given" (Devon Eriksen) | Money as a signal |
| January 2025 | Money as a "feedback mechanism" | Money as a reflection of value given |
| March 2025 | "Your ability to create is your capacity to generate wealth" (adapted from David Deutsch) | Wealth as creative capacity |
| July 2025 | "Money is a globally accepted form of value" | Money as a precondition of large undertakings |

The earliest version, from a June 2023 video on the one-person business model, is historical. The exchange of goods ("product, service, idea, whatever you sell or communicate") has been part of existence since human interaction began. Money is "a neutral form of value": individuals spend it on what they perceive as valuable. Before it existed, "if someone didn't value your clay pot," you had no currency with which to get what you wanted. Money "levels the playing field" so that anyone can exchange what they deem valuable for what they want. From here Koe derives the role of marketing and sales: "illustrating the value of your product and putting a price tag that matches it for a specific person at a specific level of awareness, with specific goals, level of mind and problems, because that composes someone's identity." That is why you need a target audience or persona, and Koe's recommendation is to target yourself, "because you understand your identity best" and can attract and sell on the basis of your own values. The clay pot story explains why money is neutral. It carries no preference of its own; it transmits the preferences of whoever spends it. The rest of the passage explains why neutrality does not make selling automatic: value is perceived by a specific identity (Section 31.2), so price must be matched to that identity.

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md (2023-06-25)

In November 2023 Koe defended currency against a different objection, which he calls "sacred economics." "I've also heard arguments about like sacred economics where we need to go back to a gifting society or how our ancestors lived, where you exchange service for service." He grants part of it: "in some cases, sure, I understand that, and you should gift more." But the argument fails at scale: "if I want a house or I want a shower that works or I want something else, there isn't something that I can trade for that in a scalable manner that allows the unification of society or humanity, or allows it to scale and actually bring a better quality of life to everyone. There has to be a currency." He names no author or book; "sacred economics" is cited as a type of argument he has heard.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

**Complementary context:** *Sacred Economics* (2011) is a book by Charles Eisenstein that criticizes money-based economies and argues for a return to elements of a gift economy. Koe does not mention the book or its author, and his reply addresses the general argument as he heard it, not Eisenstein's specific proposals.

In September 2024 the account becomes more expansive. "Money itself is a beautiful invention; it has allowed us to transcend our survival through value exchange." The mechanism connects to Koe's definition of purpose as problem-solving (Chapter 8): "We each have problems that we value solving, so we solve them and soon have the desire to help someone solve them too; but since some people don't have the time, skill or desire, the invention of money allows them to have a different resource to solve the problem with, or at least make it much easier to solve." Money lets someone without the time or skill to solve a problem borrow the solution from someone who has it. Then the conclusion: "If problems are the limits on your potential, the things that prevent you from reaching the next level of purpose, then money is a tool to achieve your potential."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The most provocative version belongs not to Koe but to his guest Devon Eriksen, the novelist, in December 2024: "money is a measure of [shit] given, and when you start out not a lot of people are going to give a [shit] about you because they haven't heard about you." (The expletive is bleeped in the transcript.) Eriksen draws two implications that turn common attitudes inside out. If you say "I don't want a lot of money," what you are saying is "I don't want a lot of people to give a [shit] about me." If you say that people with a lot of money must have done something evil, you are saying that "most people only give a [shit] about evil people." "Money is just a token that says I care about this thing, and it's how... we persuade people to care." So when money starts to come in (assuming you are not robbing banks, winning the lottery or defrauding people), "that is a signal that people care about what you're doing, it's a signal that you're on the right path, it's the way people tell you what they like and what they don't like." And: "When you hit on a formula that starts to earn, that's not you being selfish; it's you figuring out what people like and giving it." Eriksen's version is close to Koe's March 2024 definition (value as a measure of how much people care), but it adds the idea of money as information. Earnings are a message from the market about what it values. The three exceptions he names (theft, luck, fraud) are the cases in which the money carries no such message.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

Koe adopts that reading of money as information in his own voice a month later. When Sahil Bloom asks him "what is your definition of enough?", Koe hesitates: "I don't know, because for me, money is... deeply intertwined with and a reflection of the value that I give. So it's very rewarding... the more I make, the more I can kind of see that. And so it's like, do I want to ever shut that off?" If his work is his life's work, he could in principle "work for free in some way," but then "I'm not getting that feedback mechanism." He also has a practical reason: he needs to make "quite a bit" to pay his team. The question he raises (how this squares with "enough") is answered by Sahil in Section 34.5. Here the point is the definition. Money is a **feedback mechanism**: the visible reflection of the value one gives.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

In March 2025 Koe borrowed a definition of wealth from the physicist David Deutsch: wealth is the set of transformations you can make, the ability to create. His adaptation turns it into a statement about the individual: "your ability to create is your capacity to generate wealth," and business is the packaging of that creative ability for others. The adaptation shifts the meaning of wealth from possession to capacity. The wealthy person is the one who can transform the most, not the one who holds the most.

**Source:** How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md (2025-03-02)

**Complementary context:** David Deutsch is a British physicist known for his work on quantum computation and for *The Beginning of Infinity* (2011), where he argues that problems are soluble and that knowledge can grow without bound. Elsewhere in the corpus Koe draws from the same book the conclusion that value, wealth and money, as exchanges of problem-solving, are themselves infinite.

The July 2025 formulation is the most sober. "As dangerous and as evil as money can be, you can't build rockets or feed the world without it." "Money is a globally accepted form of value." If money ceased to exist, or people simply made less of it, the world would get bad fast, "because people don't work when they aren't motivated with a reward." He specifies that the reward is not necessarily the only motive, but it is why people work. This version concedes what the earlier ones did not: money "can be" dangerous and evil. The concession does not change the conclusion, because the alternative to money is not a purer world but one in which large-scale cooperation stops.

**Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md (2025-07-27)

#### Lifeblood, multiplier and spiritual energy

Beyond the definition of money as a unit of value, Koe gives it functions that are almost organic, and at times spiritual. These formulations are more metaphorical than the ones above, and they should be read as such: they are ways of making the importance of money felt, not claims about its nature that could be tested.

The oldest is the metaphor of blood, from a March 2023 video. Money is "the lifeblood of society," the fabric that holds it together. When blood stops flowing to your hand, the hand dies; if blood (money) stops flowing to a certain space in the online business arena, that space dies off. Koe extends the metaphor from the market to the individual: if your business does not fuel the market, does not provide blood to it, your business dies, "and therefore your sense of purpose dies." The link between money and purpose is a chain: if you are not making money, you are not contributing to society, and so you are not contributing to something greater than yourself, which is what having a purpose means. Conclusion: "business is the modern vessel for living with purpose, and business is a vessel for your value." In the same passage he dismisses the alternatives: online surveys, prescriptive business models found online, and most jobs are "soulless."

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md (2023-03-19)

The metaphor of the **vessel**, which recurs throughout the corpus with many shades (the business, the brand, the newsletter, the body), here takes its economic form: the business is the container through which value, and therefore purpose, reaches the world. The chain "no money, no contribution, no purpose" is the strongest claim in this section, and it is worth noting what it assumes: that contribution to society is measured, at least in the modern world, by whether someone is willing to pay for it. Koe does not discuss forms of contribution that are unpaid by design (care, volunteering, much of art), and the chain should be read as a claim about work offered to a market, not about every form of human contribution.

In December 2024, Koe gave money the role of a **multiplier** and **catalyst**. Money was not necessary to explore and discover in the past, and in many respects it still is not, but "it is a multiplier, it is a catalyst" for discovering, building, exploring, making mistakes, connecting to something bigger and contributing. Then a sentence that relocates the problem from money to the mind: "Money is simply a piece of paper; it has the meaning that you assign to it." People who assign it a negative meaning out of a lack of understanding become slaves to it. (The transcript has "swword"; "slave" is the likely word.) He proposes a test: when Koe mentions money, or says it can be good, do you react? "Why does a word have so much control over your mind?" The test applies to money the awareness practice of Part I: notice the automatic reaction, and ask who installed it.

**Source:** How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md (2024-12-15)

In February 2026, discussing a future in which AI may make many jobs disappear, Koe speculated about how money's role would change. If money moves away from being a productivity metric, he thinks it will remain a status symbol but become "a tool to express agency," a tool for growth as it is now. "We're just removing the shallower layer," transcending to what money was meant to be: "your ability to do things you weren't able to do before," "a physical representation of your growth." This is a hypothesis about the future, stated as his opinion. It connects to Chapter 35 (agency) and Chapter 36 (the economy of the individual in the age of AI), where the post-labor frame is developed.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md (2026-02-12)

The most expansive formulation is the one Koe calls **money as spiritual energy**. In a February 2024 video he says that work is a financial necessity and finances are necessary for survival, but that money "is not to be demonized but to be earned and used as a spiritual energy." It is earned to solve your own problems, "problems being the limits on your mind and other people's minds," which allows you to expand, transcend and evolve. The two halves are symmetrical. You earn money purposefully by solving your own problems and selling the solution (the principle of Section 28.2), and you spend it purposefully by fueling and energizing those aspects of reality, not on wasteful objects. Later in the video a phrase appears whose transcription is ambiguous ("money is a tool to build what you want but doesn't exist"); the most plausible reading is that money is a tool to build what you want that does not yet exist. He promises to develop "the creator philosophy" and the idea that "money is a spiritual energy" in the next video.

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md (2024-02-25)

In September 2024 he applied the idea to a specific situation: the person near the top of a career ladder. "If you're stuck in a job and you're nearing the end of the top of the career ladder, there is one thing that will launch you into the next phase: realizing that money is much, much more spiritual than you think." He contrasts it with the default script: "we work and work and work until we earn enough to rest, all to find ourselves unsatisfied with how much we have and how little we are"; "we drown in survival mode"; "wake up, go to work, deal with the boss, eat convenient foods because you don't have the time, skip the good habits you promised yourself... watch your life slowly crash as your mind, body and relationships unravel into chaos, and do nothing about it because it's the only life you know." The phrase "how little we are" is the key. The person at the top of the ladder has accumulated money without becoming more. In this context, the "spirituality" of money means using money to become something rather than to rest from work.

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

These two 2024 statements stand in tension with a 2025 statement that Section 34.2 examines: "the people who endlessly moralize or spiritualize money can't fathom a life where they aren't bound to it." The tension is presented there, after the analysis of money programming, because the 2025 criticism is part of Koe's account of how people rationalize a poor relationship with money.

### 34.2 Money Programming

Section 34.1 established what money is in Koe's account. This section explains why so many people relate to it badly. His answer applies to money the model of conditioning developed in Part I: beliefs installed early, by parents, religion and culture, determine what a person can perceive, and therefore which opportunities they can act on. The section moves from the historical roots of the demonization of money, through Koe's diagnosis and list of limiting beliefs, to the social figures he uses to make the diagnosis vivid (the cog in the machine, the noble broi, the sleazy salesman), and ends with the question of financial identity.

#### Where the demonization comes from: the ascending philosophies

Koe offers one historical explanation for why money (and sex) came to be seen as unspiritual. In a November 2024 video on cheap dopamine, discussing the agrarian period, he says it also "led to the ascending philosophies" (a topic he says he can discuss in another video). The spiritual side of human culture started to focus solely on "the ascending" and disconnected from "the descending," or the "physiosphere." This, he says, is when "the demonization of things like money and sex became a thing for religions and spiritual people," because they lost the connection to "the great chain of being: physiosphere, biosphere, noosphere." (The transcript renders the last phrase as "physios spere biosphere new sphere.") The consequence reaches the present: "people still hold this mindset that material pursuits are all bad when they are not, and they are actually connected to Spirit."

**Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md (2024-11-24)

Koe does not name a source for this passage, but the vocabulary is not his own.

**Complementary context:** The distinction between "ascending" and "descending" currents of spirituality, the "great chain of being" and the sequence physiosphere–biosphere–noosphere are central to Ken Wilber's *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality* (1995) and *A Brief History of Everything* (1996). Wilber argues that a healthy spirituality integrates the ascending current (the movement from the many to the One, away from the world) with the descending current (the One expressing itself in the many, embracing the world), and that much of Western and Eastern religious history privileged ascent and devalued matter, body and sexuality. Koe uses Wilber extensively elsewhere (Chapter 38), so the borrowing is likely, but in this passage the attribution is not made in the transcript.

The passage matters for the chapter because it gives the demonization of money a history rather than treating it as a timeless moral insight. If the aversion to material pursuits is the residue of a particular historical split, then it can be examined like any other piece of inherited programming. Koe's conclusion ("they are actually connected to Spirit") also prepares the 2024 claim that money is "spiritual energy" (Section 34.1).

#### Money problems are psychological problems

Koe's September 2024 video on money opens with a declaration of method. "I'm not here to tell you that the truth of money was somehow hidden from you like this is some esoteric secret; it's right in front of your eyes but you just can't see it." The obstacle is perceptual. And the first step he prescribes is about responsibility: "The first thing that we're going to do is not blame anyone but yourself for the situation that you're in." The reason: "you didn't question how you were raised, developed a terrible psychological relationship with money, and because of that you failed to understand that making money is a skill. You can make as much as you want; you just didn't take the time to study, practice and master that skill." His stated aim is "to install the right money programming into your head, as that's the first step to making as much as you want."

**Money programming** is Koe's term for the beliefs about money that upbringing installs. The term relies on the computational metaphor of Chapter 1, in which the mind runs programs that someone else wrote. The prescription "blame no one but yourself" may seem to contradict the claim that the beliefs were installed by others. Koe resolves the apparent contradiction by placing the fault in the failure to question: you are not responsible for how you were raised, but you are responsible for never examining it.

The same video gives the diagnosis its most quoted form. "The average person does not have a good relationship with money simply because of how they were raised": very few were raised in a household comfortable with money, "because so few people were comfortable with money in order to raise the children to be that way." The deficit reproduces itself across generations. "Your conditioning, your programming shapes the entirety of how you view situations and the opportunities available to you," so you must become aware of it in order to "notice it and start to reprogram it." Then the principle: "**Money problems don't exist, psychological problems do.** Most people's inability to make, keep or smartly use money comes down to their beliefs, because your beliefs determine the opportunities you can notice to do those things."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The principle has three verbs (make, keep, use) and one mechanism (beliefs filter perception of opportunity). It is a direct application of the model of Part III, where attention and goals act as lenses that determine what a mind can see. Koe accompanies it with two disclaimers that soften its tone: "I was in this exact same place," and "I'm not saying any of this stuff is bad, I'm just saying this is where most people are." The principle is presented as a description of the common starting point, not as an accusation.

If you don't believe money is a skill, "you won't learn that skill; you'll just avoid any and all education that allows you to make more; you won't expand your mind to the level of those that are actually making money." He caricatures the opposite expectation: it is not "I'm at this certain level of development, I have this certain skill level, just give me all this money... On the fourth (business means sleazy salesmen and long hours), Koe makes a point about what an entrepreneur does: "if you're an entrepreneur your job is to solve problems; that's what you do. It's not like those things are just an inherent part of making money; that doesn't make any sense." The objection treats as given the features that an entrepreneur is precisely free to design away. This connects with the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12, which is Koe's own solution to the "long hours" problem.

On the fifth (everyone is a grifter), Koe offers what he calls a "rant." People say "I support independent businesses, let's cheer for the underdog," and then, when those businesses start selling something, without the resources of a large corporation and unable to sell something "so mass produced," they "just get picked apart by this demonizing mob of people who don't understand that some people can actually benefit from a product that you may not benefit from." The last clause is the substance of the reply. The critic generalizes from his own lack of interest to the conclusion that the product is worthless, forgetting that value is perceived by a specific identity at a specific level of awareness (Section 31.2).

#### Ego-defending beliefs: Devon Eriksen's mechanism

Koe's list describes the content of limiting beliefs. In the December 2024 conversation, Devon Eriksen supplies a mechanism that explains why such beliefs persist even when they hurt the person holding them. He links negative views of money to self-limiting identity beliefs and reconstructs their inner logic: "I am not a billionaire, I will not be a billionaire, therefore I need to formulate my beliefs and my identity in such a way that I am prevented from feeling bad about that." The typical formulations follow: "they must have done something immoral to get there... I am not immoral and I can take pride in that"; "they had a tremendous amount of luck, therefore it's not my fault"; "their parents provided them with wealth and access that my parents did not."

"All of these identity beliefs are designed not to help this person prosper but to prevent them from feeling bad about not prospering." If we focus on defending our current identity and not feeling bad, we will not focus on the external goal of getting ahead. Eriksen concedes the factual core of the rationalizations: it may be true that reaching billionaire level requires luck, the right parents or other help, because "thriving is a team sport; no one prospers in a vacuum." But the belief does not stay confined to billionaires. It "isn't just about 'oh, I'm not going to be a billionaire'; it prevents any kind of getting ahead... any kind of striving," any statement of the form "I am not as good as I would like to be, therefore I am going to make an effort to become something that I am currently not." These **ego-defending beliefs** make people feel better where they are, "but it prevents them from progressing beyond it."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

Eriksen's mechanism fits the model of identity in Part II, where identity is defended like a body and new information that threatens it is rejected. Applied to money, the defense has a specific cost: a belief adopted to protect self-esteem against the comparison with the very rich ends up forbidding the modest striving that would have improved one's own situation. The concession ("thriving is a team sport") is important for the fairness of the argument. Eriksen does not deny luck or privilege. He denies that their existence justifies a belief system whose function is to make effort unnecessary.

#### Parents, pastor, culture: make as much money as you can

In July 2025, Koe turned the diagnosis into an instruction addressed to people in their twenties and thirties. Among the things to do at that age, the second is to make as much money as you can. He explains that this is "not because we're evil," but as a way to supercharge growth and development. Money is tricky, he says, because it is intimately tied to self-worth, morality and survival, and people hold conditioned beliefs that they think are unchangeable or that register as truth. He names three sources: your parents say save it; your pastor says shun it or don't worry about it; your culture, "that runs on money," says it is not important. The result is a person piled into the ground with bills, unable to get what they want, guilty about pursuing it, and convinced that they cannot make money and be a good person at the same time, which Koe calls "categorically false." His recommendation is to develop both your mind and your finances, to be well-rounded, and to decide about money from your own experience, seeing beyond the virtue signaling on social media.

**Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md (2025-07-27)

A week later, in an August 2025 video on getting rich, he gave the same idea a moral formulation. "That's what getting rich is for in a lot of cases: becoming a good and better person to the point where you have so much value that you can't stop attracting wealth." For those with the hangup that "getting rich is bad," the association comes from what "society has been telling me, my parents have been telling me, my pastor has been telling me." He gives the counter-description from the inside: "I'm learning these skills, I'm doing great things, I'm helping other people... I'm exchanging value." The one projecting "has this notion that it's for some reason evil to engage in a mutually beneficial transaction, and I think that says a lot more about them than it does you."

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

The phrase **mutually beneficial transaction** restates the value exchange of Section 34.1, and the claim that the moral objection "says more about them" introduces the theme of projection that the next subsection develops. Note also the reversal of the usual moral order. In the common view, getting rich competes with being good; in Koe's view, in many cases, getting rich is a consequence of becoming better, because value attracts money.

#### Business is not a dirty word

Koe's warnings against rejecting the word "business" come from his own history. In a January 2026 video, he speaks to his younger self, who was "conditioned, very programmed to have a negative outlook on money, a negative outlook on business." Entrepreneurship and business have become **dirty words**: "that's for unethical people. That's for talented people. I'm not that talented. That's for people with a lot of money." The three rationalizations (unethical, talented, rich) each place business outside the speaker's reach. The danger is perceptual: "in closing your mind off to it, … you're just closing your mind off to the thing that could change your life... You're not able to see them if you reject the idea that allows you to see them." The "them" are the opportunities. The sentence states the core of money programming in its most compact form: a rejected idea is not merely a rejected option; it is a blind spot.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md (2026-01-20)

In June 2026 Koe proposed a definition designed to disarm the word altogether. If you know how to package your interests so that they get attention, or turn them into a product people pay for, "then you have a way of monetizing your interests, you have a business. And if you don't like the word business, then just think of it as getting paid for what you do. That's what a business is. Don't overcomplicate it. Don't assign any negative connotation to the word business because you think it's not for you." Over three years, the definitions of business in the corpus become progressively more minimal: a legal structure to achieve your vision in public (2024), a legal structure to distribute value (2024), getting paid for what you do (2026). Each removes something that might provoke resistance.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md (2026-06-13)

The sharpest version of the argument, from a May 2024 video on self-discipline, treats the demonization of money as a **projection**. "Most people are programmed to have a negative reaction when they hear the word business," which Koe calls "one of the worst things that could happen to you," because it delays your perception from noticing money-making opportunities and acting on them. "If you constantly think that business and money are bad, then you aren't going to make any," which sets you up for unnecessary suffering. Money is "just a piece of paper; it's neutral until put in the hands of good or evil, and if you are evil or you think money is evil, that's probably a projection of your internal state, so maybe you shouldn't have money and maybe you should do some internal work first."

The argument then turns to what the demonization protects. When you mislabel money and business, "you hide from your desire to reach your potential, self-actualize and self-transcend by demonizing anyone and anything that would compromise the comfort you don't even know you are addicted to," and you continue to be "a **cog in the machine** of a business that is making money in potentially unethical ways." Two contradictions follow. "You don't want to start a business, but the only other option is to work for a business." And: "You say money and starting a business is superficial, but the thing that consumes most of your time is scrolling on surface-level social media." Against the charge of superficiality, Koe claims that business is "the most deep pursuit you can go into": you become an expert, dive deep, then pass that to others in exchange for money.

**Source:** It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md (2024-05-12)

The phrase "the comfort you don't even know you are addicted to" links this passage to Chapter 11 on dopamine and comfort. The demonization of business is not only a belief; it is a defense of a comfortable routine, and the person defending it does not experience the defense as such.

Koe and Devon Eriksen apply the projection argument to critics of other people's products. In the December 2024 conversation, Koe observes that under anyone's book or product promotion there are comments like "you shouldn't be earning money from this" or "this is a terrible product," even when the product is doing extremely well, from people who do not care about that thing but are "in this reactive state." Eriksen interprets the comments: "they're objecting to the fact that somebody else cares about something that they don't care about... they're essentially saying that everybody should act in accordance with their priorities, not with their own priorities." He calls it, "if you dig into it, one of the most anti-libertarian, sort of anti-humanity sentiments... people shouldn't spend their money how they want to spend it on what they care about, they should obey me. Well, who died and put you in charge?" The observation is Koe's; the interpretation is Eriksen's. It completes Koe's "rant" about independent businesses: the critic demands that his own indifference govern other people's purchases.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

#### The noble broi and the sleazy salesman

The November 2023 video "People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions" opens with a figure that Koe names and attacks: the "noble broi." (The term appears this way in the transcript; the intended spelling is uncertain, and the chapter keeps the transcribed form.) "In today's world one of the worst things you can do is get trapped in the mind of the noble broi." The **noble broi** is the intelligent person who considers marketing, sales and online business beneath them: "Many intelligent people that I know hate the thought of marketing, sales and online business because they think it's below them," even if they don't say so explicitly. "They get locked into what they think is a quote unquote higher perspective that demonizes the sleazy salesman."

The noble broi defines himself against the **sleazy salesman**, the visible surface of business that the fourth limiting belief fixes on. Koe's claim is that the opposition is false. "In reality this is an illusion: this higher perspective is filled with misunderstanding, a lack of empathy and hate; it is on par with, if not a lower level of consciousness than, the sleazy salesman himself." The noble broi's contempt is itself a failure of consciousness. Koe's stated aim addresses both figures: that "the sleazy salesman might be able to find purpose in their business, and the noble broi has the ability to actually make more of an impact on the world and help raise the collective consciousness." The phrase **raise the collective consciousness** names the goal that the following argument serves.

The argument proceeds by consequences. "If you don't start a business to provide information, education and goods that make a better humanity, then unethical businesses rise to the top without competition, making a worse humanity." Koe turns the noble broi's self-image against him: "The reason that unethical businesses flood the marketplace is that you aren't as conscious or intelligent as you think you are." Abstention is not neutral: "You are directly contributing to evil by not starting a business to raise the collective consciousness, by doing nothing but demonizing money and businesses that are the lifeblood of society." And the abstainer is not outside the system: "you are assigned a job where the employer is more than likely unethical by your standards." "You won't start a business because of your delusional assumptions and conditioning that it is unethical to make money, but you are working for a business that makes the population sick, contributes to bombing people across the world, and locks employees into robotic routines so that they wouldn't dare achieve their potential."

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

The same video adds the mechanism that makes abstention costly: money compounds attention. "By doing nothing you allow people less conscious and intelligent than you to have more attention, impact and money than you." "Money creates a **ripple effect** that allows people to spread their message further"; it lets them hire employees and build their business "to take its roots in society"; "money allows you to have the resources to compound the attention you hold as an individual or a company." If that company leans toward the unethical side, "they're going to have a lot more attention than you"; if it leans conscious, "the same thing happens," but more of that consciousness is born. The ripple effect is neutral, like money itself. It amplifies whoever holds it, which is why Koe argues that the conscious should hold it.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

Ten months later, in September 2024, Koe restated the argument in a new frame. "Most people in business are trapped in the survival and status stage; that's why you see them as unspiritual. But business is simply a legal structure to distribute value." The survival and status stages are levels of development (introduced in Chapter 9, with the levels of purpose and the criteria for entering and leaving the status stage), so the sleaziness that critics see in business is reassigned to the developmental level of particular business people, not to business as such. "Not all businesses are evil, but not starting your own ensures that the evil ones will thrive." "When you are in the survival or status stage, by not starting a business you are bound to unconsciously contribute to the businesses you deem as evil by working for a corporation that sickens the masses." "You're demonizing businesses but you're working for a business that sells a product, and by your standards it's probably unethical how they do business." He answers the fear that one's own business would turn out unethical with the projection argument ("your projections are a reflection of yourself") and with a comparative question: "is it more or less unethical than the person you work for?"

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The 2024 version is a reformulation, not a change of position. It adds two things to the 2023 argument: the developmental frame, which explains why business looks unspiritual, and the comparative question, which replaces the absolute standard ("is business ethical?") with a relative one ("is your business more or less ethical than your employer's?"). The second is the more practical. Nobody has to prove that commerce is pure; they only have to compare it with the alternative they are already living.

These arguments rest on assumptions worth naming. They assume that a person who abstains from business necessarily works for a large and probably unethical corporation, which leaves out public service, small cooperatives and other forms of employment. And they assume that more conscious founders would, on the whole, build more ethical businesses. Koe does not defend either assumption at length; they function as premises of a rhetoric aimed at a specific listener, the intelligent person who disdains business while living from it.

#### Spiritualizing money: a change of position

Section 34.1 presented two 2024 statements: money should be "earned and used as a spiritual energy" (February 2024), and "realizing that money is much, much more spiritual than you think" is what launches someone at the top of the career ladder into the next phase (September 2024). In August 2025, in the video on getting rich in one's twenties, Koe says something that appears to contradict them: "The people who endlessly moralize or spiritualize money can't fathom a life where they aren't bound to it." In this sentence, the moral or spiritual framing of money is a symptom of being bound to money, not a transcendence of it.

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

Koe does not comment on the change, and the corpus gives no reason for it. Two readings are possible, and the sources support both without settling between them. On the first, the statements address different things. The 2024 statements concern *using* money with purpose (earning it by solving problems and spending it to "energize reality"), while the 2025 statement concerns using a moral or spiritual frame to *justify rejecting* money, in line with his 2023 attack on people who "defend everything they do with the spiritual lens." On this reading there is no contradiction, only two different uses of the word "spiritual." On the second reading, the shift is real. The 2025 Koe has moved toward a more secular and pragmatic language about money ("unapologetically rich," Section 34.4), and the earlier spiritual vocabulary no longer fits it. The reader should keep both statements in view. The first gives money a place in a larger meaning; the second warns that talk of meaning can be a way of avoiding money altogether.

#### Abundant and scarce financial identity

The last element of money programming is identity: the sense of who one is in relation to money. The richest discussion here comes not from Koe but from Dickie Bush, in their long March 2023 conversation.

Bush describes himself as building "a mindset of financial abundance." He grew up "by far the poorest kid in my neighborhood," and although he has broken out financially, beliefs entrenched at a young age remain. He works on them with an identity note, a practice he uses for other areas of life (Chapter 4): "I'm someone who has an abundant financial mindset." He defines it: "I always have more than what I need and I operate that way... instead of hoarding... I let money kind of flow as energy." The scarcity he is fighting has concrete forms. Growing up, he learned (possibly from his father) not to tip valet drivers, because "they didn't do that much." Recently he tipped a valet $20 and logged it as evidence for the new identity. In the moment, he says, he feels the old belief ("you don't tip valet drivers... they're just doing their job"), but he knows he gets to write it down afterward: "it's silly but it works."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The example is small by design. Identity change, in the model of Chapter 4, proceeds by accumulating evidence for the new identity through actions that contradict the old one. A $20 tip is an action cheap enough to take and specific enough to contradict a specific inherited rule. The phrase "let money flow as energy" is close to Koe's later "spiritual energy," though the two are independent formulations.

Bush's money history, told in the same conversation, shows that the feeling of wealth does not depend on absolute income. He has been financially independent from his parents since his sophomore or junior year of high school (around 2009, at 13 or 14; his father worked in real estate, which was "at zero" after the crash, and his mother raised him and his sister). He tutored SAT math at about $40 an hour, making a couple of hundred dollars a week. He draws a business lesson from it: "don't tutor subjects, tutor to a test." SAT tutoring was the most profitable because "you're pricing off a value": parents saw the score increase as money saved, through potential scholarships. In his senior year he bought his first car for $800 in cash, under a highway: a Ford Explorer from the early 1990s with about 150,000 miles, while friends got new cars at 16. He calls this "sovereign your own money." He tutored through college, did an internship that led to BlackRock (his only full-time job and his only job interview, where he stayed three years), and expected $100,000 a year to feel like absurd money. In New York, "that feeling of scarcity came rushing back": 50–55% went to taxes, the rent was absurd, and "the take-home money of like a first-year analyst in New York City is negative if you factor in rent... taxes... any kind of social activity." He felt "wealthier when I was just a tutor." He adds an image from his floor: someone in their early or mid-thirties asking their boss for permission to go to their son's Little League game and being told no. He knew then that he would not work a full-time job forever, but he did not quit right away.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

Three lessons can be drawn from the story, and they connect to different parts of this chapter. The tutoring lesson ("price off a value") is a small version of the value-based pricing that Section 34.3 opposes to the labor theory of value: the tutor who tutors to a test is paid for an outcome, not for hours. The BlackRock episode shows that scarcity is relative to the environment. A higher salary in a more expensive city, with less control over one's time, produced a stronger feeling of scarcity than a lower income earned independently. And the Little League image anticipates the "individual choice" of the new rich (Section 34.4): money without control over one's time does not feel like wealth.

Koe's own contribution to the theme of abundance is earlier and more combative. In an August 2022 video on writing, he argues that with one to two hours of writing a day and a product or service, if you believe in yourself, have an **abundance mindset** and understand that "**the internet is infinite**," you can make a higher monthly salary than "one of the world's most highest paid doctors," because doctors "have a cap": "there is no room for improvement for a lot of them." The claim is stated as an opinion and should be read as one: Koe gives no figures. Its logic, however, is the logic of Section 34.3. The doctor is paid within a structure that limits his income; the writer with a product is paid according to how far his work spreads. Abundance, in Koe's version, is not only an emotional disposition toward money. It is a belief about the structure of the opportunity: the internet has no ceiling.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md (2022-08-28)

### 34.3 Leveraged, Not Hard-Working

Section 34.2 argued that the main obstacle to money is psychological. This section examines the most widespread of the beliefs involved, because Koe treats it separately and at length: the belief that effort entitles one to payment. He calls it "the delusion of hard work" and attaches it to what he calls "the labor theory of value." Against it he sets three related ideas: leverage, earning with the mind rather than with time, and creative income. The section ends with Devon Eriksen's argument that selling time cannot scale.

#### The delusion of hard work

The March 2024 video "Working Hard Won't Make You Rich" opens by naming the belief. **The delusion of hard work** is the belief that effort by itself entitles you to payment. "You can work hard at anything, but that doesn't mean it's useful for the progress of humanity; this is the delusion of hard work." Koe's examples are the standard investments of a conventional life: you can put four years into a degree or ten years into climbing a corporate ladder and still not be paid anywhere close to what you want. Then he describes the response of the deluded person. Instead of "taking your future into your own hands," he whines and complains: "I deserve to be paid more," "I've spent 14 years working hard and this is all I get for it," no time for family, no money for a vacation, "I slave away with no light at the end of the tunnel." According to Koe, "the complainers of the world are missing one crucial piece of the puzzle": pay follows value, not labor.

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

The same video closes off the complainer's strategy. Complaining about pay "is not going to get you paid." It will "probably just get you fired or ignored," or make you "hated by everyone that comes into contact with you," because they will think "okay, what are you going to do about it?" The conclusion is the only remaining option: "the only option is to take matters into your own hands." This is a practical observation, not a theory, and it anticipates the self-reliance of Chapter 35: when the structure that pays you does not reward effort, the solution is not to ask the structure for fairness but to change your position in it.

#### The labor theory of value and its renamings

The "crucial piece" that the complainers miss is explained by an economic term. In the same video, Koe names "**the labor theory of value**" as the belief that "you should be paid for the amount of work you do," so as to "feel as if you have jumped through hoops to feel like you deserve something." He rejects it: "that's not how reality works." "It does not make sense to pay someone based on the amount of work they do; it does make sense to pay someone based on the level of problems they solve." His example is himself as a writer. One year of hard work writing to become an author does not justify "100,000 units of currency to just be given to me." People only care whether the writing is beneficial to them; "the amount of writing I did within that year doesn't matter"; what matters is "the quality and results gotten from that writing." In this transcript Koe does not attribute the term to any thinker. He uses it as a label for the mindset he is criticizing.

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

**Complementary context:** In economics, the labor theory of value is the doctrine, developed in classical political economy by Adam Smith and David Ricardo and in a different form by Karl Marx, that the value of a commodity is determined by the labor required to produce it. Koe does not engage with that tradition or its debates. He uses the name for an everyday attitude, the expectation that pay should be proportional to effort or hours. Chapters 30 and 31 note the same loose use (Sections 30.6 and 31.1).

The idea reappears in later videos under different names, with the same core and some additions.

| Date | Name | Formulation |
|---|---|---|
| March 2024 | "The labor theory of value" | Being paid for the amount of work you do; "not how reality works" |
| July 2024 | "The labor theory of value" (as a gloss) | "Your work is worth this much per hour"; it keeps you stuck at a job (Section 34.1) |
| August 2024 | "The labor theory of value frame" | "My time is worth this much"; it limits the perception of opportunity |
| March 2026 | "The old paradigm" | "My time equals how much I earn" vs "how much I earn equals the value that I provide" |

In an August 2024 video on minimalist productivity, the belief becomes a frame. People who are not successful in business, or have not started one, "haven't broken out of the labor theory of value frame, where my time is worth this much," and Koe says he wants to break the viewer out of it. The frame matters because it limits what a person can imagine. If income equals hours multiplied by a rate, then reaching a six-year goal (such as a million dollars) in six months is unthinkable. (Chapter 7, Section 7.5, develops that thought experiment.) Later in the same video he predicts that people working 12-hour days on tasks they chose only because they felt they had to will end up in his DMs "with the hundreds of others who feel cheated by the labor theory of value."

**Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md (2024-08-11)

In a March 2026 video on building a one-person business with AI, the frame is renamed "**the old paradigm**." Discussing the objections people raise to his arithmetic of a million-dollar business (Section 30.6), he says: "The objections in your mind... 'Oh, I can't do this, this sounds unfeasible,' it's because you're probably stuck in the old paradigm. You're stuck in 'my time equals how much I earn' rather than 'how much I earn equals the value that I provide.'" The 2026 version adds an explanation of where the paradigm comes from and what replaces it. If the only life you have known is school and a job, you are "wired to believe" that earning more means a better job or better schooling. "You don't understand that if you're an entrepreneur, you hunt for your better job, you hunt for your gigs, you hunt for your customers, and you self-educate. You don't need to go to an institution to get a credential in order for someone to hire you." And it adds a skill the old paradigm never required: "A lot of people aren't willing to deal with the uncertainty," so "another skill that you have to learn is just tolerating and mitigating the risk of starting a business."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md (2026-03-15)

The renaming does not change the idea, and Koe gives no reason for it. What the later version adds is the cost of leaving the paradigm. Pay tied to value is not only higher in potential; it is uncertain, and the person who wants it has to learn to tolerate that uncertainty. Being paid by the hour buys predictability, and Devon Eriksen will name that purchase below.

#### A feature, not a bug

Devon Eriksen gives the critique of the labor theory of value its most direct economic statement in the December 2024 conversation. "Sort of the socialist-leaning types think this is a bug, like, you know, he only did 3 hours of work and he's being paid tens of thousands of dollars for it, but really that's a feature, because we want everybody to be doing things that require very little effort but have tremendous value, and so capitalism rewards those people." If you can spend four hours shooting a video and make a lot of money, or spend three hours a day on something and make "800,000 in a year or whatever," it means "you've figured out how to efficiently produce something that has a lot of value to people." Selling your time, by contrast, is "this safe route, which solves that equation well enough to keep you going, but it's maybe not the best or most profitable thing you could be doing with your time."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The **feature, not a bug** argument reverses the moral intuition behind the complainer's position. The complainer sees disproportionate pay for little time as an injustice. Eriksen sees it as the system working as intended: a society should want its members to find the highest-value use of their effort, and high pay for little time is the signal that someone has found it. The phrase "the safe route" introduces the theme of safety that returns in his argument about selling time at the end of this section.

#### Body, time or mind

Koe's alternative to the labor theory of value begins with a personal decision. In a September 2023 video he describes how he chose his path. He always knew he wanted to do something creative and never really wanted to work a job (though he did). He had an interest in fitness, but he did not want to post pictures of his body online: "if I could get super fit that would be a cool thing, but it never really stuck with me." He also knew he did not want a job. Hence the reasoning: "I didn't want to earn from both my body or my time, so I knew that I had to earn with my mind." The passage implies three sources of income (body, time and mind), and the choice is made by elimination.

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md (2023-09-24)

The same choice appears in a January 2024 video as a principle quoted from Koe's own book. A third of your life is work, he says; if that third drains your energy for the next third, and the last third is sleep, then work is important and you must take control of it. (The proportion of life given to work varies across the corpus, from about a quarter to about a third; the figure is a framing device, not a measurement.) He quotes *The Art of Focus*: "the ability to earn with your intelligence, not your time, labor or looks, is how you become in control of what you do and how much you make." He then answers a question his editor relayed from a reader: if I am into fitness, do I need to post shirtless pictures? No. Earn with your mind, your intelligence, your writing; understand how to create value. Plenty of fitness writers simply post actionable advice. The control he promises has two objects: "what you do" and "how much you make." Earning with your intelligence gives you both, because an idea can be redirected and its price is not fixed by hours.

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md (2024-01-21)

In a June 2024 video, the principle became a triple slogan for "how to play the **infinite digital game**": "get paid for your ideas, not your looks; get paid for your mind, not your time; get paid for the infinite, not the finite." The context was AI's threat to specialists' livelihoods. The third pair names the structural difference. Time and looks are finite resources, while ideas can be reproduced and distributed without limit. The phrase connects to the theme of infinite games (Chapter 10, Section 10.7).

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md (2024-06-09)

#### Labor workers and creative workers

The fullest version of the distinction comes from a November 2023 video on productizing the mind. When he was young, Koe says, he made "a crucial observation": "I had to earn with my mind, not my time." He then contrasts two kinds of workers.

| | Labor workers | Creative workers |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Accomplish as much as their body and time allow | Solve the problems that lead to the most results, regardless of time spent |
| Salary and schedule | Locked into a salary and schedule that pay a set amount for their efforts | Create their own salary and schedule |
| What they are paid for | Their time | "The level of problem they solved" |
| Example | The doctor, "paid the same amount whether they save a life or just tell someone to take aspirin" | The writer, paid according to what they write, what their product is and how far the writing spreads |

"There's nothing wrong with labor work," Koe adds, but you must understand that you are trading time for money and that your time is limited. The doctor example is chosen to show that labor work is not low-status work. A doctor is highly paid and does important things, yet the structure of the pay is the same as that of any hourly worker: it does not vary with the size of the problem solved on a given day. "The main difference between labor workers and creative workers is owning a business," because business is about solving creative problems and developing a valuable product that can sell at any time.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md (2023-11-26)

The same video adds a warning against treating the distinction as permanent. "As soon as you stop solving creative problems around product and reach, your income stagnates and you become a slave to the work you built yourself into." Owning a business does not by itself make you a creative worker. A business owner who stops solving creative problems falls back into labor work, now inside a structure of his own making. Elsewhere in the corpus Koe calls this the "self-made 9-5" (Section 30.1 discusses the same trap as "the new 9-to-5"). The reward for staying on the creative side is stated in a sentence that has the rhythm of a slogan: "Those that monetize their mind unlock a luxury unfathomable to those who monetize their time."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md (2023-11-26)

A few minutes later in the same video, Koe qualifies his own claim from experience. When starting anything new, you cannot digest the information or workload "until your mental or work-related metabolism can catch up," or until you build "the muscle that allows you to lift that emotional weight." So he focuses on quality work first, then on outsourcing and delegating to gain time back. "You can't eradicate manual labor from your life altogether. It is a slow and painful evolution, and sometimes you regress." His own case is the evidence: having taken on a larger company (Cortex, his software startup, Chapter 37), his manual work had increased substantially, though he trusted that his prior experience would let him lower it quickly.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md (2023-11-26)

The qualification is a refinement, not a reversal. The ideal of earning with the mind describes a direction of travel, not a state that can be reached once and for all. Each new stage of a business (a new product, a team, a company) brings manual labor back, and the creative worker's task is to keep reducing it. The metaphor of a "metabolism" that has to catch up places the issue in the same frame as the gradual growth of capacity in Chapter 15.

In a May 2024 video on leverage, Koe states the principle in terms of scale, as the fifth step of a process for becoming a "$1 million creator." "Value creators are those who monetize their minds, not their time, so they can remain one person without limiting their income." He gives three reasons. First, "one person's labor can't scale, but the creative ability of their minds can." Second, "they research their obsessions and distill their learning with writing, video and other internet content." Third, "they get paid for aggregated knowledge and experience in the form of digital products," or physical products "once you have the cash flow necessary to sustain that." The first reason is the theoretical core of the section. It explains why a one-person business is possible at all: the person stays one, and the output is multiplied by the medium.

**Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md (2024-05-26)

#### Strategists, visionaries and true creatives

Koe repeatedly describes the highest earners in terms of the kind of mind they have. In October 2022, introducing the skill of creating systems, he says that "such a big skill that nobody talks about is becoming a strategist." "The highest paid individuals are the visionaries, the innovators, the strategists of the world, rather than the manual labor workers that are just acting out repetitive non-leverageable actions on a daily basis for someone else." He adds a limit to what he can teach: "I can't give you a system for creating a system." Strategic ability has to be developed by the person; it cannot be handed over as a procedure.

**Source:** How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md (2022-10-16)

In September 2023 he develops the portrait. "The highest paid individuals are the strategists, the visionaries and the true creatives of the world." They understand that they have to earn with their mind, have control over their mind and protect their mind. The people who get what they want out of life "have sculpted and crafted and built their ideal self"; they "built their **mental body** so that it can spot, perceive and actually act on opportunities," using the ideas, beliefs (including the limiting beliefs they shed), skills and everything else acquired "through learning and carefully curating the inputs of your mind." The "mental body" is the counterpart, in the mind, of the physical body that labor work spends. It is built by curating inputs (Chapter 11) and by learning (Part VI), and its function is perceptual: to notice opportunities. This connects the economic argument back to money programming. The beliefs that block perception and the mental body that enables it are two descriptions of the same faculty.

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md (2023-09-24)

In March 2024 the formula took its most compact form: "The highest paid earners are the visionaries, strategists and innovators of the world." They "are not those who work hard nor smart, but leveraged." Koe rejects both popular formulas, "work hard" and "work smart," and puts **leverage** in their place as the defining variable. To secure your future, he adds, you need "the traits that allow you to adapt to any environment."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

The word "strategist" has more than one sense in the corpus. Here it is Koe's everyday use: someone who thinks at a higher level and applies general principles. In other places it refers to the "Strategist" stage of Susanne Cook-Greuter's model of ego development (Chapter 38), a stage marked by systems thinking and comfort with paradox. The two senses are related, but the passages in this section do not refer to the developmental model.

#### Creative income

What do the highest earners actually do to earn? Koe's answer is the concept of **creative income**. In a June 2022 video he explains that creators, visionaries, strategists and leaders make the most money because they make it for themselves; they "create their own job in a sense," because they understand human nature. "All you have to do is understand how other people perceive value, educate them a bit" (ethically, he specifies), "create a solution someone wants, and they buy it. That's how you make a creative income." The formula: "you have people, you have value, you bridge the gap."

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md (2022-06-25)

The three steps (understand perception, educate, create the solution) are the same as the value-exchange account of Section 34.1, seen from the earner's side. "Bridge the gap" is the role that the four-part definition of value assigns to clarity.

In March 2024, Koe defined creative income by contrast with a job. "This isn't a job where someone hands you a paycheck for completing mechanical [tasks] that will be replaced; this is a purpose that must be shared by another in order to earn a creative income." The phrase "a purpose that must be shared by another" carries the definition. Creative income requires that someone else adopt your purpose as theirs, at least to the point of paying for it. In the same passage he describes Cortex University, his educational program, as education "for synthesizers, creators, marketers, anyone that wants to build and sell things on the internet as a meaningful way of life, not just a shallow job," using **synthesizer** and **value creator** as identities for that kind of earner (Chapters 20 and 35).

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md (2024-03-10)

The same video connects creative income to the definition of value through story. "Value is experience exchange; value is the impact of your story, because it's the same thing as a story: problem, solution, what's the path in between, the highs and lows, and how are you going to navigate that, that's called clarity; then other people resonating with that story." The four parts of value (problem, solution, clarity, people) reappear as the parts of a narrative. Clarity is the path between, with its highs and lows, and people are the audience who resonate. **Experience exchange** is Koe's name for this view. Then the job description: "To make a creative income, our job is to gain experience by solving the problems that prevent our evolution and pass down the condensed wisdom to those who are ready to receive it." Creative income, on this account, is paid for condensed experience. The earner first solves a problem in his own life (Section 28.2), then compresses the path into a form someone else can use.

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md (2024-03-10)

Where do the problems worth solving lie? In the same video Koe names the **eternal markets**: health, wealth, relationships and happiness. "Those are the eternal markets in business; if you build a creative solution for a problem within those domains, you won't have a problem making a living from your experience. This is what everyone's doing, go and look online." He places himself in one of them: "What am I selling? I'm selling in the wealth domain, I'm selling career opportunity with writing, because I've solved that problem in my life and now I'm selling the solution." The eternal markets recur throughout the corpus in slightly different lists (sometimes three domains, sometimes four); their function is always the same, to assure the creator that demand exists for anyone who has solved a real problem in one of the domains of life that nobody escapes.

**Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md (2024-03-10)

#### Leverage: the multiplier between input and output

Leverage is the concept that replaces hard work in Koe's account, and he defines it precisely in a June 2026 video: "Leverage, in this context, is the multiplier between input and output. In other words, a small action can create a disproportionately large effect, and technology continues to increase the power an individual holds." His illustration is historical. With the internet and social media, you can write an article, a post, a tweet or a reel that can reach millions, which was impossible with radio ("which you'll probably never get on") or by sending "a million handwritten letters." "The average everyday person now has the potential to reach a lot of people. Maybe not millions, but even thousands is crazy."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md (2026-06-13)

**Complementary context:** Koe's vocabulary of leverage derives largely from Naval Ravikant, who distinguishes leverage from labor, capital and "products with no marginal cost of replication" (code and media), and who argues that media and code are "permissionless" forms of leverage available to anyone. Koe acknowledges elsewhere that Naval's philosophy became his own; the definition quoted here ("the multiplier between input and output") is his own phrasing.

The definition explains the most memorable comparison Koe makes on the subject. In June 2024 he asks the viewer to "hammer this into your head": "Person A can work 12 hours a day and make $50,000 a year; person B can work 1 hour a day and make 5 million a year. The difference is skill, leverage and understanding, not how hard, long or organized you work." The three variables matter. Skill is what you can do. Leverage is the multiplier on what you do. Understanding is knowing which things to do. The three things Koe denies matter are the dimensions of effort: how hard, how long, how organized. The last is a pointed inclusion for an audience of productivity enthusiasts. Being well organized, by itself, is still a form of effort.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

In a May 2024 video Koe uses leverage to define the creative person. "A creative is someone whose goal is leveraged work, that is, identifying the work that leads to the most results with the least effort." "For most people the results that you're looking for, that this leveraged work leads to, is more money, more time and more fulfillment." Here **leveraged work** is a task of identification more than of execution. The creative's first job is to find which work has the greatest multiplier. The three results (money, time, fulfillment) recur in the "new rich" of Section 34.4.

**Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md (2024-05-26)

#### Same clock, different vehicles

Koe's most concrete illustration of leverage is the comparison between ways of selling coffee, from an August 2024 video. After building your own thing, he says, the second step is to "think bigger." "We all operate on a 24-hour clock." You can open a coffee shop, limited by its foot traffic. You can start a coffee brand, limited by the billions of people online. Or you can go further and create a productivity course, a template, a tracker or another digital product that needs no inventory or physical fulfillment, which raises profitability and extends reach beyond the people who want coffee. A digital planner or a coffee home-brewing masterclass is the highest-leverage option. This is why he recommends that everyone, even the biggest businesses, create a digital product. His own software company, Cortex, will have education products for the subset of users who want to learn writing, while the users who only want to take notes will not buy them, "and that's okay."

He then makes the contrast visual. At a local coffee shop there is a line out the door, with one order every 30 to 60 seconds; sales and the number of people served are limited by the physical location. Compare an online audience the size of a football stadium during a product launch, with a sale notification every two seconds while you sit at home. "By choosing leverage you can do the same or less work and make more, or at least have the potential to."

**Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md (2024-08-11)

The three vehicles share the same owner's day and differ only in the ceiling on reach. That is why the comparison follows the phrase "we all operate on a 24-hour clock": time is equal for everyone, so the variable is the vehicle. The qualification at the end ("or at least have the potential to") is typical of Koe's more careful statements. Leverage raises the ceiling; it does not guarantee that anyone reaches it.

In July 2025 Koe applied the same reasoning to the size of a goal. "Building a $1 million company takes almost as much effort as building a $100 million company." The reason is that a massive goal changes your actions. For $1 million you take "$1 million actions"; for $100 million you go in a pretty different direction. And since you only have about eight hours of work in you a day, the effort is roughly the same. He returns to the coffee shop: a local coffee shop or restaurant owner arguably works much harder than an online business founder and makes much less, because the goal, the opportunity and the leverage are smaller. He admits that context is missing (coffee shop chains, for example). In 2025, he adds, an online business is lower in cost and much higher in upside. And with the internet, "hard work doesn't lead to much in isolation": ten years writing a book does not mean it will make you rich, get read, or be good.

**Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md (2025-07-27)

The phrase **$1 million actions** names the actions that the size of a goal dictates. It applies to money the principle of Part IV that the goal is a lens: a bigger goal does not ask for more hours but for different actions, because it makes different options visible.

Koe adds a dimension of leverage that is not about technology but about reach in people. In a December 2022 video, repeated almost word for word in a February 2024 compilation of his business teaching, he says that people skip a fact: "the more people you help, the more leverage you have." That is why authors have far more leverage than, say, an agency making $200,000 a month: their ideas are in more people's hands, they impact more people and they have more dedicated fans. "This isn't all about money," he notes, but if those authors sold coaching they could charge outrageously high prices, or run an automated or cohort-style offer and sell far more than people doing direct outreach. The agency may earn more today; the author holds more leverage, because the number of people helped is itself a multiplier that can be converted into income later.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md (2022-12-18); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

Finally, Koe locates leverage in the device everyone already holds. In the same 2024 compilation, he distinguishes two types of internet users: those who use the internet to distract themselves, and those who use it to build leverage, make an income, spread a positive message and do what they enjoy. It takes time, but this is "the main differentiator between abundance and mediocrity," and "it's literally a flip of a switch of how you use the thing that's in your hand." He wonders aloud why modern tools do not fuel people with "the most insane energy." The "flip of a switch" is not a claim that the change is easy. Building leverage, he says in the same breath, takes time. It is a claim that the tool is the same for both groups, and that the difference between abundance and mediocrity is a difference of use, not of access.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

#### Selling time doesn't scale: Devon Eriksen on ownership

The December 2024 conversation with Devon Eriksen gives the argument against selling time a foundation in ownership. It begins with a passage that Koe reads aloud from Eriksen's novel *Theft of Fire*, in which a character recalls his father: "the reason most people spend their whole lives making someone else rich is that they sell their time, get paid for the hours they spend on the job. See, if you're running a business and you make some money, you can reinvest it, buy more equipment, hire more people, scale up. But if you're selling your time, no matter how much money you earn, you can't buy more time to sell. It doesn't scale." Eriksen confirms that this belief was part of his reason for writing the book.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The passage contains the whole asymmetry. A business can turn its income into more capacity (equipment, people). Someone who sells time cannot turn income into more time. The ceiling is set by the hours in a life, however high the hourly rate.

Eriksen then generalizes the point into a claim about civilization. "The basis of all civilization is property rights, which we should probably call investment rights, because the basic idea is that you invest in something, you own it, you have the right to control it." His explanation is a story of origins. For a Pleistocene ape (the transcript has "pl's ape"), there is nothing to own but a rock, a few sticks and some grass. Things only become worth having when you take material from the environment and invest time and effort in it, such as chipping out spear points. Nobody will do that if someone else can come along and take it, because the effort is wasted. Therefore "property rights are civilization; civilization is property rights," and "any political philosophy like communism that goes against that is anti-civilization."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The renaming from "property rights" to **investment rights** carries the argument. What property protects is the investment of time and effort, and investment is what turns raw material into something valuable. The political conclusion is Eriksen's, stated in strong terms; Koe does not elaborate on it. Its relevance to this chapter is the link it draws between ownership and the motive to invest. Whoever owns nothing has no reason to invest beyond the hours he is paid for.

Eriksen next explains why so many people sell their time anyway. "We've gotten into a rut right now where a lot of people sell their time because they are low agency." He reconstructs their inner logic: "I am afraid of starting a business on my own; I am afraid of putting my effort into something that I am going to own... I want a guaranteed paycheck, so I am going to go to someone who buys my time directly, and then how to monetize that is their problem... and I can feel safe." The consequences: "that makes you dependent, and it prevents you from getting ahead, because you only have so much time, and no matter how much money you make, you can't buy more time to sell." And the safety is eroding: "usually it's not too safe anymore; gone are the days where people worked for the same company for 50 years." Selling time also rules out the dynamic of "oh, I own something, I'm going to double down on it"; "you can't get that exponential growth."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

Here the argument connects to the next chapter. Eriksen explains the choice to sell time by **low agency**, the trait Chapter 35 develops as the central bottleneck of the individual. The employee does not lack intelligence; he lacks the willingness to own the risk. And what he buys with his time is a feeling of safety that the labor market no longer reliably provides. The phrase "how to monetize that is their problem" is exact. Whoever sells his time hands the problem of value creation to someone else, and with it the reward for solving it.

The contrast is completed by an example of what ownership allows. "When I write a book, I can sell many, many copies of that book; I can sell as many copies of that book as people are willing to buy, and I can sell all sorts of stuff associated with it; I can leverage that attention." When you build an audience, you discover that some people love your work so much they want to pay you in other ways. His example is an offer he received: "here's $10,000, put my company in your next book." "You can never get that until you take that risk and start making something that can scale." The conversation applies the same point to Koe's own videos on the philosophy of business, which "people can watch over and over and over again; you produce it once with a limited amount of time and it can make revenue for you forever."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The book and the video are the purest cases of the asymmetry Koe describes throughout this section. Production is limited in time, while distribution and revenue are unlimited, and the attention they attract can be converted into further income. They are also owned. The sequence of Eriksen's argument (selling time doesn't scale, property is the basis of investment, low agency explains the choice of safety, owned work compounds) gives the principle "monetize your mind, not your time" its economic and political grounding.

### 34.4 Getting Rich

The first three sections described what money is, what blocks it and what multiplies it. This section collects Koe's practical advice about wealth: what to do with the first money, when (if ever) to invest, how to think about paying for knowledge, what being "rich" means in his sense, and the three "decisions" he says made him rich. The advice comes from several periods and does not form a single system. Where it changes over time (on investing especially), the section shows the change.

#### Bet on yourself before investing

Koe's advice on investing begins with a family story. In a June 2024 video, he recalls that his mother always told him to invest even a dollar, implying that it would become a fortune in the future. He calls this the **dominant cultural paradigm** into which children are indoctrinated: they spend their last pennies to make a few million when they are old. His alternative is to start a business, learn skills, create value, live a meaningful life, get paid more than a million a year doing so, and invest money later, living a good life along the way "rather than wasting 40 years of your life just to then live." He promises a future video on the subject.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

The promised video came a week later. He returns to his mother's insistence on investing early for retirement, a few dollars here and there, and explains why he saw it as a form of scarcity: investing the few dollars he had would have meant "I didn't bet on myself" to make more sooner. Why invest the last of your hard-earned money to have a few million when your life is nearly over, when you can start a business for close to nothing thanks to the internet, make a few million in profit as one person in two to four years, and then, with more than enough money, turn that into ten million in the stock market or crypto? For absolute beginners the conclusion is direct: you don't have money for real estate, and you "probably shouldn't be investing in the stock market anyways"; "you should be investing in your skill acquisition and education that allows you to make millions," and then invest those millions in real estate and stocks. He frames the video as an explanation of "why investing is a stupid decision for most people."

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md (2024-06-09)

**Bet on yourself** is the name of the principle. It is a comparison of returns. A few dollars in an index fund compound slowly over decades. The same dollars, and above all the same years, spent on skills and a business can compound faster, because they raise the earning capacity that produces all future dollars. The figures Koe gives (a few million in two to four years) should be read as his estimate of what is possible, not as a forecast. Section 30.6 discusses his numbers and timelines and the qualifications he attaches to them.

In a November 2024 video, the argument becomes a step in a process for "earning your control and freedom." Assuming you start from scratch (a little money, a few skills, ambition, no idea where to start): "we're not in the Industrial Age anymore, we're in the information age; getting a job and investing your savings isn't the only option." People are losing trust in the promises of "the conventional path" to secure their future. Then the strong claim: "in fact... it's one of the worst options, because your time, energy and emotional well-being are at the control of a boss," and "your beliefs shape your reality and the opportunities available to you." His evidence is anecdotal: teenagers building "million-dollar apps, products, services" through TikTok and social media, "because they took the time to study and they practiced and they iterated."

**Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md (2024-11-24)

The objection to the job-plus-savings path is not financial but a question of control. The job puts "time, energy and emotional well-being" under someone else's command, and saving and investing does nothing to recover them until much later. Koe's position on jobs varies across the corpus, from calling them modern slavery to calling them a worthy stepping stone (Chapter 2, Section 2.3, presents that oscillation in full). This passage belongs to the harder end of the range.

The same June 2024 video that introduced the mother's advice contains the most practical statement of the order of operations. Beginners ask how to make money, get the answer (which is almost the only way) and don't do it, chasing instead methods reserved for people who already have money: crypto, investing, real estate, where you need money to make substantial money. The answer is to start a business and distribute a product with a price tag. Thinking you don't have to build a product is foolish. The only way to make money outside the "money to make money" methods is to build a product and get it in front of people. Only once you have at least $20,000 to $50,000 a month in revenue should you even think about investing it as capital. (The transcript says "$220,000 to $50,000"; $20,000 to $50,000 is the likely meaning.) Even then, you would make more by reinvesting in the business. Focus on **cash flow**: "making more money will solve 99% of your problems relating to money." Freelancing, an agency, information products: do whatever you choose under the umbrella of generalized principles.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

"Making more money will solve 99% of your problems relating to money" is a deliberately blunt heuristic. It moves the beginner's attention from the management of money (saving, allocating, investing) to its generation, which is where Koe believes the beginner's leverage lies. Note also that "cash flow" is used here in a specific sense: the regular income of a business, which comes before any investment.

#### Build the digital, invest in the physical

The June 2024 video ends with a formula. Koe's parents always told him to get into real estate "because that's what all the rich people do," and viral short videos in which people driving a Mercedes are asked "how did you get rich" all seem to answer "real estate." He does not deny that real estate is a great option for people with money to invest, but most people don't have it and can't buy a rental property. (He admits he does not know that space well; maybe fixer-uppers are an exception.) What he does know is that physical space is becoming more of a premium than digital space, because digital space is affordable and easily created, while physical space is very scarce. Hence his philosophy: "I have the philosophy of building the digital, invest in the physical."

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md (2024-06-09)

**Build the digital, invest in the physical** assigns each kind of space a role according to its scarcity. Digital assets (audiences, content, products) cost little to create, so they are where a person with little capital builds wealth. Physical assets are scarce, so they are where wealth, once built, is stored.

Koe's position on investing did not stay fixed, and the formula sits in the middle of a longer evolution.

- **2023.** In the conversation with Dickie Bush (below), the "sovereign lens" makes "the idea of investing in the market go completely away," and Koe says that "the only recession or inflation proof investments are in your skills."
- **2024.** Investing early is scarcity for beginners; investment makes sense only after $20,000–$50,000 a month in revenue; build the digital, invest in the physical. In 2025 he adds that investing in the stock market for 40 years can produce some wealth, but that someone who wants to get rich can achieve it 30 years earlier.
- **2026.** In a July 2026 video about a possible collapse of wages caused by AI, Koe warns that physical assets are the first things governments will find, tax and confiscate, and that an email list is harder to confiscate.

The movement goes from rejecting traditional investment, to an order of priorities (cash flow first, physical assets later), to a warning that sits uneasily with "invest in the physical." Koe does not reconcile the 2026 warning with the 2024 formula, and the corpus does not say whether he would still recommend physical assets as the store of wealth under the scenario he describes. The reader should treat "build the digital, invest in the physical" as his 2024 position, and the 2026 warning as an unresolved complication of it.

#### The ignorance tax, the ideal scene and the entrepreneurial barbell

If the best early investment is in yourself, how should that investment be made? The most developed answer comes from Dickie Bush, in the March 2023 conversation. Three of his ideas form a sequence.

The first is the **ignorance tax**, a term Bush attributes to Alex Hormozi and calls his favorite. The ignorance tax is what it costs you not to know how to do something well. Every time Bush has invested money and time in a course, coaching or education to pay it down, he has seen "10 to 20 to 50 to a thousand times ROI": "every single 10x in income I've had, I can point to some kind of paying down that ignorance tax." He reports that Hormozi applies the idea at a higher level, saying that the fact he is not a billionaire is costing him a billion dollars a year (or that not making $100 million a year is costing him that much). The idea makes Bush uncomfortable, because it implies that "at any moment there is some piece of information out there that I could tap into to 10x my income... The path to making your first dollar is there... if you haven't done it, you just haven't given it the right amount of attention." His advice: "figure out where your ignorance tax is right now," knowing that it changes over time.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The ignorance tax reframes the cost of education. A course is usually judged by its price. Bush judges it against the cost of continuing not to know, which is invisible but often larger. The return figures are his own reports of his experience, not measured data, and the selection is his.

The second idea is the **ideal scene**, from Bush's quarterly reflection (which he takes off-grid "to break my frame"). "This picture of the ideal scene is very related to the ignorance tax: you don't know what it looks like to do this thing well, and that's costing you a ton of time and... money." His practice: "anytime I'm starting something new now, I go: who knows this and knows what doing this well looks like? How can I pay them to win back my future time?... otherwise I'm just wasting time, because someone else has figured this out." Teaching ranges from the most direct form (one-on-one tutoring) to the most indirect (a book). Ray Dalio's book costs about $10; his one-on-one time costs millions, "because of how much value you're going to get in that unique experience." Two years earlier, $5,000 for coaching seemed absurd to Bush; now "I have to be doing that." He applies the idea to his own new chapter, managing a larger team. He feels busier, which he reads as a skill deficiency (in prioritization, planning, delegation and execution), and he is considering a productivity and focus coach to find out "where am I just completely off base." "The second you see a blueprint to do something correctly, you can instantly apply it," whereas he otherwise goes for long periods doing things wrong without knowing it.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The ideal scene turns the ignorance tax into a decision procedure. Before starting something, find someone who has done it well, and pay them in proportion to the time they will save you. The Dalio comparison shows how price rises with directness. The same knowledge costs $10 as a book and millions as a conversation, because the conversation adapts to your situation.

The third idea is the **entrepreneurial barbell**, which arises from a question Koe asks. He doubts the logic of retirement accounts: why set money aside for decades "when I could just... use this money to make 10x that amount in a year... the returns on that money have never been as easy to multiply as they are now," since new skills can be learned faster than ever? Bush explains why the conventional advice made sense: investing in the market was rational when you had a fixed job and salary, where "your inputs are not tied to your financial outcome." "The second you have that sovereign lens of 'I can earn more the second I have a better skill set,' the idea of investing in the market goes completely away." Instead, he puts "every single dollar into either something fully safe that gives me the peace of mind to go take bigger risks, or in aggressively paying down that ignorance tax... that's the entrepreneurial barbell of investing your money." He admits that he is "constantly untraining" himself from the old advice to "let it compound 3–5%... put it away for retirement." Koe adds that, aside from rare cases of hype (Bitcoin), "the only other way... to get those types of returns is investing in yourself," which multiplies not only money and knowledge but quality of life, and that "the only recession or inflation proof investments are in your skills."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

**Complementary context:** A "barbell strategy" in finance, popularized by Nassim Nicholas Taleb, puts most resources in extremely safe assets and a small part in highly speculative ones, avoiding the middle. Bush's version keeps the structure (safety at one end, high-upside bets at the other) but replaces the speculative assets with education that raises one's own earning capacity.

The **sovereign lens** is the key term. Investment in the market is rational for someone whose income does not respond to their skill. For someone whose income does respond (the creative worker of Section 34.3), the best-returning asset is the skill itself. The barbell then has only two weights: safety, which buys the peace of mind to take risks, and education, which raises income.

Bush closes the exchange with a distinction between two attitudes toward money. "Financial security is very scarcity: it's 'once I have this amount of money, then... interest... takes care of me forever.'" He never felt comfortable with "get to this point and then live off the interest," and he never sees himself not working. "**Financial confidence** is you have the skills necessary to generate an income in any circumstance." His ongoing questions are how he is developing financial confidence, which skills he is missing, and which beliefs about money keep him from progressing. Koe agrees: "even if times change, we've developed the personality that we could adapt."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The distinction moves the source of safety from a stock of money to a capacity. **Financial security**, in Bush's sense, is a number reached and then defended; financial confidence is a skill that can regenerate income after any loss. It connects to the theme of self-reliance in Chapter 35 and to Koe's frequent claim that the only safety in a changing economy is adaptability.

#### The new rich

In a February 2023 video, Koe defines what he calls **the new rich**: "The new rich is about leveraging technology for fulfilling work, open schedules and individual choice," things he says are heavily lacking today. He breaks the definition into four aspects (technology, fulfilling work, open schedules and individual choice) and develops the last three in detail. (The corpus also uses "the new rich" in a second, related sense, for people who synthesize ideas and use technology, because "the internet has democratized wealth generation for the self-educated.")

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md (2023-02-19)

**Complementary context:** The phrase "the New Rich" was popularized by Tim Ferriss in *The 4-Hour Workweek* (2007) for people who abandon the "deferred-life plan" and use time and mobility as currencies. Koe does not cite Ferriss in this passage. His definition is close in spirit, especially in its emphasis on open schedules and individual choice, but it adds fulfilling work and purpose.

On **fulfilling work**, Koe distinguishes happiness from fulfillment: "Anyone can be happy, even the sheep, but fulfillment... is a different story. You need a purpose or else you will be assigned one." "The difference between a sheep and a human being is that one is assigned a purpose and the other creates their purpose." Life is not kind to those who do not know what they want. Knowing will not come in an instant, he says; it has to be at the top of your mind "almost every second of every day," so that you have something to work toward: who you want to become, where to live, what to look like, which skills to have, what income, how much free time, which activities fill your day. The sheep–human contrast is the economic version of the theme of Part I (assigned goals versus chosen goals) and Part IV (vision).

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md (2023-02-19)

On **open schedules**, he gives a timeframe. With one to two years of fulfilling, focused effort leveraging the internet, you should have much more, if not complete, control over your time, income, location, lifestyle and day-to-day life, because you will have refined your daily process and workflow to know what you want and don't want in your day. You can change your products or services to reflect that, outsource, delegate or eliminate tasks altogether. But there is a counterpart: there is no boss maintaining order in your schedule, so "you have to do that yourself." The freedom of an open schedule comes with the responsibility of self-government (Part V).

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md (2023-02-19)

The "one to two years" is one of many timeframes in the corpus for personal and financial change. They range from "years of reconditioning" in 2022 to "six months is all you need" in 2025, and the figures depend on what is being changed: testing an interest, learning a skill, building a business, reinventing oneself. This one concerns control over one's schedule after focused effort on a business. It should be read as Koe's estimate for that specific goal.

On **individual choice**, Koe is at his most severe. Without autonomy and a path of your own, "your choices are automatic for the sake of survival; you are literally a robot," a condition that stems from following the secure path laid out for you. You live in constant survival, with attention narrowed on stressful tasks and on useless things made to seem important. You are not using the default mode network (the brain's mode of undirected thought, which Koe associates with creativity; Chapter 6), and you feel (and may genuinely lack) the time for self-development, spirituality, creativity or fulfilling work. "You just can't make those things happen if your choices are chained to a paycheck." The change is not instant, "as all good things aren't," but "creating an independent income source is necessary for those that want full control over their life."

**Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md (2023-02-19)

The new rich is thus defined not by an amount of money but by what money is used to secure: work that is fulfilling, a schedule that is one's own, and choices that are not dictated by survival. The **independent income** is the means. Without it, the other three remain dependent on an employer's decisions. This definition anticipates the "enough life" of Section 34.5, which also measures wealth by lifestyle rather than by net worth, though the 2025 video discussed next pulls in a different direction.

#### "Unapologetically rich": the three conditions

In August 2025, Koe published a video with a sharper thesis than anything he had said about money before: "What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s." It opens with a dismissal of conventional wisdom. He has no patience for people "regurgitating some shallow advice... about how money doesn't buy happiness that they got from someone who doesn't have money nor happiness." The video is "for those who want to get **unapologetically rich**, not because you're a terrible or an evil person, but because the only other option in your head is to be valued at what the market thinks you're worth, and that's terrifying."

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

The motive he gives is not greed but a fear of dependence: being priced by the market as an employee, at a value set by others. The same fear runs through Section 34.3: the labor worker's pay is fixed by the structure he works in, and the "unapologetically rich" person refuses that fixing.

The thesis follows: "the only way to get rich is to (1) value your time at a delusional dollar amount, (2) be obsessed with getting rich, and (3) give yourself no other option but to get rich." In the body of the video the three conditions take technical names:

| Condition | Name in the video | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Value your time at a delusional dollar amount | The aspirational hourly rate | Decide what your time is worth before the market pays it, and act accordingly |
| 2. Be obsessed with getting rich | Concentration of force | Make getting rich your one priority and direct all resources to it |
| 3. Give yourself no other option | No other option / money as a forcing function | Commit money you cannot yet afford so that you must rise to earn it back |

At the end of the video, Koe says these "three decisions... created the perfect conditions to make more money than my younger self thought was possible." The second condition, **concentration of force**, is developed in Chapter 25 (Section 25.6), where it is presented as the general principle of focusing on one platform and one goal. This section covers the first and third.

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

Before giving the conditions, Koe makes a disclaimer that sets the status of everything that follows. "I can't tell you exactly what to do. I can tell you what I did; that's what anyone can do. Nobody can tell you what to do; they can only tell you what they did." Instead he offers "three ways to rewire how your mind thinks about money, because that's what you need to make the right decisions." "I don't want to give you the way; I want to give you the tools that will help you create your own way so that it's sustainable. In other words, I want to install the beliefs in your mind that allow you to test the inputs that are much more likely to spit wealth out of reality as the output."

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

The disclaimer frames the three conditions as beliefs, not instructions. The language of inputs and outputs comes from the cybernetic model of Chapter 6: you cannot control the output (wealth) directly, but you can change the beliefs that select which inputs you try. This also places the conditions in continuity with money programming (Section 34.2). They are a deliberate reprogramming, an attempt to install better beliefs in place of inherited ones.

#### The aspirational hourly rate

The first condition is the most practical, and Koe borrows it explicitly. "The first and most practical way to prime your mind to get rich is to adopt an aspirational hourly rate," "a concept that I got from **Naval Ravikant**." He quotes: "Set and enforce an aspirational hourly rate. If fixing a problem will save less than your rate, ignore it. If outsourcing a task will cost less than your hourly rate, outsource it." Then: "My aspirational rate was $5,000 an hour." It is unclear in the transcript whether that last sentence is part of the Naval quotation or Koe's own statement. The next passage, in which Koe describes declining $5,000 for an hour, suggests that he at least considers himself to be at that level.

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

**Complementary context:** The quotation comes from Naval Ravikant's widely circulated material on "how to get rich (without getting lucky)," where he recommends fixing an aspirational hourly rate well above one's current earnings and using it to decide what to ignore and what to delegate. Naval's own early aspirational rate is usually reported as $5,000 an hour, which supports reading the sentence as part of his quotation. In Koe's own retelling, the sentence is not clearly marked.

The rule is a decision procedure in two parts. Problems whose solution saves less than your rate are ignored; tasks that cost less than your rate to outsource are outsourced. Its function is to stop someone from spending their attention below its intended value.

Koe then describes his own situation. "I've reached a point where someone could offer me $5,000 for an hour of my time and I wouldn't do it. Not because I couldn't use the money or that I'm better than the money, but because there is time, focus and an obligation to deliver $5,000 worth of value in an hour on the line, and that's a big responsibility that pulls my time, focus, energy away from things that could potentially make me more than $5,000 an hour." "Since I have the knowledge to do so, it would be stupid of me to do that instead of the thing that would not only have more impact on more people rather than just one person." He admits it is hard to say this "without sounding arrogant."

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

The refusal has two reasons. The obvious one is opportunity cost: the hour could earn more elsewhere. The less obvious one is the obligation. Accepting $5,000 means having to deliver $5,000 of value in that hour, and the responsibility consumes focus beyond the hour itself. The second reason connects to the principle of leverage. Work that reaches many people ("more impact on more people rather than just one person") is preferred over work that serves one, even at a high price.

Koe insists that the rate is not a form of magical thinking. "It's not so you just magically make your aspirational hourly rate. This isn't about manifesting. This is about framing your mind to make the decisions that would lead to you acquiring the knowledge and taking the actions that would lead to you making that much an hour. It helps your mind notice and choose the opportunities that put you in a place of leverage." The rate works as a lens (Chapter 6). It changes which options appear worth taking, and over time the choices it favors build the knowledge that justifies it.

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

His example is ordinary on purpose. "You purchase an item, let's say off of Amazon, for 50 bucks, and it's defective or it's broken. Are you going to take the 30 to 60 minutes to return it? If so, you value your time at about $50 or less an hour," because if you had the knowledge to make more in that hour, and losing $50 was not a problem, you would do that instead. "People with a poor relationship with money just go insane" at this suggestion: "you're just going to waste $50? Shouldn't you be mad at the seller?" Koe's answer is that the "mental energy, time, focus, attention" are "so much more valuable than a measly $50"; "just reorder the item and make more money than that." He clarifies that returning an item is not wrong in itself. (The transcript has "it's not a bad thing to not return," which in context is ambiguous; the sense seems to be that the act itself is not the problem.) The point is that the situation "shines a light on a more important fact: that you aren't conscious of a problem that you can solve that would make you $50 to $500 to $1,000 an hour, because if you were, it wouldn't make sense to return that item."

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

The example works as a diagnostic, not as a rule about returns. Whether you return the item reveals the rate at which you implicitly value your time. If returning it is rational for you, the reason is that you have not yet found a problem worth more than $50 an hour, and finding one is the real task.

The aspirational rate must be high enough to be uncomfortable. "If your aspirational hourly rate doesn't seem impossibly high or delusionally high, you're doing it wrong. Why? Because **delusional goals** rewire your brain." Koe supports the claim with statements about the brain: "Your brain is literally rewiring itself based on what you're thinking about and learning about right now, this video included. Every single thing that you allow into your mind shapes who you are and what you do." "When you imagine a better future, it activates the same brain networks as if you were experiencing that future." "Every single moment of focused attention is a chance to rewire your brain in alignment with the person you want to become." Hence his verdict on "doomscrollers and people who don't actually pay attention to what they allow into their environment, both physical and digital": they are "digging their own grave one moment at a time."

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

Koe gives no source for these neuroscientific claims, and they should be read as his interpretation, not as established findings that the video demonstrates. The general idea that attention and repeated thought shape the brain draws on the concept of neuroplasticity, which Koe uses throughout the corpus (Chapter 4). The specific claim that imagining a future "activates the same brain networks" as experiencing it is stated without qualification or reference. What the passage does show is the logic of the "delusional" rate within his system. A goal sets the lens through which attention selects what to see (Part III), so a modest goal selects modest options. This is the same reasoning as the "big irrational goals" of Section 7.5 and the "$1 million actions" of Section 34.3.

The section on the rate ends with the practical consequence. "The entire point is that you can't just think about your aspirational hourly rate and expect to make more money. You need to make difficult decisions with your rate in mind. So the best way to start is just by not returning the $50 item. You'll be fine." The first decision is small and slightly uncomfortable, and that is its value. Like Dickie Bush's $20 tip (Section 34.2), it is an action that contradicts the old belief at a cost low enough to bear.

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

#### "Things I don't do" and "things I will do"

The second condition (obsession, or concentration of force) receives a qualification in the same video that prevents it from being read as monomania. "Making getting rich your one true priority doesn't mean that you just stop doing other things in life. There's obviously things that you're going to have to do aside from trying to get rich, but that pain of not wanting to do those things is a way to orient your decision-making, because the more you tolerate something in your life, the more okay you are with not changing it." The pain of unwanted obligations becomes information: it shows where change is needed. Tolerating it numbs the signal.

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

Koe turns the aspirational rate into two lists. The first: "Create a list of things that you just don't do. These are things that are literally just not worth your time based on your aspirational hourly rate." His examples: "coffee meetings, or returning items, or scrolling because you're bored, or partying because you lack novelty and stimulation, or even things like texting everyone back on time." On the last he adds a justification: "we aren't wired to instantly respond to people, and we definitely aren't wired for people to have access to our mind at any time that they see fit; put your phone on do not disturb and check it when you want to." "When these things come up, you just have to firmly state to yourself or the person involved: I don't do that."

The second list: "After that, you need to create a list of things that you will do. These are things that merge your real hourly rate with your aspirational hourly rate." His examples: "study information that drastically increases your earning potential; self-educate; practice skills that make you self-reliant; start a business, join a startup, push for an executive role. You just learn and you build." He ends with a promise of insight: "The more you think about your actions now, the more you will realize why you're not rich, and then you can change that."

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

The two lists form a small framework:

| List | Criterion | Examples | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Things I don't do | Not worth your time at your aspirational rate | Coffee meetings, returning items, boredom scrolling, partying for stimulation, instant replies | Protect attention from low-value uses; the phrase "I don't do that" makes the refusal a matter of identity |
| Things I will do | Close the gap between your real rate and your aspirational rate | Studying high-value information, self-education, self-reliance skills, starting a business, joining a startup, pushing for an executive role | Direct attention toward the actions that raise your real rate |

The **real hourly rate** is what your time currently earns; the aspirational rate is what you intend it to earn. The second list exists to close the distance between the two. The formula "I don't do that" works on identity, like Bush's identity notes. It turns a calculation into a statement about who you are, which is easier to hold under social pressure than a cost-benefit argument.

One detail of the second list stands out against the rest of the corpus. "Join a startup" and "push for an executive role" are paths inside organizations. Elsewhere, especially in 2023 and 2024, Koe sometimes presents entrepreneurship as the only path to control over one's life and calls jobs a form of slavery. In 2025 he admits paths within organizations among the actions that raise one's rate. This fits a broader pattern documented in Chapter 2 (Section 2.3): his position on employment oscillates in tone without a clear chronological turn, but from 2025 it more often allows for routes inside companies.

#### No other option: money as a forcing function

The third condition closes the video. "The last and final point for how to get rich is to give yourself no other option but to succeed, because the best decisions I've made always involved using money as a forcing function to make more. In other words, I invested in things that I couldn't afford, and I learned to trust myself to make that money back much faster." He contrasts this with the ordinary relation to money: "Most normal people only see money as a way to survive or to buy nice things. They don't see it as a way to create pressure for you to become a better version of yourself." In the video he illustrates the point with his own purchases, including email marketing software and an expensive apartment, which he committed to before he could comfortably pay for them.

**Source:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md (2025-08-03)

A **forcing function**, in the corpus, is a commitment whose consequences oblige growth. The term was used by Dickie Bush in 2023 for projects that force one to acquire many skills, and Koe applied it to the one-person business as a whole. Here it is applied to money: a purchase that exceeds current income creates pressure to raise income. The idea is the opposite of the conventional advice to spend below one's means, and it has an obvious risk that Koe does not discuss in this passage: the pressure works only for someone who can in fact raise their income, and it can become ruinous for someone who cannot. His formulation ("I learned to trust myself to make that money back") presupposes the skills and financial confidence of the creative worker. It belongs, in his own sequence, after the investment in skills that Section 34.4 began with, not before it.

Two tensions in the corpus surround the three conditions. The first concerns diversification. In December 2021, a guest, Justin Welsh, following Daniel Vassallo's idea of small bets, rejected "going all in": "I'd rather build 10 $50,000 businesses than one $500,000 business" (Section 17.6). Koe partly agreed, and the terms of his agreement are the bridge between the two positions: "the majority of the things you do should be diversified, but then when there is that pull to say, like, go monk mode on one project or something similar, that's when you go all in" (Section 11.4). Diversification was the default; total commitment was the exception, reserved for the moment when one project pulls. From 2025 the exception becomes the rule: the August 2025 video calls concentration of force "the only way to get rich" and asks you to give yourself no other option. The line continues in 2026. In June, Koe says that the way to survive mass replacement by AI is to "build your own thing and commit to it and accept no other option" (Section 6.4). In August, speaking to young people, he pairs the commitment with a hedge: cut your standard of living to almost nothing, living in a "depression apartment," as Devon calls it, so that you can take the risk with nothing to lose (Section 17.6). The corpus does not resolve the tension explicitly. The two positions can apply to different people: an operator running several small businesses, and someone trying to get rich, or to build one thing that survives replacement. The depression apartment shows how Koe's later position absorbs part of the earlier one: concentration of effort is protected not by diversifying the bets but by lowering what a failed bet can cost. The second tension concerns ambition. The 2025 video legitimizes the obsession with getting rich, while in 2024 Koe described his ideal reader as someone who wants "more than enough," not billions. The second tension is the subject of the next section.

### 34.5 Enough

The preceding sections push in one direction: money is unignorable, its demonization is a projection, hard work does not produce it, leverage does, and getting rich is legitimate. This section gathers the passages that push back, or at least asks where the movement ends. Most of it comes from guests (Sahil Bloom and Dickie Bush), and much of it takes the form of a dialogue in which Koe raises an objection and the guest answers it. The question is the one Sahil puts to Koe directly: "what is your definition of enough?"

#### Sahil Bloom's five levels of financial wealth

In the January 2025 conversation, Sahil Bloom describes "the steps... most people would go through to get from... baseline, trying to meet your basic needs, on through the level of financial independence... true... escape velocity." The ladder starts at the baseline, where "you are just trying to meet your basic needs" (shelter, food); "if that's the case for you, you're probably not listening to this." Moving up, "what you have is an accumulation of assets, and those assets start throwing off income." The upper levels are defined by what that income covers.

| Level | Sahil's description |
|---|---|
| Baseline (levels 1–2) | Trying to meet basic needs; assets begin to accumulate as one moves up. The transcript does not define levels 1 and 2 separately |
| Level 3 | Assets produce income that covers some or all basic needs: "sort of a start [of] financial independence" |
| Level 4 | "True financial independence. You could shut everything off and you would have sufficient income, cash flows coming off your assets to cover a lifestyle that you are currently living" |
| Level 5 | "The assets are producing sufficient income to afford any level of luxuries that you want... you never have to work again... true overabundant financial wealth"; "an extreme level of wealth that very few people will ever achieve" |

Sahil also describes levels 3 and 4 more loosely, as covering "basic needs, or then like basic plus, sort of level one luxuries," "the rungs... most of us are chasing." The boundary between the two levels is described somewhat inconsistently in the transcript, and the table above follows his clearer statements. The term **escape velocity** belongs to Sahil here; Koe uses the same phrase in 2026 in a different sense.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

The framework measures wealth by what assets pay for without work, not by income. That is a different measure from the one Koe uses in most of this chapter, where wealth is the capacity to earn (Section 34.1, after Deutsch) and financial confidence matters more than a stock of assets (Section 34.4, after Bush). The two measures are not contradictory, but they answer different questions. Sahil's ladder asks how far one is from not needing to work; Koe's emphasis asks how much one can create.

#### Money as feedback, and the enough life

It is at this point that Sahil asks Koe for his definition of enough, and Koe gives the answer quoted in Section 34.1: he does not know, because money is "deeply intertwined with and a reflection of the value that I give," and he is not sure he would ever want to shut off that **feedback mechanism**, quite apart from needing to pay his team. He asks how this squares with the idea of "enough."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

Sahil answers with his own concept. "The concept of enough... is all about recognizing what is in a life. That is my enough life. It's about actually visualizing the lifestyle that you have personally, when you would feel like 'I don't need to chase more money for the sake of money... because I'm living the life that I feel like is my enough life.'" The **enough life** differs for each person. His own includes "two houses": a main home and a second one, "because I love to entertain people... bring people together... create experiences. It's not about luxury... I don't care about long driveways or gated communities." And it need not be austere: it "doesn't have to be Spartan... not... really barebones... expense minimization. No, it's about living the life that you really want."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

The concept has two features worth separating. First, enough is defined by a concrete lifestyle, visualized in detail, not by a number. This links it to Koe's insistence on vision (Chapter 7), and to the new rich of Section 34.4, which also defines wealth by what it secures. Second, enough is not frugality. Sahil's second house is not a necessity; it serves what he values (hosting, connection). An enough life can be generous as long as its elements are chosen for their meaning rather than accumulated for status.

Then Sahil resolves Koe's objection. "Beyond that, pursuing more has to be grounded in something deeper than just money." Reaching enough is "not about giving up your ambition... it's about grounding the pursuit of more in that ambition, in your purpose... You are going to make a lot of money on the back of that, but it's not going to be because you were trying to make money. It's going to be because you were trying to create value for others." He claims that "most of the richest, most successful people in the world... are not actually motivated by money," citing Elon Musk, who cares about making humanity "an interplanetary species." His rule: "There is nothing wrong with the pursuit of more if it is grounded in something more meaningful [than] money. There very much is something wrong with the pursuit of more if it is purely grounded in money, because you will run yourself off a cliff... and you will find yourself in the land of the rich yet miserable inhabitants." His image of that land: "the 50-year-old man who has $100 million, but also has three ex-wives and four kids who never talk to him. And everyone will pat him on the back and say, yeah, you won the game, but is that really a game that you care to win?"

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

The resolution reconciles Koe's two commitments. Koe wants to keep earning because money reflects the value he gives, and Sahil's rule allows exactly that. Pursuing more beyond enough is legitimate when money is a by-product of creating value, and harmful when money is the goal. The **rich yet miserable** are the cautionary figure: people who won a game they did not choose to play, the financial version of the "wrong game" theme in Chapter 39. Sahil's claim about the motives of the richest people is his own opinion, offered with one example.

Koe's response turns to the public discourse about money. "With business and with social media, you're constantly, like, dancing this line between people who are like, okay, money is evil, money is great... There's no in between, it seems." He then describes a change in his own view. Previously he "didn't know whether or not... the billionaires were evil," but "the more I see what Elon's doing, he doesn't seem like a materialistic person at all. He follows Walt Disney's quote... of making money to create... You need the resources to reach the specific level that you want to, in order to provide and even make progress in humanity."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

The phrase **making money to create** has a history in the corpus. In 2023 Koe tweeted a version of it as his own idea: first you create to make money, then you make money to create. In this 2025 conversation he attributes it to Walt Disney, while admitting that his paraphrase of the quotation is not exact. The idea completes Sahil's resolution. Beyond enough, money becomes a means to create at a scale that one's own work could not reach without it. The change of view about billionaires is a personal one, which Koe reports as such and attributes to observing one person. It is not a general argument about billionaires.

#### The target creative and the obsession with getting rich

The tension Sahil resolves in this conversation is also visible across time in Koe's own work. In May 2024, describing the viewer he wanted to reach, Koe drew the portrait of the **target creative**, "a creative at heart" with interests to monetize and skills worth more than they are paid. "You're not dumb," and you can see the opportunity on the internet because you follow creators who "decided to actually take a leap and take responsibility for their lives and pass down what they learn to do what they enjoy." But "you don't care about making billions, you care about making more than enough money to do whatever you want"; "you don't care about the hustle and grind of the typical entrepreneur, you care about rest, free time and meaningful work."

**Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md (2024-05-26)

Fifteen months later, the August 2025 video on getting rich in one's twenties (Section 34.4) addressed a different reader: someone who wants to get "unapologetically rich" and who must be "obsessed" with it. In the same period Koe said that people who only want "what they need" limit their level of challenge, and he described "concentration of force" as the only way to get rich. The same video, however, also recognizes a spectrum between being rich and being comfortable, and declares that "both goals are fine."

The change is one of emphasis, and Koe gives a reason for the later emphasis rather than for the change itself: getting rich is a way of becoming a better person, and the journey matters more than the result (Section 34.2). The two portraits can be read as addressed to different people with different goals, which he declares equally valid. What the corpus does not show is a statement in which Koe explicitly revises the 2024 portrait. The reader should hold both: the 2024 ideal of more than enough with rest and meaningful work, and the 2025 legitimation of the obsession with wealth, joined by the 2025 concession that both goals are acceptable. Sahil's rule offers a criterion that applies to both. Whatever the size of the goal, the question is whether the pursuit is grounded in purpose or in money alone.

#### Growing by financial levels: equity and dividends

Dickie Bush's contribution to the question of enough is a set of lessons about how one's relation to money changes as income grows. The first is a framework he names in the March 2023 conversation, while explaining why Koe will not run an aggressive promotion that could, by Koe's own estimate, bring in $200,000 in two days: "you would tap into equity that you've been building and extract dividends too early." Koe agrees: "that's the entire way I think about it."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The mechanism of **equity versus dividends** borrows the vocabulary of finance for the relation between a creator and an audience. "You are building goodwill, stacking equity." At first you build a little; then, because of your financial situation, you tap into it and extract dividends (you ask, you sell); you go back to building; you extract a little more. "Every time that you ask, you have to kind of reset that equity a little bit." Billion-dollar brands, Bush says, such as The Rock's tequila, "have gone a long time before tapping into that equity." "The whole game is striking that balance." If you wait too long, the situation changes; and the balance shifts at each level. At the stage of $10,000 a year, extraction looked very different from how it looks now, when "single emails can generate hundreds of thousands of dollars," and "it's way easier said than done to make that adjustment."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The framework explains a decision that might otherwise look like indifference to money. Koe's refusal of the $200,000 promotion is a judgment about the cost of extraction. Each sale draws down the audience's goodwill, and a creator at a high level, whose single emails already earn large sums, can afford to extract less often and protect the equity. Early in the path, financial need forces more frequent extraction. The balance is not a fixed rule but a ratio that each financial level resets. Section 32.5 discusses the related separation of content and promotion.

In the same exchange, Bush offers a blunter view of how money changes problems. Whenever he thinks about projects of a certain kind, the solution is "almost always just make more money right now, because then you have a completely new lens"; his example is a $200,000 problem that becomes "great, done... hire someone to do it for us." Koe accepts part of it: it sounds pompous, but it holds some truth, because things would be easier from a better financial position. Then he corrects it: "I don't think the lesson there is just make more money. I think it's that you... have the resources, you just aren't aware... that you have them... if you don't know how to just make more money, then... you can start the project, learn the skills and acquire the information necessary." In the same passage he says he could probably make $200,000 in two days with a promotion strategy (product, audience, traffic), but it would "step on some toes," and he doesn't care to do it now.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The exchange, which can be labeled with Bush's phrase "**just make more money, bro**," records a disagreement between the two speakers. Bush's view is that more money provides a new lens through which many problems disappear. Koe's view is that this presupposes the ability to make more money, which not everyone has. His alternative lesson is that people underestimate the resources they already have, and that someone without the ability to earn more can still start the project and acquire the skills along the way. The two views apply to different situations. Bush speaks from the position of someone who already knows how to earn; Koe speaks for someone who does not yet. The disagreement is not resolved in the conversation.

#### Indicators of growth and the first dollar

Bush also proposes a way of tracking progress that does not depend on a number. "Growth is having problems today that you would have begged to have had a few years ago." Your self of two years ago would look at your current biggest problem and say "dude, that's your biggest problem today?... you're living an absolute dream," and if you are progressing, that should happen every two years. He extends the idea, from a tweet: "growth is making investments today that you used to think were absurd"; "growth is saying no to opportunities that you would think were absurd to turn down two years ago" (for example, "I could help you make 10 grand tomorrow"). These **indicators of growth** must be tracked, "because otherwise you're not going to continue growing."

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The three indicators are relative to the person's own past, which makes them a measure of growth rather than of wealth. They also connect to other parts of this chapter. Investments that once seemed absurd are the paid education of the ignorance tax and the purchases used as forcing functions (Section 34.4). Opportunities that one now refuses are the things on the "I don't do that" list.

Koe gives an example of the third indicator from his own inbox, and it is not entirely flattering to him. He hates checking email and has filtered it down to a few messages, yet those messages include sponsorship offers (Grammarly, for instance) and coaching requests: "money sitting there" that he will not take, because entertaining it wastes his time. He adds that he needs more gratitude about it. The admission shows the indicator from both sides. Refusing money is a sign of growth, and it is also a privilege easy to stop noticing.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The same worry appears earlier in the conversation, in Bush's memory of his first online sale. Making the first dollar on the internet "is by far the most addicting feeling ever, and you spend the next three years... chasing that feeling of that first dollar again, and it's sad that it becomes normal." He tries to keep that perspective. (The example attached to this memory in the conversation is $500 earned from a $20 product.)

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16)

The first dollar and Koe's ignored inbox describe the same process from its two ends. At the beginning, a small sum produces an intense feeling because it proves something: that value can be exchanged without an employer. Later, much larger sums produce little, because the proof has become routine. Neither speaker draws a theory from this. Both treat it as something to watch, and both answer it with gratitude and perspective rather than with a target number.

#### Materialism and the judgment of other people's purchases

The last passage on enough concerns spending. In an October 2023 video, Koe offers a short warning and a qualification. "Just don't let materialism consume you." But "mechanics can enjoy cars because that's their entire life... I'm not going to judge a mechanic for buying the most fancy Ferrari in the world, because that's what they love, that's what they've spent so much time developing their mind and expertise around, that's what makes life meaningful to them." A few minutes earlier in the same video he states the general rule: "You need to think holistically about an individual situation they're in before you judge them on 'oh, they just bought that car because they're coping' or 'they just bought that watch because they're trying to impress people.'"

**Source:** A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md (2023-10-02)

**Materialism**, in this passage, is a word Koe uses with a specific sense. It means letting objects consume you, not owning valuable objects. The mechanic's Ferrari is not materialism, because the object embodies the expertise and meaning the mechanic has built his life around. The same purchase by someone else could be compensation or display. The difference is not visible in the object, which is why the second rule forbids judging from the purchase alone.

The two rules close the chapter's argument at the level of spending. Section 34.1 called money "spiritual energy" to be spent purposefully, "not on wasteful objects"; this passage clarifies that "purposeful" is defined by the person's own meaning, not by a general standard of frugality. Sahil's enough life makes the same point with his second house. And the instruction to think holistically before judging another person's purchase applies to spending the same criticism that Section 34.2 applied to the critics of other people's products. In both cases, someone who generalizes from his own priorities misreads the value that an exchange has for someone else.

### Exercises

1. **Trace your money programming.** Write down three beliefs about money that you held at age fifteen, and for each one identify who installed it (a parent, a religious figure, a teacher, the culture, a specific experience). Then classify each belief using Koe's list of seven limiting beliefs (Section 34.2) and Devon Eriksen's "ego-defending beliefs." Which of your beliefs protect your self-esteem at the cost of striving? Which, if any, do you think are accurate, and why?

2. **Reconstruct the chain.** Koe defines money as a unit of value, value as a measure of how much people care about what you do, and personal value as the magnitude of problems you solve, the results of your solutions and your ability to make people care. Using a person you know (or yourself), explain in your own words why their income is what it is, component by component. Then identify which component would be the cheapest to improve. Where does the chain fail to explain the case, if anywhere (for example, inherited wealth, luck, unpaid work)?

3. **Evaluate the noble broi argument.** Koe argues that if conscious people do not start businesses, unethical businesses rise unopposed, and that the person who abstains contributes to evil by working for one. Identify the premises the argument depends on (Section 34.2 names two). Construct the strongest objection you can, and then say whether Koe's 2024 comparative question ("is it more or less unethical than the person you work for?") answers it.

4. **Audit your hourly rate.** Calculate your real hourly rate as precisely as you can. Then set an aspirational rate that seems "delusionally high" by Koe's standard. Using that rate, write your own "things I don't do" list (at least five items) and "things I will do" list (at least five items). For one item on each list, describe a concrete situation in the coming month where applying it would be uncomfortable, and what you would say or do.

5. **Compare two measures of wealth.** Sahil Bloom's five levels measure wealth by what assets pay for without work; Dickie Bush's "financial confidence" measures it by the ability to generate income in any circumstance; Koe, after David Deutsch, measures it by creative capacity. Which measure best describes what you want, and why? Describe a situation in which a person rich by one measure would be poor by another.

6. **Define your enough life.** Following Sahil's method, describe in concrete detail the lifestyle at which you would stop chasing money for its own sake: where you live, what your days contain, whom you spend time with, what you own and why. Then answer Koe's objection in your own case: if money is also feedback on the value you give, would you want to stop earning at that point? What would ground any pursuit of more beyond it?

7. **Judge the forcing function.** Koe says his best decisions involved investing in things he could not afford, trusting himself to earn the money back faster. Under what conditions would this be sound advice, and under what conditions would it be harmful? Relate your answer to the order of operations Koe gives elsewhere in the chapter (cash flow before investing, skills before assets) and to Bush's distinction between financial security and financial confidence.

8. **Find the contradiction, or dissolve it.** In 2024 Koe said money is "spiritual energy" and "much more spiritual than you think"; in 2025 he said that people who "endlessly moralize or spiritualize money can't fathom a life where they aren't bound to it." Write a short argument for each of the two readings presented in Section 34.2: that the statements are compatible, and that they reflect a real change. Which reading do you find more convincing, and what evidence would settle the question?
