## Chapter 32 — Persuasion and Marketing

Chapter 31 ended with two layers of value: what a thing *is* (useful, a reversal of entropy, a cause of behavior change) and how it is *perceived*. A useful thing that nobody perceives as useful produces no exchange. This chapter is about the second layer: how Dan Koe thinks perceived value is communicated, and why he treats that communication as a skill in its own right rather than an afterthought to "doing good work."

The chapter moves from foundation to tool. Section 32.1 presents marketing as applied psychology and makes the case that persuasive communication, and beneath it human nature, is the base layer under every other marketable skill. Section 32.2 takes the moral objection head-on: Koe argues that everyone already sells, that "sales equals survival," and that persuasion is only as ethical as the person who uses it. It includes his most unusual argument, that a sales or marketing career is a way to integrate one's Shadow. Section 32.3 introduces the two diagnostic instruments he borrows from copywriting: the five levels of awareness, which tell a writer where a reader is, and market sophistication, which tells a seller what the market has already seen. Section 32.4 presents his 2026 model of human nature: three tensions (survival, identity, progress) operated by five psychological levers, with the "bottom feeders" as a counterexample. Section 32.5 turns to the most neglected part of the business in his view, promotion, and reconstructs his systems for promoting consistently, including selling through direct messages. Section 32.6 assembles the pieces of the minimal funnel: the landing page, the lead magnet and the micro product.

The chapter draws on earlier ones. From Chapter 23 it needs structure: hooks, the Ten Commandments of Engagement, PAS and "sales is storytelling." From Chapters 3 and 4 it needs identity as a network of goals that defends itself, because the identity tension of Section 32.4 is that idea applied to attention. From Chapter 4 and Chapter 38 it needs the *level of mind*, because Koe's ethics of persuasion depends on it. From Chapters 19 and 25–27 it needs the funnel of social content, newsletter and product. Chapter 33 then uses all of this to build the product itself.

### 32.1 Persuasive Communication: Marketing as Applied Psychology

#### Two kinds of skill: technical skills and evergreen skills

Koe's case for persuasion starts from a distinction he drew in August 2023, while he was building a software company and admitted he had "no idea what he's doing," or only some idea. Hiring, structuring a company and legal compliance were new to him, because they differ from running a one-person business, freelancing or self-employment. But he was learning them as he went: they "are things that you can learn on the go." Other skills could not be picked up that way. "There's actually starting and running a business, and then there's what allows you to make sales and money."

The first group he calls *technical skills*: the know-how needed to start and operate a business. The second he calls *evergreen skills*: skills that generate sales, require creativity and experience, and cannot be handed off to someone else. "Something that can't be outsourced is something that must be cultivated; those are the drivers of business success."

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

The first two evergreen skills he names are marketing and sales. They require creativity, experience and a big-picture understanding of an industry, and they are "the differentiating factor between those that make money and those that don't." His counterexample is the developer who says "I'm going to build an app and make a billion dollars." Koe calls this "a little delusional," not because the app is bad but because the developer lacks marketing, sales, experience and distribution. "They build an app, they launch it in crickets," and they cannot sustain their life's work because they never studied marketing and sales or paid attention to content. On the internet, he says, there are two raw materials, code and content, and you need both: code (or some other form of building) to make the product, content to attract the people who will want it.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

The term "evergreen skills" has several definitions across the corpus (writing, speaking, marketing and sales in some versions; message, medium and a result-oriented skill in others). The version used here is the narrowest one: the skills that make sales and cannot be outsourced. The wider skill-stack discussion belongs to Chapter 35.

#### "Learn to sell, learn to build"

Later in the same video Koe lays out a career path in which, whichever route a person takes (code or content), the second step is the same: learn marketing and sales. "Learn to sell, learn to build. If you can do both you will be unstoppable." Both paths converge on the same need, and he argues the need should be met early, because marketing and sales "frame everything else you learn."

His reasoning runs as follows. Any particular modern skill may vanish in five to ten years, given how fast technology moves. But if you learn a modern skill, such as graphic design or email marketing, through the lens of marketing and sales, your first attempts are already more valuable than those of someone who never thinks about how other people will perceive the work. The lens does not replace the skill. It directs it toward what someone will pay for. He then names the two typical failures: software companies and coders who can build but not sell, and content creators who build an audience with good writing but cannot create products people want. "Marketing and sales are your weapons on the entrepreneurial hunt."

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

The phrase "learn to sell, learn to build" is closely associated with Naval Ravikant, and in a 2024 video Koe attributes a version of it to someone whose name the transcript renders as "poppy Nal." In this 2023 passage he does not attribute it. The corpus therefore leaves the attribution ambiguous; what is clearly Koe's is the use he makes of it, as an argument for learning marketing *first* so that it shapes every later skill.

**Complementary context:** Naval Ravikant, the investor and essayist whose ideas on leverage and specific knowledge Koe adapts throughout the book (Chapters 19, 34 and 36), popularized the formulation "learn to sell, learn to build; if you can do both, you will be unstoppable" in his 2018 thread "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)."

The metaphor of the hunt is not decorative. It ties to Koe's account of modern survival (Section 32.2): we no longer hunt for food, but we hunt for the resources that let us get food and everything else. Marketing and sales are the weapons of that hunt.

#### Marketing is applied psychology

Why should marketing frame everything else? Koe's answer, from a 2022 video on making money as a creative, is that marketing is a branch of something deeper. While you build your "creative muscle," he says, you should harness it by studying *human nature* and *human understanding*: the people who will give you money. You need people who deem what you create valuable. So study psychology, spirituality, human nature, philosophy, storytelling, how we make sense of the world, "possibly consciousness": "all of the things that dictate our experience and what makes our experience better." The premise behind the list is simple. People do not pay to make their experience worse. "People want to make their experience better": to feel better, live a better life, have novel experiences, become passionate about life.

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md (2022-06-25)

The next step of the argument is the claim that these fields are one field. "By studying one of these fields you're studying all of them." Marketing "is just applied psychology or applied persuasion": a structured, creative way to market something to a human, to understand them and to tap into what makes them perceive a product as valuable. Koe uses "applied psychology" in his own sense here. He does not mean the academic discipline. He means persuasion applied to the exchange of value. Then comes the image that organizes this whole section: "Human understanding is an iceberg that everyone should master." The visible tip is the tactic or the skill; the mass below the waterline is the understanding of people that makes the tactic work.

His practical advice follows directly. If you do not know what to learn, dive into marketing, persuasion and sales, because they are "the easiest way to understand yourself and others and what they perceive as valuable," so that you can create valuable things and exchange that value for money. He summarizes the whole project of a creative income in four parts: developing yourself, understanding value perception, marketing online to attract people (traffic), and creating an offer that seems valuable. Only one of the four is "marketing" in the narrow sense; all four depend on human understanding.

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md (2022-06-25)

#### The house and its foundation

A November 2022 video adds a structural metaphor. Because business is transactional, Koe says, you have to articulate the value of yourself, your product and your content to the person receiving it; that is how a following grows. The tactics and strategies we call marketing or sales are "just a layer over human nature and psychology," and learning more of them will not change that. Templates may get results at the beginning. But "if you are not building the house of your business on a solid foundation of human understanding," the house falls.

**Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md (2022-11-13)

The metaphor gives the iceberg a direction of dependency. The tactic is not wrong; it is a floor of the house. But a floor without a foundation does not stand for long, and this is why Koe's later stance toward direct-response tactics (Section 32.3) is "principles, not tactics." It also connects with Chapter 23, where frameworks and templates are described as training wheels: useful at first, dangerous if they become the whole of what one knows.

#### The base layer: persuasive communication

In November 2023 Koe gave the foundation a name. "The greatest skill of the 21st century is not email marketing, graphic design, web design, artificial intelligence, personal branding, videography, photography, video or photo editing. It is the base layer of all of the skills I just mentioned": *persuasive communication*. Nobody tells you to learn that specific skill, he observes. They tell you to learn copywriting, marketing or sales, which are "all somewhat synonymous." What he wants is for people to understand the framework beneath those three: how they work and how they act on human psychology. The reason is the logic of exchange. We learn skills to give value and therefore to acquire value, and an exchange between two individuals means you have to understand the other individual.

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md (2023-11-05)

What does "communication" cover? In the August 2023 video Koe insists that he is not talking only about starting a social media brand; that is just a good place to practice. "What about everything else?" When he sells a course, persuasive communication structures the curriculum, so that the knowledge arrives as a sequence rather than "an unorganized mess." When he texts high-level business people to get what he wants in an exchange of value, he is using it. It governs "every single thing you do, because communication is writing or speaking." If you want money, someone else has it, and you need to persuade them to give it to you in exchange for something valuable you created, a product or a service. Even in social situations you exchange "the value you have to offer packaged up in your head, distributed via words."

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

In that passage Koe uses the word "manipulating" in a neutral sense: "You're a survival-based creature and you're manipulating your environment at any given moment," and he says he is manipulating his viewers at that very moment, "hopefully in a positive way, mutual benefit." Section 32.2 shows that by 2024 he had stopped using the word this way.

In February 2026 he gave the shortest definition of the skill: persuasion is "the ability to make people care about what you do." The definition is aimed at creatives, whose typical problem is that they put enormous effort into something nobody sees. His example is an author who works ten years on a book that nobody reads: "it's worthless." The judgment is harsh by design. It does not say the book has no intrinsic quality. It says that, as an exchange of value, a work nobody perceives produces nothing, which is exactly the second layer of value from Chapter 31. How does one learn it? By studying marketing, sales and "probably copywriting," and by building on social media: doing things that require persuasion, making mistakes, learning new techniques and trying again. "That's just how you learn."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md (2026-02-12)

#### Human nature: the meta skill

The last and deepest formulation came in July 2026. "There is one skill that will never go out of style. It is a meta skill, and if you learn it, it simply increases the power of every other skill that you learn and it allows you to learn those skills much faster. And that skill is called human nature." A *meta skill*, in Koe's vocabulary, is a skill that multiplies all the others; over the years he has given the title to code and content, to entrepreneurship and to agency, and here he gives it to human nature.

If you understand how the mind works, what makes it tick, pay attention and take action, Koe says, you can make any business successful, work your way into new opportunities, "make friends and influence people," and navigate life "with elegance and grace because you are no longer a slave to your mind but a master of it and a master of others." The claim applies whether you build your own thing or seek a dream job. Humans hold the money, the resources and the opportunities: "how else are you going to get paid if another human with money doesn't see you as valuable enough to pay?" An employee must likewise position themselves as valuable to an employer. His promise for the video is a competitive advantage over those racing to succeed "because they think AI will somehow save them from their lack of experience."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

**Complementary context:** "Make friends and influence people" echoes the title of Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People* (1936), one of the best-known books on interpersonal persuasion. Koe uses the phrase without citing it.

The phrase "a master of others" is the kind of line that triggers the ethical objection taken up in Section 32.2, and Koe addresses it in the same video. The mechanism that makes human nature a meta skill comes at the end: "The more you practice human nature, the more it impacts every other skill that you acquire. And when people go and learn the latest high-value skills, essentially, if it's a good skill, you're just learning a lower layer of human nature." The image reverses the usual hierarchy of skills. A new skill is not a new territory but a lower layer of the same iceberg. If you practice everything from that lens, you can pick up almost any high-value skill and do it better than most people, even though skills keep changing and new ones appear every year. And the reason to study humans is the same one as in 2022: "The people who have the money that you want to support your work and the opportunities and the resources are humans. So you need to study them."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

#### A shifting title: which skill is "the greatest"?

Readers who follow Koe across years will notice that "the greatest skill" changes. The record in the corpus is roughly this:

| Period | Skill declared supreme | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | Writing (also, in parallel, simplifying complex topics; "the ability to order consciousness") | Chapter 21 |
| November 2023 | Persuasive communication, "the base layer" beneath copywriting, marketing and sales | This section |
| September 2024 | Persuasion, "the greatest skill of the 21st century" | Section 32.2 |
| 2025 | Agency | Chapter 35 |
| February 2026 | Persuasion as "the ability to make people care about what you do" | This section |
| July 2026 | Human nature, the meta skill | This section |

Koe himself tells his audience to read such titles metaphorically, as a way of "getting the point" across rather than as literal rankings. Read that way, the sequence is less a series of reversals than a descent through layers of the same iceberg. Writing is the vehicle; persuasion is the function the vehicle serves; human nature is the understanding the function depends on; agency (Chapter 35) is the trait that makes a person act on any of it. The 2026 statement contains the 2022 one: "study human nature, then study whatever you want." What the reader should not do is take any single claim as the final word, since the author has not.

#### Persuasive content: capture, hold and deliver value on attention

How does persuasion show up in a single post? In an October 2023 video on building an audience from zero, Koe gives a standard he would repeat everywhere: "The key word here is persuasive: you have to capture, hold and deliver value on attention. You can't just write whatever you want and expect it to capture, hold and deliver value on attention." The phrase has three verbs, and each names a stage: capture (the reader stops), hold (the reader keeps going), deliver value (the reader leaves with something). He applies the same standard to replies and quote posts: a reply must "capture, hold and deliver value on attention to the point where they click on your profile."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md (2023-10-15)

He then lists six elements to check in every post:

| Element | What Koe asks of it |
|---|---|
| Hook | "Be mindful as to whether or not your first sentence will grab their attention." Beyond the words, "the structure and design of the post can do this, even on X." |
| Big problem | "Imply or state a problem that people can relate to; don't make it too high level." |
| Clear solution | "An actionable way to overcome the problem, preferably with a unique way of doing so from personal experience." |
| Big benefit | "Imply or state a desirable benefit that people will receive from your advice." |
| Confidence or polarization | "Stand firm in your beliefs but be willing to change them; nobody wants to follow someone who doesn't sound confident or can't choose a side... you will get hate like everyone else." |
| Novel perspective or big idea | "This is why reading or consuming content is important." Explaining an idea "that isn't normal will give your readers a dopamine hit and advance their understanding." |

The instruction for use is as important as the list. Save it, and when you write, "check the box of at least three to four of them at the start." Then develop the skill and "create your own rules of the game." The list is a beginner's scaffold, not a formula to satisfy completely in every post. The four middle elements (problem, solution, benefit, confidence) reproduce in miniature the structure the chapter returns to again and again: a problem, a path, a desirable outcome, stated by someone who believes it. The last element explains why Koe insists on reading: novelty comes from input, and a "dopamine hit" here means the small reward of seeing something familiar from a new angle (Chapter 11 discusses dopamine as the signal of something relevant to a goal; Chapter 18 develops novel perspective).

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md (2023-10-15)

This six-element checklist is a predecessor of the Ten Commandments of Engagement reconstructed in Chapter 23. Between 2022 and 2025 Koe's engagement checklists grew from three psychological hooks (problems, numbers and statistics) to six elements and then to ten commandments; "confidence or polarization" became "confidence and conviction." He gives no reason for the change, and the lists were always meant to be used selectively, never all at once.

#### Transferring your consciousness to the reader

The same video adds a rule and a method. The rule is to be persuasive everywhere: "Learn how to write persuasive content and do not not write persuasive content in every single situation you're in on social media. I don't care if you're in the DMs... writing your own post... in the replies... in a quote post... in an email... in a YouTube video... be persuasive at any given moment." The method is a mental act: "you have to transfer your consciousness to your reader and understand: are they going to read this, are they going to continue reading this, are they going to click off."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don’t Tell You).md (2023-10-15)

*Transferring your consciousness* is a coined phrase that recurs in the corpus with related meanings: adopting another person's perspective in a situation, empathizing with someone while holding both versions of the truth, and, here, imagining oneself into the reader's mind in order to predict whether they will keep reading. The persuasive version is the most operational. It turns empathy into a test applied sentence by sentence. It also explains why Koe can say that practice happens everywhere: every message is a chance to run the test and see the result.

The section's argument can now be stated as a chain. People pay only to improve their experience. To know what improves someone's experience you must understand human nature. Marketing, sales and copywriting are applied forms of that understanding, and persuasive communication is the layer they share. Every specific skill is a lower layer of the same iceberg. Therefore learn the base layer early, and practice it in every message by placing your mind in the reader's. The next section asks whether doing so is ethical.

### 32.2 Selling Is Survival: The Ethics of Persuasion

#### "If you aren't selling your product, you are selling someone else's"

Many of the people Koe writes for are drawn to creative work and repelled by selling. His response, repeated with little variation from 2023 to 2026, is that the choice they think they have does not exist. At the end of 2023 he read aloud a tweet he had written: "If you aren't selling your product you are selling someone else's." Everyone is selling something, he argued, and people see this as bad rather than as "a necessity for modern survival." The tweet continued: "Selling equals survival. Do it daily or you'll get stuck selling something you hate for someone you hate."

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md (2023-12-31)

The occasion was concrete. Chris Williamson had posted about a new productivity drink, and the comments complained that "everyone's selling a product nowadays." Koe's answer was to widen the word "product." Do not interpret "selling" literally, he says: a product is anything you communicate or exchange, and in a conversation your ideas are the product. The employee in a draining job is also selling, but selling the boss's product, without realizing it, while the boss "is competing well in this game of life." Koe frames the situation as competition, while granting that collaboration and other perspectives are also present, and the competition is for survival. "Try not selling... try not surviving, you're not going to survive." We no longer hunt for food, he adds, but we hunt for the resources that let us get food and everything else.

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md (2023-12-31)

This is the argument of *conceptual survival* from Chapter 1 applied to commerce: in modern conditions the struggle for survival has moved from the physical plane to the plane of ideas, skills and resources, and selling is its ordinary form.

#### Sales equals survival: four formulations

The principle reappears in several forms, each adding a nuance.

In March 2024, as step four of a sequence for building a creative income, Koe states it as an instruction: "Do what you want but learn to persuade so you can actually make a living out of it. Sales equals survival. If you don't sell your own product you will be forced to sell someone else's." Everyone sells "because they have to if they (1) want to survive, (2) contribute to humanity, and (3) receive money in return." The list matters: selling is not only self-preservation but also the channel through which a contribution reaches other people. He adds that "people only invest in things that they care about," which ties sales to persuasion (making people care, in the 2026 definition), and warns that if you do not learn to sell "you will be disappointed when you put your product in front of a crowd of crickets," the same image as the developer of Section 32.1.

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

In July 2024 he adds the condition that makes the principle urgent for someone who has left employment: "If you weren't selling or promoting yourself every single day you're not going to survive, because someone isn't there to give you a paycheck every week or every two weeks. You have to do it yourself, and that has to become a habit, and you need to get good at that." The employee has a sales department; the independent person does not. This is the bridge to Section 32.5, where the same point becomes "you are the marketing and sales department."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md (2024-07-28)

In October 2024 he clarifies what he is *not* saying. Selling a product is not bad: "it's the only way out of selling a product for someone else, AKA working a job." Jobs are not bad either; they are "just stepping stones" (the term he uses throughout the corpus for temporary stages that are useful only if one moves past them). Work is a necessary part of life, and money is "a tool that has allowed us to transcend our survival." His only demand: "at least try to have a shred of positive impact."

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md (2024-10-06)

In June 2026 he answers the most common objection in one line. "Oh, I don't want to sell an offer. You're already selling one. You're playing the role that your boss gave you to sell the product that they're selling. So, all you need to do is sell a product that you actually want to sell, and then create media to sell it." He admits there is a lot that goes into that: "that's why we're here."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md (2026-06-13)

Taken together, the formulations make one argument. Selling cannot be avoided; it can only be done for oneself or for someone else, consciously or unconsciously, with or without positive impact. The real choice is among those options, and the employee who refuses to sell has already made it without noticing.

#### The game of life: attention, resources, money and power

The harshest version of the argument comes from a September 2024 video. Trying to get what you want out of life is "playing the game of life," Koe says; if you were not playing it, you probably would not be alive. You have no choice: to get what you want, you need to persuade people to give you attention, resources, money and "most of all power." The only people who did well in society, then and now, had power and influence: they worked to acquire the resources to achieve their goals, become valuable and generate wealth. "There has never been a time in history when weak, average or mindless people weren't treated like cattle."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md (2024-09-08)

The sentence about cattle is a historical generalization offered as rhetoric, not a documented thesis, and it should be read as such. Its function in the argument is a warning that follows it: be careful of your mind hijacking your thoughts and making you "a slave to the ideas" of others who do not have the life you want, ideas that were programmed into your head. That warning is the Matrix of Chapter 1 in another register. The belief that selling is beneath you may itself be a borrowed belief, installed by people who never had what you want. The "game of life" here is not the life-as-a-game metaphor of Chapter 10 in its full sense, but a narrower coined phrase: the pursuit of what one wants by persuading others to provide attention, resources, money and power.

#### What sales and persuasion are

If everyone sells, what exactly is selling? Koe's March 2024 definitions are worth quoting together. "Sales is how you persuade people to see the value in what you have to offer." "Persuasion is inspiring people to make a good decision for their life." And: "The sales process is not inherently sleazy; it is guiding people through a story, because that's how humans relate to and determine value."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

Three points follow. First, sales is defined by its object: the *value* of what you offer, not the transaction. It is the perception layer of Chapter 31 in action. Second, persuasion is defined by its effect on the other person: a good decision *for their life*. A persuasion that leads someone to a bad decision for their life does not count as persuasion in this definition; Koe has another word for it (below). Third, the method is story, which links the definitions to "sales is storytelling" in Chapter 23 and to the problem–transformation–solution structure of Section 32.4.

The definition of what is being sold is wider still. As early as January 2023 Koe told his audience that they need to learn to create a compelling message that attracts people to them and to the value they offer, and to articulate the value of both. "You are selling ideas through content," and "whenever you interact with another person you are selling something: you are selling your worldview, your beliefs, whatever it may be."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md (2023-01-08)

#### Persuasion is not manipulation

The September 2024 video opens with the thesis "persuasion is the greatest skill of the 21st century," and immediately separates two words. Persuasion is not manipulation, they are "two separate words," and it is not unethical. If it were, Koe argues, you would be the most unethical person on the planet, because you persuade every day without noticing. You sell everyone on your worldview, your identity and your beliefs daily. When someone challenges those beliefs you feel threatened and have an emotional response to defend yourself; you try to convince your friends to do something with you; you present arguments for why people should work with you. So you are persuading, "but if it's unconscious to you then you're manipulating." The goal is to persuade consciously, benefiting yourself (getting what you want) and others (giving them what they want): "mutual benefit versus selfish benefit."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md (2024-09-08)

Earlier that year he had given the same idea its slogan. "If you don't like sales you may not realize that you are selling people on your beliefs, ideas and worldview every single day." The instruction: "flip the switch from mindlessly manipulating people to mindfully helping people."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

The distinction rests on two criteria, and it helps to keep them apart. One is *consciousness*: manipulation is persuasion done unconsciously, without knowing what one is doing to the other person. The other is *benefit*: persuasion seeks mutual benefit, manipulation selfish benefit. Koe's slogan joins them ("mindlessly" against "mindfully," "manipulating" against "helping"), but they can come apart. Someone can persuade consciously toward a selfish end. In his framework that would fail the benefit test even though it passes the consciousness test; the corpus does not discuss that case explicitly, and the next subsection shows that he handles it by appealing to the persuader's level of development instead.

The vocabulary itself changed. In August 2023 (Section 32.1) Koe used "manipulating" in a neutral sense, for any survival-driven influence on the environment, and said he was manipulating his viewers "hopefully in a positive way, mutual benefit." By March and September 2024 "manipulation" had become a pejorative term reserved for unconscious or selfish persuasion. He gives no reason for the change. The criterion of mutual benefit is stable across both versions; only the word moved, so the change is terminological rather than substantive.

#### A tool only as ethical as the hand that holds it

The July 2026 video on human nature, having promised to show "the anatomy of human nature and how to exploit it," returns to ethics in the middle. "Pulling psychological levers" or "exploiting human nature," Koe says, really means learning to persuade. And "persuasion is only as unethical as the person that wields the tool," like money or AI, which are neutral until they are in the hands of the stupid or the smart, the unethical or the ethical.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

He then explains where unethical use comes from, using the *level of mind* from Chapter 4. Someone at a lower level of mind, in survival mode, who can see only this month's bills, will do anything and "will inevitably use persuasion in unethical ways." Money is their sole priority, rather than meaning or truth, because they do not feel financially comfortable, and so they manipulate and scheme "until those values fundamentally shift." The remedy is therefore not to avoid persuasion but to develop yourself: pursue personal development and truth until you reach a higher level of mind. The lower level is not bad in itself, he adds; "most people just don't have control over it." Development lets you have a more positive effect on your customers or readers. And he declines to legislate: "I'm not here to tell you how to use persuasion."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

The argument has the structure of a diagnosis, not a rule. Unethical persuasion is a symptom of survival-mode values; the treatment is development, and the ethical use follows from the higher level rather than from a code of conduct. This is consistent with Koe's general preference for changing the person rather than adding constraints (Chapter 4), and it explains why a chapter on marketing keeps returning to development.

In the same video he states the positive version. "You're a value creator, you're not a manipulator, you're not exploiting human nature to be unethical and just make a bunch of money. You're exploiting it, so to say, so you can help people rise through these stages. That's the ethical and meaningful version of this." *Value creator* is one of Koe's central coined terms (Chapter 35); here its meaning is narrowed to the person who uses knowledge of human nature to help people climb the stages of development described in Section 32.4 and Chapter 38. The scare quotes he puts around "exploit" ("so to say") are doing work: the word is retained for its force, and redefined.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

A much earlier remark, from October 2022, adds the practical limit Koe places on his own responsibility. Use your own head and critical thinking to understand when it is justified to sell a product. "The people that are going to sell a product will sell a product"; that is on the individual. His content is a free video, not one-on-one advice, so "just have some common sense and be smart about what you're selling."

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md (2022-10-02)

#### Integrating the Shadow: "unethical" skills used ethically

Koe's most unusual contribution to the ethics of persuasion comes from 2023, and it uses the language of the *Shadow*. He uses the term without attributing it.

**Complementary context:** The *shadow* is a concept from Carl Jung's analytical psychology: the parts of the personality a person refuses to acknowledge in themselves, which, when repressed, tend to be projected onto others or to act unconsciously. Jung held that the shadow must be integrated into consciousness rather than denied.

In March 2023 Koe recalls a tweet of his: "the most successful people use unethical strategies and spin them in an ethical way." That, he says, "is how you start to integrate your Shadow," and it is much better than repressing your emotions and then starting to manipulate people unconsciously. The argument has four steps. Everyone has a dark side, a shadow. Thinking you are absolutely pure is "idiotic and even more egoic than you think, because you're thinking it's not egoic." You need "a modality or a vessel" through which to integrate your Shadow. And sales, marketing, persuasion and influence, practiced in business in order to sell a beneficial product, are such a vessel, "a very useful thing to do with your life." So these things are not bad; there is a better way of going about them.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12)

In November 2023 he develops the idea at length. "You can't avoid learning unethical skills. The most successful people are those who study unethical tactics and use them in an ethical way: sales, marketing, persuasion, hypnosis, influence, game." (He notes that he teaches all of these in his program Digital Economics "except for hypnosis.") "Their work, business and pursuit of lofty goals are the modalities for integrating their Shadow." These skills are "neutral until put in the hand of the good or evil," the same neutrality argument he would make about persuasion in 2026.

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

The justification is cosmological. "Everyone has a dark side, like the ugly underside of a floor rug or the bass note of a song." "The universe is a balance of creation and destruction, unity and division, birth and death": buildings are destroyed so new ones can be built, and "your past self dies so your ideal self can be birthed." Therefore "you are not pure good; it is impossible to be pure good when good is relative and would not exist without evil." And the conclusion that bears on marketing: "People that try to suppress this rather than integrate this have that dark side manifest in unconscious ways; they are often more manipulative than the people that do marketing, sales and other things consciously."

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

The polarity argument (good exists only in relation to evil) is Koe's philosophical position, developed further in Chapter 39, not an empirical finding; the claim that repressors are "often more manipulative" is an observation offered without data. What makes the argument coherent is that it lines up with the 2024 definition of manipulation: if manipulation is *unconscious* persuasion, then the person who refuses to look at their own persuasive drives is, by definition, the one most likely to manipulate.

He then gives the practical consequence. "Getting a sales job or starting a marketing business is a great way to integrate your Shadow: you are given a choice to use skills for good or evil; you get to see that outcome and how it impacts you and your customers." The value of the sales job is the feedback: the effect of one's choices becomes visible. And he extends the point to spiritual figures: "Spiritual masters are not all good; they've just learned to integrate the bad and not let their song of a life be written as a depressing one. There will be lows in your story, but when you zoom out, how will it turn out?"

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

#### From thirty thousand feet: marketing as story structure

Between those two passages Koe offers a reframing that turns the "unethical" skills into a single understanding. "These things are all the same thing when you zoom out and view them from a 30,000-foot point of view": "all of these unethical skills are just understanding human nature; they are understanding psychology and the patterns of the universe, like storytelling." He then quotes "Goethe, if I said that right": "music is liquid architecture, architecture is frozen music." His gloss: "everything has this story structure: human nature, the path in your life, the story that you're living out, the problems, the climaxes, the rising actions, the conclusions." And: "That's what marketing and sales is. You're guiding people through that journey, you're guiding people towards the transformation for a better life, and that's what makes money; that's what people want and that's what people pay for."

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

**Complementary context:** The line "architecture is frozen music" is traditionally attributed to Goethe (recorded in his conversations with Eckermann, 1829) and also to the philosopher Friedrich Schelling, who used a similar formula earlier; the companion phrase "music is liquid architecture" circulates as its inversion. Koe's own hedge ("if I said that right") reflects this uncertain wording.

Koe adapts the quote rather than interpreting it. In his use it says that one structure appears in different media: a building, a piece of music, a life and a sales page are all the same arc of problem, rising tension, climax and resolution, frozen in one and flowing in another. Marketing, then, is not a manipulation imposed on people from outside; it is guidance along a structure they already live in. This is why Section 32.4 can treat "sales in a nutshell" and "storytelling in a nutshell" as the same thing.

#### "You're doing the same thing": the anti-marketing argument as a sales page

The Shadow argument has a sharp edge, which Koe points at his critics. "When people are coming at you and saying 'oh, money is evil, you're persuading me, you're selling me, you're marketing me,' you're doing the same thing, just unconsciously and more manipulative." He offers a demonstration: "I can break down your exact argument and put it up neck and neck with a landing page or sales page that you think is scammy and unethical, and I can show you the exact parts that match up and how your message is the exact same, because it's human nature."

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12)

The claim is testable in principle. The anti-money argument names a problem (greed, manipulation), amplifies its consequences, offers a solution (refuse to sell, stay pure) and promises a better state (integrity, authenticity). That is the problem–agitation–solution structure of a sales page, which Chapter 23 reconstructs as PAS. Koe does not actually carry out the side-by-side comparison in the passage; he asserts that it can be done.

#### Sellouts and the moral high ground

Two moral objections remain, both about products in particular.

The first is the feeling of selling out. In a September 2023 video on micro education businesses Koe offers a test: "If you don't feel like a sellout by building something for yourself that's actually helping your life and you enjoy, you shouldn't feel like one if you're giving it to someone else." If you still feel that way, "that just shows that you have more inner work to do or you need to have a perspective shift around money." The heuristic relocates the problem from the act to the person: the feeling is information about one's relationship with money (Chapter 34 develops money programming) rather than about the product.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md (2023-09-17)

The second is the posture of superiority adopted by some people who work with clients. In February 2025 Koe says he "hates" people who take a "moral high ground" of the kind: "I do the hard work, I have a freelance offer, I work with clients... I'll never sell a course because that's just weird and scam and unethical." His verdict: "It's just a stupid way of thinking." His argument is structural rather than moral. If you want to avoid client work, to work on your own projects for as long as you want and to control your day without filling it with calls, then once you leave freelancing, coaching and other beginner models, only two options remain: an education product or a software product. Refusing the first, on principle, forecloses half of the exits.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

The tone of the passage is opinion, and strongly worded. It sits alongside Chapter 31's defense of education products against their objections, and Chapter 33 picks up the choice between education and software.

#### Summary of the ethical position

Koe's position on the ethics of persuasion has been stable from 2022 to 2026, even as his vocabulary changed. It can be summarized in five claims, each with its own status:

| Claim | Status in the corpus |
|---|---|
| Everyone sells, all the time; the only choices are whose product and how consciously | Principle, repeated 2023–2026 |
| Persuasion aims at a good decision for the other person's life; manipulation is unconscious or selfish persuasion | Definition (terminology settled in 2024) |
| Persuasion, like money and AI, is neutral; its ethics are those of the person using it | Principle |
| Unethical use comes from survival-mode values and is corrected by development, not avoidance | Diagnosis drawn from his model of levels of mind |
| Practicing sales consciously integrates the Shadow; repressing it produces unconscious manipulation | Philosophical interpretation, with no evidence offered beyond observation |

With the objection answered, the chapter can turn to technique: how to know where a reader is before trying to move them.

### 32.3 Levels of Awareness and Market Sophistication

#### The foundation of marketing: raising awareness of problems

In an August 2024 video Koe describes a person's worldview as having several components, of which the second is *problems*: "the conscious or unconscious problems that prevent you from reaching your goals and ideal lifestyle." Then he states what he calls the foundation of marketing: "The foundation of marketing as a whole is to raise your readers' levels of awareness surrounding their problems over time. So if you've solved your own problems and are helping your audience do the same, you are in a great position."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md (2024-08-25)

The definition rests on two ideas already in place. From Chapter 31: value is perceived only by a mind that can receive it, and a person unaware of a problem perceives its solution as noise. From Chapter 19: "solve your own problems and sell the solution," which is why having solved your own problems puts you "in a great position." The new element is the phrase "over time." Marketing, in this definition, is not a single pitch but a gradual process of raising awareness, which is why it needs a scale to measure where a person is.

#### The five levels of awareness

The scale Koe uses is the *levels of awareness*. In September 2023 he introduced it with a strong claim: "There is a concept called the levels of awareness that will change your life." He described it as a simple copywriting framework popularized by Eugene Schwartz in *Breakthrough Advertising*, and said it applies not only to marketing but "to everything."

**Complementary context:** Eugene Schwartz (1927–1995) was an American direct-response copywriter. His *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) argued that the copywriter does not create desire but channels desire that already exists, and proposed two diagnostic scales: the prospect's *state of awareness* (from completely unaware to most aware) and the market's *stage of sophistication* (how many similar claims it has already heard). Both scales appear in this section as Koe adapts them.

The five levels, in the names Koe uses consistently, are *unaware*, *problem aware*, *solution aware*, *product aware* and *most aware*. The first four are stable across his versions. Their content, gathered from his 2023 and 2024 formulations, is as follows.

At level 1, *unaware*, the person does not know they have the problem, or does not know how it hurts their quality of life. At level 2, *problem aware*, they know the problem but do not know how to solve it, or are not aware a solution exists, so they do not act. At level 3, *solution aware*, they know the problem and know there is a solution, "education or knowledge" that solves it, but not necessarily *your* solution. At level 4, *product aware*, they know your solution, and others on the market; in the 2024 wording, they know there is "a streamlined path or system to solve it."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md (2024-08-25); Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md (2023-04-09)

Level 5 is where Koe's versions diverge, and the divergence is instructive enough to lay out.

| Date | Attribution | Level 5, "most aware," as Koe defines it |
|---|---|---|
| April 2023 | None named; overlap noted with ego development and Spiral Dynamics | Aware of the problems, of the market's solutions and of yours; they are "sold" and need an incentive or promotion, the "final tip over" |
| September 2023 | Eugene Schwartz | "Aware of the vast impact that problem is having on their quality of life. This is where they're most ready to buy"; the most educated about the problems, benefits and goals related to what they want |
| August 2024 (and March, September 2024) | None named | "They are ready to change. They just need the right why that changes behavior instantly. Like when you read an idea in the book and it changes your entire outlook on life" |
| July 2026 | Eugene Schwartz | They simply have not implemented yet and need a nudge over the edge |

Koe states that his definition of "most aware" is his own interpretation. The four versions are compatible: all describe a person who no longer needs information but a final reason or push. What changes is the nature of the push: a commercial incentive (2023), an emotional realization of the problem's cost (2023), a "why" that changes behavior (2024), or a nudge into action (2026). The 2024 version is the most characteristic of Koe, because it links level 5 to behavior change, his practical test of value in Chapter 31.

The attribution also oscillates. He credits Schwartz in September 2023 and July 2026, does not name a source in April 2023 or throughout 2024, and in one February 2023 video refers to "the customer levels of awareness" and recommends researching them without naming anyone. The framework is clearly a third-party framework adapted, not Koe's invention; what is his own is the level-5 interpretation, the extension "to everything," and the mapping of levels to content formats (below).

#### Content at each level: three tweets and a product message

The April 2023 version, which Koe presents as step one of "value creation," gives a content example for each level and is the most useful for seeing the levels in practice. He notes that the levels overlap with the nine stages of ego development (as presented in an Actualized.org video) and with Spiral Dynamics, both of which Chapter 38 discusses.

At level 1 most viral content is positioned, and the task is to show how a problem impacts the reader's life. His example tweet: "You're always tired because you stuff your face with Hot Pockets, Uber Eats and foods that lead to nutrient deficiency." He adds: "a bit harsh, but that's my style." At level 2 the person knows they are tired but does not understand why or what to do, so the content gives them the solution as a list of actions: "If you're tired all the time, weigh yourself every morning, eat your body weight in protein, drink water until you pee clear, walk 10,000 steps a day, thank me later." At level 3, solution aware, you put your solution in front of them and give steps. At level 4, product aware, they know your solution and others on the market, so you present an argument for why yours is better, and "benefits-rich language is key." His example: "Why you should start intermittent fasting: morning focus is insane, you don't have to plan morning food, lunch and dinner can be massive meals..." At level 5 they are sold and need an incentive or a promotion: a limited-time sale or another scarcity tactic, "just be careful with those."

**Source:** Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md (2023-04-09)

His conclusion is a diagnostic question to ask of every asset: "Everyone is in a specific level of awareness at any time when they are interacting online." So ask which level you target in your bio, your website, your emails, your content. Are you too high-level, or too broad?

In March 2024 the levels reappear as one of the *micronutrients* of the Value Equation (Chapter 31): the small elements that shape how value is perceived. Most people, Koe says, fall between levels 1 and 4. "Your job is to speak to each level at various stages": in your bio, content, website, emails, DMs and calls, until they are aware that your product or value is the one to implement. You track where people start the buyer's journey, where they are now and where they will end. His example applies the scale to his own "4-hour workday" product. To a problem-aware person, give a solution: "If you hate working long hours, add to your skill set, finally start a business, work on it for one hour each day, fire your boss... you will never work less if you don't create your own way of doing so." In the Value Equation's vocabulary, this uses the *process* macronutrient to give a solution to someone at level 2.

**Source:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md (2024-03-17)

#### An education brand hits all five levels

What follows from the scale for a creator? In September 2023 Koe's answer is: "Your job as an education brand is to hit all of these." Make your audience aware of the problems you solved in your own life and how those problems affect theirs; give advice, lessons and tips to show there is a solution; and market a product that solves the problem faster "just by the nature of invested attention." The last phrase compresses an argument from Chapter 31: someone who has paid for a product invests attention in it, and that invested attention is part of why the product works faster than free content.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md (2023-09-17)

A February 2023 version, looser in its definitions (it lists stage two as "problem aware" and stage three as "aware of the problem," so that the two overlap), draws the strategic consequence more sharply. Your whole audience falls within the five levels, and your job is to hit all of them with content. Promotions reach only the people who are aware and ready to buy, and somewhat those who are solution aware. The other levels, where most of the audience lies, have to be educated: introduce them to problems associated with, for example, health, and slowly guide them toward how your specific niche interest, where you plan a product or service, can help. "The beauty" of this, Koe says, is that to sell biomechanical movement therapy you can start by talking about health as a whole. "You are not supposed to just attract people that only know about your interest... this is personal branding, not advertising."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md (2023-02-05)

The distinction between personal branding and advertising is the hinge of Koe's whole approach. Advertising addresses the few who are already near the purchase; a personal brand educates the many who are not, and raises them level by level. This is why the strategy of Chapters 25–27 relies on broad content rather than ads.

#### Matching formats to levels

From 2023 on, Koe maps content formats onto the scale. The mapping has three versions that differ in detail.

In April 2023: long-form content can illustrate the problem and provide a solution, walking through several levels. Short-form content (a tweet, a short, a reel, an Instagram post) usually targets levels 1–3, to generate the most attention and capture most of the audience, "because 95% of the market are beginners." Hence a rule: "With short-form content you don't want to be too high level or clever; save that for long form," where you can present an entire argument: introduce the problem, make them aware, provide a solution and encourage action.

**Source:** Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md (2023-04-09)

In June 2024: social content is broad, beginner-level and aspirational, attracting people to who you are and what you do; the newsletter is more advanced. Using "the five levels of awareness in marketing," Koe targets levels 0 through 3 on social media and "let's say 2 through 5" on the newsletter, still catering somewhat to beginners. He suggests thinking of it as a funnel from beginner to advanced, with the newsletter as the place to give deeper insights and to monetize by promoting products, because that is where you provide more value. (He numbers the levels from 0 in this passage while naming five stages, so the numbering is loose.)

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md (2024-06-09)

In August 2024: "Top-of-funnel social media content is usually in between awareness levels one and three. That's who you're writing to. It's mostly beginner level. Then a newsletter or a free download is usually a step beyond that. It's like two to four. And then during that you target the most aware, level five, to sell them on your product on your landing page." And the purpose of all of it: "When your worldview is your niche, your job is to write in a creative fashion that increases people's awareness of their problems. That way, they act on a solution toward reaching their goals."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md (2024-08-25)

The most complete version came in September 2024, where the levels serve as the "last tip" in a video about writing frameworks: *know who you're talking to*. Words persuade only if the listener perceives them as valuable, and "high-level gibberish" about a topic you know well will not matter to a beginner. The five levels, Koe says, "come before the pyramid principle" (the top-down structure of Chapter 23): before deciding how to structure a message, decide who is receiving it. He then assigns a content focus to each level.

| Level | State of the reader | Content focus (September 2024) | Typical format |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Unaware | Unaware of the problem and how it hurts their quality of life | Pain points | Social post; the opening of a newsletter |
| 2. Problem aware | Aware of the problem, does not know how to solve it | The *impact* of the pain points (amplifying) | Social post |
| 3. Solution aware | Knows there is a solution, education or knowledge | Actionable advice: the solution, the process | Social post; newsletter |
| 4. Product aware | Knows there is a streamlined path or system | Your unique way of doing things | Newsletter; free download |
| 5. Most aware | Ready to change; needs the right reason to act | Different angles: "say one thing 1,000 different ways" | Landing page; product |

Frameworks such as pain → process → offer can raise people through all the levels to the point of buying, in a landing page or a newsletter. In social posts, Koe says, you sprinkle them, focusing about 80% on levels 1–3 (pain points and solutions); newsletters, products and free downloads raise people "up the entire ladder."

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md (2024-09-08)

The 2024 version cleans up the 2023 ones. The ranges oscillate (levels 1–3 or 0–3 for social; 2–4 or 2–5 for the newsletter), but the shape is constant: short and broad formats address the lower levels, long and owned formats carry people upward, and the landing page or the product speaks to the top. The level-5 instruction, "say one thing 1,000 different ways," reappears in Section 32.5 as the principle behind testing promotional angles.

#### Persuading the non-interested: broad and desirable

A June 2025 video explains *why* social content must sit low on the scale. Most people on social media are not intentionally learning; if they were, they would search for a video, a course or an article. And you are shown to random people: you do not control who sees you, as you would with targeted Facebook ads. On social media "nobody is searching; they're discovering new things." Hence the instruction: "you need to frame your ideas as broad and desirable." His negative example: do not open a personal-branding account with instructions on how to pick topics or write a bio. That is boring and will not reach a new audience. A product, by contrast, speaks to a higher level of awareness (the buyer was aware enough of their problem to buy), so inside a product you can go straight to the details.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md (2025-06-08)

#### "95% of the market are beginners"

The format mapping relies on a heuristic Koe repeats often: "95% of the market are beginners." In a February 2024 guide he turns it into advice for starting out. You are ahead of other people in some area and can pass down very beginner-level, actionable advice: what everyone does to grow at the start. Look at the early videos of a vlogger: they did not start with "I bought a new car" vlogs; they taught and provided value at a beginner level. A health creator posted the foundations of maintaining health, building muscle and losing fat. For writing, the equivalent is how to get started, the benefits, the foundational principles, persuasive communication. "Dissect your interests and dish out actionable advice in the form of the fundamentals."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

The figure is a rule of thumb, not a measurement; in other places Koe says 90%, and in 2026 he reformulates it in developmental terms (Section 32.4). In the same 2024 guide it is balanced by a call to prioritize depth and clarity, and in 2025 he presents beginner content as surface ("vanity") that should come before depth ("therapy"). The two are reconciled by format: the basics in short form, depth in long form and in the product.

#### What learners actually need: belief, not another step-by-step

From the buyer's side of education, Koe makes two observations that qualify the scale.

The first is that what most learners lack is not information. Explaining in June 2026 why the product section of a video had not been hyper-practical, he says it was on purpose, "because I don't think that's what you need. You just need clarity that this path is viable. That's why you came to this video. It's not for the step-by-step how to do it, because you can find that anywhere. You just need the belief, and once you have that belief, you can figure out the rest because you love learning." The video was meant as "a spark to show you what's possible."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md (2026-06-13)

This is a statement about level 5 in disguise. Someone who is most aware does not need more steps; they need the "right why," and belief in the viability of a path is one form of it. It also explains why so much of Koe's content is argument rather than instruction.

The second observation is a warning to the buyer. People buy a few courses and never branch out of what those courses teach. "The courses aren't meant to be your mind," Koe says in October 2024; they are not meant to take over or become an ideology or dogma. They help you reach a stepping stone, like a job. If you become a slave to the teachings of a course or a video, "you're not out of the Matrix, you just put yourself in a new Matrix."

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md (2024-10-06)

The phrase *new Matrix* ties education back to Chapter 1: escaping one set of programmed beliefs by adopting another wholesale is not escape. For the seller, it is a reminder that the goal of raising someone's awareness is their independence, which is consistent with the ethical position of Section 32.2: helping people rise, not capturing them.

#### Market sophistication: what the market has already seen

The levels of awareness describe an individual. *Market sophistication* describes a market. Koe first used the term in March 2023, in a video that grew out of a Zoom call with Michael, a member of his Modern Mastery community, about the future of the creator economy. They noticed patterns, and the first was that "the level of market sophistication across the board is increasing." Market sophistication "in this sense is the awareness of the products and services available on the market." Koe's 2023 definition is his own, and broad: how much a market already knows about what is on offer.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12)

The market, he said, was "pretty dang flooded" with shallow, cheap advice ("how to get laid 101," "how to pick up girls," "how to start this agency business"): *quick-fix products* that neglect the big picture of business. And without a big picture, without being able to see into the future and without a sense of fulfillment or mastery behind what you do, over a long enough period you will burn out and stop enjoying your life, when enjoying life was the reason you started.

#### The internet marketing of the last decade

The same video opens with a portrait of what had flooded the market. About a decade earlier, internet marketing meant complex sales funnels, upsells with countdown timers, absurd guarantees, flexing big numbers, rented Lamborghinis and women on a yacht in a paid photo shoot. "You still see some of it." Over those ten years, many of the big names people were exposed to "seem like they're walking on eggshells and bending the truth to make an income." Koe caricatures the implied teachings: start an agency to make $100K fast by learning one skill you hate to work with people you hate; start a dropshipping store shipping useless products from China and contribute to "the cheap dopamine extravaganza"; pick a niche and never break out, never evolve, never bring your interests into your work; always squeeze the most money out of customers. Then he admits: "Nobody actually said that, but it was implied in their teachings."

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12)

He immediately qualifies the portrait. Most of the things he criticizes in his videos, he says, he does not actually believe are all bad: "I'm just using them to help frame a discussion." He does not care if you go and do agency work, dropshipping or similar models; they "can be a decent stepping stone." The critique is about what such models lack (soul, evolution, fulfillment), not a moral ban.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12)

There is a tension inside the video: later in the same recording he describes agencies and dropshipping as soulless in much harsher terms. Over the following years his position settles into a sequence rather than a verdict: by late 2024 he recommends building a skill-based offer first and pivoting to an agency or e-commerce afterward if one wants. Read together, the passages say that such models are acceptable as a stage or an expansion and inadequate as a long-term identity. The reader should also register the author's own warning about method: some of his criticisms are framing devices.

#### Loss of trust and "clarity above all"

Rising sophistication has a consequence for trust. Koe compares it to the school system: most people, especially his viewers, have lost trust in the school system, and now people are losing trust (and never had much) in the shady marketing tactics of the last decade of internet marketing. The comparison frames a demand for a different kind of creator.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12)

What kind? The third pattern Koe and Michael noticed answers this. The past decade "banked on complexity": products and services offered the same simple solutions repackaged in a new way, "because that is marketing." Any self-help book on habits says the same thing, differing only in how it is told, and Koe calls that a good thing *for books*, because books are usually more holistic, with the author's personal experience and different anecdotes. Courses and internet content, however, are "step, step, step, step, here's how you do it," with nothing unique, and the next person repackages the same steps. Everyone gets stuck in a "surface-level bubble" and never seeks the depth or the root cause. What people now want is "a clear, simple and practical solution from the lens they learn best from, or from the teacher they learn best from." Koe names the principle *clarity above all*.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md (2023-03-12)

The two halves of the principle should be kept together. Clarity means simplicity of solution, against complexity for its own sake. "The lens they learn best from" means that the same clear solution is worth more coming from a teacher whose worldview resonates, which is the "buying a worldview" argument of Chapter 31 and the basis of "you are the niche" in Chapter 19.

#### A believable goal instead of hype

In June 2024 Koe draws the practical consequence for offers. You do not need exaggerated promises and countdown timers that make you look like a scammer; market sophistication has risen. You need a *believable goal* that you help people achieve, and "believable means not a stretched goal, it means a clear goal." His example is his own monetization course. He did not promise $10K or $1M a month; he says plainly that it will not make you that much. The promise is "monetize your creative work with a digital product," because that is what he teaches.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02)

This heuristic is the endpoint of a longer movement in Koe's relation to direct-response copywriting. In 2021 he recommended *Cashvertising*, which opened "a huge rabbit hole of marketing and sales" for him. In 2022 he advised learning direct response and then moving to an authentic voice while keeping the principles, because the tactics (funnels, countdown timers) had become "wonky." In April 2023 he still allowed scarcity at level 5, with a caution. By 2024 he said such tactics make you "look like a scammer," and by 2025 his formula was to study direct response "for the principles, not tactics." The reason he gives is the one in this section: market sophistication grew, and those tactics now "scream that it is a scam." The psychology is kept; the packaging is dropped.

#### Schwartz's five stages: why info products hit the final stage

In January 2026 Koe returned to the term with a different definition, taken explicitly from Eugene Schwartz, "legendary copywriter," and used to explain why, in his view, info products are dying. Markets move through predictable stages of awareness and saturation, and Schwartz categorized five, which Koe recounts as follows:

| Stage | Market condition | What the seller must do (Koe's recounting) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nobody is in the market yet | Simply state what the product does |
| 2 | Competitors arrive | Make bigger claims |
| 3 | The market becomes skeptical | Explain your mechanism. "I will help you make money online" started to sound like a scam, so you got specific: "implement the two-hour content ecosystem" |
| 4 | Competitors copy the mechanism | (Koe does not state a separate response) |
| 5 | Everyone is exhausted by claims | Brand becomes the major differentiator; this is happening now with personal branding |

At the fifth stage, Koe argues, people crave belonging, which they get from joining a tribe with a mission. He cites his own newsletter's formula: "your mission is your niche," rather than a skill or a topic. Community and authenticity matter more than the best-sounding product. His application to his own field is stark: "we've hit the final stage of market sophistication across the board with info products." The very good ones will still do very well; the average ones, which are the majority, will do badly. And since the market is at the top of this stage, authenticity alone no longer makes a difference; people are tired of courses and coaching.

**Source:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md (2026-01-03)

Three changes of position meet in this passage, and they should be stated plainly. First, the definition of market sophistication changes from Koe's own general one (2023: awareness of the products available) to Schwartz's staged model (2026). The second refines the first rather than contradicting it: the stages describe *how* awareness of products accumulates. Second, the niche formula moves from "you are the niche" (2023–2024, Chapter 19) to "your mission is your niche"; Koe uses the new formula to explain why brand and a tribe with a mission are now the differentiator. Third, the verdict on information products changes. From 2022 to 2025 Koe called them "the greatest product one can sell"; in January 2026 he says they have reached the final stage and that static courses no longer "cut it," while in the same video also saying they are not dead and must be "transcended." Later in 2026 he says information products are abundant and will not die. The reconciling reading, which Chapter 33 develops, is that what is "dying" is the average static format; education as a function remains, and the course becomes the knowledge base for implementation tools. The 2026 passage itself contains the qualification: even at the final stage, "the very good ones will still do very well."

The two instruments of this section now fit together. The levels of awareness tell you where a person is relative to their problem; market sophistication tells you how many times the market has already heard your kind of promise. A message has to match both: speak to the reader's level, and make a claim the market has not already learned to distrust. The next section adds what Koe considers the force underneath both: the tensions in the human mind that make anyone pay attention at all.

### 32.4 The Three Tensions

#### From eight human desires to three tensions

The July 2026 video in which Koe calls human nature the meta skill (Section 32.1) promises "the anatomy of human nature and how to exploit it," with the ethics addressed later (Section 32.2). The anatomy starts from a list of *eight human desires*: survival, life enjoyment, social acceptance, sexual companionship, freedom from fear, comfort and clarity, perceived status, and safety of tribe. Koe does not name a source for the list. He then condenses the eight into "what I call the three tensions": psychological tensions which, if you hit them, get attention and position you, your work, your writing, your speaking, your posts, your landing pages or your products as valuable. Finally, he makes the tensions actionable through five "psychological levers" for speaking, writing and daily life. "If you practice those five things, you'll be unstoppable, as hyperbolic as that sounds."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

**Complementary context:** A list of eight basic human desires very close to Koe's (survival and life extension; enjoyment of food and drink; freedom from fear, pain and danger; sexual companionship; comfortable living conditions; being superior, or "keeping up with the Joneses"; care and protection of loved ones; social approval) is the "Life-Force 8" of Drew Eric Whitman's *Cashvertising* (2009), a direct-response book Koe recommended in 2021. The corpus does not connect the two lists explicitly, and Koe's version differs in several items ("comfort and clarity," "perceived status," "safety of tribe").

The model has a history. In 2023 Koe used the eight desires as angles for positioning: pick one desire and attack a problem from it (in that version the last item was "safety of community"). In 2026 the eight become three tensions operated by five levers in sequence. The change is from choosing an angle to running a procedure, and Koe's stated reason is to make the model actionable. The 2026 model contains the 2023 one: each of the eight desires falls under one of the three tensions.

The three tensions are *survival*, *identity* and *progress*. The video defines the first two at length; the third is defined mainly through the levers that pull it (painting the transformation and giving the first step, below), so its content has to be reconstructed from them: the desire to move toward a better state, which, in Koe's broader writing, also links to the "progress" stages of Spiral Dynamics. The material gathered here does not contain a standalone definition of the progress tension, and the reconstruction should be read with that caveat.

#### The mind's pressure points

Why "tensions"? Koe's image is physical. "The mind has three pressure points. And if you press any one of them, them giving you attention is almost involuntary." The claim is conditional: it holds *if* the person is prone to that specific tension. "If you press all three of them, you have a grip on someone that logic alone can't break." The practical instruction is to keep the three tensions in mind whenever you interact with someone or write anything, because "writing on social media is interacting with a bunch of people at once."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

The language of "grip" and "involuntary" attention is strong, and it is the language that Section 32.2's ethics was written to govern. As a psychological claim it is Koe's interpretation, not a cited finding. As a writing heuristic its content is clear: attention follows threat, identity and the promise of progress more reliably than it follows information.

#### Tension 1: survival

"The mind is a story engine, but it's also a set of survival strategies." We live in "a constant subconscious threat detection mode," with hunter-gatherer wiring, and we desire safety and solutions to problems. From that premise Koe derives a definition of two crafts at once: "If you can make someone aware of a problem, then provide a solution, that's sales in a nutshell and storytelling in a nutshell." His "storytelling" here has three parts: problem, transformation (the in-between), solution.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

Two qualifications widen the tension. Safety is not only a matter of life and death: a small problem can be amplified into a big one (amplification is the "A" of PAS in Chapter 23). And problems can be financial, social, or a missed opportunity, which is to say FOMO. The mind constantly looks for a better chance at survival, and today that means better skills and knowledge; staying on top of AI is his example. This is the goal-seeking, threat-detecting mind of Part III and the conceptual survival of Chapter 1, now seen from the side of the person trying to capture its attention.

#### Tension 2: identity

Identity comes second because it is deeply intertwined with survival. Animals reproduce their genetic information physically; humans do too, "but we also try to reproduce the information in our consciousness through ideology, speaking, socialization. That's all we're doing. It's just all survival." Hence the reaction to a threatened identity. Someone says "your sports team sucks," and the person who is too identified with the team nearly fights, or gets into a bar fight. The same happens with criticism of being Republican, Democrat, Christian or atheist, "any set of concepts that you hold as a part of yourself in your little mind": "you feel the response as if someone's holding a knife to your throat, even though they're not."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

Humans also desire a tribe, because a tribe helps survival, and they identify with it; they desire status and belonging. Koe's domestic example: at home, if you rebel against your parents, "they're going to kick you out, potentially," so you conform to their values, beliefs and rules.

This is the theory of identity from Chapter 3, where identity is a network of goals and beliefs that the mind defends like a body, applied to persuasion. The claim that humans "reproduce the information in their consciousness" is Koe's interpretive framework, and it is offered as explanation rather than evidence. For the persuader, its consequence is concrete: a message that touches someone's identity is processed with survival-level urgency, for better or worse.

#### Who is in which tension: 90–95% in survival and identity

The tensions also map onto the stages of development of Chapter 38, and this produces a heuristic for choosing topics. Koe sees a common problem among creators, personal brands, writers and speakers: they speak too much to the meaning-and-purpose crowd "without understanding that 90% to 95% of people are not only beginners, but in the survival and identity stages. You have to work them up the ladder."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

So if you want your content to be seen, its topics usually revolve around the survival and identity tensions. Then your products, lead magnets and the deeper parts of the funnel, if you are a value creator and not "an influencer trying to sell a weird product," help people solve their lower-level problems first. Only then can you help with what you may be most experienced in: meaning, purpose, spirituality, fulfillment, deep philosophy. Koe calls this *working them up the ladder*.

The heuristic is the "95% of the market are beginners" of Section 32.3 reformulated in developmental terms. In 2023 the 95% were beginners in a skill; in 2026 they are people at the survival and identity stages of development. The figure is again a rule of thumb (it oscillates between 90% and 95%), and the strategic conclusion is the same: the surface first, depth deeper in the funnel. It also explains why the ethical version of "exploiting" human nature (Section 32.2) is described as helping people "rise through these stages": the funnel itself is meant to be a ladder of development.

#### The five psychological levers

The tensions are what the mind responds to. The levers are what the writer does. "There are five psychological levers that pull one or more of the three tensions. So you create tension first. This is what gets people to pay attention when you write, speak, post, or sell, and then you offer a solution that preferably changes behavior in a positive way." Koe says he pulled all five in the first three minutes of the video in which he presents them.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

| Lever | Tension it pulls | Function in the sequence | Koe's example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Name the threat | Survival | Capture attention by making the problem conscious | The five levels of awareness |
| 2. Mirror the identity | Identity | Filter: the reader recognizes themselves | "You're Not Forgetful"; "If you're..." |
| 3. Exclude people | Identity, deepened | Filter: create belonging and force a side | "This isn't for people who want a dinky side hustle" |
| 4. Paint the transformation | Progress | Create desire by simulating the future | (Imagining the outcome) |
| 5. Give the first step | Progress, activated | Make action unavoidable | "Just go to bed an hour earlier" |

The sequence has a logic: attention first (lever 1), then filtering (levers 2 and 3), then desire (lever 4), then action (lever 5). Tension is created before any solution is offered, and the solution is judged by whether it changes behavior. The order mirrors the structure problem → transformation → solution of the survival tension, extended with the identity filter in the middle.

#### Lever 1: name the threat

The first lever aligns with survival. Before anyone cares about a solution, whether a video or a product, "they need to be aware of the problem." Here Koe uses the five levels of awareness, this time attributed to Eugene Schwartz, as the instrument for naming the threat at the right depth. At level 1, unaware, people do not know they have a problem: make them aware by naming it. At level 2, problem aware, they know the problem but not that a solution exists: name the solution. At level 3, solution aware, they know solutions exist and are comparing options, but do not know your unique solution. At level 4, product aware, they know your solution but need proof, objection handling and testimonials. At level 5, most aware, they simply have not implemented yet and need a nudge over the edge.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

This 2026 version adds something the earlier ones lack: at each level, what the reader needs is stated as a task for the writer (name it, name the solution, differentiate, prove, nudge). It turns the scale of Section 32.3 into a checklist for the first lever.

#### Lever 2: mirror the identity

The second lever aligns with identity. People identify with groups without realizing it: morning person, coffee drinker, Christian, atheist, Republican, Democrat, anti-AI, pro-AI. Koe's example is a YouTube video with 3.9 million views, titled "You're Not Forgetful, My System for Memorizing Everything." "It quite literally packages everything we've discussed into eight words: problem, identity, solution." "Forgetful" is the problem; "you're not" addresses (and rescues) an identity; "my system" is the solution.

His favorite way to pull the lever is to start with "if you are" or "if you're": "if you're lazy, if you're broke, if you're unhappy, if you're a writer." The formula hits the survival tension (a problem) and the identity tension in a single move, and each such opening could start a near-viral post, a talk or a YouTube title.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

The lever is a sharper version of the "group call-out" or "target call-out," Commandment 4 of the Ten Commandments of Engagement in Chapter 23.

#### Lever 3: exclude people

The third lever is "the identity tension deepened": explicitly name who the message is *not* for, "because exclusion creates that sense of belonging and it pushes people to pick a side." It works best after mirroring the identity, because it draws a line that filters out "the wrong people who weren't going to support you anyways." Koe's examples: "This isn't for people who want to try to get in shape. This is for people tired of their own excuses and ready to completely change their life." "If you're looking for a productivity hack or morning routine, you're in the wrong place." "This isn't for people who want a dinky side hustle; we're building a real business here."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

This is a change of emphasis. In 2023, discussing the group call-out, Koe said that niching down intelligently "isn't alienating anyone." In 2026 exclusion is deliberate. He gives a reason only for the new position: the excluded were not going to support you anyway. The two can be read as applying at different moments, one to attract a group, the other to deepen the identity of those who have already recognized themselves, but the corpus does not reconcile them explicitly. The lever also connects to "your mission is your niche" (Section 32.3): a tribe with a mission is defined as much by who is outside it as by who is inside.

#### Lever 4: paint the transformation

The fourth lever aligns with progress, and Koe introduces it with the purpose of the whole enterprise. "Mastering human nature is about changing people's lives for the better." That is how you build a life's work as a "massive value creator": you hone in on a purpose that revolves around solving a problem for as many people as you can, and "persuasion is simply how you get people on board with that." The sequence so far: attention captured (name the threat), audience filtered (mirror the identity, exclude), "and now we create desire by simulating the future."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

His explanation of why it works: the problem catches attention, and the desired outcome causes the reader to "actually experience the feeling of having that thing... The same neural circuitry fires when you imagine an experience as when you actually have it." Koe states this as fact without citing research. It is better read as his interpretation of a general idea in psychology, that imagining an experience recruits some of the same processes as having it, than as an established equivalence; the corpus offers no evidence for the strong version. Its role in his argument is clear either way: the reader of a transformation lives it in advance, as Chapter 23's "they will read it as if they were changing theirs" put it.

He adds a limitation. The lever is hard to practice without a *vessel*, his term for the medium through which a skill is exercised: posting, giving public talks, persuading your boss to promote you. Most jobs lack direct feedback from reality, he says, because "you aren't an entrepreneur." The point echoes the Shadow argument of Section 32.2: persuasion is learned where its consequences are visible.

#### Lever 5: give the first step

The fifth lever is "the progress tension activated, to an extent." Most people do not change, Koe says, for three reasons: they do not believe they can, they lack clarity, and "the comfort of their current life is more desirable than the discomfort of doing what it takes." The first step answers all three, by making change look possible, clear and small. In a tweet: "Don't overhaul your life overnight. Just go to bed an hour earlier." In a product: make the first step "so stupidly simple that they just can't avoid getting results. That's what makes your product valuable to them: *time to result*." The coined term means the speed with which a customer gets a result, which Koe treats as the measure of a product's value to that customer.

The step also triggers what he calls "the Zeigarnik effect": "the brain hates uncompleted tasks," so once someone starts, or even thinks about starting, they cannot help but complete it.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

**Complementary context:** The Zeigarnik effect is named after the psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik, whose 1927 experiments suggested that people remember interrupted or unfinished tasks better than completed ones. Koe extends the finding from memory to motivation ("they can't help but complete it"); that extension is his reading, and replication of the original effect has been mixed. He uses the same idea elsewhere for ending work blocks early and for starting rituals (Part V).

#### Bottom feeders: the counterexample

The three tensions and the five levers are tools for the value creator. Koe's counterexample is a type of operator he calls, in a coined and deliberately unflattering term, *bottom feeders*. In March 2023 he warned that the latest strategies, tactics or business models may not work, and if they do, "they probably won't be around in three to six months," because "you're fighting other bottom feeders that lack purpose behind their work." Bottom feeders are in a state of survival. They have not mastered themselves or done the self-improvement or mental work needed to break out of wanting the quick fix. Their mind is so narrowed on the desired end result that it is the only thing they can think of, and they cannot zoom out to see things from the perspective of a 10-, 20- or 30-year life's work. He acknowledges that people dislike the term, "but that's what they are." His examples are short-form content agencies (producing reels, TikToks and shorts for creators) and Twitter ghostwriting agencies, which are "very lucrative, don't get me wrong."

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md (2023-03-19)

He illustrates with his own inbox. He gets about a hundred DMs and emails a week from people wanting to sell him "the same Walmart-quality captions on a reel that isn't going to help grow my brand," with subject lines like "Hey, do you want more views? Do you want more subscribers? Let's blow up your account," all made in CapCut, the same as everyone else's. The lesson: "People don't care to get good. They care to get money." He calls it a brutal mistake that he made himself and that the viewer will make too, and offers the observation as "a seed of awareness" that will register later, as one goes through the mistakes.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md (2023-03-19)

The bottom feeder belongs in this section because it is the persuader operating from the survival tension *in himself*. The ethics of Section 32.2 said that survival-mode values produce unethical persuasion; the bottom feeder shows that they also produce bad persuasion. His pitch names a threat ("do you want more views?") but mirrors no identity, paints no specific transformation and offers nothing that differs from the hundred others; it is generic because its author's attention is on the money rather than on the reader. The result is what market sophistication (Section 32.3) predicts: a flooded market in which nobody believes the claim. The value creator works the same tensions from the other side, from a life's-work perspective, getting good before getting paid.

### 32.5 Promoting Yourself

#### "For the love of God, promote yourself"

After the theory of persuasion, Koe's most frequent practical diagnosis is almost embarrassingly simple. In December 2024, as step five of a guide to building a one-person business: "For the love of God, promote yourself." The only reason people do not make money, he says, is that they do not promote. They write content, wonder why they are not earning, and nowhere in their profile or their newsletters have they linked a product or service, or hit the pain points that make people want to pay. He tells his audience to do it even with zero followers or subscribers, to show that they have value, and he claims that more people follow you if you sell something. "People think selling is bad when it's the thing that builds your authority."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024-12-01)

He gives three reasons to start promoting early. The first is *incentive*: without a linked product you have no incentive to improve your writing; you will come to see it as pointless and go back to the comfortable job. His own case: he wrote his first ebook when he had about 40 followers and had made $3,000 from it by the time he reached 500, because he wanted it and drove as much traffic to it as he could. (The transcript renders the first figure as "$40"; the context indicates followers.) The second reason is that people *will not know they can pay you*: followers of three years tell him they did not know about his program 2 Hour Writer, and buyers, half the time, are not paying attention, so you have to promote consistently. The third is that otherwise *you will never get over your fear of selling*.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024-12-01)

The September 2024 version of the diagnosis is more psychological. Step four of a sequence for meaningful wealth is "to systemize the promotion and delivery of the solution to that audience." "People will ask me why they aren't making money from their creative work, and 99% of the time it's because they just don't do the things that make the money: they don't promote themselves, or they used to and they stopped; they don't have a schedule or a system; they stopped trying to get customers." They knew it subconsciously, Koe says, but needed someone to tell them, because "they secretly want it to fail so that they don't have to continue doing the hard thing." His way out: "just create a repeatable weekly process for promoting your solution or your product or service."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

The claim that some people secretly want their business to fail is an interpretation, not a measurement, and the 99% is rhetorical. But it connects promotion to the theory of identity: a person who has not yet become someone who sells will find reasons not to, and the failure of the business protects the old identity (Chapter 4). The remedy is the one Koe proposes throughout the book: replace a decision that must be made every day with a system.

In the June 2023 version of his one-person business model, marketing is the fourth pillar, which Koe calls *selling to yourself*. People ask him, stressed and overwhelmed, why they are not making money; it is because they do not self-promote. They are not giving themselves a chance, and they focus on everything that does not result in money. "You won't make a single penny if you never self-promote." Marketing exists across the whole brand, he adds, but it is most visible in four places: landing pages for products and services, emails promoting them, content promoting them, and outreach messages checking whether others are a fit. (He says the exact systems are in his programs Two Hour Writer and Digital Economics.)

**Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md (2023-06-25)

#### Followers do not know what you sell

An October 2023 video supplies the definition and the evidence. The fourth principle of that video is "promotions, which is the destroyer of brands," a phrase Koe says with an ambiguous tone (it reads as ironic, given what follows). He had talked about his book *The Art of Focus* for close to three years, the time it took to write, "and I still have people message or comment that they didn't know I was writing a book, even if they've been following me for three years." The lesson: "just because someone has followed you for a certain amount of time does not mean that they know what you sell or how they can pay you, even if they're a perfect fit. You have to promote." And the definition: "promoting means that you're telling people about your product or service and sending them to a landing page with a payment link."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md (2023-10-08)

Koe uses the common word "promotion" in this precise sense. Promotion is not content about a topic near the product, and not brand-building in general. It is the explicit act of naming what you sell and sending people to where they can pay. Much content that creators think of as promotion fails this definition.

#### The vocal 1%

What stops people, besides fear, is the visible hostility of some commenters. Koe's December 2024 answer is that they are a minority. There is a vocal 1% who despise ads and self-promotion and who, while claiming to support independent artists, would rather you begged for donations or sponsorships than pitched your own product. Ignore them. You will see them in the comments and think nobody buys, but "for every one negative comment I get, 50 to 100 people buy my product. They just don't say anything, because they aren't idiots."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024-12-01)

He adds a fairness note. Not all products are good, but you do not need to hate a product simply because it is not for you. You see value in spending $10 a day on food, but not in something else costing $10 that could benefit another area of your life. If a product is genuinely bad, "go crazy," but you are still wasting your time. The 50–100 ratio is Koe's report of his own experience, not a general statistic.

#### You are the marketing and sales department

The October 2023 video also locates the problem in the calendar. "Whenever people come to me with problems with their marketing, it's usually just a lack of promotion, and good promotion: getting people to click and then guiding them down the page." "They can't monetize consistently because they don't promote consistently. They don't have a schedule or system in place to promote every single day. They don't have that block on their calendar that says go and make some money." The reason is structural: "You don't have a marketing and sales department like the job you work at. You are the marketing and sales department."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md (2023-10-08)

This is the "sales equals survival" of Section 32.2 turned into an organizational fact. In a company, promotion is someone's job; in a one-person business it is a block of time the owner either schedules or does not.

#### The opposite trap: cashing in goodwill and short-term money games

Koe's insistence on promotion is balanced by two warnings against the opposite excess.

The first, from November 2023, is a trap he lists first among several: people focus too much on promotions, or only talk about their products. Instead, talk about your beliefs and opinions and educate people, 80% of the time, on the information necessary to become above average in your interests and skills. "Promote sparingly; it's like cashing in goodwill with your audience." The coined phrase *cashing in goodwill* treats the audience relationship as an account: education deposits goodwill, promotion withdraws it.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md (2023-11-26)

The second, from the same October 2023 video that defines promotion: "The main trap I see is people playing short-term money games, blasting their audience with promotions on a crappy product and never improving what they offer." The remedy: "evolve over time, evolve what you write about, diversify platforms, create new products." Koe refuses to give "tactics and hacks": "this is a very long-term game; this is preferably something that you're doing for life."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md (2023-10-08)

The emphasis shifts across the years, and the shift is worth seeing whole.

| Period | Koe's stance on promotion |
|---|---|
| 2022 | Rarely promotes on the timeline except at launches (though linking the newsletter under posts was already indirect promotion); promotes once a week at the end of a long newsletter |
| October 2023 | In one video: promotions are "the destroyer of brands" (ambiguous tone), blasting a crappy product is the main trap, and yet you must promote because followers do not know what you sell |
| November 2023 | Educate 80% of the time; promote sparingly ("cashing in goodwill") |
| August–September 2024 | An explicit promotion schedule with a diagnostic; "it's better to promote bad than not at all" |
| December 2024 | "For the love of God, promote yourself" |

The direction is from nearly invisible promotion to scheduled promotion. Koe's own explanation, implicit in the passages, removes the apparent contradiction: what he criticizes is promoting a bad product that never improves, or promoting without giving more value than you ask for. Consistent promotion of a product that keeps improving, inside content that educates most of the time, is what he recommends. The 2024 schedules are also addressed to beginners who already have a product and are not selling it.

#### The monetization lever: promotion built into content creation

What does systematic promotion look like? Koe's earliest account, from November 2022, calls it the *monetization lever*. You must promote consistently; one thread will not do it. You need "a systemized, consistent promotion schedule" (he made the program Two Hour Writer partly to share his). At that time he promoted his products once a week, at the end of his newsletter, which was long enough that once a week was not too much: "I'm delivering more value than I am promoting." If he did not put offers in front of people every day, week and month, he did not make money. "Systemized" means worked into content creation: if you write three tweets a day, plug your product under one of them, every day. And iterate on the product according to customer feedback.

**Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md (2022-11-06)

In May 2023 he explained where promotion works best. The newsletter or blog post is "the best place to promote your products or services... the most trustworthy and authoritative place to promote them." His counterexample: a one-liner tweet saying "go live your best life," followed by "buy my course on how to live your best life." "You're not gonna make too many sales." Instead, write a longer piece, upload it to the blog with "a good call to action to your product or service," and link the blog under related tweets every week. "That's how my revenue shot up."

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md (2023-05-05)

The reasoning ties back to Section 32.3. A one-liner sits at levels 1–3 of awareness and cannot carry a reader to a purchase. A long piece can walk the reader through several levels, and it builds the trust that makes the call to action credible. The tweet's job is to send people to the long piece, not to sell.

#### Two schedules and a diagnostic

In August 2024 Koe proposes separating the two activities. "Separate the two things, content, product." Make a *content creation schedule*, for example three social posts a day, one newsletter a week and one thread a week, "maybe maximum." Then make a *content promotion schedule*, for example promoting twice inside the newsletter and three times a week under a social post. "Create a system and then you test it and then you try it out and then you change it. You put it on your calendar and you actually do the thing."

The separation makes diagnosis possible, because each schedule answers a different question. If you are not making sales, increase promotions; if that still does not work, make the promotions better. If you are not growing on social media, change your content schedule; if that still does not work, make the content better. "It's pretty freaking simple."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md (2024-08-25)

| Symptom | First adjustment | Second adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| No sales | More promotions (promotion schedule) | Better promotions |
| No growth | Change the content schedule | Better content |

A month later Koe gave a sample weekly system: promote your product, service, lead magnet or newsletter every day under one post (in the comments or in your story); send a weekly newsletter that promotes your product or service; and actively talk to five to ten people a week in your DMs about your product. "That's just one example, one option," but it gets many eyes on the offer, gives you the confidence that "you are at least trying," and gives you "data points to improve." "If you don't even promote your product then there's no way to improve; it's better to promote bad than not at all." Delivery of a digital product, he adds, "is relatively simple": usually a course platform or a coaching service.

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md (2024-09-29)

"It's better to promote bad than not at all" is the promotional version of the build-to-learn principle of Chapter 14: feedback exists only for what is put into the world. A bad promotion produces data; no promotion produces nothing to improve.

#### Evergreen pieces and the educating landing page

The same August 2024 video explains how long-form material lightens the load of promotion. You can write one newsletter, post it as a blog article, turn it into a YouTube video, break it into content ideas, and plug those things under posts, "because then those are evergreen pieces of information." Social posts disappear, so it is wise to keep writing them to attract more people. Along the way you occasionally write about what you sell and plug a newsletter, an article, a video, a free download or the product's landing page. And the key point: "If my product landing page actually educates people to the point of trusting me and knowing that this is valuable for their life, then I don't really need to write about that thing too much, because it already lives on the internet and all I have to do is link to it."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md (2024-08-25)

*Evergreen pieces* are long-form assets that keep working after the post that links to them has disappeared. The educating landing page is the most important of them, which is why Section 32.6 treats its structure in detail.

#### Baking promotions into the system

A January 2023 video, from the period when Koe was developing the "niche of one," explains what systematic promotion buys beyond revenue: freedom of topic. "If you can create a system that bakes in promotions, then you have much more free space in your mind to talk about whatever you want." Promotion becomes scheduled and structural, so the rest of the content can follow interest. The coined phrase *bakes in promotions* names the idea.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md (2023-01-29)

His example: if you offer web design, you can talk about whatever you want (fitness, business, self-improvement, philosophy) throughout the week, and every Tuesday and Thursday write a web-design and business-specific thread with a promotion of your offer at the bottom. "That's a system that I can milk endlessly." If those threads are good, tackle a common problem and give value ("you understand persuasion"), that is how you make sales.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md (2023-01-29)

In the same passage he describes a sequence for selling a product or service while still talking about whatever he wants. The transcript numbers the steps unevenly, and the fourth is not clearly labeled, so the reconstruction below is partly interpretive.

1. Talk about whatever you want in an interesting way that leads to growth.
2. Craft a three-week strategy based on the topics and on the product or service. This builds authority. He was doing it at the time with the launch letters for Solopreneur Sprints, and asks his audience to check: "did I talk about business in the last letter, or did I talk about whatever I wanted?"
3. Start at beginner level "for the sake of customer awareness" and increase to advanced as the launch approaches.
4. (Presumably) Produce long-, medium- and short-form content "from the top down," at which point "the promotions write themselves."
5. Systemize what worked and incorporate promotions throughout the week to keep sales high, such as the twice-weekly threads on a topic related to the offer.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md (2023-01-29)

Step 3 is the levels of awareness of Section 32.3 applied to a launch calendar: the sequence walks the audience up the scale as the sale approaches. "The promotions write themselves" describes the effect: when the content has already raised awareness of the problem and the solution, the promotion only has to name the product.

#### Testing angles: one validated thing, a thousand ways

How do you make promotions better, as the diagnostic demands? Koe's method, stated in December 2022 and repeated in February 2024, is to test *angles*. Put out a promotion with a specific angle; his example, for a writing product, is "here's a skill you can learn in your free time," which he calls "a crappy angle." If it sells nothing, dig in: how many clicks did it get? How many people reached the landing page? Which tweet was it under, and what was I talking about that day? Change things day after day until you hit an angle that sells well, then make *spin-offs* of it, because "content and promotions is about saying one validated valuable thing 1,000 different ways from 1,000 different angles."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md (2022-12-04); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

The 2024 version places this inside a model of the business's moving parts. The brand changes little once it is solidified. The product gets updated a few times, few and far between. Content and promotions change the most. Content builds distribution (an audience, an email list, a community if you like); marketing distributes the product through promotions (plugs in social posts, newsletter promotions, changes of angle and message). Success requires "testing, persistence and iteration."

Both versions describe the same time scale. It is a game of time and consistency: in the first year you hit wall after wall unless you have prior experience; within one to three years, with validated ideas and angles solidified in your head, growth becomes exponential as you consistently put out top content and promotions, and you can rely on self-awareness and intuition. The 2024 version ends with encouragement. Start writing, building and promoting; if you are anxious, "the unknown becomes known with time." Koe's analogy is being thrown into the ocean without knowing how to swim: if you could still breathe and live, you would eventually learn. He admits the analogy is imperfect. Lean into it: "it might as well be now."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024-02-06)

The formula "one validated thing 1,000 ways" is the same instruction Koe gives for level 5 of awareness (Section 32.3): the most aware reader needs the right reason, and different angles are different reasons. Validation comes first (an angle that has sold), variation second.

#### Long-form and short-form promotions

In November 2023 Koe distinguishes two sizes of promotion. *Long-form promotions* are like a full sales page: you fill the page by guiding people through the structure of problem, outcome and solution; the introduction of the video he was recording was itself an example. *Short-form promotions* are like a call to action in an email. He then performed one live, for his program Digital Economics: "If you've tried the latest and greatest business models without any success and are considering accepting your fate at a day job, it's because you are falling for tactics. In Digital Economics I teach the principles of building a purposeful business (brand, content, product and promotion) so you can talk about your interests and earn a creative income." And then: "You see what I did there."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md (2023-11-26)

The example is worth dissecting with the tools of Section 32.4. "If you've tried the latest and greatest business models without any success" mirrors an identity and names a threat. "Considering accepting your fate at a day job" amplifies the threat into a negative outcome. "It's because you are falling for tactics" gives a diagnosis that is also a novel perspective. "I teach the principles..." names the solution and its mechanism. "So you can talk about your interests and earn a creative income" paints the transformation. Three sentences contain problem, cause, solution and benefit.

#### Magnetic content

In September 2024 Koe names the content that surrounds an offer once it exists. "Magnetic content is short or long posts that are written on pain points, benefits, the ideal lifestyle, quick tips, personal takes on common advice, rewrites of high-performing content from other accounts, and actionable steps to overcome pain points, all relating to your offer." Once you have an offer, he says, writing around it is relatively simple. And the posts do a second job: "the people who comment or share these posts are people expressing interest in that topic," so they are "perfect to reach out to on a warm note."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md (2024-09-15)

Magnetic content is the bridge between content and direct selling. It sits at levels 1–3 of awareness (pain points, tips, steps), it is anchored to the offer rather than to whatever the writer feels like discussing, and it generates a list of warm prospects for the DM process described below.

#### The ecosystem behind promotion

Promotion is one part of a connected system, and in July 2024 Koe described both the system and the technical skills that operate it. He separates future-proof skills from the "technical know-how" needed in "this digital Renaissance environment": "this is what's relevant right now, but it may not be relevant in the future." People worry about the technical skills, he says, "because they don't have the previous future-proof skills that allow them to adapt and learn the technical skills of the time." The technical skills are five:

| Technical skill | Koe's description |
|---|---|
| Social media | "Building a name for yourself as your storefront for the value you create, the command center for your business" |
| Content | Writing, design or video "to educate, entertain and inspire people to see your value," attracting an audience that wants to buy |
| Email marketing | Newsletters or sequences "to nurture the audience you acquire" |
| Visual design | "Illustrating the vibe of your brand to spark emotion in your viewers" |
| Funnel building | "The inner sections of landing pages, websites and how you fuel them with the audience" |

The skills matter only as an *ecosystem*: "you need to understand the ecosystem." The flow is: attract people with social media → get them onto your newsletter → send them to a landing page → they purchase the product → "then you talk to them about buying a service from you." And he expects the requirements to change: "The requirements to learn these skills are going to change as AI shakes the industry; they're going to be easier to learn because AI is going to be able to do these specialized tasks faster."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md (2024-07-28)

Note the order of the flow: product before service. In this version the service is the higher-priced step offered to people who have already bought; elsewhere (Section 32.6, Chapter 29) Koe recommends building the service first to make money faster. The two are not contradictory: one is the order of building, the other the order of a mature customer's path.

Later in the same video, as pillar 4 (marketing) of his business model, Koe lays out a concrete system. "If you don't promote yourself you're not going to make money, it's as simple as that." People who ask why they are not making money (anymore) often "stop promoting yourself and you don't realize it; you cut off a certain channel... and you didn't realize how much it was contributing to your revenue." The system:

1. Content: "promote yourself every single day under one of your posts."
2. A lead magnet or free download "based on the basics around what it is that you sell." For a web designer: how to set up WordPress or a landing page, or a checklist.
3. People who download it enter your newsletter; promote the lead magnet every day to get subscribers.
4. Write a weekly newsletter with details, insights and valuable advice on the topic.
5. Promote your product or service in the newsletter.
6. Refine over time: "if you aren't making as much money as you want, then you change something, you try a new strategy... it's just trial and error until you get there."

He closes with a remark about courses that also applies to this chapter: you do not take one course in your lifetime and get cured; "life is a course and most people aren't paying attention to it."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md (2024-07-28)

#### The qualifying questionnaire: a landing page for one-to-one offers

For a beginner selling a one-to-one service, Koe proposes in September 2024 a substitute for the landing page: the *qualifying questionnaire*, "a replacement for a landing page or a website," easier to build and doing the same job, since most clients come from content anyway. Link it in your bio and in your content, promote it under your content almost daily, and when someone fills it out, reach out, answer their questions and send the payment link. Use JotForm, Typeform or Google Forms (free and well known).

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md (2024-09-15)

The form has a title, a description and six questions.

| Element | Content | Function Koe assigns to it |
|---|---|---|
| Title | "Work with me one-on-one" | — |
| Description | "Implement [your unique process] so you can reach [desired outcome]": the offer in one or two sentences | — |
| Q1–Q3 | Name; email; social media handle | Contact |
| Q4 | "What are your biggest struggles?" (multiple choice, relevant to your topic and offer) | Makes the prospect aware of their problem |
| Q5 | "Where do you want to be 30 days from now?" (multiple choice) | Sparks the desire to change |
| Q6 | "This is not a free service. Are you serious about working together to achieve [desired outcome]?" (yes/no) | Plants the idea of payment |

"Question four makes the prospect aware of their problem, question five sparks the desire to change, question six plants the idea of payment in their head." The questionnaire is, in miniature, a persuasive sequence: it raises awareness of the problem (lever 1), paints the destination (lever 4) and qualifies by commitment (a mild form of lever 3). On YouTube, where there are no DMs, the same process runs differently: create content around pain points, pitch the offer with its benefits, put the questionnaire in the description, and email the people who fill it out.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md (2024-09-15)

#### Selling through DMs: the four-step script

The same September 2024 guide gives the script for the conversation that follows, whether the prospect came from magnetic content or from the questionnaire.

**Step 1: pick up the conversation where it left off.** If they commented on or shared a post, send them the link to the post and respond to their comment in the DMs. If they submitted the questionnaire, briefly walk them through its questions: "Hey [name], I saw you submitted the form to work with me. Can you tell me more about why you filled it out? What are you looking to get out of working with me?"

**Step 2: ask how their endeavors are going.** This lets you understand their goals and struggles, and it also makes them aware of a problem or blocker. For fitness: "How's the weight-loss journey going? Is there anything I can help with?" The question reminds them of the lifestyle they want and of their sticking points. For questionnaire replies, ask what they have already done to fix the pain points they listed.

**Step 3: give novel advice and mention your offer.** Start with free value, to prove authority, and make it non-basic: "telling them to drink water is going to make them think you aren't worth working with; prove you know your stuff." End with the offer and its price: "I actually offer a pack of four sessions to help with this. I help you implement [unique process]... The total cost is on par with a good personal trainer: $1,000 for the four sessions." For questionnaire replies, explain the next steps instead: what each session covers, scheduling, projects, and the $1,000 invoice. "Just state the price in the message."

**Step 4: handle objections and send the invoice** (through Stripe or PayPal). They pay or they don't, and you move on.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md (2024-09-15)

The script's logic is the logic of the chapter applied to a conversation. Step 1 re-establishes context. Step 2 raises awareness of the problem, and does so by asking rather than telling. Step 3 earns the right to sell by demonstrating competence (the "novel advice" is the novel perspective of Section 32.1, now as proof of authority), and anchors the price to a familiar comparison. Step 4 is deliberately short.

#### "Bad at sales, good at sales": content as the sales call

Koe's comment on step 4 reveals his whole view of selling. "I am not a sales expert. I'm actually fairly bad at sales, but I am good at sales because I'm good at content and I'm good at giving education and value to make people trust me." He does little objection handling and has not studied it. When people say "can I talk to my wife about it?" or "can I answer in seven days?" he does not push back: "yeah, no problem, and things tend to work out." A sales call can help but is not necessary, "especially if you write content: people trust you, and people have already been through four sales calls with you because they've been following you for a month, your sales calls being the content that you write."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md (2024-09-15)

The *four sales calls* image summarizes the content strategy of Part IX from the point of view of selling. Every piece of content a follower has read has already done what a salesperson does on a call: raised awareness, answered objections, demonstrated competence, built trust. By the time the DM arrives, the sale is mostly made. This is also why Koe can say he is bad at sales without contradiction: he is bad at the *call*, and good at the system that makes the call unnecessary.

#### The beginner's route: 100 DMs and the ROI

For someone without an audience, Koe's 2026 advice is blunter. "You absolutely can send 100 DMs today, ask AI how to send a good DM, and if you have a decent offer that people want, they may be willing to pay you." The argument concerns business buyers: "If you aren't a business owner you may not understand that yet, because you don't understand that businesses want to grow, and if you can help them grow or make more money, they're going to want to pay you." He offers his own behavior as evidence: "The amount of people that I have hired for 1, 5, 10, 50,000 dollars after starting a business is absurd, but if the ROI is there, the ROI is there."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md (2026-03-15)

This is outreach without warmth: no content history, no four sales calls. It works, on Koe's account, for a different reason: the buyer is a business evaluating a return on investment, not a person evaluating trust. Chapter 29 places this route among the ways to get a first client.

#### The simplest first-offer process

Finally, a January 2025 passage, given during a promotional mention of the platform Stan (which he likes because on login you see five or six options: digital product, coaching call, community), compresses the whole section into a beginner's process. Find something you want to create and name it (a coaching call, for example). "Write the copy for it, just pain point, what I offer," and "start testing the waters." "Don't overcomplicate it"; if you need help, "just Google copywriting frameworks, try it out, experiment." Then, for "the main interest that you're talking about," you need to "teach more around that, build authority around that thing, hit pain points around that thing and then pitch." Pitching is hard for many people, "but you just do it and then eventually you get used to it, you get a payment and you're like, oh crap, that worked."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28)

The four verbs at the end (teach, build authority, hit pain points, pitch) are the section in miniature. Teaching is the 80% of education; authority is what the education buys; pain points are the first lever; the pitch is the promotion that so many creators never make. The last sentence describes the fear of selling from the other side: it disappears with the first payment, which is why Koe tells people to promote even with no audience at all.

### 32.6 Landing Page and Lead Magnet: The Minimal Funnel

The promotion systems of Section 32.5 all point somewhere: a landing page with a payment link, a free download that captures an email, a small product that earns trust. This section assembles those pieces. Together with the newsletter (Chapter 24) they make up what can be called the minimal funnel: the fewest assets a one-person business needs to turn attention into customers.

#### The landing page outline

In a February 2025 guide to making a first profitable product, Koe gives the general outline of a landing page. It runs, from top to bottom: headline; subheadline; a button (possible on a custom page, though not on Stan); an illustration of the problem and its negative outcome; the desired outcome and the personal system; social proof; the offer, with features and benefits; a call to action; and an optional FAQ.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

| Part | Content | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Headline and subheadline | The most potent combination of big problem, desired outcome, personal system, time frame and possibly target persona | "A condensation of all of the value inside of your offer" |
| Button | A link to purchase, high on the page (custom pages only) | Let the already-convinced buy immediately |
| The lead | A few short sentences amplifying the problem, then a bullet list of pain points; or a personal story | Raise awareness of the problem; give belief that life can change |
| Personal system | The turning point and the system that solved the problem; why it is better than most solutions on the market; a graphic if possible | Present the mechanism |
| Social proof | Testimonials, or your own results | Make the promise believable |
| Offer introduction | Product name, each feature and how it is structured, each with a benefit | Show what is bought and why each part matters |
| Call to action | Ask them to buy; a before/after comparison | Restate the problem and the main benefits at the moment of decision |
| FAQ (optional) | Answers to common questions | Handle objections (omit on Stan) |

#### Headline and subheadline: a condensation of the offer

Koe's rule for the top of the page is that the headline and subheadline "should be a condensation of all of the value inside of your offer." They combine the most potent parts of five ingredients: the big problem, the desired outcome, the personal system, the time frame and, perhaps, the target persona. His example comes from a hypothetical study program. Headline: "Study for 12 hours a day without Adderall with a 14-day program." That combines the desired outcome and the time frame, which he judges the most potent pair. The subheadline adds what is missing: "Use the Attention Marathon system to avoid a low-paying 9-to-5 and conquer cheap dopamine." Here the personal system ("the Attention Marathon system") and the big problem appear, together with a high-performing topic ("cheap dopamine") chosen to capture attention. (He notes that templates are in his program Mental Monetization.)

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

The example also illustrates the believable goal of Section 32.3: the claim is specific (twelve hours, fourteen days) and names a mechanism, which is what Schwartz's third stage of sophistication requires. Whether "twelve hours a day" is believable is a judgment the reader can make; Koe uses it as an illustration of structure, not as a recommended promise.

#### The lead and the personal system

After the headline comes "a wall of text that's called the *lead*," where you illustrate and amplify the big problem and its negative outcome. "Lead" is a copywriting term that Koe uses in its standard sense: the opening section after the headline. His reason for starting with the problem is the levels of awareness: "When you start with the problem, you increase the reader's awareness around the problem," and you give them the belief that their life can change by using a system that helped you.

His own style, used on all his landing pages, is a few short sentences that state the problem, followed by a bullet list of pain points that stem from it. If you do not know what to write, use a personal story: where did you start, where were you before, why did you want to change, what did you try that did not work? Then transition into the personal system, continuing the story: what was the *turning point*, how did you discover and create the system, why is it better than most solutions on the market? Add a graphic to explain the system if you can.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

The lead and the system section together are a long-form promotion in the sense of Section 32.5: a guided walk through problem, outcome and solution. They also apply the first and fourth levers of Section 32.4: naming the threat (the bullet list of pain points) and painting the transformation (the story of the turning point). The personal story is the "tell people why it changed your life, because they will read it as if they were changing theirs" heuristic from Chapter 23.

#### Social proof, the offer introduction and the call to action

After the personal system come results or testimonials: images, text, your own results. If you have no social proof yet, list your own: screenshots, pictures of yourself (a six-pack if that is what you sell; you at a desk). "You need to show something to show that it's believable." Testimonials accumulate as you get customers, and you can also ask friends and your network to go through the product for free in exchange for a testimonial.

Next comes the features-and-benefits section, which Koe calls the *offer introduction*: a headline that introduces the product's name, then each feature and how it is structured, each paired with a benefit. Then the main call to action, asking the reader to buy. Koe likes a comparison of where you were before the program and where you will be after it, which brings up the problem again and reminds the reader of the main benefits at the moment of decision. Finally an optional FAQ. On Stan, he says, "just get straight to the point" and leave it out, because it increases the time it takes to reach payment.

**Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md (2025-02-10)

The advice to skip the FAQ shows the page's governing principle: every section must move the reader toward payment, and a section that lengthens the path without moving them is cut. The same principle produced the "time to result" of Section 32.4, applied here to the purchase itself.

#### Learning landing pages with AI

How does a beginner learn to write all this? In March 2026 Koe applies to landing pages the method of learning with AI that he describes for other skills (Chapter 16). "If you're going to create a product or service, then why would you just try to write the landing page yourself without learning anything first? Normally you could take a course, but most people aren't going to do that either. But now you can find a landing page from someone that you like, that you know their stuff is working well, and you can just tell the AI to teach you how it works and then help you write your own landing page like that."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md (2026-03-15)

The method is "imitate, then innovate" from Chapter 23, with the AI as tutor: a working example is deconstructed into its structure, the structure is explained, and a new page is written on the same skeleton. It presupposes the judgment to choose a page that works and is in a style one likes, which is where the human-nature foundation of Section 32.1 comes back in.

#### The lead magnet as your first niche

The second piece of the funnel is the *lead magnet*: a free product offered in exchange for an email address. Koe gives the common term a meaning of his own. In July 2023, in a video arguing that "niche down" is bad advice for smart people, he presents the lead magnet as "your first niche." He has built and tested eight to ten of them: an hour-long video on how he creates websites; the basics of marketing; the Power Planner; "Seven Days to Genius Ideas"; "The Internet Profit Starter Pack"; and "The Path to Power," an ebook on his life philosophy.

The argument is about freedom of topic. People with a niche brand "consistently have to talk about the one topic." Take video editing as an example (the same applies to health, nutrition, marketing, sales or philosophy). If you create a lead magnet on the basics of video editing, you do not always have to write content about it. You can branch into something new, sprinkle video-editing content here and there, and plug the lead magnet, and people have "everything I could ever want to say about that thing right in their hands." Along the way you build your newsletter by capturing emails.

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md (2023-07-04)

The lead magnet thus does for topic what "baked-in promotions" (Section 32.5) does for selling. One concentrated, authoritative asset holds the niche, so the timeline is free to follow the writer's interests, which is the practical mechanism behind "you are the niche" in Chapter 19.

His examples of formats, from the same video: checklists that get results (a web-design checklist); Notion templates for systems such as writing or habit formation; beginner-level ebooks covering the fundamentals, to get people up to speed with your more advanced content; video tutorials or screen recordings of the same beginner fundamentals. Create a lead magnet "to establish authority in the area that you want to monetize, or at least you think you want to monetize, because you can always create another lead magnet; it takes like a day." Over time you build "a niche audience of downloaders under your much broader audience of your brand." If you later want to branch into health or music production, you do the same thing again.

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md (2023-07-04)

*A niche audience of downloaders* is the coined name for the result: within the broad audience of a personal brand, a set of sub-audiences, each defined by the free product it downloaded and therefore by the problem it has declared. Each sub-audience is a market for a corresponding paid offer. The cost of each experiment, about a day, is what makes the strategy compatible with a multi-interest brand.

#### Authority in one place: plug it often

In June 2024 Koe restates the lead magnet's second purpose as "writing what you want." With all the authoritative information on one subject in one place, you do not need to write about it so much on the timeline. But you must plug the lead magnet often, to maintain the authority. His example: with a writing lead magnet, he can talk about meditation or the power of long walks, and then build a bridge: "If you want to use your walks as a way to generate ideas for your writing, I teach writing; download my free product."

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md (2024-06-09)

The bridge sentence is the technique. A post on any topic can end with a link from that topic to the lead magnet's topic, as long as the connection is plausible. This is the short-form promotion of Section 32.5 pointed at a free product rather than a paid one.

#### Education on autopilot, even with a disconnect

A February 2023 passage adds what some creators forget about the lead magnet: it keeps working even when it does not match the content that sent people to it. Suppose Koe talks about emotional management, sells web design, and offers a web-design lead magnet framed as "Do you want to learn a new skill, or do you have a business and want more customers?" There is a bit of a disconnect, but people will still click and become educated "without any of your doing." "Once you write a lead magnet, that content is there and it's going to be educating as many people that go through it." You can pitch your service inside it; or the downloaders are on your email list and will eventually buy once you talk more about web design "and hit the right pain point."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md (2023-02-05)

This is the argument from Section 32.3 seen from the funnel's side. The lead magnet sits at levels 2–4 of awareness: it takes a person who has expressed interest in a problem and educates them toward a solution, on autopilot. The disconnect between content and lead magnet is tolerable because the lead magnet's own framing names a broad and desirable problem (a new skill; more customers), which is how Section 32.3 says content should be framed for people who are discovering rather than searching.

#### The micro product: a $10 resume

The third piece is a small paid product. Koe's illustration, told in December 2024 and again in June 2025, concerns John Hu (rendered "John Hugh" in the transcripts), a co-founder and the CEO of Stan, the creator platform for hosting digital products that Koe recommends as affordable and well-rounded (he mentions that Steven Bartlett, of *The Diary of a CEO*, became a co-owner). Hu got his start on TikTok after working at Goldman Sachs, making career-advice videos. His audience kept hinting that they wanted his resume, the document that got him the Goldman job; he mentioned it here and there, then realized he could put it up "for 10 bucks as a template" and "see how it does." It made $1,000 in a short time, and thousands of dollars in total. "He didn't overcomplicate it."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024-12-01); How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md (2025-06-08)

According to the Stan team, Koe says, many people do this on the platform: some have made millions, some thousands, some hundreds, depending on their audience, their traffic from social media and newsletters, their promotion, and how they improve based on results. His lesson for the viewer: "You probably already have something you can upload to the internet, put a small price tag on, create content around and build a side income." It may be on your hard drive, in your notes, or still in your head, waiting to be written as a short ten-page ebook or guide. Koe calls this a *micro product*.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024-12-01)

The case is also an example of the levels of awareness at work. The audience was asking for the resume: they were at level 5 for that specific product before it existed. Hu's contribution was to notice the demand and attach a price to it.

#### The micro product's job: trust for a bigger purchase

What is the micro product for? Koe's answer in the same December 2024 video is that it builds trust for a higher-priced service. Many more people pay for a higher-priced service once they have already invested in you; some do not want to go all-in on an expensive offer and want to pay something small first to see whether they trust you. So inside the micro product, link to the service, so that people who go through it click and are more likely to buy.

But he sets a priority. You do not strictly need a micro product; it is lower priority than the *micro service* (the small first service of Chapter 29). "I would build the service first so you can make money faster." If you have time and an idea, build the micro product quickly, as another income stream.

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024-12-01)

The order matches the start-up sequence discussed in Chapters 29 and 30: the service makes money fastest; products come as the business grows. The 2024 ecosystem of Section 32.5 (product first, then service) describes a later stage, when the product has become the entry point to a higher-priced service.

#### Trust tripwires: Justin Welsh's term

Koe did not invent the logic of the micro product. In a December 2021 interview, Justin Welsh, the creator and solo-business operator who appears throughout the book as a guest and a case, described his own products in these terms: "Most of my stuff is what I call *trust tripwires*, right? So, it's getting people in with a lower-cost product that delivers incredible value, and having them start to say, like, this is someone we can trust. When I spend money with Justin, I know that I'm going to get significant value from the product." The product's main function is the trust it produces, which feeds later purchases. Welsh ties this to organic growth: "the best way to do that is to continue to go out and build an organic following."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md (2021-12-20)

The term is Welsh's, and Koe uses it as a third-party idea. His own "micro product" (2024) is a close relative: Welsh emphasizes the *trust* that a low-cost, high-value product creates; Koe emphasizes the *simplicity* of creating one from what you already have, and its place in a sequence after the service. The two descriptions fit together: what the small product really sells, in Welsh's account, is the expectation of value in future purchases.

**Complementary context:** In direct-response marketing, a "tripwire" is a low-priced first offer designed to turn a prospect into a paying customer, after which larger offers follow. Welsh's phrase keeps the mechanism and renames its purpose: the first purchase is meant to create trust rather than merely to break the buying barrier.

#### Why people buy: trust and perceived benefit

An August 2024 passage states the principle that runs through all three pieces of the funnel. From traffic, you promote a newsletter or a product. People look at your content, then at the product, and buy it if it is good. Then you write emails to *nurture* the audience beyond top-of-funnel content: you get very specific and help people, they trust you more, and they want your product. That, Koe says, is why you buy anything (not always via email): because you trust it, you want it, and it benefits your life. And the corollary for critics: "You're only calling it a scam because it doesn't benefit your life, or you can't see the benefit for your life."

**Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md (2024-08-11)

He adds an estimate of what this makes possible. With an audience flow in the hundreds of thousands for six to twelve months, and iteration, he finds it hard to believe you could not convert a fraction of it into buyers. Skilled people do it better, so a possible mindset is to try for three or four years and then make a million in six months. The figure is aspirational framing, of the kind Chapter 30 discusses, not a forecast.

The remark about "scam" uses the word in a sense Koe gives it elsewhere: a scam is promising something you do not deliver and not refunding it, and, in this second sense, it is what people call a product whose benefit they cannot see. The second use returns the chapter to its starting point. Value is perceived, and a person who does not perceive the benefit of a product will not buy it and may condemn it. The job of the landing page, the lead magnet, the micro product and the nurturing emails is the same as the job of persuasion in general: to make the benefit visible to the person for whom it is real.

#### The minimal funnel assembled

The pieces of this section and the previous one form a single path, which can be laid out from the reader's side:

| Stage | Asset | Level of awareness addressed | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery | Social posts, including magnetic content | 1–3 | Name problems, offer quick solutions, attract attention broadly |
| Capture | Lead magnet (or, for one-to-one offers, the qualifying questionnaire) | 2–4 | Concentrate authority on one problem; collect the email or the application |
| Nurture | Weekly newsletter; emails | 2–5 | Deepen trust; give specific help; promote the product or service |
| Conversion | Landing page; DMs | 4–5 | Present the system, the proof and the offer; ask for payment |
| Trust for the next purchase | Micro product (trust tripwire) | 5, for a larger offer | Prove value at low cost; link to the service |

Koe's statements about this path vary in order and emphasis across the years, as the chapter has shown, but the structure is stable: content raises awareness, owned assets concentrate trust, the landing page or the conversation converts, and promotion, scheduled and repeated, connects every stage to the next. Chapter 33 turns from the path to what sits at its end: the product itself.

### Exercises

1. **Diagnose your own resistance to selling.** Koe argues that anyone who dislikes selling is already selling (a worldview, an employer's product, their beliefs) and that the feeling of being a "sellout" signals inner work or a money perspective that needs to change. Write down the strongest objection you hold against promoting your own work. Then analyze it with the chapter's tools: is it a principle you arrived at, or a belief installed by people who do not have the life you want? Does your objection, laid out as an argument, have the structure of problem, amplification, solution and promised benefit that Koe says it would share with a sales page?

2. **Test the line between persuasion and manipulation.** Koe uses two criteria: whether persuasion is conscious, and whether it seeks mutual or selfish benefit. Construct a case in which someone persuades fully consciously toward a selfish end, and another in which someone persuades unconsciously toward an outcome that genuinely benefits the other person. How does Koe's framework classify each? Is his answer (that unethical use comes from survival-mode values and is cured by development) sufficient, or does the first case require a rule his framework does not supply?

3. **Map a real funnel onto the levels of awareness.** Take one creator or business whose content you follow and identify one example of their output for each of the five levels (social posts, newsletter, free download, landing page, product). Where does their material cluster? Which level is missing or weakest? Using Koe's 2024 table of content focus per level, propose one piece of content that would fill the gap, and state which level-5 definition (incentive, felt impact, "the right why," or a nudge) you are applying and why.

4. **Rewrite a pitch with the five levers.** Take a weak promotional message you have received (a cold DM, an ad, a generic email; the "Walmart-quality captions" pitch is a model) and rewrite it so that it pulls all five levers in order: name the threat, mirror the identity, exclude people, paint the transformation, give the first step. Then evaluate your version against the ethics of Section 32.2: at what point, if any, did the rewrite cross from mindful helping into something you would be uncomfortable defending?

5. **Reconcile "exclude people" with "isn't alienating anyone."** In 2023 Koe said that calling out a group intelligently alienates no one; in 2026 he recommends deliberately naming who a message is not for. Write a short argument for the conditions under which each approach is correct, considering the stage of the audience relationship, market sophistication and the size of the creator's audience. Identify one situation in which exclusion would clearly backfire.

6. **Design a promotion system and its diagnostic.** For a product or service you have or could plausibly create, design separate content-creation and content-promotion schedules for one month, including at least one evergreen long-form piece and one baked-in recurring promotion. Specify in advance which data you will look at (clicks, landing-page visits, which post the promotion sat under) and what you will change if you get no sales and if you get no growth. Explain how your plan avoids both of Koe's traps: never promoting, and "cashing in goodwill" on a product that never improves.

7. **Question the market-sophistication verdict.** In January 2026 Koe argued that info products have reached Schwartz's final stage of sophistication, where brand and a tribe with a mission become the differentiator; in the same period he also said they would not die. Choose a market you know well and decide which stage it is in, giving evidence from the claims you see repeated in it. What would a believable goal and a credible mechanism look like in that market? Under what conditions would "your mission is your niche" be bad advice?

8. **Build your minimal funnel on paper.** Draft, for your own work, the headline and subheadline of a landing page (combining at least desired outcome, time frame and personal system), the opening lines of its lead, the title of a lead magnet that could be made in about a day, and one micro product you could price small from something you already have. Then transfer your consciousness to a reader at level 2 of awareness and identify the first sentence at which they would click away.
