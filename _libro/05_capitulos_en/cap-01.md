## Chapter 1 — The Matrix: How a Mind Gets Programmed

Everything else in this book is an answer to a problem, and this chapter describes the problem. Dan Koe returns to it under many names — the Matrix, conditioning, programming, the default path, assigned goals, autopilot, the NPC — but underneath the changing vocabulary there is a single diagnosis: most of what a person thinks, wants, values and does was installed from the outside before that person was able to question it, and unless it is examined it will keep running for the rest of their life. The chapter builds that diagnosis in layers. It begins with the Matrix as a web of inherited beliefs and its collective dimension (1.1), explains the mechanism by which childhood installs that web (1.2), states the thesis that organizes the whole system — you are always pursuing goals, and if you do not choose them they are assigned to you (1.3) — shows why imitation cannot be avoided and only made conscious (1.4), describes how today's information environment amplifies the programming (1.5), characterizes the resulting life on autopilot (1.6) and ends with the defensive mechanism that keeps the program in place: the closed mind that turns ideology into identity (1.7). No prior knowledge is assumed. What the chapter prepares is the question of Part II: if the self is largely a product of this program, what exactly is the self, and how can it change?

A word of caution before starting. The author speaks about "the masses" with a set of percentages — 90%, 95%, 99%, "less than 1%" — that recur throughout this chapter. They are not statistics. They are rhetorical estimates that measure different things (who works a job, who thinks clearly, who questions their upbringing, who lives the life they want), and they do not reconcile with one another. When the author is asked where they come from, he says they are an impression from observation; once he claims statistics back a figure but does not name them. They should be read as emphasis, not as data.

### 1.1 The Matrix as an Inherited Web of Beliefs

#### A metaphor borrowed from a film

The word "Matrix" comes from the 1999 film, and the author uses it the way he uses most cultural references: as a pointer, not as a literal claim. In the video where he first develops the idea at length, he admits he has seen the movie once and does not remember much of it. What he keeps is the famous choice offered to Neo: take the red pill and you break out of the Matrix; take the blue pill and you remain "kind of a slave to it, you're just an NPC, a robot". He introduces the image to define a vice he considers the antagonist of deep thinking. Closed-mindedness, he says, "is accepting the Matrix as your reality". **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`.

That definition already contains the whole structure of the idea. There is something called the Matrix; it is not reality but something that can be mistaken for reality; and the error of mistaking it is a mental posture — closedness — rather than a lack of information. To understand what is being mistaken for reality, one has to follow the chain the author builds.

#### The Matrix as a web of expectations

The author's chain starts with mental constructions. Humans make sense of the world through constructions — labels, concepts, stories — and those constructions became more advanced over time, each one built on top of the last. Language advanced. We wrote books, which he describes as "strings of language". We made videos. Then came the school system, government, culture, society, and "a load of other expectations, assumptions and predictions that we call beliefs for what life has been and should be, not what it is". The last clause is the key. A belief, in this account, is not primarily a description of how things are; it is an expectation about how they should be, inherited from the people who built the layer beneath you. Hence his definition: "think of the Matrix as a web of expectations that has been conditioned into our minds". Elsewhere in the same video he compresses it further — "The Matrix is what you think you know" — and describes it as "this elaborate and complex Matrix or construction in our mind that we have created and reside in for comfort". **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`.

The author grounds the abstraction in an ordinary experience. Look around the room you are in. Every object in it has a meaning that your mind projects onto it without any conscious effort: you do not decide what a chair is for, or what a phone means, or what a diploma on a wall signifies. The meaning simply arrives. It arrives effortlessly because it has been repeated to you over time, and, as he puts it, "we are repetitive beings".

This is where the first of his recurring metaphors appears. Because we are repetitive, the neural networks in the brain are "like the Grand Canyon with water flowing through it and creating the grooves in our head so that the water can flow effortlessly". Water carved the canyon by running through the same path for an immense length of time; once the canyon exists, water has nowhere else to go. In the same way, meaning that has been repeated enough carves a channel, and once the channel exists, the meaning flows through it automatically. The result is that "we project meaning and accept what we see as law". The metaphor matters because it explains why the Matrix is invisible from inside: what feels like simply seeing the world is the water running through grooves that were carved long before you noticed them. The same image returns in the next section as the core mechanism of conditioning. **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`.

In a 2023 video the author reformulates the web of expectations from the side of information. "Learning is the fundamental human drive." At birth you are an information sponge, and the people around you — parents, friends, society, teachers, later bosses — project their worldview onto you. They got that worldview from the same sources, unless at some point they questioned it. By the time you are between 12 and 18, he estimates, you know less than 1% of the world's information — he would say less than 0.1% — and, crucially, it is "the same .1% that every single other person knows", because it was handed down rather than discovered. "That's what the Matrix is: it's a web of expectations." Its effect is to create identities that are the same as everyone else's, locked into the same routines and cycles, which he calls "the known", and to make people slaves to it. **Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`. The figure is, again, an illustrative estimate; the point is not the number but the overlap — the fact that the small slice of reality each person receives is the same slice.

#### Perception is backed by conditioning

If the Matrix were only a set of opinions, it would be easy to correct: one would simply check the opinions against what one sees. The author's argument is that this check is not available, because perception itself is shaped by the same conditioning. He presents the argument as a chain, and it is worth following step by step. First, we take whatever we perceive through any of the five senses as truth. Second, our sensory perceptions, and above all the way we interpret them, are "backed by our conditioning": by how we were raised, the environment we live in, the context or lens through which we view the world, and the goals each individual is pursuing, consciously or not. Third, therefore, everyone will interpret what they see differently. Fourth, closing your mind around your one interpretation is what produces the arguments and debates "in the comment section" — the inability to see beyond what is said, heard or seen. **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`.

Two details of this argument deserve attention. The first is the presence of goals in the list of things that shape interpretation. His example: if you work a job, you are probably unconsciously pursuing the goal of your boss, and that goal colors what you notice and how you read it. This is the first appearance in the book of an idea that will become central from section 1.3 onward and that Part III develops fully: a goal is a lens. The second detail is the comparison with animals. Dogs hear frequencies we never will; some frogs see colors differently ("I don't know which ones", he admits); there may be more colors out there, "who knows". The comparison is offered as an illustration rather than a scientific argument, and the author marks his own uncertainty. Its function is to make vivid that the human sensory field is one particular slice of what exists.

From this he draws a practice he calls **radical open-mindedness**: being open and critical with your own thinking, which he considers essential to creative work, and not taking what you see as law. He invites the viewer to practice it while watching the very video in which he says it.

The author applies the same reasoning to a habit he finds pervasive online: judging other people's lives. In comment sections people declare that a celebrity is unhappy, or what that person should do with their money. In his reading, they are projecting their own web of expectations — assumptions conditioned into their brain — onto someone whose situation they have never lived. "You don't know what they know, you don't know what they should do with their money until you've experienced where they're at through action and gaining experience." Until then, most if not all of what you say about them is, in his words, nonsense and worthless. He adds a psychological observation: if a celebrity gave a million dollars to charity, the critic would just pick something else to blame, caught in a continual trap of blaming others instead of attending to their own problems. "No one's ever going to make you happy." The better move, he argues, is to improve your own life so you can experience more and make better decisions. **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`. The example is small, but it shows the diagnosis operating at the scale of daily behavior: projection is what the Matrix looks like when it is pointed at another person.

#### Intellectual structures and the closed system of perception

The author supports the idea that mental constructions are places one can live in with a line from Terence McKenna, a lecture a commenter recommended to him: "you can live and die inside these intellectual structures if you choose to, but people of curiosity usually find themselves unsatisfied with the conventional answers". He uses the quotation to argue that constructions are houses — and later applies it to politics, where an ideology becomes a "mental home" people reside in (section 1.7 develops this). He recommends McKenna's lectures, Alan Watts's lectures and actualize.org as places to expand the mind. **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`.

In a 2023 video titled "Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix)", he goes further. He connects Donald Hoffman's theory of perception to McKenna, "who say that language is virtual reality, perception is a closed system, and psychedelics allow you to see beyond our fitness-based perceptions". He also links Hoffman's idea to non-duality, infinite consciousness, mentalism, idealism, Hinduism and Buddhism, and says that to him "this feels more right than most other theories of everything". **Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`. It is important to register the status of this claim. The author is not presenting evidence; he is stating an intuition about which metaphysical picture resonates with him, and he labels it as such ("feels more right").

**Complementary context:** Donald Hoffman is a cognitive scientist who proposes an "interface theory of perception": natural selection tuned our senses to track fitness payoffs, not objective reality, so what we perceive works like a desktop interface that hides the underlying machinery. Terence McKenna (1946–2000) was an ethnobotanist and lecturer known for speculative talks on language, consciousness and psychedelics. Both are used by the author as sources of a frame, not as authorities on a settled fact.

The video's title also marks a shift in the author's position that the rest of this section will trace: in 2022 the Matrix was something you could break out of; by 2023, at the level of perception, it was something you "can't escape".

#### The oak tree: most people live on a single branch

The author offers one more image for the same idea, this time from the side of what lies outside the Matrix. Think of the old world as an acorn and the new world as an oak tree. Oaks can live 600 to 2,000 years and grow more complex over time. There is a core — which "could be the essence of being" — that splits into three to six major areas, which keep splitting into branches; some branches live, some fall off; and there are thousands of leaves, each with its own ecosystem similar to that of the whole tree. The oak, he says, is "a watered-down description of reality beyond what you can see with your limited perception as a human" — again the frogs that see colors we cannot. If you are not open to the fact that there is more than you perceive, your creativity and your spiritual connection are massively hindered. Within the metaphor there is also pruning: sometimes a branch must be cut so it can grow better or in a new direction, which he reads as humans interacting with evolution and shaping where we go.

The diagnostic use of the image comes at the end. There are infinite branches and leaves to explore, "but most people are conditioned into the singular branch and possibly singular leaf that they were conditioned into throughout their childhood" — go to school, get a job, retire at 65 — together with the worldview that comes with it. That single branch or leaf, he says, is what people call "the Matrix", and many remain trapped in that perspective for life. **Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`. The value of the oak over the web is that it shows what is lost: not just freedom, but the size of the reality available to be explored.

#### The social matrix: a loop that reproduces itself

From 2024 onward the author describes the Matrix less as a private web and more as a social system with a structure that can be mapped. In 2024 he defines "the social matrix or just the matrix that everyone talks about" as "a web of ideas and beliefs that act as anchors for your mind to make sense of the world" — "anchors" being one of his words for the reference points a mind uses to orient itself. He then lays out the cycle. Government and culture influence the school system. The school creates resources for students. Students do not question what they are taught. Students become parents. Parents teach their children what they know and send them to school. Children make friends and conform further to fit in. Children grow up, get jobs, become teachers, parents and politicians, and create the knowledge and resources, online and offline, that influence culture. Parents, students and children vote. The cycle continues. **Source:** `Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md`.

His verdict at this point is notably calm: "This isn't a bad thing. This is just how we learn to survive." The problem appears only if we never question what we learned during an upbringing that gave us "a very limited worldview". Then we never discover our potential or what lies in the unknown, and "we end up a clone like everyone else".

In 2025 he returns to the subject in a long video, "You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape", and this time he is explicit about his source. Everything in it is "a condensation and my own thoughts on the video The Social Matrix by actualize.org", a video about three hours long; he says actualize.org "has shaped my life in more ways than one". **Complementary context:** actualize.org (also written Actualized.org) is the YouTube channel and website of Leo Gura, a self-development teacher; his name does not appear in the transcript. The author frames the social matrix as "a logical sequence of steps, this loop", and adds a clarification aimed at his own earlier rhetoric: "we're not talking about some kind of red pill: oh you need to escape the matrix and start a business and achieve freedom and break off from society. No. We're talking about understanding the actual matrix because there is one." The only way to break out of it, he says, "is by understanding it". **Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`.

He then presents the loop as a series of layers. Because the analysis is adapted from a third party, it should be read as the author's condensation of actualize.org's framework rather than as his original theory.

The first layer is the parents. "Parents themselves were brainwashed by the previous generation. They pass on their worldview, biases and beliefs unconsciously." The mechanism that makes this transfer stick is survival: "Most parents only love and approve of you if you conform to their values and beliefs. And you conform because you want to survive." A child does not want to be kicked out of the house or the culture and depends on the parents to be fed. And the parents got their information from the same system they now perpetuate.

The second layer is early childhood conditioning, and its central claim concerns language. "Language shapes and limits what you can actually think about." The only reason you think the thoughts you do is that the language you speak lets you articulate them: "Think about how many things you haven't thought of or could think of if you weren't bound by a specific language." Cultural norms and values are planted before you can think critically, and "children learn to seek approval by conforming to authority".

The third layer is the school. "Public schools were explicitly created to reinforce obedience, as researched by UC San Diego" — a study the author cites without further detail. He acknowledges that criticism of education angers people who are attached to it or dependent on it and were told since childhood that it is what success requires, "when that's just not the case anymore". In his account, the Prussian education model was designed "as a long-term solution to social unrest through the standardization of thought", "to create obedient soldiers, compliant citizens and well-behaved workers", and Horace Mann brought it to America during industrialization to educate large numbers of immigrant children; from it came mandatory attendance, teacher training, national curricula and testing, division by age and grade levels. "Students were taught how to work and obey, not how to think because that's what most benefited society." The school is also a closed loop: teachers were educated by the previous generation in the same system; curricula are set by school boards, often made up of parents from the same system; grade levels reward memorizing over questioning; universities are funded by corporations with agendas; journal gatekeepers come from the same system; and professors must publish and approve journals to keep their careers.

**Complementary context:** Horace Mann (1796–1859) was a Massachusetts education reformer who promoted the "common school" movement and drew on Prussian practices such as graded classrooms and teacher training. Whether those reforms were designed primarily to produce obedience is a contested historical interpretation; the author presents it as part of the actualize.org condensation. Elsewhere in the corpus he oscillates between this strong formulation and a systemic one in which nobody planned it and obedience is "a property of the system"; that tension, and the critique of school as a whole, belong to Chapter 2.

The fourth layer is information sources and authority. "Media and the internet are the primary sources of mass information." News outlets are owned by profit-driven corporations. Google's algorithms prioritize university and establishment sources, so most people end up getting their information from universities whose teachers were trained by teachers. Wikipedia is written by academics within a biased system. Social media reinforces biases through echo chambers. Entertainment subtly programs values and worldviews. In science and academia, "scientists and students rely on faith in thousands of studies they haven't personally validated", and research funding comes from corporations and governments with agendas — "most of the time, not all of the time", he qualifies. The circularity is the point: "students and scientists trust the information provided by other students and scientists who were raised by parents who got their information from prior students and scientists. Do you see the problem with this?" The loop closes even at the level of the classroom: teachers were trained on government-provided material, and that same material found its way into the information sources everyone else consults, so the authority one would check against is downstream of the authority being checked. His reaction is unusually strong for him: "This should just terrify you."

The fifth layer, the propaganda model of the media amplified by social media and AI, belongs to the information environment and is treated in section 1.5.

The sixth layer is the claim that "society is rigged against you", delivered as a rapid list. The need for a job forces you to comply with your boss's worldview, and the boss went through the same education. Marketing and advertising manipulate desires from childhood. The food industry "is incentivized to poison you", because people get addicted to hyperpalatable foods, which affects mental clarity. "The health care system profits from illness, not health." Social circles punish those who question too deeply, and you will be cast out of the tribe. Dating and relationships require conformity, especially if you are lonely and not self-reliant. Politicians and therapists come from the same education system.

From all of this the author derives the defining property of the system: **circular validation**. "It's a matrix. It has circular validation. Every entity that composes society and culture — media, education, science, government, business, religion, etc. — justifies and reinforces the others." When you question one source, you meet "programmed responses from NPCs like trust the experts, or it's in the textbook, or it's the law and it's tradition". And therefore: "Everything is validated against the same system. External validation is not possible. Therefore we live in an illusion of our own construction." The term and the idea are adapted from actualize.org; what the author adds is to make circular validation the definition of the Matrix itself. **Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`.

Finally, he summarizes the whole system as a nine-step loop:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 | You are born into a family already programmed by the matrix. |
| 2 | You are conditioned by language, culture and early peer pressure. |
| 3 | You are educated in schools that reinforce the worldview that benefits the system. |
| 4 | You consume media and entertainment that further shape your beliefs and desires. |
| 5 | You enter the workforce, where survival depends on conformity. |
| 6 | You participate in social, religious and cultural rituals that reinforce group identity. |
| 7 | You seek information and validation from sources that are themselves products of the matrix. |
| 8 | You are incentivized to conform and disincentivized to question. |
| 9 | You in turn pass on the same programming to the next generation. |

The loop closes on itself at step 9, which is why it reproduces across generations without anyone needing to direct it. The author ends the summary with the problem it creates for any attempt at escape: how do you get out, "especially since people who teach you how to escape the matrix are a byproduct of the matrix"? (The transcript reads "create", most likely a mistranscription of "escape".) The question is left open there; Chapter 2 takes it up with the idea of awareness.

#### How the author's position on "breaking free" changed

Placed side by side, the 2022 and 2025 treatments of the Matrix do not say the same thing, and the difference should be stated rather than smoothed over.

In 2022 the Matrix is "what you think you know", closed-mindedness is accepting it, and there is a way out: "the way you break free of the Matrix or create a glitch in the Matrix is to break free of your robotic behavior, to become conscious of and break free of your robotic behavior by doing something different". The mechanism he describes is concrete. When you interrupt a cycle, you notice how your mind reacts and tries to revert, and in that reaction you make discoveries about yourself and the world. It applies to the cycles you have control over — personal cycles — and his examples are skipping a night of sleep, fasting, and breaking the employee cycle. If you never do it, "you are extremely similar to other people and you are going to get the same results as other people in life". **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`. A **glitch in the Matrix**, in his coined sense, is therefore a deliberate interruption of an automatic pattern in order to see what the pattern was hiding.

As early as December 2022 he starts distancing himself from the term ("break free of the Matrix if you want to frame it that way"). In 2023 the Matrix becomes the single branch of the oak and, at the level of perception, a closed system you "can't escape". In 2025 it is a structural system with circular validation, the "red pill … break off from society" reading is explicitly rejected, and the only exit is understanding. Even then, later in the same video he says that agency most often leads to entrepreneurship, so the rejection of the "start a business and break free" reading is not total.

The change, then, is from a problem of individual habits that can be broken to a structural and epistemological problem that can be understood but not left. The author does not explain the shift; he simply relies on the actualize.org condensation. The two positions are not strictly incompatible. "Breaking" applies to conditioned expectations at the psychological level and survives as a practice inside the systemic frame — interrupting a cycle is still how you notice it. "Not escaping" applies to perception, which is a closed system by nature, and to society, from which the author does not want anyone to isolate themselves.

A related change concerns tone. In 2023 he says the social structure "isn't a bad thing, it creates an intellectual structure to anchor our attention"; in 2024, "This isn't a bad thing. This is just how we learn to survive." In 2025, the circularity of authority "should just terrify you". The thesis never changes — he always asks the reader to question the structure — but the emphasis moves from necessity to danger. The two readings fit different stages of life: the structure is necessary for a child to survive and dangerous for an adult who never questions it. A third, smaller tension sits in the same territory. In 2022 he says "the system is rigged, you can't change it but you can learn it, that is how you rig yourself in the system's favor"; in 2025, "society is rigged against you". These are compatible if read in sequence — the system works against you by default, which is precisely why it is worth learning its rules — but the shift from a playful, strategic attitude to an enumeration of threats is real.

#### Memes: why the Matrix wants to persist

In 2026 the author adds yet another explanatory frame, this time evolutionary and cultural. He gives the gist of evolution as he understands it: billions of years ago self-replicating molecules existed; some replicated more successfully than others; those that built protective structures around themselves — cell walls, then cells, then multicellular bodies — replicated better; and after billions of iterations of natural selection "we have genes that are very good at building bodies that can survive". Then human consciousness appeared ("I don't know how. Don't ask me how"), and the same process continued at a new level: genes, and then memes. "What a meme really is is a unit of culture that is spread from mind to mind." His examples are personal: the words, language, concepts and beliefs your parents passed down that you could not question as a child; school's system of reward and punishment through grades; being told "go to school, get a job, retire at 65", which with AI accelerating "is not beneficial for your future". Many people are stuck in that paradigm because the memes composing their mind are not aligned with what success requires today. **Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`.

The parallel is exact in his formulation: "our bodies are built by genes to replicate genes. Our identities are built by memes to replicate memes." If genes compose the body, memes compose the mind — ideas, beliefs, habits, practices, traditions. Memes have been naturally selected over time and culture, "through creation and destruction", to the point where their "sole goal is to persist", to survive and self-replicate. And that gives the Matrix a motive: "That's why the memes that compose who you are right now don't want you to change." The idea that identity resists change as if it were fighting for survival is the core of Part II; here it receives a cultural foundation.

**Complementary context:** the term "meme" was coined by the biologist Richard Dawkins in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) as a cultural analogue of the gene. The author uses the concept without naming its originator. His application to identity is his own adaptation.

He extends the argument to the structures that protect memes. "One important survival mechanism of memes are institutions and ideologies." We create larger structures — religions, nations, political parties, corporations — "that have the goal of protecting, perpetuating, and replicating the beliefs and values they were founded on. Religions want more members, nations want more control, and corporations want more customers." His illustration is a thought experiment: a religion that tells its adherents to have children and convert others will spread faster than one that tells people not to have children and to stay quiet. The memes in your head — who you are, how you act — have been shaped by this kind of selection and are "fighting for survival because they're like genes". **Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`. The institutions in the nine-step loop above can be reread in this light: they are not only channels through which beliefs pass, but structures whose function is to keep certain beliefs alive.

#### Everything is a religion

The meme logic, the author argues, applies far beyond religion in the narrow sense: to political ideologies, brands, conspiracy theories, jokes, fashion, video games and the culture you identify with, coffee, alcohol, team Apple versus Android, team Claude versus ChatGPT versus anti-AI, team keto versus carnivore versus vegan, "and anything else". He condenses it into a coined phrase: "What you need to understand is that everything is a religion. Your morning routine is a religion. Your political opinions are a religion. Your identity as a gamer, a lifter, a minimalist, a stoic, a craft beer enthusiast, they're all religions." With social media and global access to information, "we're experiencing the religionization of everything. Very few people actually do things because they want to anymore." Almost everyone's life is built around actions that let them fit into what he calls **the digital tribe** or the digital culture. **Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`.

"Religion" here is a common word used in a special sense. It does not refer to belief in the divine; it names any identity that is held as a group membership, defended as such and acted out in order to belong. The term is diagnostic, not theological.

He applies it to a current example. Identity is "also why you choose to be anti-AI or pro-AI, which is the new religion", arguing "into oblivion about it all day on social media with nothing to show for it". The alternative is empirical: "You can regurgitate how AI is rotting your brain, or you can test that theory against reality and actually educate yourself on it." This, he says, is how you make yourself immune to people who target your **identity tension** — his term for the weak point others exploit when they aim at your identity. "Every time you argue about the anti-AI stuff or pro-AI stuff, you are a slave to someone else's ideals. And you do not seek truth, you seek hype, and you have not created your own mind." **Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md`. The warning connects directly to section 1.7: a belief that becomes a team stops being a tool for seeing and becomes something to defend.

The author offers his own biography as a lived instance. He grew up in the LDS (Mormon) church and saw identity defense daily: when he asked a church leader a question, the leader would go through "this regurgitated script of things that didn't make sense to me". He is careful to say this is nothing against that religion or any other. Today he enjoys looking at different perspectives online and listens to both Christian and atheist content, and he is "not identified with either side because that prevents me from seeing truth". **Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`. The story is an experience, not an argument; what it shows is the posture the author recommends — exposure to opposed views without membership in either camp.

#### The collective dimension: children of society and a giant mind

The Matrix is not only something that happens inside individual heads; the author also describes it as a collective entity. In a 2023 video he argues that we read books and adopt ideas that align with our goals as part of our identity. Since most people have society's goals assigned to them, they perceive and store information that reinforces that assigned identity. "The masses are the children of society." Society, in this formulation, is a **behavior system** that reaches children early through parents who do not question anything and teach what they learned from their own parents and schools. Society as a collective ego, a collective self, the identity of humanity, "has 95% of children" — unless they question their identity and create themselves through information gathered by their own curiosity. Later in the same discussion he adds that social media now does what society used to do, creating "the most children or selves". **Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`. The term **children of society** names this condition: being raised, mentally, by the collective rather than by oneself.

The same internet appears in a very different light in a 2022 video, where the author offers what he explicitly labels one of his "weird theories": humans are documenting the collective consciousness online, and the internet — like the universe — is "a giant mind". His chain: humans make sense of things through stories, labels and concepts; what we post online is what our minds can make sense of and what we think will make sense to others — stories, opinions, advice; therefore the internet is becoming a giant mind. The consequence he draws is optimistic. Creators, personal brands and "thought leaders" (a term he calls cringe) will be among "the greatest evolvers of humanity", because "ideas beget ideas": nothing can be built unless the idea for it is born first, so we need thinkers who expand their minds. **Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`. The status of the claim matters: it is a theory, and he presents it as one.

The tension between these two pictures — the internet as a factory of "children of society" and the internet as a collective mind that can evolve humanity — runs through the whole corpus and is traced in section 1.5. The author never resolves it by choosing one side; what changes is the emphasis.

The collective dimension also explains a priority he states openly. Exploring other planets is done out of passion ("Musk is doing that out of passion"); it is "not an absolute necessity for our species". We can evolve, transcend and self-actualize by first solving the meaningful problems in our own lives. "We need to raise the collective consciousness of that 90 to 95% of the masses so that we don't head off a cliff." **Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`. This is an opinion, and it is the opinion that gives the whole diagnosis its stakes: the Matrix is not only a private obstacle to a good life but, in the author's view, a collective risk. Chapter 2 returns to that civilizational frame; the last chapter of the book closes with it.

This section has described what the Matrix is: a web of inherited expectations, reinforced by a loop of institutions that validate each other, carried by memes that want to persist, and lived as a set of tribal "religions". It has not yet explained how the web gets into a particular mind. That is the subject of conditioning.

### 1.2 Conditioning: Childhood as Programming

#### What conditioning is

The author's most precise definition of the mechanism comes from a 2023 video on NPCs: "conditioning equals the information you process with your mind repeated over time until those thought processes are automatic". He uses **conditioning** and **programming** as synonyms. The definition has three parts, and each matters. It concerns *information* — not only lessons, but anything the mind processes. It requires *repetition over time* — a single exposure does little. And its result is *automaticity* — thought processes that no longer need conscious attention to run. **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`.

He explains it by analogy with the body. Conditioning the body means repeating a movement until you get stronger and more efficient at lifting a specific weight. The mind works the same way: the more you go through a situation, the more you unconsciously learn to navigate it; what was uncomfortable becomes normal, and your direction becomes automatic. This is not in itself a defect. Habit formation, he says, is great "if it is intentional and in alignment with a specific self-generated goal". The defect appears when the goal is assigned — school, job, retirement — because then your programming is set to reach a goal you never chose, and you live on autopilot. This qualification is the hinge of the whole chapter: conditioning is neutral machinery, and what makes it a trap is whose goal it serves. Section 1.3 develops that point.

An earlier video (2022) gives the same mechanism its most memorable image. We are "creatures of habit", and the mind works through conditioning, which you can think of as repetition, "like reps at the gym". When something is consistently exposed to your conscious mind — his example is a religious teaching — you start to believe it; it "becomes law", and "neural networks are carved out in your brain like the Grand Canyon with flowing water". Over time the channel becomes massive and hard to bury or redo. But here the author adds the turn that makes the metaphor hopeful: you cannot refill the Grand Canyon, but you can do it to your brain — through repetition and conditioning in the opposite direction, toward the character or person you want to create and be authentic with. **Source:** `Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md`. The same machinery that installed the program is the one that can rewrite it, an idea that Chapter 4 turns into a method of reprogramming by immersion.

The neural vocabulary should be read with its status in mind. The author presents "carving neural networks" as an explanatory picture, not as a neuroscience claim he defends; in the same period he says he is "not too interested in the science" and prefers the spiritual side of the topic.

**Complementary context:** the idea that repeated co-activation strengthens neural connections is commonly summarized as Hebb's rule ("neurons that fire together wire together"). It is a reasonable background for the author's metaphor, but he does not cite it here.

The same mechanism shapes what we value. In a 2022 video on dopamine, the author asks whether value, psychologically and culturally, is determined by dopamine, and answers that it is not, because we like to think we spend our time on things that are valuable. On social media we see "hot women, fancy cars, mansions" — things far away from us, out of our control, which we want to take control of and possess. Because humans are "beings of conditioning" — "the more things that are repeated over time the more we value it or the more that it becomes a part of our own reality" — "we are culturally and societally conditioned to have a specific viewpoint or point of view of the world". He contrasts perspectives: to him, not living in a third-world country, rice is not as valuable as a car; to someone else, rice is more practical, gives more benefits and holds more value than a car. The "big problem" is that in the West, in Europe, "or really with most people that have a Wi-Fi connection", we are conditioned to believe that shiny, expensive things hold great value, and we keep desiring them "because dopamine is the desired molecule, it is something that is away from you". **Source:** `Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md`. Value, in this account, is not discovered in objects; it is repeated into them. (The neurochemistry of desire is treated in Chapter 11.)

In 2025 the author reaches for a philosophical authority on the same mechanism. He quotes Jiddu Krishnamurti: "Thought is always conditioned and therefore thought is always mechanical." His gloss turns the quotation into a strong thesis about thinking itself: "thinking is mostly the automatic playback of previously encoded patterns, not conscious deliberation. Every thought comes from memory. Memory is in the past. And when you are thinking, you are just accessing stored patterns, not creating something new." He uses the quote to introduce the social matrix described in section 1.1. **Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`.

**Complementary context:** Jiddu Krishnamurti (1895–1986) was an Indian spiritual teacher who rejected organized religion and gurus and taught that thought, as the response of memory, is inherently limited and conditioned, and that freedom requires seeing that conditioning directly.

The author's explanations of conditioning have changed frame over the years without changing substance. In 2022 he speaks of wired neural networks and body chemistry, with a preference for the spiritual reading and the Grand Canyon image. In 2023 he gives the operational definition above. In 2025 the frame is Krishnamurti's philosophy of thought. In 2026 it becomes evolutionary and cultural, with self-replicating memes protected by institutions (section 1.1). The mechanism moves from neural-spiritual to operational, to philosophical, to memetic, and the layers are compatible: the neural level explains the individual habit, the memetic level explains why cultural patterns persist across people. The author does not explain the changes of frame.

#### Born a learning sponge, conforming to survive

Conditioning becomes a diagnosis of a whole life when it is applied to childhood, and here the author repeats one argument across many videos from 2022 to 2026. Its starting point is biological. "The day you were born you became a learning sponge": your biological goal was survival, so you pulled from your environment whatever information allowed you to survive — language, crawling, walking, running, the "eyes, ears, mouth and nose" nursery rhyme you needed because you could not see your own face. Parents, teachers, friends, books and every stimulus told you what was good or bad, and your mind retained only what helped you survive that particular environment: touching fire is bad; praising your God is good. In this way you adopted programmed beliefs that may not serve your present goals, and you feel a tension "in heart and mind" to do what you want while feeling that you cannot, because you are "a slave to your mind that wasn't created by you". **Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`.

The same passage contains a structural definition worth isolating, because it anticipates the core of Parts II and III: "a worldview or perspective is composed of goals and problems that the goals allow you to become aware of". A worldview is not a list of facts; it is a set of goals together with the problems those goals make visible. Only once you are aware of a problem can you solve it, achieve the goal and open room for the next one. The inherited worldview, then, is an inherited set of goals and an inherited set of visible problems.

The earliest version of the argument (2022) frames it through curiosity. To understand curiosity, you must understand conditioning. For the first 18 years you were told what to be curious about; things were given to you. You imitated to survive: parents told you what to eat, how to walk, how to use the bathroom; school told you what to learn and to do your homework; friends did the same with their own parents and projected their opinions onto you. "You start forming these beliefs that eventually create your identity." He contrasts this with the small child before all that learning: outside, finding beauty in flowers, laughing at everything — crying too, but not because of attachment to an identity. **Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`.

Why does the child accept all this so readily? The author's answer is consistent: dependence. As a baby — "this babbling blob of flesh" — your only instinct is survival, and not even a conscious one. You depend on your parents to learn how to survive, so you have to conform to their beliefs; and unless they broke the pattern themselves, those beliefs carry the conditioning of their culture, their parents and the industrial age — goals that may not serve you. Since most teaching works through reward and punishment, if you do not conform — if you do not "become their child mentally" — you are punished. You do not know better, so you listen; you do not have a single original thought unless you see through this. "If you have never questioned this, then you are not an independent thinker." And then the qualification that keeps the claim honest: "nobody … is a completely independent thinker. There's degrees." **Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`. **Independent thinker** is thus a common term used in a graded sense: not a state one reaches but a degree to which one has questioned what was inherited.

The motive of conformity is spelled out in tribal terms in a 2025 video. As a child you have no choice but to trust parents and teachers, and you adopt much of your parents' beliefs "so that you don't get cast out of the tribe" — their values, becoming Christian or Muslim, Republican or Democrat. You can rebel, and some parents accept it, but it is often unwise to lose your parents' trust when your survival is in their hands. The author is careful: not everyone, but "the need for approval" is a massive influence. And it has a natural endpoint: once you are out on your own, you no longer have to pursue those goals in order to survive. **Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`. That last sentence is quietly important. It means that the conformity which was rational in childhood becomes, in adulthood, a residue — an adaptation that outlived its reason.

The author states the stakes most bluntly in 2026: "Most people in today's world haven't thought an original thought a single day in their life." The chain is the same: you were born into a part of the world called a culture; that culture holds its own value and belief system; your parents were raised in it and raised you in it; the beliefs were "probably imposed on you"; and "since you weren't able to think for yourself, you adopted them for the sake of survival, because you have to survive or else you'll be kicked out of the house or something worse" — in some parts of the world, he notes, "you could literally get killed". So you conform. You start at level one, and "those beliefs limit how far you can [think] from the get-go. And most people don't ever question that… That is the greatest trap you can fall into." The consequence is observable: "If you only think within the confines set by your parents, teachers, employers, and peers on social media, the outcome of your life isn't going to be pretty. Just observe where the average person ends up." His prescription anticipates Chapter 17: "increase the space", "expand how many perspectives you can hold", learn to "navigate multiple different scenarios". **Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`.

#### You don't have your own opinion

In a 2025 video on original thinking the author pushes the argument to a provocative conclusion: "You don't have your own opinion because your survival is dependent on agreeing with others' opinions." Unpacked: you needed approval to avoid being cast out of the tribe, so you absorbed the values of your environment because you needed them to survive. He illustrates with examples of escalating danger. Parents may be set on you getting a degree even if it is not best for you, and you are pressured into being "a good little student" or risk losing their approval. Racist, narrow-minded parents may not approve of you unless you adopt their racism, and you are too young to know better; they may kick you out or stop supporting you. If you were raised Christian and went to "an underdeveloped part of the world", "your beliefs could literally get you jailed or killed… it doesn't matter how true your beliefs are" — and he notes that "Christian" is interchangeable with any belief. Immigrants to America are instantly judged and demonized, with no opportunities and no circle. He explicitly says he is not proposing a political solution or trying to sway anyone; the examples illustrate a mechanism. **Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`.

The mechanism is one of hardening. "Once your worldview forms then it starts to solidify and once it solidifies it becomes very rigid… and you don't even notice it's there." It hardens further because "you are seeking confirmation of those values and beliefs from the tribe". The conclusion is meant to sting: "you haven't been thinking independently for your entire life." The idea of solidification returns in section 1.7, where the closed mind is precisely a worldview that has hardened into identity.

He gives the same idea a video-game form. After you create your character in a role-playing game, you are thrown into a specific starting zone for your race — "very similar to the culture you were raised in". All you know is one little spot on the map; the rest is dark and unknown. The culture you are raised in determines your values and beliefs because that is necessary for survival there; born on the other side of the world, the same beliefs would probably get you outcast or killed. Hence "lesson number one": be conscious of other people's beliefs and understand that "your beliefs are not absolute truth and they are only serving you for the culture that you live in", which is why they are worth questioning. **Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`. The **starting zone** is a useful complement to the oak tree of section 1.1: the oak shows how much reality exists, the starting zone shows why your beliefs fit only one corner of it.

#### The default trajectory and the "curse of knowledge"

In a 2023 video the author connects the drive to learn with the direction a life takes. The chain runs in five steps. First, "the fundamental human drive is to learn", which lets us soak up any information around us that aids survival, especially when we know nothing and have only the drive to survive. Second, with the information gathered as children, "we are set on the default trajectory for life". Third, society has evolved to the point it is at today; nobody likes every aspect of it, but "who's to say that Mother Nature is wrong for it being what it is today" — it is what it is, and we can change it through conscious choices. Fourth, societal and cultural information passes from grandparents to parents, who add the news and information of their own time, to you, and the cycle continues as you learn to operate in the world. Fifth, "what blows my mind is that this could be completely different". From that **default trajectory**, he concludes, "we were sent like a train hurling down the tracks and if we don't learn to conduct our own adventure then we may be heading towards a dead end". **Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`.

The default trajectory is related to, but not identical with, the "default path" that Chapter 2 analyzes (school, job, retirement). The trajectory is defined by the information absorbed in childhood; the path is the institutional route that information points you down. The train image adds something the other metaphors lack: momentum. A child's programming is not a static belief system but a direction already in motion, which is why changing it later feels less like changing one's mind and more like stopping a train.

The third step of the chain also deserves a note, because it shows a register that coexists with the critical one throughout the corpus. Alongside his sharp critiques of society, the author repeatedly takes a cosmic, accepting stance — Mother Nature is not "wrong" for being what it is. He never reconciles these two registers explicitly; the critical one motivates individual change, the accepting one removes blame and drama.

The drive to learn has an ambiguous name in the author's vocabulary: **curse of knowledge**. He uses the phrase in an idiosyncratic sense, not its usual one. In common usage the "curse of knowledge" refers to the difficulty experts have in imagining what it is like not to know something. For the author, it means "humans wanting to know more and learn more and learning being the foundation of the human experience" — the fact that as we age we learn new things over and over and "we don't question those things". In that use, it is the mechanism by which the conventional game is absorbed before we notice it. Later in the same compilation he reuses it in a neutral or even positive way: "humans, we have a natural desire to know more, the curse of knowledge, we have an endless thirst to learn things that will aid in our survival". **Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`. It is not clear whether "curse" is meant pejoratively, and the author never clarifies it. The most coherent reading is that the same drive serves both purposes: it is what conditions us, and it is also what lets us build systems of our own.

#### A new computer with eighteen years of code

The author's computing metaphors make the same point in a vocabulary his audience finds natural. "When you're born, your mind is like a new computer. There's a base operating system, but the hard drive is completely blank. For the first 20 years of your life, you do not think independently. And that's okay. Nobody does." Even the feeling of independence is, at that age, "just another form of conformity": "the little kid that thinks he's rebelling against his parents is just conforming to what his friends want him to do." **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md`. The **base operating system** is what comes with the hardware — the biological drives; everything else is installed.

A 2024 video describes how the installation goes wrong. As children we love adventure, discovery and figuring things out; we make real mistakes and learn from them — "we touch fire". Then learning stops being about real mistakes and becomes about traits parents and teachers dislike: being annoying, "uncivilized", not heading toward the version of success they were conditioned to believe is "the one true path". But "there is never only one true path", and the belief that there is one is precisely why you feel lost, overwhelmed, anxious and confused. The world changes. You are conditioned with the mind of parents from the last generation who, unless they were in the top 1%, probably did not get great results even then — advice for their generation, not yours. You feel lost because you know better — you learn online, you read books — and you are battling "18 years of code being wired into your brain", which is painful. "The path that was supposed to be safe and secure was the complete opposite." **Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`. The phrase **one true path** names the single version of success transmitted by conditioned adults; **18 years of code** names the programming that defends it.

In 2026 the author condenses this into a coined principle: "Conditioning is the enemy of wonder." We associate creativity with children "because children see the world through such fresh eyes. They haven't received the negative feedback yet from their parents or teachers or peers." His example is a child asking ChatGPT to build a teleportation device to go to Pluto: the response is "Oh, you're so silly"; if an adult did the same, "they'd think you're an idiot… You need to be more mature". The issue is "receiving negative feedback for thinking about imaginary things". And the feedback has a direction: "The typical feedback that people receive is just guidance down the default path… created during industrialization and past generations… you go to school and you receive grades so that you correct your behavior to fit into society more… the entire system is just meant to keep you on this one path turning everyone into the same person" — school, a high-paying job, "praise this God… if you disobey, you're going to hell". The result: "By the time most people turn 20 years old, they're the exact same as everyone else… going down the life path that was assigned to them, not the one they chose or created." The counter-principle anticipates Chapter 18: "Creativity requires holding beliefs loosely and entertaining an idea without immediately rejecting or demonizing it as everyone does on social media because it drives engagement and facilitates groupthink." **Source:** `If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md`.

The author also describes what the programming produces by the time a person is in their twenties. People are programmed young to rely on everyone but themselves: "the parents who are living in the past and out of touch with new opportunities, the schools that are funded by the system they train you into, the employers who benefit from you not seeking better options and will probably lay you off in the next 10 years, the society that distracts you with news, memes and other noise that controls your emotional well-being." "This is the perfect formula for an insecure future": "learn this, do that, take your exams, believe in this God, get this job." And: "By the time you start navigating your 20s your mind is a computer program that runs the same functions over and over again: wake up, go to work, put in minimal effort, pay the bills, scroll social media, wish for the past, worry about tomorrow and have brief glimpses of a future where your delusional sense of security will be threatened by AI automation or just the randomness of life." **Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`. The **delusional sense of security** is the coined name for the felt safety of the programmed path, which is exactly what makes it insecure.

Two further consequences of childhood programming appear in the corpus, both pointing toward the work of Part II. First, people want things handed to them because "they've been programmed to obey authority and take orders" and to be unable to act without permission. Overcoming that, the author says, is where identity work and the alter ego come in: getting rid of the **residual conditioning** from childhood. (The **alter ego**, a term he takes from Todd Herman and adapts, is for him a sub-identity that bridges who you are and who you need to become — a vehicle for identity change, not a mask; Chapter 4, section 4.5, develops it.) In the framework where he says it, this is why the third step is "Identity — who must you become?" **Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`. Second, even personality traits are products of the same process: "traits are programmed, they're trained, they're conditioned; you can develop any trait you want." "You're probably not an introvert, you're just in a culture that would lead to you thinking that you're an introvert." And, he adds, even if you are, entrepreneurship is a good path for an introvert, because most of the time you build in your room and talk to people online — acknowledging "of course there's nuance to that". **Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`. The claim that traits are fully trainable is the author's opinion, offered without evidence; its function in his system is to remove the excuse of a fixed nature.

#### Externally ordered consciousness

In 2022 the author describes the same childhood from a slightly different angle, using the vocabulary of "ordered consciousness" that Chapter 5 develops at length. From birth to about 18, through school, "our reality has been externally created": we are assigned things to read and to become conscious of. He immediately clarifies that this is "a blessing and a curse". School provides the fundamental aspects of reality that get everyone on the same page so that society can operate, and external conditioning is impossible to avoid. It is how you learn to survive: imitating, soaking in information, building complex systems of good and bad from negative feedback. His example is a child who touches a candle, is made conscious that it hurts, and tries something else; over time that system gets conditioned. The danger is that in the modern world it is easy to go through life unconsciously, constantly distracted, doing what is assigned, following "the ordered consciousness or the hierarchy of goals" that corporations, organizations and religion set out for you. **Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`.

**Externally ordered consciousness** (also "external hierarchy of goals") is therefore the author's name for the order and clarity provided by institutions rather than by oneself. The second phrase uses one of his technical terms. A **hierarchy of goals**, in his sense, is the structure of an end goal broken into sub-goals, checkpoints or "quests" around which every action revolves; it gives clarity and orders the mind, and it is what makes games addictive. An external hierarchy is one whose top was set by someone else. The term is introduced here only in passing: Chapter 2 (section 2.1) shows the default path as such a hierarchy, and Chapter 8 (section 8.1) develops how to build one of your own. **Complementary context:** the phrase "order in consciousness" is central to Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow*, a book the author cites elsewhere as a major influence; in these 2022 passages he uses the vocabulary without naming the source. The same is true of "hierarchy of goals": the term is the author's own usage, but it grows out of his reading of Csikszentmihalyi, for whom flow depends on clear goals that order attention, and he uses it in the same breath as "ordered consciousness". The concession that it is a blessing matters for reading his later and harsher critiques of school: the critique is aimed at an order that is never replaced by one's own, not at order as such.

In the same video he compares the present to the fifth century BC, a time of abundant ideas and information. Today people are questioning things; information and ideas are abundant; uncertainty and anxiety are present because people are losing trust in what originally ordered their consciousness and created clarity — corporate culture, which gave steps and clarity about what to show up and work on, and religion, which assigned values and beliefs that helped people make sense of reality and operate from a non-anxious, non-chaotic place. For some, this is frightening: "I have no one to tell me what to do." **Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`.

**Complementary context:** the fifth century BC in Greece was the period of the Sophists and Socrates, when traditional religious and civic values were openly questioned in Athens; it was also the broader era some historians associate with a wave of new philosophies across several civilizations. The author does not specify which features of the period he has in mind beyond the abundance of ideas and the loss of trust in traditional orders.

A few weeks later he draws the practical conclusion. Because people are losing trust in "the externally ordered consciousness or the external hierarchy of goals that people have set for us", which brought some clarity to life — external clarity, not internal clarity — and because there is so much information that it is easy to get lost in the weeds, "you have to actively construct your life". A religious organization, a corporation, your job or the school system can only teach and lead you so far and provide certainty for a limited time; even if you align with them, they are external, not your own. You need your own rules, values, vision and mission — your own lens. Other people can only give you perspectives, "lenses to see the world from", and "that is not your lens". "You have to create your own lens in order to operate in a conducive manner that will lead to beneficial outcomes in your life." This comes with time, practice, refinement and iteration. **Source:** `Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`. The distinction between **external clarity** (borrowed and temporary) and **internal clarity** (generated from one's own lens) is the seed of Part IV, where vision is defined precisely as a lens of one's own.

#### Swapped tribes: conditioning after childhood

Leaving home does not end conditioning; it changes its source. "In today's world it's even more difficult. You don't just absorb the values, beliefs, identity and worldview that your parents installed into you; you absorb the ideas of the hundreds of thousands of people you are exposed to on the internet over the course of a few months." The need for approval does not disappear either: "Just because you don't live with your parents or go to school anymore doesn't mean you don't want approval… You've just swapped tribes… you still want to be approved, but this time it's by the digital group you belong to." The author qualifies it: the need for approval and conformity "is necessary up to a certain point until you're on your own, but at that point that's not how you do anything great. You have to rewire your mind." **Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`.

In 2026 he makes the same point with more alarm. When you were young, people expected you to act, think and behave a certain way; your parents were influenced by theirs and by their culture and passed their worldview to you. Maybe you rebelled, but you could not escape the subtle programming of teachers and peers and, "even worse in today's world, social media". You could search any information and "program yourself into being a part of a tribe whose general actions aren't beneficial to a meaningful future". "Literally, the places that you hang out on the internet could dictate the entire outcome of your life, and you don't pay any attention to it." You let all of it go "unguarded" into your mind. **Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`.

The author reconstructs the whole sequence as a process in a 2026 video, presented as the first thing to understand if you want to recondition yourself. First, you were born into a family and a culture with specific values — indoctrinated even if not forced. Second, you went to a school with specific values, taught by teachers with specific values. Third, you were exposed to an absurd amount of information that may have shifted your values toward rebellion, laziness, victimhood or success; some people "magically" end up successful because they unknowingly surrounded themselves with the right people. Fourth, you got a phone, and conditioning increased exponentially thanks to social media and "our monkey brains not being able to control ourselves". He notes there is more to the process than these four stages. **Source:** `How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md`. The third stage contains an observation worth keeping: the same mechanism that produces "children of society" can, by accident of environment, produce success. Conditioning is not inherently toward failure; it is toward whatever the environment repeats.

Before smartphones were central to his argument, the author had already identified the effect of constant social exposure on originality. In a 2022 video he builds a chain. In earlier videos he argued that manual labor and time are not scalable and that robotic, specialized tasks are being automated "all over the place" — humans build systems to handle repetitive work. Therefore the future belongs to everyone, but "a good future, a lucrative future" belongs to people who can harness the creative power of their mind. But "if you're trying to leverage a mind that was created by the external world" — its thoughts, its beliefs, "a matrix" — "then you are going to be thinking and creating the same things as everyone else". So you must go inward to "tap into that infinite intelligence", because what is wanted is originality, personality and wisdom. Originality does not come from accepting what everyone tells you, taking everyone's advice and lacking "a shred of self-reliance". Visionary ideas come from intrinsic goals: pursuing what you really want, creating a vision out of it and leading others toward it. **Source:** `Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md`.

In the same video he names the environment that produces the externally created mind: social media can lock you into a "socialization chamber", and "socialization makes you stupid". The point is not to avoid socialization but to strike a balance — and social media in any capacity, he says, is not a balance. You need time alone; "that's how you be yourself". You cannot be yourself if you are constantly overexposed to other people's opinions, especially at the mass scale social media creates: "You can't be yourself if you're never alone with yourself." **Source:** `Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md`. The consequence he draws is economic as well as personal. **Over-socialization** makes your thoughts, writing, ideas and work unoriginal, and therefore you get the same results as everyone else — who are in the rat race, "whether a corporate rat race or a social media one-person business rat race". Hence the advice: "Don't worry about your niche, don't worry about how well your topic's performing, prioritize developing your mind." **Source:** `Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md`. "Rat race" is used in a slightly special sense here: not only the corporate treadmill, but any game in which everyone produces the same thing and gets the same results — including the online creator economy the author himself teaches. That self-inclusion matters; in the same video he concedes he may himself be conditioned by the medium.

The author turns this observation into a short exercise in self-awareness, presented in a video about original thinking. Step back and use your own awareness — "the power of self-awareness in marketing, in business, is the most powerful marketing strategy of all". "Think of who you follow. You follow 100, 200, 300, 400 people. Out of those people, how many (1) talk about the same topics and (2) have you heard the same idea articulated in a different way from their own point of view? Do you see those two things and say 'oh, this guy copied this guy', or do you see each of them as their own unique opinion?" And then: "what's stopping you from doing that?" **Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`. The exercise makes the swapped tribe visible from the inside: the people you follow are already repeating each other, and you experience them as original. Section 1.4 develops what follows from this — that imitation is unavoidable and the only question is whether it is conscious.

#### Copycat by design: conditioning creates the self

All of these strands converge on a claim about the self, which the author states as early as 2022: "you are a copycat by design, right? How else would you survive?" From the moment you were born you copied other people; your parents taught you to eat, to walk, to use the bathroom. He asks the viewer to internalize the concept as a whole — imitation, copying, cultural conditioning, other people's opinions projected onto you, and your unconscious or subconscious absorption of information from whatever stimuli you are exposed to — because this is what forms your identity, your beliefs and much else. And these things "can cause a lot of pain if you attach to them, if they are something you need". **Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`.

In 2023 he makes the conclusion explicit. What creates the self "is your conditioning": the ideas, beliefs, skills, education, religious doctrine — "literally anything that you can consume is soaked into your mind", especially as a child, when it happens unconsciously and you are a sponge. Millions of bits of information accumulate over time and create who you are. The consequence is uncomfortable: "you have much less self-control than you think", because so much is firing in your brain behind every action. At any moment you could make millions or billions of choices, but your conditioning limits you "to a select few because of how you perceive life". Therefore "destiny, or just the default path, or where you're going to end up, is drastically influenced by your identity" — which is why, he says, you need to build what he calls your mental body (the subject of Chapter 3). **Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`.

The same claim appears in more operational form in 2024: "your personality or identity are created by the information that you're repeatedly exposed to and that you deem important and worth accepting into your mind." He makes it in a discussion of niches: most people do not realize they already belong to a very specific niche, because they already follow a broad set of people who talk about various interests, and that mix is what created their current identity. **Source:** `Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md`. Note the added condition: not all information, but information repeatedly encountered *and* judged important. That condition is what leaves room for choice — what you deem worth accepting is the lever Chapter 4 pulls.

From here follows a reply to an obvious objection. "What about taking action?" You already act every day, the author answers, because your mind is programmed with information conducive to those actions. "Your identity is forged by the information you've been fed and that alone determines what you perceive as an opportunity to act." If you surround yourself with the right people, you can achieve anything. **Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`. The point inverts the usual advice: a lack of action is not a lack of willpower but a lack of perceived opportunities, and what is perceived as an opportunity is set by the programmed identity.

The author's preferred external support for this claim comes from Maxwell Maltz. As the author quotes him, it does not matter how you got an idea or where it came from — yourself, your teachers, your parents, your friends, advertisements — if you have accepted it and are firmly convinced it is true, "it has the same power over you as the hypnotist's words have over a hypnotized subject". The author uses the quotation to open what he calls his "anatomy of identity", the account of how accepted ideas shape identity and action. **Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`.

**Complementary context:** Maxwell Maltz (1899–1975) was a plastic surgeon who wrote *Psycho-Cybernetics* (1960), arguing that a person's self-image governs behavior and that the mind works like a goal-seeking servomechanism. The author draws on him repeatedly in Parts II and III; he has said he read only the first chapter of the book.

Finally, the author turns the whole diagnosis into the first step of a method. In a 2025 framework for "engineering an identity", step one is recognition. "Who you are determines what you consider important. You must first recognize that you are pursuing a series of goals whether you are conscious of them or not. Those goals frame how you interpret reality, and you usually notice things that confirm your current way of life." The goals stem from conditioning, and conditioning stems from environment — parents, teachers, peers, culture, "the goals that society gave you when you were a child". Somewhere between 15 and 18 you can begin to ask: "Why am I on this life trajectory? Did I even choose any of this?" "And the answer is probably no, unless your parents were extremely high agency and taught you how to navigate your teachers and peers while you were young." **Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`.

The chain environment → conditioning → goals → interpretation → confirmation is the bridge to the next section. Up to here, conditioning has been described as the installation of beliefs. The author's deeper claim is that what gets installed are, above all, goals — and that goals are what actually run a life.

### 1.3 You Are Always Pursuing Goals: Assigned or Self-Generated

#### The thesis: every action serves a goal

The previous section ended with a chain: environment produces conditioning, conditioning produces goals, goals frame how reality is interpreted. This section makes the middle term explicit, because it is the idea on which the whole book turns. The author states it in many forms, and the simplest is this: "you're working towards goals with every single action you take; much of the time that's just unconscious." **Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`.

His illustration is a comparison of two people looking at the same behavior. A gamer can sit in front of a screen eight to ten hours a day "and like their life". A bodybuilder "would see that as a living hell", because they have studied bodybuilding and nutrition enough to know it is "the worst thing that they could do for their health"; if their goal is health and bodybuilding, "they're not going to do that because it's not their goal." The same hours mean opposite things depending on the goal they are measured against. The prescription follows directly: when you make your goals conscious and invest energy into a new goal for who you want to become, "it's going to be difficult at first, but you're eventually going to create the identity that you want to create, and those actions are going to come naturally." **Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`. The claim that actions eventually "come naturally" once the goal is internalized is the root of the author's view of discipline as a trait of identity, developed in Chapter 3.

If every action points toward a goal, then results reveal goals — including goals no one would admit to. In a 2023 video the author describes looking at society with this reasoning: "it seemed like everyone was eager to be overweight, unhealthy, overworked and broke." Not, he immediately clarifies, because anyone wanted those outcomes, but because they "didn't have self-generated goals that shaped their decision-making". Without self-generated goals, unconscious default goals produce default outcomes. His examples are mundane — the grease-leaking breakfast sandwich and the dessert drink — and pointed: why do people return every day to a job that "clearly caused them immense suffering", and how do they fail to see the opportunity of the digital world "unfolding right before their eyes"? **Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`. The reasoning is an inference from behavior to goal, and it is the author's interpretive move, not an empirical finding; its value is that it reframes failure as the successful pursuit of a goal nobody chose.

The same thesis appears without a vision attached: "every single one of your actions are meaningless" if you have no vision, and you view life "from a lens of unconscious goals because everything is goal oriented". If you do not have your own goals, you operate from those society projected onto you — a better short-term job, quick superficial sex, "every other low conscious problem" people focus on that never gets them anywhere. The term **low conscious problem** names the short-term problems society proposes. The author is careful not to dismiss them: you do have to solve them, but from a long-term standpoint. Even if all you want is sex, he says, expose yourself to the experience and fail a few times — "failures are the foundation for your quality of life" — learn by exposure to reality, "don't just sit around mentally masturbating over things", and channel the lessons of failure toward your goals. **Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`. Here two ideas that will return often appear together: goals give meaning to action, and failure in contact with reality is how one learns what goals are worth having.

#### The social matrix of goals

The most radical version of the thesis concerns the smallest actions. "You are already acting toward layers of unconscious goals every second of the day; they've been conditioned to the point of not requiring conscious thought." You wake up, walk, brush your teeth, "put on your clothes so that you aren't seen as an outcast" — all skills developed "with the goal of fitting in and surviving in society". Biological goals clearly influence this, the author grants, but much of it is social, and he makes the point with deliberate absurdity: "The social matrix of goals creates humans that all operate in a similar fashion. With a slightly different goal of society we could all be walking around naked and communicating with each other by tapping people on the head with a stick." **Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`.

The term **social matrix of goals** connects this section to section 1.1: the web of expectations is, seen from the side of action, a hierarchy of nested unconscious goals. Later in the same video the author repeats his neutral verdict — the social matrix "isn't a bad thing, it creates an intellectual structure to anchor our attention so that we can actually survive here" — and then poses the real question: "are you just surviving or are you creating?" The distinction between surviving and creating is the first appearance of the book's title idea.

#### Society as a behavior system and the three big goals

The author compresses the social matrix of goals into a short formula he repeats between 2023 and 2024. "Society is a behavior system", and right when you learn to comprehend your language it injects **three big goals** into your mind: go to school, get a job, retire — at whatever age you can, "at an old age when you don't really have much time to actually use the rewards". "99% of people" interpret everyday situations only through the lens of those goals and are "practicing the skills and programming their minds to live a mediocre life without even knowing it". The masses are "shepherded to an unfulfilling life", because the systems that compose their mind, identity, perspective and perception become more efficient as they age. **Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`. The last clause is a quiet but important mechanism: conditioning does not just persist with age, it improves — the program gets better at running itself.

A 2024 version adds a cybernetic frame and a new consequence. "Your mind is a cybernetic system. Society is a behavior system, and there are three big goals that they injected into your mind right when you learn to comprehend the language you speak: one, school; two, job; three, retire." Because 99% interpret everyday situations in ways that lead to those goals, they study what they are told for status and survival and close their minds to what really matters. "They become a specialist slave who's dependent on those who need that specific knowledge; they don't have the deep generalized skill set that allows them to pursue their own path." His example: "A writer who only learns writing doesn't have a chance at making an independent income." They program their minds "to live a mediocre life without even knowing it because they failed to question and revolt." **Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`. The **specialist slave** — dependent on whoever needs one narrow kind of knowledge — anticipates the case for the generalist in Chapter 20, and the cybernetic frame anticipates Chapter 6.

The author also shows how the three goals operate as a lens in a single conversation. If your goal is school, job and retirement at 65, you will interpret conversations through that lens even when they are not about those things. Talking with one of your parents' friends, you will be thinking: "what are they doing for work, do I want to end up like that, what did they do for schooling, what major did they go through, how much do they make, do they seem like they enjoy their life?" That interpretation shapes your actions in pursuit of the goal — "but you're probably not going to leave that goal unless they challenge those beliefs of yours." **Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`. The example makes concrete the claim that the goal decides what is noticed: the conversation is not experienced as it is, but as information relevant to an assigned plan.

#### Assignments

The author has a coined term for any goal or path received from outside: an **assignment**. "As we are growing up, as we are going to school, as we are getting a job, we are given assignments; this is what I call something that is assigned to you: you don't question and you continue doing it." The definition has two criteria — the thing comes from outside and it is not questioned — and both are needed. The consequence is cumulative: "before you know it you have so many responsibilities that you can't really do anything with your life; all of your time is taken up to the point where you don't have the money, time or resources to create a better life for yourself", and it becomes "so much more difficult to actually change". From this he derives a principle stated in the same video: "There is absolutely no set path that someone can give you to achieve this, to get what you want, to forge your own path, or else that's an assignment: it was assigned to you, it wasn't created by you." **Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`.

The principle creates an obvious tension. The author himself teaches step-by-step methods, sells courses and recommends learning from people who have done what you want to do. If any given path is an assignment, what is his own teaching? He addresses the tension in the same video, and his answer is the concept of the stepping stone: "I see assignments as a stepping stone, so meaning I see schools and jobs as a stepping stone; I don't see them as something to avoid or something that's absolutely evil." He notes that people often attribute the opposite position to him. What he promotes is entrepreneurship, forging your own path, diving into the unknown, solving problems, new experiences, and "not getting locked in a repetitive day-to-day mindless routine that comes from schools and jobs". Advice and tools from others are useful for direction, "but inevitably you're going to reach whatever that destination was and it's not going to be as nice as you wanted it to, so you have to course correct, learn from your mistakes and start to pursue your own path. You have to eventually take the training wheels off." **Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`.

The resolution is partial, and it is fair to say so. Across the corpus the rejection of any given path coexists with the author's own step-by-step instruction; his recurring reconciliation is that prescriptions are valuable to a beginner and should then give way to experimentation and a form of one's own — the given path as temporary scaffolding, not destination. His attitude toward employment shows the same oscillation, between "the life of the masses" and "a stepping stone", within single videos; that tension is examined in Chapter 2.

The same video draws the most practical consequence of the concept of assignment, one that answers a question many readers bring to this kind of book — "I don't know what I want." "You don't know what you want out of life because you haven't tried anything outside of what's been assigned to you; you haven't experimented with new domains, you haven't learned outside of high school or college, you haven't dug into a lesser explored crevice of reality that isn't taught in schools." So "it's no wonder you don't know what you want": you have not exposed yourself to the thing that would make you realize it. **Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`. The **crevice of reality** — the less-explored corner of reality that intrigues you — is a term the author uses often; here it names what lies outside the assigned curriculum. Not knowing what you want, on this account, is not a personal flaw but the predictable result of a life composed entirely of assignments. Chapter 7 develops the corollary that one never knows exactly what one wants and discovers it by exposure.

#### Goal-oriented creatures and the assigned identity

Why does the absence of chosen goals not simply produce an absence of goals? Because, the author argues, humans cannot stop pursuing goals. The passage appears in a 2023 video and reappears almost verbatim in a 2024 compilation: "If you aren't working towards your ideal future you're working towards someone else's, because either way we are goal-oriented creatures, and if you do not create your own goal and start to invest energy in that identity, you're going to invest energy into the identity that is assigned to you — like being a college student or having a corporate job title or being a retired-at-65-years-old dude — and your actions are going to follow to survive that belief, whether you are conscious of it or want to frame it as this whole conceptual survival thing or not." **Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`; also `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`.

The phrase **goal-oriented creatures** is the anthropological premise, and the **assigned identity** is its consequence: the energy that would have gone into a chosen identity flows instead into whichever identity was given. The mention of "conceptual survival" points ahead to Chapter 3, where the author's law of conceptual survival explains why actions then organize themselves to protect that identity. Here it suffices to see the structure: goal → identity → actions that defend the identity.

In 2025 he states the identity consequence in its sharpest form: "since those goals are not your own and you didn't think them through, the information that is programming your mind is creating an identity that you didn't create, because you didn't create the goals that your mind operates on." **Source:** `How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md`. This is the precise sense in which a person can be "created" rather than creating: not that they lack an identity, but that the identity they have was assembled from goals they never chose.

The author identifies what makes assigned goals so attractive: they come with clarity. "A huge problem is that people just don't have an intrinsic goal that they're trying to achieve; they go and they adopt the goal of someone else because it's clarity that someone else has created, like a job." They give mental energy to it and align their work with it, and eventually "they're so deep in this meta system called society that their attention is just enslaved in that and they don't have any time to go and create something for themselves". **Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`. This ties back to externally ordered consciousness (section 1.2): a job is borrowed clarity, and borrowed clarity is the price at which attention is sold.

A related observation appears in a 2023 video about feeling lost. "Stress can be very good for pushing you towards certain goals", but in the state of not being able to think clearly — which he estimates, rhetorically, at "99% of the population" — "most people adopt goals that they are told to adopt, that are assigned to them", and get on what he calls a **hamster wheel**. They pursue a goal (a high-paying job, say — "there's nothing wrong with that"), but not through their own conscious reasoning; they reach it, ask "what's next", choose another goal; "none of these are self-generated, none of these are actually what they want to do". They are "slowly digging themselves further into a hole rather than being able to climb upwards so they can see the past mistakes". **Source:** `If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md`. The hamster wheel is the chained version of assignment: one assigned goal leads to the next, so the absence of choice never becomes visible as a single decision.

In 2025 the author restates the problem as a matter of which game is being played. "Most people are pursuing the unconscious goals that their parents and society put into their head. They are playing the game of being a useful worker rather than a free and fulfilled individual." The consequence explains a common failure: people try to play a new game — starting a business, losing weight — "with the programmed mind of an employee or overweight person and wonder why they fail". The conclusion has two conditions, and both are required: "To achieve anything in life you must become the person who both sees that as a possibility and has the skill to achieve it." **Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`. The pairing of **useful worker** and **free and fulfilled individual** names two games installed by different sources; the requirement of both perception and skill anticipates the two halves of the book's practical layer — changing identity (Parts II–IV) and building skill (Parts VI–VIII).

The author applies the same diagnosis to his own audience, which gives it a self-critical edge. Someone who starts a business because "you've been assigned a goal of like 'oh I need to make money for survival so I'm going to start this potential business opportunity' that says 'oh Dan says I can make a million dollars'" is still pursuing an assigned goal — assigned now by a creator rather than by an employer. "If you don't have a vision, then why are you doing this?" It is "completely up to you", it "is going to take a few years", and "many people are watching this video for the wrong reasons, and, I hate to break it to you, unless you wake up it's not going to work." **Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`. The point is that "assigned" refers to the relation between a person and their goal, not to the content of the goal: entrepreneurship can be as assigned as a job.

#### Why the chosen goal decides what you learn

The author's most rigorous argument for why goals matter runs through learning. It appears in a 2024 video as a four-step chain. First, "a goal implies knowledge and skills that must be learned to solve problems that prevent you from achieving the goal." Second, "if you don't choose your own goal, you do not choose what you learn or what problems you solve." Third, "your mind remains narrow by default because the goal is narrow." Fourth, "your destiny is decided for you because the only potential you know is the one you were assigned." **Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`.

He develops the same chain in a video built on an interview with the novelist Devon Eriksen. Eriksen's point was that "a free man is expected to act on his interests and do many things throughout his life". The author connects it to a sentence from his own book: "if you don't create a purpose you will be assigned one" (sometimes "if you don't choose a purpose"). A purpose, he says, is a goal, and most people do not set and pursue their own. Goals are everything — they are how you have survived since birth — and you were given them by parents, teachers and employers, "and that's usually it" for people who cannot zoom out to choose long-term goals. Then the chain, in more detail: a goal implies learning the knowledge and skills needed to achieve it; assigned goals mean you learn only what those goals require; you explore nothing outside them; you take the same path others took; you get the results others have; and 99% are not satisfied with those results. **Source:** `The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md`. The idea of the free man acting on many interests is Eriksen's; the link to assigned purpose and the learning chain are the author's adaptation.

**Complementary context:** Devon Eriksen is a science-fiction novelist (*Theft of Fire*) and commentator who appeared in a long conversation with the author in 2024; several ideas in the corpus about education, agency and identity come from that conversation and are attributed to him where they appear.

From the same period comes a principle that explains why assigned goals become economically dangerous: "self-generated goals evolve when achieved; assigned goals stay the same and the modes of achieving them become efficient with time and get replaced by technology." **Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`. The mechanism is precise. A goal you generated changes once you reach it, because reaching it changes you and reveals new problems. A goal assigned to a role stays fixed — the same report, the same process — so all the improvement goes into its means, and an optimized, fixed means is exactly what a machine can take over. The principle links this chapter to the book's later argument about AI (Chapter 36): replaceability is a property of static, assigned goals.

#### The mind as an operating system made of goals

In 2024 the author gives the thesis its most compact architecture. "Goals determine how you see the world; goals determine what you consider success or failure." "You can try to enjoy the journey, but if you pursue the wrong goal you are making it 100 times more difficult." "Your mind is the operating system for reality; that system is composed of goals. For most people those goals are assigned to them, programmed like lines of code in their psyche: go to school, get the job, get offended, play victim, retire at 65 — a known path that doesn't work." The prescription: "to become more intelligent you must reject the known path, dive into the unknown, set new higher goals to expand your mind, embrace the chaos and allow for growth, study the generalized principles of nature and become a deep generalist." **Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`. The last phrase is not the author's coinage. **Deep generalist** comes from the social philosopher Daniel Schmachtenberger (whose "epistemic commons" reappears in section 1.5); the author adopts it and turns it into what he presents as a future-proof identity: someone who studies the general principles of nature — patterns that hold across domains — instead of locking the mind into one specialty. Chapter 20 develops the generalist at length.

The list of programmed lines is worth reading closely. Alongside the institutional goals (school, job, retirement) it includes emotional ones — "get offended, play victim". These are presented as goals in the same sense: patterns the mind pursues because they were installed and rewarded, not because they were chosen. The **operating system for reality** metaphor thus extends the computer of section 1.2: the base operating system came with birth; the programs it runs are goals.

In December 2025 the author repeats the passage almost word for word, but frames it "from the lens of teleology or the Greek cosmos with a K": everything serves a purpose as part of a greater whole. The reformulation changes one significant word. In 2024 the wrong goal makes things "100 times more difficult"; in 2025, "if you pursue the wrong goal, you will not enjoy it." **Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`. The thesis is the same; what is added is an explicit philosophical frame and a shift of emphasis from difficulty to enjoyment. The author does not explain the change.

**Complementary context:** teleology (from Greek *telos*, end or purpose) is the explanation of things by the ends they serve; Aristotle's "final cause" is its classical form. *Kosmos* in Greek means an ordered whole, and the author spells it "with a K" to point to that sense rather than to physical outer space. Chapter 3 returns to teleology when the identity itself is described as a network of goals, including the hidden goal of staying the same.

The prescription "embrace the chaos" also marks a shift the author makes in this period, from seeing chaos as an enemy to seeing it as the precondition of reorganization at a higher level; that change belongs to Chapter 5.

The author also offers a positive counterpart to the operating-system thesis, from a 2022 video. When your goals — "the ones that you create" — align with who you really are deep down, "the unconscious sends resources to help fuel that lifestyle that you are trying to build". The condition is that the goals are self-created, intrinsic, not adopted from a company, school or culture. He offers several ways of saying it — "what you truly enjoy", "what aligns with you the most", "what speaks to you the most" — and says he prefers to keep an open mind toward "the spiritual way of saying these things" instead of dismissing it as "hippie or new age", because the only way to connect it to something you truly understand is to have an open mind to everything, not only to what he says. **Source:** `Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md`. The coined phrase **when the external aligns with the internal** names this condition. Its status is that of a principle stated from experience and openly framed in quasi-spiritual language; the author does not offer a mechanism for how "the unconscious sends resources", and the reader should not take it as one.

#### Three options under psychological insecurity

What happens to a person on an assigned path when the path stops providing meaning? In a 2024 video the author lays out the options as a framework. For the 99% in careers or life directions "that lead to this psychological insecurity that you think is security", there are only three. One: "focus on the predictable future and get anxious, so you numb your mind with distractions." Two: "focus on the familiar past and get bored, so again you numb your mind with distractions." Three: "set a new goal so you can finally take control of your life and start navigating the unknown, the only place you can learn, grow and expand." And the rule that summarizes it: "If you don't have a goal, the only option is to get distracted." **Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`.

| Orientation | What the mind fixes on | Resulting state | Typical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predictable future | What the assigned path will bring | Anxiety | Distraction to numb it |
| Familiar past | What has already been lived | Boredom | Distraction to numb it |
| New goal | The unknown that a chosen goal opens | Learning, growth, expansion | Navigation of the unknown |

The framework is compact but carries a strong thesis: distraction is not a character flaw but the only available response of a mind without a goal of its own. The term **psychological insecurity** names the felt security of the career that in fact produces anxiety or boredom. Chapter 2 develops the relationship between the predictable future and the familiar past as "psychological time"; Chapter 11 develops distraction. Here the point is that goallessness has a predictable phenomenology.

#### Chosen problems and the axis of suffering

The author extends the distinction between assigned and chosen from goals to problems, and from problems to suffering. "Realize that work is a necessary part of life; work is about solving problems, so if you don't work your life is going to be pretty problematic." "Humans love to solve problems, but not just any problems: problems that they deem meaningful and the ones that they choose." And then the key contrast: "The right problems narrow our attention and allow us to forget our worries; the wrong problems enslave our attention and amplify our worries. The difference between the two is that one is chosen and the other is assigned." Finally: "Problems are the limits on your mind and potential; once solved they allow for growth, expansion and evolution." **Source:** `You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md`.

The contrast between **chosen and assigned problems** is psychologically precise. Both kinds of problem occupy attention. A chosen problem narrows attention in the way absorption does — it pushes worries out. An assigned problem occupies attention without absorbing it, so worries remain alongside it and grow. The idea anticipates the treatment of flow in Chapter 10.

In a 2024 video he pushes the same logic to suffering itself: "The goals are the axis of your suffering, and you get to choose which goal you want to pursue. Most people have goals that are assigned to them, and that's why they suffer and they can't escape it. But when you choose the problem you want to solve, that leads to meaningful living in challenge and purpose in life; there's going to be suffering along with that, but it's worth suffering through. That's a good sign that you're on the right path: that you're willing to take the suffering that comes from pursuing the goal that you chose." **Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`. The **axis of your suffering** does not promise a life without suffering; it relocates the choice. Suffering is fixed; what varies is whether it is the suffering of an assigned goal one cannot escape or of a chosen goal one is willing to bear. The willingness itself becomes a diagnostic signal of the right path.

A 2025 video states the underlying value judgment plainly. Conformity is the default state, and "we don't want conformity, we want creativity" — to be different, unique, to create our own lives and make new discoveries instead of repeating past generations' results, because most people around you do not have anything close to the future you want. Goals, he concludes, "can trap us or help us". **Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`. The sentence is the shortest summary of this section: goals are the mechanism of both the trap and the exit.

The verdict on conformity, however, is not the author's last word, and the change should be recorded here because this is where the reader first meets it. In early 2025 conformity is the enemy without qualification: "the problem is that we conform, we copy other people, we lack vision for a future and the agency to make it a reality" (**Source:** `How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md`, 2025-02-23). In December 2025, in a video built around Krishnamurti's claim that only someone "in constant revolt" discovers what is true, he turns the critique on himself: "we're all conformist in some ways", and since conformity is a survival strategy, "it can be used like a tool" — he admits that he conforms in order to stay relevant on YouTube (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md`, 2025-12-21). The move is from condemning conformity to accepting it as a conscious survival tool. The reason he gives is the same survival logic this chapter describes; both positions can stand if one distinguishes the unconscious conformity that defines an identity, which remains the problem, from conformity chosen strategically, which is instrumental. The author does not draw that distinction himself in so many words. Chapter 35 develops the later position.

#### The consequence: a purpose crisis

The author describes the lived state produced by assigned goals in several videos, and the descriptions are best read together. In 2024 he addresses the viewer directly: "you got distracted, you lost the battle, you gave up your dreams and sought security in someone else's." You did not pay attention to the path you were on: you went to school, got the job, "and now what?" Your mind is occupied with bills, work you do not care about, student loans "pushed under the rug" and stacking up, the lack of time or energy to be healthy, to start the business, to give life your all. Those thoughts split into a thousand more and walk you into a "narrow-minded state of stress and survival": life loses its zest, days become robotic, opportunities become scarce. **Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`.

At the scale of society he calls the same state a **purpose crisis**. "The big problem with today's society seems to be that we just can't get the work done." "We're in the middle of a purpose crisis": we lack meaning in our careers but do not know what to do; tasks are repetitive and endless "because that's all we're skilled to do"; distractions everywhere pull us from what is important; and, worst of all, "we lack clarity on what we want out of life". This, he says, is why dopamine has become such a popular subject — and he ties the two themes of the video together explicitly: dopamine "plays a crucial role in flow but goes beyond" it, so the popular fixation on dopamine is a symptom of the same crisis that a better understanding of flow is meant to answer. Many are stuck in **narrow-minded living**: go to school, get a job, scroll phones, chase material objects, never experiencing life's depth. Against this he sets the promise of the video — finding enjoyment even in the boring, mundane or terrible parts of life, using flow to achieve meaningful goals "in 6 months rather than 6 years", discovering one's life's work — and a principle: "life enjoyment isn't dictated by randomness or luck, it's created by deconstructing life as the game it is and learning how to play." **Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`. The game metaphor is developed in Chapter 10; here it signals that the exit from the purpose crisis is not more effort inside the assigned game but a different relation to the game itself.

From inside, the crisis feels like a flooded mind. "Everyone is stressed out of their minds. We all want to achieve big things, but it is becoming infinitely more difficult to focus on those big things." The mind is flooded with "the risk of pursuing your own goals instead of the ones your parents or friends or partner or boss want for you"; with being told what to care about, which skills to learn, which countries to support, which foods to avoid, "by people with goals that don't align with your own"; with thoughts about the job you have to show up to, the time wasted, what you will not be able to do, and "what people will think if you decide to go your own way". If friends or parents do not support you, or even try to stop you, it is "incredibly painful": you may lose people from your life and "a part of your identity". **Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`. This passage names the cost that keeps people on assigned paths: choosing one's own goals is experienced as a risk to belonging, and therefore to identity — which is exactly what the survival logic of section 1.2 would predict.

The earliest version of the diagnosis (2022) describes the suppressed alternative. If everyone from age 18 learned to harness their curiosity or intuition — "that calling or that force in you that you suppress" — things would be different. Instead people say: I'm supposed to go to work, I'm tired after work, I don't have time to read, to learn, to go to the gym, to develop myself, or to open new room in the unknown to expand my mind. That, he says, is the life led by "people that are externally conditioned and unconscious their entire lives". **Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`. "I don't have time" is, in this reading, the verbal form that a suppressed calling takes in an externally conditioned life.

The author adds a figure to convey the emotional load: humans average 60,000 to 80,000 thoughts a day, and for every thought of a better life there are 100 negative ones beating you back down. **Source:** `You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md`. He cites no source for either number, and the ratio is plainly rhetorical. It should be read as an image of how outnumbered a new intention is by the habitual thoughts of the old program, not as a measurement.

#### If you don't know what you want, you will be told

The section's thesis has a one-sentence form that the author takes from his own book *The Art of Focus* and describes as the shortest illustration of what he calls Nature's Compass: "If you don't know what you want, you will be told what you want, and you will believe it." Growing up, going to school, getting a job, we are given assignments; if you do not generate your own wants, the defaults handed to you fill the space, and you take them as your own. **Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`. The final clause — "and you will believe it" — is what makes the sentence a diagnosis rather than a warning: assigned wants are not experienced as assigned.

The same video names the way out. "The path out of meaningless living is already available to you; it is deep in your core; it is something that we all have access to, and it is called Nature's Compass." What is it? "Experimentation doesn't stop… it's Nature's Compass, it's experimentation." The video's whole method — experimenting inward, outward, downward and upward — is the operation of this compass, and it points toward "what their nature really wants" as opposed to what the ego wants. **Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`. **Nature's Compass** is a coined term with several glosses across the corpus — trial and error with negative feedback that orients you toward a goal, mistakes as light in the dark — but its core is constant: direction is found by experimenting and reading the feedback, not by receiving a plan. The four directions of experimentation belong to Chapter 7.

In a later video the author makes the method explicit. You need your own goals, not assigned ones: "if you don't create a purpose, you will be assigned one", and only self-created goals keep you from being susceptible to the goals society tries to assign "at every waking second". Get absolutely fed up with where you are, reject everything you thought true, and start from scratch. How do you figure out what you want? "It's trial and error" — mistakes give direction. If you latch onto someone else's prescription, you expect their results but often will not get them, unless the results are mediocre and easy, like a job or a degree that everyone gets, which is not fulfilling precisely because you are not unique in it. You already know how to pave the way, he concludes; you are just afraid to start and enter the unknown. **Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`.

A 2024 video adds the distinction that completes the picture: **discovered versus assigned** — or chosen versus assigned. Life passes you by because you are exposed to the same information every day, and it has not changed much since you achieved society's goals. When you start a new path, things compound: your conversations with friends shift; your search history goes from "something you probably shouldn't be searching" to "best business models of 2024"; you get "a little taste of what life could be". You will probably fail the first and second time, "but your potential will always be there; even if you quit, it won't stop nagging you; there's no going back." Why? Because the goals your mind operates on have fundamentally changed — from what parents, teachers and society assigned to something you chose through discovery and reading. "That's the entire difference: discovered versus assigned, or chosen versus assigned. But you have to discover it first in order to choose it." So you need a new goal and the self-education to reach it, and "that simple process alone will be responsible for much of the joy in your life". He closes with a definition that Chapter 2 develops: "True education is about curiosity and discovery, not memorization and conformity." **Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`.

The sentence "you have to discover it first in order to choose it" resolves an apparent paradox in the section. The author demands self-generated goals but admits people do not know what they want. The two claims fit because choice presupposes exposure: one cannot choose a goal one has never encountered, so the first act of self-generation is not choosing but exploring. Here the section reaches its limit. If goals can only be chosen from what one has been exposed to, and exposure comes largely from other people, then the self-generated goal is always built partly from borrowed material. The next section takes up this problem directly: imitation cannot be avoided, and the only question is whether it is done consciously.

### 1.4 Imitation Is Inevitable: NPC or Main Character

#### Create or be created

The book's title comes from a phrase the author first uses in a December 2022 video about turning his life into a video game: "We're in a world where it's create or be created." The argument behind it runs as follows. If you cannot mold your mind to create your own reality — your own game, one you enjoy playing — "your mind will be molded, and it already has been molded, to play external status games that society has created for you": going to college, getting a job, or any other structured approach someone else laid out. These may not fit you, and they lead to "unnecessary suffering and pain", because you are following steps someone else presented that may not align with your own goals. Why do most people accept them? Because the alternative — what he calls **sovereign living** or **autonomous living**, building your own way of surviving — is difficult and uncertain, and "the mind craves order, it craves certainty". Wherever there is an easier, more certain, secure path, "that's where 99% of people flock". He says the video's purpose is awareness for those who share his values: to build their own thing, be independent, "break free of the Matrix if you want to frame it that way", and live enjoyably on their own terms. **Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`; the passage reappears almost verbatim in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`.

Three ideas are packed into this argument. **Create or be created** is a disjunction without a third option: a mind that does not create its own game is not left neutral, it is already shaped for someone else's. **External status games** — college, job, the structures society designs — are the games it is shaped for. And the reason people choose them is not stupidity but a built-in preference of the mind for order and certainty, a preference the author treats in Part III under the heading of entropy. The hedge "if you want to frame it that way" is also worth noticing: as section 1.1 showed, already in 2022 he was distancing himself from the literal language of escape.

He restates the disjunction in 2024 as a principle about the impossibility of neutrality: "there's no such thing as doing nothing with life; there's only doing what you want to do or doing what others want you to do, and if you choose the latter your life becomes more and more mechanical until it's near impossible to escape." The solution he proposes is a set of imperatives: "become independent, think for yourself, earn for yourself, dive into the unknown, create your own security, revolt against all conventional paths in life, as that is the only way to allow room for truth." **Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`. The phrase **create your own security** inverts the conventional promise of the assigned path, whose security section 1.2 described as delusional. The image of life becoming "more and more mechanical" — the author's **mechanical living** — names the endpoint of the assigned path: not failure, but a life that runs without its owner.

He applies the same logic to economics. "Everyone has a product nowadays" because it is a requirement "if you don't want to be assigned one". People who say selling is sleazy do not understand sales: "you are selling me your ideas right now to reproduce and survive your narrow identity." His example is a comment left under one of Chris Williamson's posts announcing a new productivity drink, complaining that "everyone is selling a product nowadays": the commenter, he observes, almost certainly works for someone who sells a product, and so is selling that person's product for them, directly or indirectly. By doing nothing, he argues, you allow people "less conscious and intelligent than you" to have more attention, impact and money, and you become "a puppet to society", assigned an unconscious life of selling others' products without realizing it. **Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`. The distinction between **assigned and created products** is the economic face of create or be created: an employee sells, too — just someone else's product. The point anticipates Part X, where the one-person business is presented as the way to own the product one sells.

#### Two paths from the same imitation

If conditioning creates the self, and the self is "a copycat by design" (section 1.2), then imitation is not something one can opt out of. The author's 2023 video on NPCs starts exactly here: "You have been copying people since you were born; that's how you learn how to operate within society." That copying can lead down two paths — the path of the NPC (non-player character) and the path of the main character. The difference, he insists, is not *whether* you imitate but whether you do it consciously. **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`.

**Complementary context:** in video games, a non-player character (NPC) is any character controlled by the game's program rather than by a human player; NPCs typically repeat scripted lines and follow fixed routines. "NPC" became internet slang for a person who seems to act without independent thought, and "main character" for someone who lives as the protagonist of their own story. The author adapts both terms.

#### The NPC

The author defines the NPC by moving from the game to life. In a game, an NPC is anyone programmed to execute specific things, as opposed to a player character that requires conscious input; you interact with them, and they have pre-programmed beliefs, ideas or scripts that they say in order to help the conscious individual play the game better. In real life, "the NPC or the non-player character are the people that are a slave to their programming". They never question their beliefs, the thoughts in their head or what they are saying; they do not become aware of what they are doing and its impact on their own lives and others'. Their beliefs were projected onto them and "accepted without a fight". They never seek to change the default path — school, job and retirement at 65 are their only big goals. They never branch out into the unknown, so life becomes "boring, mechanical and animalistic"; they never discover new potentials, and if they do, it scares them back into their comfortable hole. **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`.

The definition gathers the threads of the previous sections into a single figure: programming (1.2), the default path and its three goals (1.3), unquestioned beliefs (1.1). The phrase "accepted without a fight" is precise: the NPC is not someone who was conditioned — everyone was — but someone who never contested the conditioning.

The author had already described the NPC's inner life in a 2022 video on feeling lost. Beliefs and perceptions are programmed into us as we grow up — through school, parents, the people we interact with, and "the inevitable socialization that we have to go through". Beliefs automate thoughts. So the question becomes whether we accept that we have no control, together with "the emotional baggage that comes along with an automated perception of our thoughts", living "a robotic and mechanical and very reactive life that you think you have control over but you don't". "That, my friends, is what we call a puppet, an NPC or non-player character in a game, or just an emotional light switch that people can turn on and off: angry, happy, angry, happy." **Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`. The **emotional light switch** is one of the author's most exact images. It locates the NPC's lack of control not in action but in emotion: whoever controls the inputs controls the states. Section 1.6 shows how media exploits exactly this.

In the 2024 summary of his book *The Art of Focus*, the author adds a feature taken from game design: NPCs are programmed simply to exist, with zero conscious thought; you can interact with them, but they stay the same for the whole game and never level up. He quotes his book: "We learn and learn and learn from our parents, teachers and friends who learn from their parents, teachers and friends, who are influenced by their life path, culture and society." Schools, governments and religious institutions rank highest in authority and are often the first sources you consult. To be good in school, society and religion, you must wash your mind with their teachings — "a social fabric that relies solely on itself to make sense; groundless but necessary; a tool, not a master". Through social conditioning, most people remain NPCs, playing the game they were programmed to play. **Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`. The formula "groundless but necessary, a tool, not a master" is the author's most balanced verdict on the **social fabric**: groundless because it validates itself (the circular validation of section 1.1), necessary because no one can live outside it, and the error lies only in letting it become the master rather than a tool.

The 2022 video-game video makes a further point that softens the figure considerably. Opposite those actively trying to solve the faulty system are those who, in a game, would be NPCs: "the people that just do what they're told, they go along with the system, they never question it, and they kind of just are there." In a game, these are the townsfolk, the "environment people", even the bosses in a battle — characters who "keep the game interesting for players" and allow the game "to maintain its structure and keep on going". By contrast, "the people that question the system and understand it from a big picture can actually play the game and navigate the game and have their own success within that game." **Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`. In this version the NPC is not contemptible but structural: a game needs its townsfolk. What distinguishes the player — the main character — is understanding the big picture, which is what allows navigation.

The author also attaches a moral weight to the distinction. The character playing the game of life is conscious or unconscious — "they're either NPC or they are a main character" — and that character's actions, heavily influenced by their mindless or mindful state, contribute to society: "every single action you take makes some small contribution towards the future", personal or collective, small or large. Hence the ideas, beliefs and information you hold in mind at all times, aware of them or not, feed your actions and lead to a better future — or not. **Source:** `Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`. This links the individual distinction to the collective stakes of section 1.1: the quality of the future is the sum of conscious and unconscious actions.

The NPC vocabulary has a harsher use in the corpus, which section 1.6 and section 1.7 take up: "if you have an opinion on the drama of the week, you're an NPC", and the author's contempt for "the masses" sometimes coexists uneasily with his calls for empathy. The term is clearest when read as the description of a mode of functioning that everyone falls into, not as a category of people.

#### Archetypes and metatypes: NPC as a level

In 2025 the author places the NPC inside a larger map, his framework for life reinvention built on four quadrants (mind, body, spirit and vocation) and three levels of development. He defines two terms. "An archetype is a pattern of people that show up in specific quadrants and levels within those quadrants." "Metatypes are the synthesis of a single person's four archetypes within each quadrant." Within each quadrant there is a general progression from low to high consciousness. **Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`.

The main progressions he gives are these. In the mind: NPC, "running on a script", then player (level 2), then creator (level 3). In the body: "incel" (a "kind of skinny-fat body"), then "Chad" ("only focused on vanity"), then "sigma" (someone who "has a developed body but has tacked on a philosophical sense of mastery behind their actual fitness and health"). In vocation: job, career, calling. In spirit — "this one's going to make some people angry", he warns — religion, atheism, mysticism. He then gives lists of possible archetypes for each level of each quadrant:

| Quadrant | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mind (NPC → player → creator) | program, sleeper, follower, repeater, echo | questioner, skeptic, contrarian, philosopher, analyst | synthesizer, architect, oracle, systems thinker, meta-mind |
| Body (incel → Chad → sigma) | couch potato, skinny fat, walking dead, mall walker, diet hopper | gym bro, cardio bunny, biohacker, athlete, orthorexic | integrated mover, physical artist, body master, longevity optimizer |
| Spirit (religion → atheism → mysticism) | true believer, fundamentalist, tribalist, tradition keeper, blind faith | spiritual shopper, nihilist, hedonist, new age wanderer, militant atheist | bridge builder, sacred secular, modern mystic, community weaver, integral |
| Vocation (job → career → calling) | clock puncher, wage slave, complainer, cog | hustler, wantrepreneur, freelancer, ladder climber, side gigger | mission driven, system builder, value creator, Renaissance professional, game designer |

The lists are illustrative. After the body list the author says "these aren't specific to any level, these are just examples"; the remark is ambiguous, but the most plausible reading is that the names are examples rather than a fixed taxonomy. **Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`.

He then gives a worked example of a metatype. On a graph he plots sample points for one person — four in the red area, eight in the blue, twelve in the green — and concludes: "Overall, this person at this level of development is Human 2.0, and the metatype we came up with is called the outlier": "someone who has drifted away from mainstream patterns without rebellion and is operating from a countercultural lens". The transcript does not explain how points are assigned or how they are aggregated; indeed, despite having the most points in green, the person is classified overall as Human 2.0, and the logic of that classification cannot be reconstructed from what is said. The procedure appears to rely on a prompt the author used, which is not in the transcript. **Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`.

**Complementary context:** the four-quadrant, multi-level structure echoes Ken Wilber's integral model (AQAL) and the colored stages of Spiral Dynamics, both of which the author draws on in the developmental maps of Chapter 38, where "Human 1.0, 2.0 and 3.0" are also explained. In this chapter, the framework matters for one reason: it places the NPC not as a fixed type of person but as the first level of a progression in the mind quadrant — the level that runs on a script — beyond which lie the player, who learns the rules of the game, and the creator, who makes their own.

#### Intelligent imitation

If imitation cannot be avoided, the question is how to do it well, and the author's answer is a term he coined: **intelligent imitation**. Its first statement is from 2022: "Success is a game of intelligent imitation." Imitation can be bad when it is unconscious, creating things we attach to that cause suffering, so we need to do it intelligently — "or in another way, consciously" — because imitation, conditioning or programming "can't be escaped": the mind processes information whether we want it to or not. To make the most of this survival mechanism, we must step back and think about what we are exposed to. He is candid about the term's status: "intelligent imitation is just something I made up", a concept to help understand the matter better. In that video he presents a graphic called "the intelligent imitation process", referred to throughout, which cannot be reconstructed from the transcript; the steps are given verbally later and are reported below. **Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`.

The 2023 NPC video connects the term to the two paths. One becomes the main character "through what I call intelligent imitation". Since you have copied people since birth, you need to keep copying, because that is how you learn: you see someone do something, try it, practice, get better. But you do it "with a conscious mind": with a filter and with self-reflection, so you are not heading in the wrong direction. To condition your mind for success, you set new goals for a higher quality of life, immerse yourself in information that conditions a new perspective, and make progress, noticing its positive effect on your life. The NPC is unconscious of how they imitate; "the main character is someone who is conscious or aware of how they are imitating other people and what they are doing with that information" — whether they are creating with it, doing good with it — and they reflect on their decisions, see what went right or wrong, and correct their path. **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`. The **main character**, then, is defined by three capacities: awareness of whom one imitates, a filter on what one takes, and reflection that corrects the path.

The *Art of Focus* summary states the purpose of the practice as one of the book's principles: "Your mind is an information processing machine; we imitate the environment around us to survive. Intelligent imitation is how you leverage the benefits of fitting in while not becoming a mindless slave to the systems of society." The next principle pairs it with its cognitive precondition: "Close-mindedness closes you off to discovery, insights and truths that otherwise wouldn't be found; radical open-mindedness allows you to expand as an individual and collect the perspective necessary to succeed." **Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`. The formulation is important because it keeps the benefits of conformity. Intelligent imitation does not ask you to stop fitting in; it asks you to fit in deliberately, keeping the survival advantages without the slavery.

A 2022 list of lessons from his business gives the same idea in its bluntest form: "Copy your way to success, or steal like an artist." Many people get it wrong by plagiarizing and pretending they did not take anything. Do not act as if you were not the product of others: since birth you have taken what people told you, what you experienced and what teachers said to create who you are; you are "a clone of a bunch of different stimuli". Born on the other side of the world, you would speak a different language. You are "a copycat by nature": you see something, interpret it, and either discard or adopt it. He describes the intelligent imitation graphic from an earlier video in more detail than elsewhere: you pursue your curiosities and interests; you as a whole are a **perspective vessel** who reads and interprets information differently from anyone else; and you package and distribute what you learn or discover, from your unique lens, according to the goal you or your audience are pursuing. The graphic cannot be reconstructed beyond this description. **Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`.

**Complementary context:** *Steal Like an Artist* (2012) is a short book by Austin Kleon arguing that all creative work builds on what came before and that the honest artist steals widely and transforms what he takes; the author uses the phrase without naming the book.

The perspective vessel is the concept that makes intelligent imitation compatible with originality, and Chapter 3 develops it as a model of the person. The idea is that originality does not lie in the inputs — everyone's inputs are borrowed — but in the vessel through which they pass: the unique combination of exposures, interpretations and goals that each person brings.

The author's view of authenticity has moved over time on precisely this point, and the change should be stated. In 2022 the stated goal was "consistent authenticity", something that takes thousands of hours to achieve. In 2023 intelligent imitation appears: imitation is unavoidable, and the key is to do it consciously. In March 2025 he takes the next step while answering people who find an alter ego inauthentic: he recalls a high-school paper in which he wrote that he acts as a different person in class, at church, with friends, with his parents and in online gaming lobbies — why speak to your parents the way you speak to someone who just "360 no-scoped" you? — and his teacher privately agreed, admitting he did the same but disliked talking about it because it made him feel inauthentic (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02; Chapter 3, section 3.5, tells the story in full). Playing different selves is, on this view, ordinary rather than fake. In 2026 he goes on the offensive against what he calls the **authenticity bros** — people who claim they do not imitate. "The conditioning process isn't bad; imitation is necessary." You walk on two feet, speak English and live in a house: "you imitate because you learn." If you want to be "super authentic", he says, start walking on four legs and see whether you are cast out of your group and whether you can survive. Conditioning becomes bad only "when your behavior isn't conducive to the life that your core, or that voice inside you, is calling out for" — the whisper that "you're meant for more", felt especially, he adds, in a fluorescent-lit cubicle. **Source:** `How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md`. The result of the evolution is that authenticity stops being the opposite of imitation and becomes imitation in the service of one's own core. The criterion for "bad" conditioning has also become precise: not that it came from outside, but that the behavior it produces does not lead to the life one's core calls for.

#### Why imitating is not the same as being saturated

The author applies intelligent imitation to business with a reversal of a common fear. "The key to initial success in most areas of life is intelligent imitation." We all imitate others growing up, often unconsciously, and end up in places we do not like; that does not make imitation useless as a survival strategy. If you do it "consciously, intelligently", imitating the people you want to become like, you are more likely to become like them. People overcomplicate business by thinking that "if everyone else is doing it, it's saturated. No, not even close. If everyone else is doing it, that means it's profitable; it means people actually want it." Your job: "do the same thing but make it just a little bit more unique." **Source:** `The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md`. The qualifier "initial" matters: imitation is the starting strategy, and synthesis — producing something genuinely one's own from many sources — is the long-term goal that later chapters on creativity and originality develop.

He connects imitation to a realization from his own history. If you study someone who talks about something you are deeply interested in, and they do it as a job, it is an actual business even if you do not see that on the front end — which means you can do it too, with the same level of expertise. He realized he knew everything the fitness YouTubers he followed talked about. He had learned it from someone else, "and that's how you learn: from others, and then you go on to pass that information on. That's intelligent imitation." **Source:** `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md`. The observation turns the consumer of a niche into its potential producer: the people you learn from demonstrate that a business exists.

#### The intelligent imitation process

The author gives the process in two versions, and the numbering changes between them. In 2022, "choose three to five mentors" is step one. In 2023, the process has four steps — create a perspective you can grow into, choose three to five mentors, become a mad scientist, create or be created — so the mentors move to step two and a step of experimentation is added as step three. Both versions are reconstructed here.

**Create a perspective you can grow into.** The 2023 version opens with what the author calls "one of the most impactful lessons": think of perspective as something that expands and contracts, and that determines whether you are open- or closed-minded, openly or narrowly focused, stressed or creative and happy. The highest perspective you can adopt is that of God, the universe, "the Tao", "what everything is". His two favorite and most conducive expansions are your ideal future self and the universe. When you face a problem, become conscious of it, zoom out, open your perspective, and train your mind to hold the wider perspective for longer. The practice for today: when you are stressed or negative, zoom out — first ask how your ideal self, ten to twenty years from now, would interpret the situation; then zoom out to the universe — "zoom out beyond everything, stop caring and see what happens". His examples: a sixteen-year-old crying after a breakup can zoom out to the universe — in a thousand years I will be dead and nobody will know me; in a year I won't care, I'll be in another grade, maybe another state; there are billions of other people. In business, losing $100,000 "is a problem, that sucks", but what can you do — can you make it back? **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`. The step comes first for a reason: imitation needs a direction, and the perspective you can grow into supplies it. It is also the first appearance of the zoom-out/zoom-in technique that Chapter 6 develops as a model of attention.

**Choose three to five mentors.** In both versions the step rests on the same principle: "you become what you consume, so consume in accordance with who you want to become." In 2022 he specifies that the mentors should stick out to you — favorite authors, social media accounts, podcasters, YouTubers — people with "a congruent voice that makes sense to you", so that by combining them you can make sense to others. And the imitation goes beyond their teachings. If you like their product, why not make a similar one? You already know it sells and you know the information (hopefully with experience before you sell), but you make it your own from your perspective and sell it to people with whom you share a congruent voice. The same applies to brand colors, tone, the way they speak, how you want to speak, write or design posts. Online business is "intuitive", he says, because people are already doing it: you pull aspects from several businesses and people you like to form your own unique brand, content and products. **Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`.

The 2023 version explains why this produces authenticity rather than copying. Write down the three to five people who changed your perspective: the author of the favorite book you recommend to everyone; the YouTuber, podcaster or blogger you cannot stop watching or reading; the social media account that makes you think "how did they say that so perfectly, I want to articulate myself like that". Then the mechanism: everyone reading is thinking of a different three to five people, so the combination and connection of ideas you pull from them is "incredibly unique, and that is authentic", because the mentors are your choice, and what you consciously interpret and make your own is your conscious choice; nobody can replicate it. An easy start: reread the first chapter of your favorite book and see whether it changes your perspective again. **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`. The **three to five mentors** thus convert the fact that you are made of others into a source of uniqueness: the self is a mix, so choose the mix.

The author anchors the step in a quotation he attributes to Wilson Mizner: "when you take stuff from one writer it's plagiarism, but when you take from many writers it's called research", linking it to "the whole steal like an artist thing". His own voice, tone and message, he says, are "a combination of the voice, tone or message that I like from other people". **Source:** `The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md`. **Complementary context:** Wilson Mizner (1876–1933) was an American playwright and raconteur to whom this quip is commonly attributed; the attribution is traditional rather than documented.

In the same 2023 video he describes how he applies it. When reading, he notes things like "I like how this guy said this one thing, I'm gonna take that, or at least take note of it and then practice it later", or "I like how this guy talks about flow, I want to talk about that too". And he uses what he dislikes as well, linking it to his idea of anti-vision and vision — observing a good and a bad example in order to decide well. He loves *The Power of Now* but finds its writing "too airy-fairy", so he wants "to be able to distill those concepts to the people that don't really resonate with the way that he's saying it", coming at the message from a different angle "to attract a new audience to that very life-changing message". This applies to everything in a brand, not only voice: colors, even minor details such as the rounded edges of buttons on a website. **Source:** `The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md`. The like/dislike method is the step's practical core: the gap between what you admire and what you find lacking in your mentors is where your own contribution lies.

He also gives a more immersive version of the step: write down the books that impacted you most; write down the creators, authors or public figures whose style you love most; immerse yourself in their work; condition your mind to think in that style; write down their tone, voice and what makes them great; research their products, landing pages and every detail of how they make an income. "In short, create a tribe of mentors and exclusively consume their content." You will not have to copy them directly "if you're so immersed in their work that your subconscious bursts original ideas for you"; you will notice you naturally start to talk that way. **Source:** `The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md`. This advice sits in unresolved tension with other passages of the corpus, where the author recommends studying the opposite of your beliefs, investigating both sides and connecting disciplines so as not to "decapitate" your thinking with a single perspective. The two pieces of advice can be reconciled by purpose — the **tribe of mentors** serves to learn a style of expression (voice, tone), diversity serves to think and form opinions — but the author never reconciles them explicitly.

**Become a mad scientist.** The third step of the 2023 version names experimentation: trying, testing and adjusting rather than following. In the videos drawn on here, the author names the step without developing its content; experimentation as a method belongs to the treatment of Nature's Compass and learning in Chapters 7 and 14.

**Create or be created.** The final step returns to the title idea and gives it its most precise form. "The primary difference between the NPC and the main character is creation." The NPC is created through years of conditioning; the main character creates themselves through years of **conscious conditioning** — they choose their inputs. "Nobody escapes conditioning; it's happening all around you; this video is conditioning you." "The modern world is create or be created: you have to create yourself or you will be created; you have to create or you will be given something created to give." "The choice is yours literally every millisecond of the day." **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`.

This sentence completes the chapter's argument about conditioning. Section 1.2 showed that conditioning cannot be avoided; section 1.3 showed that goals cannot be avoided; here the conclusion is that the only freedom available is the choice of inputs. The author's remark that his own video is conditioning the viewer is not a rhetorical flourish but a consistent application of the thesis: no teacher, including him, stands outside it.

#### Practices of intelligent imitation

The author gives a list of concrete practices in a 2022 video. Practice writing in your favorite author's voice. Use the topic your favorite account posted as inspiration. "Swipe" the problems your competitor targets on their landing page. Deconstruct why certain posts went viral and whether they brought in money. Look at what everyone is selling — "meaning it sells" — and think about how to create your own version with your unique experience. Read about an idea that excites you, without highlighting, and then write it without looking at the text, recalling what you learned from memory and filling the blanks with your prior experience. **Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`. The last practice is the most revealing, because it shows where imitation turns into creation: in the gap between the source and recall, one's own experience fills in what memory has lost.

For someone who does not know where to begin, he gives a heuristic: "If you don't know what project to create, you just need to look at what others have done and emulate and start there." His example uses himself: "this guy Dan is doing what I want to do by writing for 2 hours a day and making a living" — so write a newsletter as a project, a batch of tweets as a project, a YouTube video as a project. For Photoshop art, look up a tutorial of someone building a project and build it along with them. **Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`.

He also marks the limit of copying, from the receiving end. Most people think they should stop copying or emulating others, which he calls "impossible". At the same time, he describes people copying his work literally, word for word: "three to five people a day send me DMs" about someone copying him — even his website design, his reels. His response: "whatever, you aren't going to make it, it doesn't matter, you're just giving more traffic and more money to me." And then the principle that separates intelligent imitation from plagiarism: you have to get ideas from somewhere, but "you have to be able to take them in, contemplate, throw them into the mix of the chaos in your head and then come back with something new" and distribute that. **Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`. The phrase "the chaos in your head" is precise: an idea becomes yours only after it has been mixed with everything else you know.

Finally, he warns against expecting quick results. Intelligent imitation is "how you stand out in a world of copy-paste products, brands and content", but it takes time and experience. With awareness you can start working toward it; "you may not escape" the influencer, personal-brand and creator patterns, but you now have something to work toward — "this doesn't change in an instant". **Source:** `The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md`. The modesty of the claim fits the chapter's diagnosis: if conditioning took eighteen years to install, conscious conditioning will not replace it in a week. Awareness is the starting point, not the result.

Intelligent imitation answers the question of how to deal with inputs one chooses. But most inputs are not chosen. They arrive through the information environment — feeds, algorithms, news, entertainment — which is the next layer of the diagnosis.

### 1.5 The Information Environment: Algorithms, Memes and Entropic Content

#### From a social media post to a life trajectory

Section 1.2 ended with the claim that identity is created by the information one is repeatedly exposed to and accepts as important. In 2026 the author draws the full consequence in a chain that links the smallest act of consumption to the largest outcome. Identity is created through a feedback loop: information leads to conditioning, or information leads to action, which, once repeated, leads to conditioning — "and that becomes who you are". Identity, shaped by the information you consume, influences behavior, and behavior determines your trajectory in life: "it determines the outcome of your life". Hence his warning: "You think something as simple as reading a little social media post doesn't determine where you end up in life, you were wrong." **Source:** `I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md`.

The chain — information → conditioning → identity → behavior → trajectory — is the backbone of this section. If it holds, then the information environment is not a neutral background to a life; it is one of its main causes. The author therefore turns his attention to what that environment is like today.

#### The epistemic commons

To describe the information environment as a shared resource, the author borrows a term from Daniel Schmachtenberger: the **epistemic commons**. He glosses it with an analogy of his own: "you can think of it as the water supply, but for information" — social media, news, any public information resource. His example is the news: most people watch it to stay informed, but they are becoming complacent rather than thinking; their lives are not changing for the better; more people are becoming jaded, polarized, violent. Every time anyone posts on social media, makes a TV show or a movie, or publishes music on Spotify, the epistemic commons — the public information environment — grows. It is complex and requires systems thinking, but the principle is simple: if the content published in public hurts more than it helps, and is not counterbalanced by content that helps, "our intellectual water source becomes contaminated." **Source:** `I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md`.

**Complementary context:** Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher associated with the "metacrisis" framework, the idea that many global risks share common underlying drivers. In that framework the "epistemic commons" is the shared capacity of a society to know things together — its common sources of information and sense-making — which can be degraded much as a physical commons can be polluted. The water analogy is the author's.

The analogy carries two implications that a merely individual account of consumption lacks. First, contamination is collective: you drink from the same supply everyone else pollutes or cleans. Second, every producer is responsible for the supply, which is why the author, a content creator, places the obligation on creators as well as consumers.

He adds that it is not only what we consume that matters, but its form. Beyond content, the form — a short tweet versus long books, articles or podcasts — trains your attention span, your tolerance for complexity, your ability to hold contradictions and your capacity for nuance. These capacities are exactly why you learn: to equip yourself with the knowledge and cognitive capacity to get what you want. At the civilizational level, the argument scales up: before solving any civilizational threat — he lists climate, governance, AI alignment and public health — you need a population capable of understanding the problem coherently, and "99% of people don't even know what these problems entail because they're happy drooling over the cat video". The question he leaves the viewer with is a test that can be applied to any input or output: "Does what you consume or create lead to a beneficial behavior change in yourself and others?" — or are you unconsciously soaking in information that silently worsens your life and, through what you contribute, poisons the environment? **Source:** `I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md`. **Tolerance for complexity** is the coined name for the capacity that form trains or atrophies.

The author explains the contamination partly by demographics. Everyone with an internet connection can access social media, and "most people have not developed themselves… to any meaningful degree" — in mind, critical thinking, body, health, worldview, skills, spiritual connection or meaningful outlook. When they create content, "the internet becomes filled with level one thinking and low consciousness thinking", and it is hard to navigate "because you think that's all there is", since it is the majority. **Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`. **Level one thinking** refers to the lowest level in the author's developmental maps (Chapter 38); here it names the kind of content an undeveloped majority produces.

The author's preferred antidote, in this period, is the essay. He quotes a line from one of his own earlier newsletters, adding that he is not sure whether he wrote it or someone else did — "so I hope that I'm not just stealing someone's quote here": "The modern information environment is breaking our ability to think, and most people don't even notice. Essays might be one of the last forms of content that actually develops your capacity to make sense of reality." **Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md`. The attribution is uncertain by the author's own admission. The idea is developed in Part VIII; here it marks the point at which the diagnosis of the information environment turns into a prescription about writing.

#### Algorithms compound the conditioning

Long before the language of epistemic commons, the author had identified the specific role of recommendation algorithms. In a 2023 video he describes how a person who wants to improve normally changes: through **Nature's Compass** (section 1.3) — trial and error, the negative feedback of what they consume and do — their actions shift over time toward their goal. But this process gets "like 10 times worse with social media, because the algorithm feeds you what you want; it helps you achieve your goals even more". If your goal is mediocrity, "then it's going to feed you" — attractive bodies, memes, entertainment — you get trapped in the cycle, and "the conditioning process compounds". This, he says, is why "99% of people just aren't heading towards a good life". **Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`. The "10 times" is rhetorical, but the mechanism is clear and connects to the thesis of section 1.3: an algorithm is a goal amplifier. It does not care which goal; it serves the one your behavior reveals, including the unconscious ones.

In 2025 the author locates this mechanism in the social matrix as its fifth layer (the layer section 1.1 left for here). He starts from the propaganda model of Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky, which identified five filters through which media shapes thought: corporate ownership, advertising pressures, sourcing from elites, organized "flak" campaigns, and dominant ideology. That, he says, was the problem with traditional propaganda; "social media and AI amplify unconsciousness" and take it to "an entirely new level". **Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`.

**Complementary context:** Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky set out the propaganda model in *Manufacturing Consent* (1988), arguing that news in market democracies is filtered by ownership, dependence on advertising, reliance on official and expert sources, "flak" (organized negative responses that discipline the media) and a dominant ideology (originally anti-communism). The author uses the five filters as a reference point, not as an analysis he develops.

The new level has a name: "Social media is a mimetic desire amplification machine." The author says that one reason Peter Thiel invested in Facebook is that "he understood mimetics and knew how powerful it could be". "Social media algorithms say they show you what you want to see, but the reality is that they program you to want what others appear to have. Every scroll reinforces neural pathways that lead you to thinking that you actually want the highlights of someone else's life." **Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`.

**Complementary context:** the theory of mimetic desire comes from the French thinker René Girard, who argued that humans learn what to want by imitating the desires of others. Peter Thiel, an early investor in Facebook, studied with Girard at Stanford and has cited his ideas as an influence on that investment. The author attributes the insight to Thiel without naming Girard.

The **mimetic desire amplification machine** connects directly to section 1.4. If humans are copycats by design, then whoever controls what we see controls what we copy — and, according to mimetic theory, what we copy most deeply is not behavior but desire. Combined with the rice-and-car example of section 1.2, the picture is complete: what is repeated is what is valued, and the feed decides what is repeated.

The author draws the practical conclusion in a 2026 reply to an objection. Some say that if people simply followed their own interest, they would scroll all day and indulge in cheap pleasures, dopamine and gambling. "Take a second and look: whose interest does that actually serve? That is usually not your actual interest, but an interest that you have become addicted to or an interest that's been assigned to you" — an interest that usually comes from "the corporations who actually own the social media companies that you're scrolling on each and every day, getting sucked into the algorithm and not curating or being intentional about your own feed". **Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`. The concept of assignment from section 1.3 thus extends from goals to interests: an interest, too, can be assigned, and an addicted interest feels exactly like a chosen one.

#### The fast food of socialization

The author's most developed explanation of why the information environment is so hard to resist is an argument from evolutionary mismatch, which he gives in a 2024 video and condenses in 2025. He opens with a chain of parallels that has become one of his most quoted lines: "Social media is the fast food of socialization, porn is the fast food of intimacy, video games are the fast food of achievement, Netflix is the fast food of entertainment." Fast food itself is "a combination of fat, sugar and salt, ingredients that used to be incredibly scarce", combined and tested in infinite ways "to see which keeps consumers coming back for more". Each technology in the list delivers a cheap, engineered version of something humans needed and that used to be scarce, and the companies behind it iterate on combinations to maximize repeat consumption. **Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`. In 2025 he repeats the four analogies almost word for word and frames them more dramatically: attention hijacking, he says, "could lead to the end of the world" — "I'm not joking". **Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`.

A small inconsistency is worth noting for accuracy. In a later 2025 video the author attributes to an earlier video the phrase "video games are the fast food of entertainment", whereas in the videos of the corpus he says video games are the fast food "of achievement" and Netflix the fast food of entertainment. The self-quotation is inexact; the original pairing is the one given here.

The argument behind the metaphor is laid out as a chain, which the 2025 version presents most completely:

1. Our brains evolved reward circuits for a hunter-gatherer environment. Seeking scarce fat, sugar and salt raised dopamine to signal that they mattered for survival.
2. The timeline: the human genus (transcribed as the "homogeneous species") emerged around two million years ago, Homo sapiens around 300,000 years ago, and the industrial age around 300 years ago — "1/1,000th of human evolution". We live in a technological time with ancestral brains.
3. The industrial age made fat, sugar and salt mass-manufactured and widely distributed.
4. In the 1950s came fast food chains and the golden age of television, the mass spread of information.
5. Then the internet: incredible at first, then profit-driven companies such as Google and Facebook competing in "a race to the top".
6. Fast food companies had already tested combinations of fat, sugar and salt to maximize pleasure; internet companies could likewise study the reward mechanism, which led them to adopt the advertising model.
7. They found that the most polarizing or inflammatory headlines and content captured and held the most attention, so they prioritized it and served people more of what they responded to — "that's how the For You page was born".

**Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`.

The 2024 version fills in details at several links. On the timeline, he asks the viewer to "take note": most of human life was spent in foraging, horticultural and agrarian societies "where none of what we had now exists", and "that is very important to this whole dopamine thing", because the industrial and information ages "change everything, because we're not wired for that". **Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`. On the history: as the industrial age progressed, fat, sugar and salt became less scarce (salt traded in coastal areas, fat and sugar produced rapidly); between 1920 and 1950 assembly lines and nine-to-five jobs became the norm ("thanks, Henry Ford"), shaping the workforce we know today; humans had more money and potential, and with more resources and trade they became wealthier; after 1950 fast food chains spread; then television spread information, the internet made it "grow and spread like wildfire", and Google, Facebook and others competed in the race to the top. These companies "weren't all bad at first", he grants, but "the desire for power caused technology to evolve in a way towards poor incentives", because everything was new, fast and unprecedented, and "everyone was trying to win the game". **Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`. On the hook: humans are not adapted to have fat, sugar and salt in excess; we are adapted for survival. Scarce ingredients "spurt dopamine into our brain because we need to remember them for survival, we need to see them as important". Once companies discovered this, they could test fat, sugar and salt "in infinite different ways to see which kept the customers hooked" — hence "quadruple patty burgers and quesa-chalupa burrito tacos", new combinations constantly released and promoted so you keep going back to Taco Bell, McDonald's or Carl's Jr. **Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`.

**Complementary context:** the figures the author gives are broadly in line with mainstream estimates: the genus *Homo* is usually dated to roughly 2 to 2.8 million years ago, *Homo sapiens* to about 300,000 years ago, and the Industrial Revolution began in the second half of the eighteenth century. The ratio "1/1,000th" holds against the 300,000 years of *Homo sapiens*; against the two million years of the genus it would be closer to 1/6,000. The rhetorical point — that industrial abundance is a tiny fraction of the time our reward systems were shaped in — holds either way. The account of dopamine as a "survival signal" for scarce resources is the author's simplified framing; Chapter 11 discusses his use of dopamine in more detail.

The author's coined term for the digital equivalent is **digital fat, sugar and salt**. The same mechanism, he says, is "reflected in the digital world at a much faster rate because it's digital, it's not physical". When internet companies adopted an advertising model for growth and monetization, they realized that "inflammatory headlines, negativity and things like that" captured and retained the most attention, so they served users more of what they paid attention to: "in comes the For You page, the thing that you're stuck scrolling all day". The mapping is:

| Ingredient | Digital equivalent | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Fat | Fearmongering | Fear-induced content spurts dopamine and "narrows our thinking because we need the information for later survival" |
| Sugar | Quick and desirable promises | Offers the reward without the process |
| Salt | Absolutistic takes ("in many cases, not all cases") | Gives the flavor of certainty |

Companies test combinations, and "the algorithm feeds us as much as we want until we're mentally obese". The result is "quick money, quick sex, quick knowledge, quick food, quick entertainment — a ticking time bomb that feels good at first but feels terrible later". **Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`. In a 2025 video he condenses the same chain and names its product: "there's processed food and there's also processed content: digital fat, sugar and salt", which amounts to cheap dopamine, instant gratification — and entropy. **Source:** `The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md`. **Processed content** and **mentally obese** are the author's names for the input and the resulting state; the **For You page** is a common term he uses in a specific sense, as the end product of the advertising model, the interface that serves you more of whatever held your attention.

The parenthetical "in many cases, not all cases" attached to salt is characteristic: the author does not claim every strong opinion is engineered, only that absolutism, as an ingredient, is selected for.

#### Content and consciousness: entropic versus syntropic

The word "entropy" in the last paragraph points to the second explanatory frame the author applies to information, which Part III develops in full. In a 2022 video, reissued in a 2024 compilation, he states the premise: the mind is a system, and systems tend toward disorder because of entropy. To maintain order we must consume or receive structured, ordered information that we understand. "People hate what they don't understand" — he admits not everything fits this — because their mind is not ordered: they do not understand the information, they are missing a side of the argument. Then he introduces a distinction he names **content and consciousness**: "there is content and there is consciousness". Consciousness is our senses, our sensory field; content is what we interact with and receive information from. "The structure of that content dictates the structure of our consciousness", ordering it "to the point of just feeling good". **Source:** `You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md`; also `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`.

The principle that the structure of content dictates the structure of consciousness is the key to everything the author says about media from then on. If it holds, then disordered content is not just useless; it disorders the mind that receives it.

That is exactly the claim he makes about short, shallow content in 2025. "Quick and shallow content is just that: entropic. The more you consume, the more chaotic your mind becomes." It offers no long-term sense-making — only useless ideas without a goal or vision to apply them to. Digital food does not energize you or build muscle; it makes you sluggish. Being a **WALL-E citizen**, drooling over a curated algorithm, keeps you in stress and survival; fear and desire pull you away from the present, and "psychic entropy, or disorder in the mind, increases". What seemed like one harmless post was actually fifty posts read in unison that silently induce chaos. You regress or stay the same, locked into "a paradigm of political ideologies and static opinions" that is not conducive to anything worthwhile — and you do not care, or do not realize, or realize and forget. He admits it happens to him too. **Source:** `The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md`. **Complementary context:** in the Pixar film *WALL-E* (2008), the humans of the future float in automated chairs on a spaceship, obese and passive, fed drinks and screen entertainment; the author uses the image as shorthand for a life without curation. "Psychic entropy" is Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's term for disorder in consciousness; here the author uses it without attribution, as he does in most of the corpus.

A 2025 video turns the same idea into a vivid scene. Level-one content, he says, is "99% entropic": quick certainty, a quick dopamine hit, instant gratification, no critical thought — "10-second swipe, 10-second swipe". It has no through-line, nothing connecting it to a greater whole, nothing useful to apply. "It's not knowledge": you do not acquire it, use it and gain experience from it; it just bounces around causing trouble. Picture a party in your head with a thousand ideas from social media; afterward "your mental room is so messy", red solo cups and people passed out everywhere — "a terrible place, your mind". **Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`. The **mental room** is a useful image because it shows that the harm is cumulative and spatial: not any single idea, but the clutter.

In the same video he generalizes from the individual to society. Since media is "the mass transfer of information", the result is "a society optimized for one marshmallow thinking": one marshmallow now or two later, "and we can't help but choose now". Instant gratification "is inherently entropic and degrades systems". Short-form, attention-hacking content "has no through-line… no greater purpose… no sensemaking". Thousands of meaningless ideas flood the mind; we cannot make sense of the chaos, and it "traps us in a low consciousness state". **Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`. **Complementary context:** the image alludes to the Stanford "marshmallow test" of delayed gratification (Walter Mischel and colleagues, from the late 1960s), in which children could eat one marshmallow immediately or wait for two; the author does not name the study. **One marshmallow thinking** and **through-line** are his terms: the first for a culture that always takes the immediate reward, the second for the connecting thread that turns information into understanding.

The author does not, however, blame the format as such, and this nuance is essential to reading his critique correctly. In the same video he points to James Clear, Naval Ravikant and Andrew Huberman: it is hard to argue their contribution is not overwhelmingly good. They have bad takes and say wrong things, "but that's just human nature", not evil; noticing and correcting errors is how one improves. Short-form content, usually entropic when produced at level one, is "very syntropic" in their hands: "entropy versus syntropy, disorder versus order, creating chaos versus creating clarity". The difference is that their short form "has a meaningful aim towards something that they're helping people achieve". Clear and Huberman wrote books; Huberman's long-form podcast demands one- to two-hour blocks; Naval focuses "on the highest signal ideas", so each tweet has "an iceberg of insight and wisdom under it". You probably follow lesser-known people doing the same and "making an honest living with very few followers". **Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`. **Syntropy** (transcribed "centropy") is the author's word for the opposite of entropy: content and action that create order and clarity. The criterion, then, is not length but aim. A short piece that points to a meaningful end and rests on depth is syntropic; a long piece without a through-line can still be entropic.

In 2026 the author formulates the distinction most precisely, as **fast content** versus **slow content**. Fast content — entropic, pre-digested, algorithmic, AI-generated — skips the ordering process and delivers pre-packaged conclusions; the reader's consciousness stays disordered and becomes more so: "They received information, but didn't generate meaning. That's why they feel informed, but empty." Slow content — essays, genuine thinking in public, insight that requires effort — requires both writer and reader to engage in the ordering process: the writer orders their consciousness by writing; the reader reorders their thoughts by properly digesting the thinking. **Source:** `I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md`. The phrase "informed but empty" captures the whole critique: fast content imitates the result of thinking (a conclusion) while skipping the process that produces meaning (ordering).

The author's view has moved here, and the movement should be tracked. In 2022 the thesis was general: structured content orders consciousness. In 2024 he defended clickbait hooks — "clickbait hooks aren't necessarily bad" if the full narrative expands the mind — and the practice of meeting people at their stage with superficial titles. In 2025 short content becomes entropic, with the syntropic exception. In 2026 he criticizes creators who optimize for engagement rather than transformation, without retracting his earlier defense of the hook. The two positions can coexist: a hook or a superficial entry point can be the door to a deep piece; what he criticizes is the piece that only feels like it changes your thinking.

#### The digital dark ages

The author gives the era a name in a 2023 video. The Middle Ages — the "Dark Ages" — were a time of war, famine and pandemics; what came after, the Renaissance, set the scene for modern civilization and produced many thinkers and artists. "History repeats itself, and the digital dark ages are among us, but there is a light on the horizon." The **digital dark ages** are "a time of distraction, close-mindedness and cheap dopamine". We have lost touch with, and lost trust in, the belief systems that used to give individuals certainty and a positive outlook — the same loss of externally ordered consciousness described in section 1.2. If we do not take it upon ourselves to create our own sense of purpose and order our lives in alignment with it, it will be hard to maintain "the little sanity that we have left". **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The "light on the horizon" — a new renaissance — is stated metaphorically and not developed in that passage.

**Complementary context:** historians today generally avoid the label "Dark Ages" for the whole medieval period, regarding it as a Renaissance-era caricature; the author uses the popular image as a metaphor, not as a historical judgment.

The author's name for the era is not stable. In 2024 he says we are living through "what seems to be a second Golden Age" and "the second Renaissance"; later, the renaissance is reserved for "conscious and intentional individuals", and in 2026 the parallel with the printing press is presented as "prediction… speculation… better than doing nothing". The most coherent synthesis is the one implicit in the 2023 video itself — dark ages with a renaissance on the horizon, available to those who become conscious of the environment rather than to everyone. Chapter 20 develops the second renaissance.

He supports the urgency with figures. There is an endless supply of "hyper addictive brain dead content". Average screen time for the average American is, he says, "a little over 7 hours"; some of it is work-related, but most, even at work, goes to social media. Reports of depression, uncertainty and stress "have gone through the roof", especially with AI threatening jobs, "leaving us grasping at our last sources of purpose" — even though most people say they hate their jobs. With AI breaking benchmarks every other week, individuals and companies can produce hundreds to thousands of pieces of content a day, and since it is addictive, stress and anxiety will worsen "unless we learn to control ourselves". **Source:** `The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md`. The author cites no source for the screen-time figure in that passage; it is offered as a commonly reported average and should be read as such.

He describes the experience of the feed from inside. Nothing feels exciting the way it did when the internet first appeared, yet we cannot pull away from our screens. A select few are getting tired: of cute dances and immature pranks, of "being robbed of your attention 10 seconds at a time", of "the digital slot machine increasing anxiety and depression across the board", of saying "I'll just check Instagram" and finding yourself an hour later having opened every social app at least three times — "your attention was hijacked". **Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`. The **digital slot machine** is the author's image for the variable-reward design of feeds.

But the author refuses two easy conclusions. The first is that pleasure is the enemy. Will society end up like *WALL-E*? He is not sure. Processed food is bad, but you can have a cookie or two, "or maybe an entire buttercake to yourself at 1:00 a.m." while watching *The Office* after birthday espresso martinis, "to balance out the over-optimization of our lives". The problem arises when we cannot control ourselves for an extended period — which is easy, because fast food chains and streaming, gaming and social media companies pour hundreds of billions into "optimizing dopamine-driven feedback loops designed to keep consumers addicted"; Facebook alone, he says, spends over $20 billion a year on research and development. **Source:** `The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md`. The distinction between indulgence and a prolonged lack of self-control is the nuance that keeps the critique from becoming asceticism; Chapter 11 builds on it.

The second easy conclusion he refuses is that one should leave. Social media still has "so much potential" and great people; he still loves YouTube and many creators, even if it feels worse than it did five years ago. He will not tell you to quit and "live off in the woods": "I don't think that's a solution at all." **Source:** `The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md`.

What he proposes instead is responsibility. "If the incentives of social media companies were different we would all be in a much better place, but we are too deep into the hole now, and it's wise not to trust anyone, especially a mega corporation, with increasing your quality of life; that's on you." Since the companies will not change their own incentives, "it's up to you to create your own order, your own environment, your own catalyst for success". Digital information spreads and is created extremely fast; "if it's not conscious by you, if it's not intentional, if it's not curated, then your life is moving in a negative direction by definition" — but the same access lets you be intentional and consume content "that leads to more centropy" (his word for syntropy). **Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`. The phrase "by definition" is the logical conclusion of the section: in an environment optimized against you, neutrality is not available, and an uncurated feed is a curated feed — curated by someone else.

#### Contrarian inputs: the author's early response

The author's own response to the information environment predates all of these frameworks, and he tells it as a story about his youth. If everyone did something, he wanted the opposite. Everyone wanted to retire at 65; he wanted to retire at 30. Everyone watched TV at night; he went to the gym or watched educational YouTube to build his skills, so he would not get stuck in a low-skill labor job of the kind that is consistently being phased out. Watching the news, "keeping up with the Kardashians", staying informed on everything did not appeal to him; he wanted to read books and develop skills. The connection he made was simple: "if everyone is consuming the same information and it's leading to this specific outcome in life, then why would I consume that specific information?" **Source:** `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md`.

This is a personal heuristic, not a law — "do the opposite of everyone" is not always good advice, and the author does not present it as universal. But its logic follows directly from the chain at the start of this section. If information produces trajectory, then the inputs that produce the average trajectory are the ones to avoid if you do not want the average outcome.

#### The other side: the internet as a collective mind

It would misrepresent the author to leave the impression that he sees the information environment only as poison. In a 2023 video he says the opposite with equal force: "While most see social media as a net negative to humanity, I couldn't disagree more. Social media is where we are documenting the collective mind: we share thoughts, opinions, ideas, creations and our identity; we are uploading our minds to the internet, not just creators, because content is the collective mind." "Social media dissolves the boundary of how we are communicating; it's like people are waiting in line to go inside their screens; they stare at it all day like there is a primordial pull to become one with every other mind." "Your entire identity is already on the screen anyway, but there's just one barrier left: the screen." And his question to the viewer: "You have the entire world's knowledge at your fingertips; how does that make you feel? You have the capacity to learn, expand your sense of self and start a business that provides value to the world." **Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`. The **primordial pull** — an attraction "to become one with every other mind" — is a speculative image, consistent with the "giant mind" theory of section 1.1, not a claim offered with evidence.

How do these views fit together? The author's position on the internet has not moved in a straight line. In 2022 the internet was a "giant mind" whose creators would be "the greatest evolvers of humanity", while in the same year social media was a "socialization chamber" that makes conditioning "10 times easier", with the self-critical admission that he himself might be conditioned by it. In 2023 and 2024, social media becomes "a reflection of yourself", "not an app, it's a new society" whose problem is unconscious consumption, a "devil or vessel", the engine of 95% of his success. From 2025 the emphasis is on systemic contamination: attention consumed faster than it recovers, level-one thinking, the mimetic amplification machine, the poisoned commons, the religionization of everything. Throughout, he never abandons the idea that creating good content — essays above all — is part of the solution.

The positions are compatible if one distinguishes two users of the same medium: the person who creates and learns with it, for whom it is a tool of extraordinary leverage, and the passive consumer, for whom it is a conditioning machine. The difference is the one this whole chapter keeps returning to — conscious versus unconscious relation to inputs. The author's later practical protocols (Chapter 11) serve as the bridge between the two: using the medium deliberately, in limited doses, as a producer rather than a consumer.

What the information environment produces in the unconscious consumer is a particular way of living, which the next section describes.

### 1.6 Living on Autopilot

#### Born asleep

The state produced by the mechanisms of the previous sections has a common name, and the author uses it: **autopilot**. A 2023 video opens with a quotation he does not attribute: most people "are asleep — they're born asleep, they live asleep, they marry in their sleep, they breed children in their sleep, they die in their sleep without ever waking up", never understanding the loveliness and beauty of human existence. His gloss turns the image into the chapter's thesis: "people are living on autopilot; they were assigned goals by society when they were children." **Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`.

**Complementary context:** the quotation closely resembles a passage from the Jesuit writer Anthony de Mello (*Awareness*, 1990): "Most people, even though they don't know it, are asleep. They're born asleep, they live asleep, they marry in their sleep, they breed children in their sleep, they die in their sleep without ever waking up." The author does not name the source in the transcript.

The same opening introduces a logical chain that explains why autopilot is so stable: goals require a system to be achieved, and a system becomes more efficient through trial and error. Applied to assigned goals, the chain has an uncomfortable consequence, already noted in section 1.3: the system that serves an assigned goal also gets better with time. Autopilot is not a malfunction. It is a well-tuned system pursuing a goal its owner did not choose.

**Asleep** and **autopilot** thus name the same condition from two angles. "Asleep" emphasizes the absence of awareness; "autopilot" emphasizes that the absence of awareness does not mean the absence of direction. The plane is still flying — just not where its passenger would choose.

#### The diagnostic question: why am I doing this?

If autopilot is the absence of a chosen why, the simplest diagnostic is to ask for one. The author proposes it in a 2022 video: "If you do not have a why behind what you're doing, then why are you doing it?" Ask yourself, and if you do not like the answer, ask again why you are doing it. Usually, when you do not like the answer, or do not have one, "it's because you've kind of turned on autopilot, you have gone into survival mode, you are just going about going through the motions of life as you were programmed to do". **Source:** `How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md`. **Survival mode** is here a near-synonym of autopilot: action organized around getting through rather than toward something. (Later in the corpus the author gives the term a second, more specific sense — the state in which the comforts of a job, a mortgage and responsibilities leave no time, money or energy — but the core is the same.)

In 2024 he turns the question into a procedure, the first step of a framework for self-discipline. Ask yourself: "Do you know why you're doing what you're doing? Have you asked yourself that question in the last month? Why are you going to school, working that job, building that business, doing that workout? Was it your choice, or was it a goal that was programmed into your head by your parents, friends or culture?" Then **audit your life** "for goals that you didn't know you were pursuing": sit down with a notebook and write down everything you do throughout the day and why. "If you don't have a good answer, that's a distraction. You are wasting emotional energy on other people's dreams so that you don't have enough for your own." **Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`.

The procedure is short, but its logic is worth reconstructing, because it applies everything the chapter has built so far. Section 1.3 established that every action serves a goal, conscious or not. If that is true, then every activity written in the notebook has a goal behind it. Asking "why" makes the goal visible. If the answer traces back to a goal you chose, the activity belongs to your life. If it traces back to a goal installed by parents, friends or culture — or if there is no answer at all — the activity is serving someone else's dream. The author's word for such activities is "distraction", and the word is used in a special sense: not a momentary lapse of attention, but any expenditure of energy on a goal that is not one's own. The final clause explains why this matters even for someone with a goal of their own: emotional energy is finite, and what is spent on others' dreams is unavailable for yours. Chapter 3 returns to this framework, where the audit is the first step toward discipline as a trait of identity.

#### Robotic cycles

In the 2022 video on the Matrix, the author approaches autopilot from the side of the body and of time. "We are such a robotic species; we have much less self-control than we believe we do." As evidence he lists the cycles that run personal life: an ingestion cycle (what we eat), an excretion cycle (going to the bathroom daily without thinking about it), a sex cycle ("even if you don't have a partner"), a work cycle. These micro-cycles run day to day without conscious choice. **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`. The term **robotic species** makes the point that self-control is overestimated: much of what we do follows rhythms we did not select. The phrase "much less self-control than you think" recurs, with the same meaning, in his 2023 account of how conditioning creates the self (section 1.2).

Cycles also vary with goals and activities. A bodybuilder has cutting and bulking cycles and a more frequent ingestion cycle — five or six meals a day — that "kind of controls their life". Business has its own cycles, because business is fairly unpredictable: some content does very well and some very badly; some months are high and some low. He can control that to an extent — income, content — but "overall the high highs aren't sustainable; high highs imply that there are going to be low lows." **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`. The stance he takes toward these **business cycles** is acceptance: a cycle that comes with a chosen goal is to be understood and ridden, not fought. The example shows that the author's critique is not of cycles as such — a bodybuilder's chosen cycles serve a chosen goal — but of cycles that run without being seen.

He then widens the frame into a nested structure. The world and the cosmos as a whole "seem to occur in cycles from the top down", and these cycles affect human behavior. The order he gives is: cosmic, worldly, cultural, societal, social, personal — "there may be more in between, but those are major things". His examples sit at different levels. Socially, you mirror someone's body language as a conversation goes on, a cycle you may not be aware of or control. Culturally, in the United States people celebrate Halloween, Christmas and Thanksgiving and prepare and act for them unconsciously. At the worldly or seasonal level, people change their clothes when winter or summer arrives. We do not give these cycles conscious thought, he says, because they are cyclical, repetitive and conditioned into our brains. **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`.

| Level of cycle | Example the author gives |
|---|---|
| Cosmic | (named, not illustrated) |
| Worldly | Changing clothes when winter or summer comes |
| Cultural | Halloween, Christmas, Thanksgiving in the United States |
| Societal | (named, not illustrated) |
| Social | Mirroring someone's body language in a conversation |
| Personal | Ingestion, excretion, sex and work cycles; goal-specific cycles such as bulking and cutting |

The framework is a sketch rather than a developed theory — two of its six levels receive no examples in the transcript, and the boundaries between "cultural" and "societal" are not explained. Its function in the video is to show that the cycles of a single life are embedded in larger cycles, and that most of the embedding is invisible.

This is where the 2022 prescription from section 1.1 belongs. The way to "create a glitch in the Matrix" is to interrupt a cycle one has control over — the personal ones — and to watch how the mind reacts and tries to revert. The nested framework explains why the prescription is limited to personal cycles: the cosmic and worldly cycles cannot be interrupted, and many cultural ones only at a social cost. Making cycles conscious is part of what the author means, in that video, by getting out of the Matrix.

#### Leave the drama of society

Autopilot has an emotional variant that the author singles out in 2024: the reactive engagement with what he calls **the drama of society**. "Leave the drama of society": the starving children in Africa, the next election, celebrity feuds "fabricated to make them money and keep you dumb" — what will actually solve the problem? Election cycles, or any cycle that raises emotions unconsciously, "narrows your mind and makes you dumb", because it makes you adopt goals and problems that others have for you, and those goals frame your attention and perception so that you cannot notice information relevant to your own goals — "because you forgot your goals". Is crying and changing your profile picture going to solve the problem, or upskilling, pursuing your goals, doing your life's work and "creating change on the individual level that ripples out"? **Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`.

The mechanism is the one from section 1.3, applied to emotion: an emotionally charged issue is a ready-made goal with ready-made problems, and adopting it reframes perception just as an assigned career does. The emotional light switch of section 1.4 is the person to whom this happens repeatedly.

His alternative is a theory of how change spreads. "You make a change by changing yourself", and by pursuing your life's work "you pass along your obsession to others". His example is the owner of a beer company who hands out beer with passion and effort: customers are happy, make it part of their routine, and pass the joy on. Projecting and forcing beliefs on others, by contrast, only creates more destruction; they will not accept it. Then the line that closes the argument: "When people ask what your thoughts are on insert-the-flavor-of-the-week drama, your only response should be: I don't care. Otherwise you're an NPC." **Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`. **Pass along your obsession** is the author's name for change by contagion rather than persuasion.

This passage sits in tension with other attitudes in the corpus, and the tension is unresolved. In 2023 the author recommends transferring your consciousness to other people, assuming a positive goal behind their actions, moving "from judgment to discernment", and raising the collective consciousness of the masses (section 1.1). Here, and in other places (section 1.7), he dismisses those who engage with public drama as NPCs and speaks of "the masses" with contempt. He never reconciles the two attitudes explicitly. A plausible division of labor is that empathy serves to understand other perspectives, while dismissal serves to decide whose advice to take and where to spend one's attention; but that division is an interpretation, not a statement of his. A reader may also reasonably ask whether "I don't care" is compatible with the civilizational concern the author voices elsewhere; his implicit answer is that individual change is the route by which that concern is acted on, but the question remains open.

#### From autopilot living to autocomplete living

In 2025 the author projects the diagnosis into the age of AI. Will the AI revolution further remove people from autonomy and freedom to act on their interests? It is "absolutely possible that most people will shift from autopilot living to autocomplete living, from having work assigned to them to having work done for them". But for those who value creative work and control over their choices — their agency — there is another option. **Source:** `The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md`.

The coinage is precise. **Autopilot living** means following goals and work that others assigned; **autocomplete living** means no longer even executing them, because the execution is done by a machine that predicts the next step. The first removes choice of direction; the second removes action as well. The author presents it as a possibility, not a prediction, and the alternative he names — agency — is the subject of Chapter 35. The extension does not change the diagnosis; it shows that the same mechanism, unexamined, deepens as technology improves.

Autopilot describes how a programmed life runs. The final section of the chapter describes how it defends itself.

### 1.7 The Closed Mind: Ideology as Identity

#### Why the program defends itself

The previous sections described how a mind is programmed and how a programmed life runs. One question remains: why is the program so hard to change, even after it has been noticed? The author's answer, which this section develops and Part II generalizes, is that a belief that has become part of identity is defended as if it were the body. The closed mind is not ignorance; it is a defense.

He states the mechanism most plainly in 2022. Pertaining to survival and human development, humans are, he claims, "really the only being" that reacts to having a belief they are attached to challenged in the same way they reacted, in "past lives", when survival was threatened. Someone very set on a religious belief, for example, will, when it is challenged, do anything to confirm or defend it "like their life depends on it": they go into fight or flight, their focus and perspective narrow onto that one thing, they attach to it, and nothing else matters. And yet "it's just a belief, it's just a thought in your head, and that's what you're narrowing in on." This causes pain and suffering, and can even bring past traumatic experiences into one's present sensory experience. **Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`. The claim that humans are the only beings that react this way, and the reference to "past lives" (most plausibly meaning earlier stages of human evolution), are the author's own framing, not established findings. What matters for the argument is the analogy: conceptual threat is processed like physical threat.

A 2023 video illustrates the same mechanism — which he there calls conceptual survival, the subject of Chapter 3 — with two parallel cases. If you subscribe to a religion and someone says another religion is better, or questions your beliefs, how do you feel? How tied are you to "something so static that you can't break free of"? If someone threatens it, do you feel physically threatened, have a stress response, get angry and reactive? The same applies to diet. Many fitness people love the keto diet and adopt it "as a part of who they are" because it got them results after a bad diet — without "experimenting with everything and noting the principles or truths between them all and then creating their own path". When someone says "I love to eat carbs", they feel threatened and react: "you're gonna die because you're eating so many carbs". This, he says, "stems from a lack of understanding and awareness". **Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`. The implied alternative — experimenting widely and extracting the common principles to build one's own path — is the practical opposite of ideological identity, and it returns at the end of this section.

#### The mental home

In the 2022 video on the Matrix, the author connects this reaction to Terence McKenna's line about living and dying inside intellectual structures (section 1.1). If you subscribe to a political ideology, it is a **mental home** you live in. If someone threatens that house — tries to tear its foundation apart — "you as a being are going to feel threatened": you have a stress response, get emotional without your own control, are not mindful of it, "and then things are going to go to [ __ ]". He advises becoming conscious of political cycles, during which many people get overly emotional "simply because they're unconscious" and hold expectations and beliefs about the mental constructions others have created around certain individuals. You will not enjoy your life, he adds, because you do not understand the impacts of closed-mindedness. **Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`. The metaphor clarifies why the reaction is so intense: an argument against an ideology one lives in is experienced not as a disagreement but as an attempted demolition of one's shelter.

The same structure appears in a 2023 video about online business, which opens with a list of camps: "This is similar to how Republicans and Democrats fight, or how carnivores and vegans fight: people get trapped in a specific ideology and they identify with it, so it becomes their identity and something that threatens their survival when the idea of their identity is threatened." In "narrow-minded discussions" — "the online business person versus the everything-is-a-scam person, or the everyone-should-go-out-and-live-in-the-woods-and-meditate-all-day person" — "there is no chance at discovering the holistic truth of the situation". "Obviously both extremes are wrong", so he sets out to "deconstruct what is at least a bit closer to truth". **Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`. **Holistic truth** is his term for the truth that becomes unreachable once an ideology has become an identity: the view that includes what each camp sees.

In 2026 he states the principle as a theory of thinking. "Stupid thinking is just when you stop thinking. But what makes a person stop thinking?… The answer here is identity, and you latching on to beliefs, ideas or constructs and just making them a part of who you are, because that's inherently limiting, especially if those ideas… are small." "Your ability to think is determined by your world model… by your beliefs. So you should, in my opinion, adopt the most encompassing world model that you can." If you willingly adopt something dogmatic, ideological or one-dimensional, "your thinking is going to take the shape of that". Die-hard Republicans or Democrats cannot think out of the bubble and "will never be able to find the truth… just arguing in circles". Then the nuance that prevents the principle from becoming a new dogma of its own: this does not mean you cannot adopt values from either side or recognize that each side holds certain truths, nor that you must turn against both. "It simply means that you don't need to feel threatened when someone else disagrees with you, because that threat is signaling that you think… that's who you are." It applies to every domain — religion, vocation, even, he notes, people arguing endlessly online about Alex Hormozi's business methods. **Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`.

The last sentence gives the reader a usable test. The feeling of threat in a disagreement is diagnostic: it signals that a belief has crossed from something one holds to something one is. A **world model**, in the author's sense, is the set of beliefs that fixes the shape of thought; the advice to adopt "the most encompassing" one is the positive counterpart of the warning, and Chapter 17 develops it.

#### Life as a conglomeration of perspectives

The author's earliest formulations of the closed mind come with warnings about his own field. In 2022 he cautions against adopting belief systems wholesale, in health or anything else. Life is "a conglomeration of perspectives and goals that people are trying to hit, problems and benefits" which together form the single perspective that makes someone believe something. You must see multiple perspectives; "accepting a specific ideology as law is just closing your mind off to any opportunity you can have". His example is the claim that "raw liver is the best thing you can eat" — a trap, he admits, he fell into himself. **Source:** `If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md`. The definition of a perspective as a composite of goals, problems and benefits echoes section 1.2's definition of a worldview as goals plus the problems they make visible: to understand why someone believes something, find the goal it serves.

In another 2022 video he applies the same principle to a belief common among his own critics: that people who are successful online are secretly unhappy. Some people on social media live flashy lifestyles, are too extreme or claim to be happy all the time, so people conclude that all rich people and influencers are miserable deep down, know nothing and wear a facade. His response: "everything is a half truth"; some are that way. But believing every person online is that way, and that their success is "a fart in the wind", is "the most closed-minded perspective you could ever adopt". Why are you not questioning that belief? That questioning is curiosity. Question your own limiting beliefs and reactions: what if some are extremely happy? You would have to be in their exact shoes, with access to their state of mind — which you will never have — to know for sure. So it is not worth worrying about; it is worth working toward, if you want it, rather than talking badly about it and throwing others off a good path. You can be happy in any phase of life, he adds; that is what spirituality is about — whether you play "the external game of riches" or not, you can be happy and enlightened. **Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`. The **half truth** principle — every total generalization is partly true and therefore partly false — is the author's compact antidote to closed generalizations. It also connects to section 1.1's argument about projection onto celebrities: in both cases, a belief about other people's inner lives is held with a certainty the believer cannot have.

He applies the principle to himself as well. At the end of a long guide to the one-person business, he quotes a line without naming its author: "as to methods there may be a million and then some, but principles are few; the man who grasps principles can successfully select his own method." The guide, he says, was partly principles but mostly his own method, so it should not be taken as law: question it, test it, experience it, and if you find a better solution, create content about it. This is not the only way of doing business. **Source:** `The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md`. **Complementary context:** the quotation is commonly attributed to the efficiency engineer Harrington Emerson (1853–1931), and the author attributes it to him elsewhere in the corpus. The self-application is consistent with the stepping-stone resolution of section 1.3: a teacher's method is scaffolding, not a mental home.

#### Close-mindedness: the greatest trap of the twenty-first century

The author's most systematic treatment of the closed mind comes in a January 2023 video that calls it "the greatest trap of the 21st century". "The most dangerous habit young people can form is close-mindedness; you are capable of more than you've been told you are." His definition: **close-mindedness** is "your inability to see beyond your narrow perspective in any situation" — worse if that perspective only shines a negative light. It "seeks to prove others wrong rather than prove yourself wrong" and does not seek common ground. And it is "another embodiment of the quick fix mindset", which is why it belongs with distractions and momentary pleasures. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The link to the **quick fix mindset** — the search for immediate relief — is the key to the definition: closing the mind is a way of getting the relief of certainty without the work of understanding, just as distraction gets the relief of stimulation without the work of focus.

His example is the comment section. On Reddit or Instagram it is common for someone to completely miss the point, misinterpret, cherry-pick something, spin it negatively and leave a comment that creates negativity and turns people away from the real message. Someone reads a polarizing political post, gets offended, and comments in a way that conveniently leaves out the perspective that would prove them wrong; perspectives clash; there is no common ground. It is a quick fix: you get "that quick hit of whether it's dopamine or just a bad feeling that you are addicted to" — "that's why you're making the post; why else would you make it?" Zoom out, he says: your post does nothing in the grand scheme; you are wasting time, and time matters. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The observation that one can be addicted to a bad feeling is worth keeping: outrage is a reward, and the closed mind is partly maintained by that reward.

The same video gives close-mindedness a structural image. "The universe is an infinite song: uni-verse, one song." Songs symbolize stories, with a story's structure — highs, lows, conflict, rising action, climax, falling action, a happy ending — and the same structure is reflected in games. We find interest and meaning in it; that is why people get obsessed with video games, why those who see business as a game get obsessed with business, and why those who can zoom out and turn a situation into a game find enjoyment in most of life. Close-mindedness fails to see the overarching story: it takes "the smallest story possible", such as a current conflict, ignores the universal perspective and expects that fragment to make sense on its own. If you are at a low point and your mind sees only that — not understanding that after a low comes a high, and before a low a high, with a climax eventually — you get trapped in a negative thought loop. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The **one song** metaphor rests on a playful etymology (uni-verse as "one verse"), and its point is temporal: the closed mind mistakes a moment for the whole. The game structure it invokes is developed in Chapter 10.

#### The three signs of close-mindedness

The author then gives three signs by which close-mindedness can be recognized. He presents them in sequence; they are reconstructed here with their mechanisms.

The first sign is **negative assumption and expectation**. A vision, he says, is a kind of positive assumption or expectation; negative ones can be extremely dangerous. "Your reality is determined by your conditioning, that is, your identity houses your perspective and your perspective houses your beliefs, thoughts and prior experiences" that you hold as truth — and for most people that worldview is shared with parents, teachers and others. If all you know is school, work, retirement, your behavior follows, because conditioning limits what you think is possible, and you will not act to actualize a possibility you do not know exists. These expectations are what you work in alignment with. Even a vision you think is positive, if you have not zoomed out to see where that path leads, is "a masquerade": negative and not conducive to what you want. The web of expectations, he concludes, eventually produces behavior that actualizes it. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The nested formula — identity houses perspective, perspective houses beliefs — anticipates Chapter 3's account of identity as a filter; the "masquerade" is the assigned goal that presents itself as a chosen vision.

The second sign is an **overly selfish perspective**. Everyone who acts is acting toward a goal — conscious, subconscious or unconscious — mostly aligned with survival. Since humans survive on a conceptual level, they act to survive their identity or maintain their narrow perspective: "they're going to act in a way that preserves their self-image." His examples are die-hard Republicans and Democrats arguing online, some willing to die for a split, black-and-white party rather than pulling truth from both sides for a holistic perspective; and, in business, people arguing about which business model is best — freelancers say freelancing, because that is what they did, and likewise agency owners, e-commerce sellers, startup founders, software builders. He admits being a victim of this himself with the one-person business model, though he tries to make clear it is for someone with a specific set of goals. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The self-inclusion is significant, and the corpus records a later nuance: in 2024 one of his principles makes selfishness a legitimate priority until one is independent, which qualifies but does not cancel the critique. The same self-critical move returns at the end of 2025, when he admits that he too conforms and calls conformity a usable tool (section 1.3).

The third sign is **missing the point**, which often comes from literal interpretation. If he, as a non-Christian, reads the Bible literally — word for word, "this is what happened" — it will turn him off; read metaphorically, as symbolic of some truth, he can learn from it. It also relates to taking a single piece of the story, game or universe — the one song: "you wouldn't take one lyric from a song… take it out of context… and expect it to make sense on its own", nor one sentence from his video. To avoid missing the point, you must understand the goal the person is working toward; the goal of this video, for instance, is to open your mind, so taking something from it and using it to close your mind misses the point. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`.

He illustrates the third sign with his own experience. When he first read Eckhart Tolle's *The Power of Now*, he identified as an atheist, and whenever the book mentioned the concept of God, he got "this weird feeling". He tried hard to overcome it, because he knew something in the book could help him; he tried to see past it, and over time worked past the problem. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The story matters because *The Power of Now* later became, in his own account, a catalyst for his change (Chapter 2): had he read it literally, the closed mind would have rejected the very book that opened it.

| Sign | What happens | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Negative assumption and expectation | You expect only what your conditioning says is possible | Identity houses perspective, perspective houses beliefs; behavior actualizes the expectations |
| Overly selfish perspective | You defend the position that preserves your self-image | Conceptual survival: acting to keep the identity intact |
| Missing the point | You read literally or take a fragment out of its whole | Ignoring the goal of the speaker and the arc of the story |

#### Opening the mind: transferring consciousness and proving yourself wrong

Against the selfish perspective, the same video proposes a method for reaching what he calls a more holistic version of truth. First, "practice transferring consciousness into that person": have empathy, or at least piece together a positive assumption about the goal they are acting toward. Second, be willing to prove yourself wrong: if you push ideas as law, study the opposite perspective and empathize with someone who pushes the opposite. Then you hold both versions of truth and "unlock this wisdom or intelligence that allows you to navigate between those two domains". Collecting more perspectives aligned with other people's goals gives you "so much more firepower" to make your own thing work holistically. He adds the reminder from section 1.2: some Western beliefs would get you executed in other countries, where nobody cares how right you think you are. And from the universe's perspective — which he describes in terms of division, unity and self-reflection — "everyone is right and everyone is wrong, it's all relative; this reality is subjective." **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`.

**Transferring consciousness** is a coined practice the author uses in several contexts (empathy in conflict, writing for a reader); here it is the first tool against closedness. The relativist conclusion is a philosophical stance he takes in this video, and it coexists with sharper judgments elsewhere, as the end of this section shows.

In a June 2023 video he turns the method into an exercise for the viewer. Look inward: did you feel threatened by anything he said? He understands the mind of the atheist; he was raised Mormon; he understands many perspectives because he sought to understand them, to challenge his beliefs and to prove himself wrong "even when I thought I was the most right person in the world". The pleasure of recognizing patterns across different perspectives — he calls it "the meaningful dopamine" — is a sign you are moving in the right direction: "it just feels good to expand your mind in a non-pleasurable way." **Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`. The phrase is paradoxical by design: the reward of an open mind is real but unlike the quick hit of the comment section; it comes through discomfort.

#### Labels as tools: the conversation with Devon Eriksen

The deepest treatment of identity labels in the corpus comes not from the author but from his 2024 conversation with Devon Eriksen, and the attributions must be kept clear: the ideas in this subsection are Eriksen's unless otherwise stated.

Asked by the author whether he has a guiding religion or philosophy with a label, Eriksen answers: "you have to beware of self-limiting beliefs." He would describe his views as somewhat anarcho-capitalist and may call himself one "sometimes for convenience" — "we don't want to fight the great linguistic crusade" — "but if I do it so consistently within my own head, then I have made that my identity, and then I am unable to critically examine anarcho-capitalism and to critique it as a tool and say does this or doesn't this serve my goals right now." **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`.

He gives an example. Calling himself an anarcho-capitalist or libertarian draws other libertarians into "the favorite hobby of libertarians, which is calling other libertarians not real libertarians". He says he would like to see seed oils banned, and they reply that this is "a statist position". His diagnosis: "you have identified with a political philosophy to the point where you are thinking about how to serve the political philosophy, not how to achieve your goals. The political philosophy was made to serve you, not the other way around." He calls himself a libertarian because he wants people to be free, to have control over their own destinies; with that **motivating goal** clear, he can ask, in a specific situation, which move gives people more control over their destinies — "which is more important: the freedom to poison people or the freedom to not be poisoned?" The author adds his own nuance: "the thing that makes a person most free isn't always absolute freedom"; if someone does not have the choice, or does not know, that is not inherently freeing. **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`.

From the example Eriksen draws a principle: "political philosophies have to be understood as tools, not as goals." "We don't do classical liberalism to achieve a classical liberal society; we implement ideas from classical liberalism because it is good for people's quality of life, because it is good for the economic productivity of society as a whole." And "any tool that you have that you're using to do part of a project, you have to know when to set it down and when to pick up another tool." **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`. The principle fits the chapter's central thesis exactly. Section 1.3 argued that goals determine what is perceived; Eriksen's point is that an ideology adopted as identity usurps the place of the goal it was meant to serve. The cure is to restore the order: goal first, philosophy as instrument.

He extends the warning to every self-description: "anytime you say to yourself 'I am an artist', 'I am an author', 'I am an anarcho-capitalist', 'I am a Republican', 'I am a Democrat', 'I am a communist'… 'I am white', 'I am male' — all of these things are useful ways to describe yourself, but you have to be careful with them, because any of these can become self-limiting." Even "I am male", which "is a biological fact": writing the sequel to his novel *Theft of Fire*, in which Miranda is a point-of-view character for part of the book, he has to write from a woman's perspective. "The fact that I am male is the truth, but I have to set that tool down and pick up another tool of 'oh, this is my mental model of how women work and what it feels like to be one' in order to achieve the task." **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`. The example is chosen to be extreme: if even a true biological description can limit what one is able to do, then the question is never whether a label is true but whether it is being held as a tool or as a self.

Eriksen connects this to agency. "One of the biggest reasons why people are unwilling to take risks and try new things, one of the biggest things that can be a real risk to someone's agency, are these self-limiting beliefs: I can't do that, I can't try that, because it threatens my sense of identity, my sense of who I am, from where I get my sense of value." If you say "I am an artist and I am worthwhile because I am a great artist", you are threatened not only by an AI that can draw but "also threatened by doing things that aren't art". "Ultimately we are not a tool for doing any particular task; we are here to navigate our own lives in a way that makes sense to us." And: "what it really means to develop as a human being is to have this toolbox, have the ability to expand the toolbox, and be able to express whatever part of our capacity helps us to do what we want." **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`. The **self-limiting identity belief** and the **toolbox** are Eriksen's terms; the author later develops a similar idea of perspectives as lenses one picks up and sets down.

Underneath this lies Eriksen's account of what a human is for: **executive function**. "What a human being is really for is that executive function: deciding what do I want, what are my goals, what do I wish to achieve; okay, how can I do that with the tools I have; and if I can't do that with the tools I have, what kind of new tool do I need to create." The sequence has three steps — want, means with existing tools, creation of new tools — and Eriksen draws a contemporary consequence: "when you are able to navigate your life on this level, then ChatGPT doesn't feel like a threat to you." **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`. This is the positive picture against which the closed mind is defined: a person whose identity rests on the capacity to set goals and choose tools, rather than on any particular tool, has nothing to defend when a tool is questioned.

Eriksen illustrates it with his own life. He spent many years as a software engineer and was told he was very good, but he always had friction with the people he worked for, whom he found shortsighted. He wanted to build for the next five or ten years; they would say, in effect, here is a list of features for our next two-month release cycle — why are you building foundational cryptography toolkits? "Just write the login page." He realized that "these people didn't trust me because I did what was good for them rather than what they said to do", and also that he was giving vent to a creative impulse. Had he said "my life goal is to be a really great software engineer", he would have been stuck in a self-limiting identity belief. It took time — "I got gray hair" — to understand that he did it "because software is the ultimate set of Lego blocks" and he wants to create, to "bring new stuff out of my imagination and into the world", which his employers did not want. "My real goal is I want to imagine something that no one has imagined before and make it real." So he retired early and started writing science fiction, which "blossomed out" into political opinion writing and philosophical musings: "I take something out of my brain and I show it to people and they go 'ooh', and that is what I really wanted out of being a software engineer all along." **Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`. The story is the label principle in biographical form: the identity "software engineer" was a tool for a deeper goal, and seeing that freed him to change tools.

#### Keep your identity small — or use it

The author's own position on identity labels has moved, and here the movement must be shown rather than resolved. In a 2023 conversation, the writer and entrepreneur Dickie Bush connected a point of the author's to Paul Graham's essay "Keep Your Identity Small": minimize the number of labels you give yourself — "I am someone who follows the ketogenic diet", "I am an intermittent faster". The more labels, the more you "fall victim to consistency bias", which Bush describes as Robert Cialdini's "number one driver of human behavior": aligning with some identity you have formed for yourself. Bush's interpretation of the suffering the author had described is that it "is literally acting out of alignment to some kind of label or identity that you've crafted for yourself". **Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`. In the same conversation, however, the author immediately gives labels a positive spin — "firm beliefs held loosely" — and Bush himself goes on to propose "identity lists" that use labels deliberately.

**Complementary context:** Paul Graham's essay "Keep Your Identity Small" (2009) argues that people cannot think clearly about topics that have become part of their identity, so the fewer things one makes part of one's identity, the better one can think. Robert Cialdini's *Influence* (1984) describes "commitment and consistency" as a principle of persuasion: once people take a position, they feel pressure to behave consistently with it.

Across the corpus, the author's attitude toward identification passes through three moments. In 2023 he cites spiritual teachers to the effect that tying identity to something static causes unnecessary suffering, and recommends being "like water"; but in the same period he argues that identification "can be spun for good", recommends basing one's identity on "the highest version of yourself", and gives Bush's warning a positive twist. From 2024, with Eriksen's warnings, the emphasis turns: it is "wise not to become dogmatic about one" identity, humans are shape-shifters, identifying with beliefs is what makes people stop thinking (as quoted above, 2026), and even deliberately "weaponizing" the survival instinct leaves one "still playing the game". The reason he gives for the deliberate-identification side is that humans survive conceptually, so the instinct might as well be directed. The two sides apply to different situations: deliberate identification as a lever for changing behavior toward one's own goals (Chapter 4); a light, tool-like identity toward inherited ideologies and to avoid dogmatism (this section).

The 2023 "be like water" passage is worth giving in full, because it shows that the closed mind is not only individual. Collective identities — a sports team (his example is "the Snowbirds"), a corporate job, a family — form a **collective ego**. Identifying as a Snowbird forms a collective ego that the team works to survive; that spirit spreads to the fans and affects how the game goes when the team acts in that energy. The same happens with a corporate job, whose perspective is limited to that job, or a family. These identities are not bad; become aware of them and "be like water", able to pour into different cups. He clarifies that he means identifying "in mind or spirit here, not physicality or intellect", and adds a joke: if you want to identify as something "hyper limiting", like a toaster oven or an Apache attack helicopter (internet memes), "be my guest" — or revisit the part about intentionally missing the point, unless the point is humor, which is useful and harmless unless people deliberately miss it. **Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`. The collective ego links the closed mind back to section 1.1: the "religions" of the digital tribe are collective egos too.

At the far end of the spectrum, the author places an image he borrows from what he calls "some circles": **Christ Consciousness**. The concept points to how Jesus Christ had a very high level of consciousness, "to that of the universe or God", because his identity was not attached to anything; so his perspective was not limited — he could expand it to everything and see situations for what they are. The author disclaims any lecture on religion, ideology or dogma — "the exact opposite of what I'm pointing at" — and says he is pointing at "first order being, not second order knowing": being is the landscape of everything; knowing emerges from it; knowing cannot contain being, because "it is impossible to know because it can't be put into words, because it came from words or words came from it" (the transcript is ambiguous here). **Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`. The author is not a Christian and uses the figure as an image of an identity attached to nothing — the limit case opposite the mental home. The distinction between **first-order being and second-order knowing** points ahead to the book's final chapter, where the limits of concepts are treated.

#### A case study: the diet prophet

The author returns repeatedly to one example that shows all the mechanisms of this section working together, and he reinterprets it over time. In 2023 he describes it in detail. Imagine you are in a season of struggle with low energy, a problem you want to solve, so "you're subconsciously in search for a solution". Someone pops onto your timeline "with confidence and conviction saying something like the vegan diet is going to solve all of these specific problems" — "because that's what marketers do, and there's not a problem with this" — but you must think critically and holistically, "and not just latch on to this diet ideology". The common path, he says (he has done similar things, "not specifically with veganism"): "from this unenlightened state you clean up your diet, you follow their advice as if it were law, and you see the results"; then you "attach to that diet ideology and become a prophet of it", attribute your results to that method and "defend that ideology or that identity… like their life depended on it… since their identity is threatened". What actually happened, he argues, "is independent of veganism": you ate more nutrient-dense foods; "your actions work to survive your vegan identity"; and "you had clarity, not chaos, by following a disciplined nutrition regimen". "Yeah, if you clean up your diet you're going to have a bit more energy, but most of it is psychic energy being freed up, so you actually have the space and open focus and you're not so narrowed in and stressed on solving the problem." **Source:** `Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md`. **Psychic energy** here means attention treated as a limited quantity of mental energy: when a mind stops spinning on an unsolved problem, the attention it was burning becomes available again, and that release is felt as "energy". The phrase comes from the same Csikszentmihalyi vocabulary as "order in consciousness" (section 1.2), and Chapter 5 (section 5.3) develops it.

Later in 2023 he reframes the same example through goals. Say your goal is a six-pack and someone tells you veganism will solve all your problems. You clean up your diet, follow it as law, see results and attach to the ideology as the best way — because it generated clarity, which is why you felt good. What really happened applies to any fad diet adopted as an identity rather than as "a lens that they can take off and put on": more nutrient-dense food, actions working to survive the new identity, and "clarity, not chaos" — fewer options and choices: "you add rules to a game that you were playing, and games are fun to play." That, he says, is the purpose of a hierarchy of goals (the structure of end goal, sub-goals and checkpoints defined in section 1.2), as long as one stays open-minded about how to achieve them: rules or limits frame attention, eliminate distractions and make goals easier to reach. **Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`.

In 2024 he reframes it a third time, through games. In health, "fad diets are an example": you have to zoom out and study the big-picture principles of diet and nutrition across various perspectives and come up with your own way. Following a fad diet, you stick with the routine and feel very good for a while, "but then you're just going to fall off" and wonder why you no longer feel as energetic. "Usually it's not the diet that's giving you energy, it's the clarity that comes from the routine that you're adopting from someone else that is the fad diet model", and once it wears off you go pursue another. Carnivores switch to something else; the Atkins diet, he says, "doesn't even exist anymore; they just all change". "You can notice these finite games and choose not to play them." **Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`. **Finite games** is not used here in a loose sense. In the author's vocabulary a finite game is one with a fixed goal, an end and winners, played by rules someone else set; its counterpart, the **infinite game**, has no final state, its rules and goals evolve, and the player is the one who creates them — the author calls it "the house of all of the finite games that you are playing". A fad diet is finite in exactly this sense: a borrowed rule set that runs its course and is then replaced by the next one. Chapter 10 (section 10.7) develops the pair. **Complementary context:** the distinction between finite and infinite games comes from the philosopher James P. Carse's *Finite and Infinite Games* (1986); the author uses the vocabulary without naming the source and adapts it to goals, relationships, diets and work.

The three readings — psychological (freed psychic energy), goal-theoretic (rules that frame attention) and game-theoretic (finite games with borrowed clarity) — are compatible, and the author does not explain why he moved between them. Together they make the case's lesson clear. The ideology is credited with results that come from clarity and identity; the identity then defends the ideology against evidence; and the clarity, being borrowed, fades. The alternative is the one the keto example pointed to: study principles across perspectives and build one's own approach, using any particular diet as a lens to be put on and taken off. The phrase **clarity not chaos** names the real active ingredient, and Chapter 5 explains why clarity has such power over a mind that tends toward disorder.

The author tells a version of this story about himself, which shows the closed mind from inside. Fitness was where he started, and he experimented with it "to no end". He noticed himself becoming dogmatic about things promoted on Twitter, "in this little pocket of advice that didn't seem to have the right context behind it". He experimented with eating raw liver — he mentions the influencer Liver King as the extreme example — noticed some benefits, and "conformed to that mindset of like 'I'm doing this weird thing, I'm gonna talk about it because it gets engagement'; it got engagement, that reinforced it". He realized he was pushing advice as if it were "an absolute necessity for every single person", straying from principles and basics toward esoterica: "here's how to 10x your energy: eat raw liver, drink mountain spring water… put salt in your water". He felt bad when he did not abide by it: "if I had an artificial sweetener I'd be like, I need to tell Twitter… I need to confess for my sins." To keep fitness in his brand, he would have needed far more time to bring a holistic perspective and educate properly; that did not feel right, so he stopped talking about it and went off-grid for two or three weeks. When he came back, he sent Dickie Bush a voice note: from then on he would talk only about the one-person business and the creator economy. **Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`.

The story shows a mechanism the earlier examples only implied: the audience can harden an ideology. Engagement rewarded the extreme claims, the rewards reinforced the identity, and the identity produced guilt at every deviation — the language of confession is not accidental, given section 1.1's "everything is a religion".

In 2025 the story has a sequel. The author says he always had an obsession with health and fitness but never knew how to fit it into a brand about productivity, business and philosophy without a "go beast mode" masculine aura that would not motivate people. So he built **Superhuman 90**, "a 90-day reset, a complete reset on your life, but not in the business domain". He worked with his trainer, Randy, on training and nutrition — a spreadsheet to map weights, how to track, how to get into the gym and feel confident without feeling stared at — and added a dopamine detox side, a monk mode side and a lifestyle redesign side. (A **dopamine detox**, in the author's usage, is a fixed period of cutting out cheap, high-stimulation inputs so that ordinary work and boredom become tolerable again; **monk mode**, a common self-help phrase he gives his own meaning, is a period of focused withdrawal from bad habits and distractions; in what he calls "mental monk mode" you disappear from your bad habits, not from your friends. Chapter 11, section 11.4, treats both.) The structure: first dissect your psyche to understand your motivations; turn that into fuel to commit to 90 days of change; at the end, a transformation of mind and body. The landing page maps day 1 to day 90 — what each milestone feels and looks like, what happens to brain and body — and the product's protocols include a dopamine detox protocol, a nutrition protocol, a lifestyle design protocol and the training program "that I personally use". **Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`.

The change from 2023 to 2025 is from abandoning fitness as a brand subject to reintegrating it as a separate product outside the business domain. The reason he gives is not that the dogmatism problem disappeared but that he found a way to present health without the aesthetic that had made it dogmatic. The case belongs in this section because it shows that the cure for an ideological identity is not necessarily to abandon the domain, but to change one's relation to it — from prophet to designer of a structured, holistic program.

#### Why others resist your path

The closed mind is not only something to recognize in oneself; it is also, the author argues, what anyone who leaves the default path will meet in others. His explanation applies the chapter's own mechanisms to the people around the reader.

In 2024 he explains the resistance of parents and others with sympathy rather than contempt. "They don't know what you know. They haven't been exposed to the same information, education and potential, so they don't see the opportunity. Their mind is still programmed with beliefs that serve their outdated goals." "All they know to be possible is what they've done." Parents "were assigned goals at birth, they pursued those goals and saw results, their identity was shaped and so was their mind"; they did not continue education, expansion and exploration after high school or college, and "their minds harden up", locked into the same beliefs. Then: "people hate what they don't understand". Whatever does not align with what is true or possible in "their limited mind", they will not believe and will try to avoid and stop. **Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`. The phrase **minds harden up** is the closed mind in its most ordinary form: not ideology, just the end of learning.

A 2024 video adds a generational mechanism. Most people struggle to find their highest-leverage tasks because cultural beliefs are still dominated by the oldest generations. Beliefs crystallize around age 25 — "various studies have shown", he says, without naming any — when the belief system "becomes ideology to you, it becomes dogma to you", and one rarely navigates out of it. Those crystallized beliefs shape schools, government policy and parenting, so children are raised inside that belief system and it dominates society for a long time. What he proposes would not have been possible twenty years ago; technology has evolved. This, he says, explains why many 16- to 25-year-olds without a **crystallized identity** make absurd amounts of money posting TikToks and sending traffic to dropshipping products — a model he does not recommend, but notes people make millions with. **Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`. The age of 25 is a claim with unspecified support and should be read as a rough heuristic; the argument's structure — the crystallized beliefs of older generations become the institutions that program the young — restates the nine-step loop of section 1.1 in generational terms.

Part of the author's motive for telling his own story is this resistance. Much of what he wanted to do with his life, he says, "was deemed impossible because the closed mind of many": people project their opinions and say things that discourage you, pull you down or lead you down another path — which is not what you want for yourself, nor what he wants for you. **Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`. He condenses it into a provocative heuristic in 2023: new jobs are being created "left and right", and "you know you're on the right track if a boomer tells you to get a real job, because every great idea started as blasphemy until it became normal". To verify it, look up projections for online education or the creator economy. **Source:** `The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md`. The heuristic is rhetorical and admits obvious exceptions — not every idea a parent dislikes is a great one — but it captures a real pattern: novelty is first perceived through the closed minds that have no category for it.

His harshest formulation comes in 2025, in a video addressed to people in their twenties. People with the life you want are extremely rare — "less than 1% of the population"; the other 99% "blabber about how you should live your life when they wasted their own". They insist the best things to do are what they did. Bring them near-impossible goals and they will tell you to be more realistic, create a budget, invest your measly savings, go have fun and make the most of your twenties (without knowing what that means), and set yourself up in a boring, mechanical life through a job you hate until 50, "as if that's some twisted rite of passage". They have an "industrial mindset" — school and a job because their ancestors did it — and do not understand how technology is changing career paths. Your alternatives do not register; they put them in survival mode, because they fear you will not survive. "Many people say they want the best for you, but in reality, they just don't want to feel inferior to you." **Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`.

His response is a coined practice: **deliberate ignorance**. "You have my permission, and you should give yourself permission, to ignore most of what these people say. Just practice deliberate ignorance. You can entertain the thought, but don't let it into your mind." Then do your own independent research and actively search for the less than 1% who have the life you want; listen to their advice, take it, experiment with it — "still don't take it as law" — and eventually take the best parts and turn them into your own through trial and error. **Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`.

Deliberate ignorance is carefully defined. It is not closedness: one still entertains the thought. What one refuses is admission — letting it become a belief that shapes perception. It is, in effect, the closed mind's mechanism turned into a conscious tool: a filter on inputs, consistent with the conscious conditioning of section 1.4. And the advice from the less than 1% is subject to the same rule as any assignment: experiment with it, never take it as law.

It must also be said that this attitude sits in tension with others in the corpus. The same author who, in 2023, says "everyone is right and everyone is wrong", recommends transferring consciousness into opponents and wants to raise the collective consciousness of the masses, also says, in 2023, "if a boomer tells you to get a real job, you're on the right track", in 2024 that the only answer to the drama of the week is "I don't care, otherwise you're an NPC", and in 2025 that the 99% "just don't want to feel inferior to you". He does not reconcile these registers explicitly. The most coherent reading is functional: empathy serves to understand perspectives; dismissal serves to choose whose life advice to take and where to spend attention; and deliberate ignorance — entertain the thought, do not admit it — is the bridge between the two. A reader is entitled to ask whether the confident dismissal of "the 99%" is itself an instance of the closed mind this section describes; the author's own three signs provide the tools to examine the question.

He offers one final consolation, from a 2022 video, about the limits of helping others. You cannot help people who do not want to be helped; people will twist your words, and you have to be all right with hate and criticism. He half-jokes that dislikes are a form of credibility: if everyone agreed with him, he would not be saying anything closer to the truth. If people do not want to improve their quality of life and are stuck in an ego trap, that is fine — you cannot help them. He ends with a line from *The Kybalion*: "the lips of wisdom are closed except to the ears of understanding", adding that the ancient masters say the same thing. **Source:** `If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md`. **Complementary context:** *The Kybalion* (1908), published anonymously under the name "Three Initiates", presents a modern version of Hermetic philosophy; the author cites it several times as a source of general principles.

The quotation closes the diagnosis on the right note. A closed mind cannot be opened from outside — every argument against its mental home is experienced as an attack. It can only be opened from inside, by the person who notices the feeling of threat and asks what it is protecting. That act of noticing is what the author calls awareness, and it is where the next chapter begins. Before that, Chapter 2 shows how the institutions of the default path — school, employment, hierarchies, the industrial paradigm — reproduce the program this chapter has described; and Part II asks who exactly the "self" is that the program created and that the closed mind defends.

### Exercises

1. Choose one belief you hold firmly about how a life should go (for example, about work, money, religion, health or success). Trace its origin as precisely as you can through the nine-step loop of the social matrix: at which steps did it enter, who transmitted it, and what "programmed response" (in the author's sense of circular validation) would you give if it were challenged? Then decide whether the belief is one you would choose today if you encountered it for the first time, and explain why.

2. For three ordinary days, keep the notebook audit the author proposes: write down what you do and why. Classify each activity by the goal it serves and by where that goal came from (chosen, assigned by family, assigned by employer or school, assigned by a feed). What proportion of your energy goes to goals you did not choose? Which result surprised you, and what does it reveal about your current "operating system for reality"?

3. The author claims that every action serves a goal, conscious or not, and infers people's unconscious goals from their results ("everyone seemed eager to be overweight, unhealthy, overworked and broke"). Evaluate this inference. Under what conditions is it a useful diagnostic, and under what conditions does it become unfalsifiable or unfair (consider illness, poverty, accidents, constraints a person did not choose)? Propose a version of the principle that keeps its usefulness while avoiding its weaknesses.

4. Apply the intelligent imitation process to a skill or project you care about. Name your three to five mentors, and for each write one thing you want to take and one thing you find lacking. Describe the "gap" that results — the contribution none of them makes — and explain how your combination would differ from simply copying the person you admire most. Then identify where your current inputs are unchosen, and design one change that turns unconscious conditioning into conscious conditioning.

5. Compare the author's 2022 position (break the Matrix by creating a "glitch" in your personal cycles) with his 2025 position (you cannot escape the Matrix; the only way out is to understand it). Are they contradictory, complementary, or answers to different questions? Use the distinction between personal cycles and the structural loop to defend your answer, and say what each position would recommend to someone who feels trapped in a job they dislike.

6. Take one recent disagreement in which you felt threatened. Analyze it with the author's three signs of close-mindedness (negative assumption and expectation, overly selfish perspective, missing the point) and with Devon Eriksen's distinction between philosophies as tools and as goals. What motivating goal was your position supposed to serve? Write the strongest version of the opposing view, as if you had "transferred your consciousness" into its holder, and note what you would have to give up — in identity, not in argument — to accept any part of it.

7. The author recommends "deliberate ignorance" toward people who do not have the life you want, while also recommending empathy, transferring consciousness and raising the collective consciousness of "the masses". Construct a concrete situation in which these two pieces of advice conflict. How would you decide which to follow? Identify at least one way in which the confident dismissal of "the 99%" could itself become an instance of the closed mind the chapter describes.

8. Classify your own information diet for one week using the chapter's distinctions: entropic versus syntropic content, fast versus slow content, and the "digital fat, sugar and salt" of fearmongering, quick promises and absolutist takes. For two items you consume regularly, apply the author's test — does this lead to a beneficial behavior change in me or others? — and decide what you would keep, change or remove. Then consider the opposite case: when, if ever, could a short or superficial piece be the right input?
