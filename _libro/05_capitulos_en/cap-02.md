## Chapter 2 — The Institutions of the Default Path and the Conscious Exit

Chapter 1 described how a mind gets programmed: repetition, the need for approval, inherited beliefs, and the thesis that you are always pursuing some goal, either one you chose or one that was assigned to you. This chapter moves from the mechanism to the machinery. It looks at the concrete institutions that install and maintain assigned goals — school, the job, the hierarchies of status and attention, and the industrial worldview that holds them together — and at the first tool the author offers for stepping outside them: awareness. The chapter closes by widening the frame from the individual to civilization, which prepares the question that opens Part II: if someone wakes up, who exactly is the one waking up?

A warning about tone runs through the whole chapter. The author's critique of these institutions is harsh in its rhetoric and, at the same time, full of concessions. He calls school "the education of slaves" and also says it gives "a base operating system"; he calls employment "modern slavery" and also "a very worthy stepping stone." These are not slips. The two registers coexist across his entire output, and the chapter presents both rather than quietly choosing one.

### 2.1 The Default Path

#### What the default path is

The **default path** is the author's name for the life script most people inherit: "go to school, get a job, retire at 65." Over the years he has given it several formulations — school, degree and job followed for twelve to sixteen years; school, a well-paid job, religion and retirement; the destination you will reach by default given your conditioned identity — but the core is stable from 2022 to 2026. What makes a path "default" is not its content but its origin: it was assigned, not chosen, and it is accepted as one's own.

The phrase has an echo in a conversation with Sahil Bloom, who borrows from David Foster Wallace. Bloom describes people who "are handed this default path, this default definition of success, these default settings of meaning... and accept them as their own and march blindly down that path, and they wake up in 50 years and maybe wonder what the hell they just did with their one life." The line the author picks up from Bloom's section on mental wealth condenses the consequence: "Some people die at 25 and aren't buried until 75." The idea of "default settings" belongs to Wallace via Bloom; "default path" is the author's own recurring term, which he uses well before and after this conversation. **Fuente:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md.

**Contexto complementario:** David Foster Wallace used the phrase "default setting" in his 2005 Kenyon College commencement speech ("This Is Water") to describe the automatic, self-centred way of interpreting experience that one must consciously choose to override.

#### The default path as a game tutorial

The author's most structural description of the default path borrows from video games, a metaphor that becomes central in Chapter 10. In a game, a tutorial phase teaches you the mechanics before the quests begin. Society, he argues, is designed in the same way: "you go to school to make sense of the game society wants you to play," and a **hierarchy of goals** — the author's term for the structure of final goal, sub-goals and checkpoints around which all action revolves — is placed in front of you: go to school, get a job, praise your God. At first the arrangement works. There is "ample challenge to keep the mind narrow," and the narrowing feels like direction. But soon life becomes repetitive and mundane. It is human nature to gravitate toward comfort and security, "but that can turn on us fast." **Fuente:** Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md.

The decisive flaw is not that the tutorial exists — some initial structure is useful — but that it is offered as the whole game, and "one size doesn't fit all." People enjoy different things, and what they enjoy evolves as their identity evolves. The author's illustration: "a level 100 player won't enjoy a level one challenge, a level one player won't understand a level 100 challenge." A single script for everyone is guaranteed to bore some and confuse others. This argument anticipates two later ideas: that identity evolves toward complexity (Chapter 4), and that enjoyment depends on the balance between challenge and skill (Chapter 10).

A related complaint concerns timing. "It's insane to think that you should know what you want to do with your life by the time you turn 18 years old" — what to study, what career, how your life will end up. The script on offer is usually a copy of the parents' life: "a job, a spouse, a mortgage, a few vacations, a few nice dinners, maybe a cat or dog, maybe some kids, maybe not because you know the economy. At least that's what you're supposed to do. That's what Society wants for you." The author's own five years of switching majors are, for him, proof that "I don't think anyone knows what they want to do at 18 — it's impossible." **Fuente:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md; If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md.

#### The mechanism: how an assigned destiny comes true

The most important addition to the concept arrives late, in 2026, when the author explains the default path with the cybernetic model of the mind (developed fully in Chapter 6). The argument proceeds in steps.

First, a person deeply conditioned by the default path has that path as the goal directing their learning and behaviour. Second, like any goal-directed system, they behave in a direction, make an error, and then "unconsciously most of the time they correct the error to maintain the direction that was set for them by society." Third, because the correction is automatic, "the destiny that they were assigned inevitably will come true even if the person... didn't want it." The person does not need to want the outcome; the steering mechanism delivers it anyway.

Fourth — "the pernicious thing" — the goal also determines what the person can perceive. Opportunities to make money, improve health or improve life beyond the promised outcome do not register. Retiring at 65 is "your bar. That's the highest you can go with that goal." Starting a business, becoming a creator, building a personal brand, leveraging AI intelligently — none of it gets noticed, "because your brain is actively filtering it out... you're not working toward a goal that would demand that as input." The conclusion: "These people are not in control of their lives. They are a puppet of someone else's ideals, and this is most people." **Fuente:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md.

This explanation matters because it turns the default path from a sociological complaint into a mechanism. The problem is not only that someone else wrote the script; it is that once the script is installed as a goal, perception and error-correction work to fulfil it. That is why, in the author's system, changing the goal (Part IV) comes before changing tactics.

#### Trained to bear the shocks

A second mechanism explains why people stay on the path even when it hurts. The author cites what the transcription records as "Sellingman's dog experiment": dogs exposed to unavoidable electric shocks learned that they had no control; later, placed where they could escape simply by jumping a small wall, they did not try — "they just whined and bore the shocks even when escaping was easily available." His application: society does this to most people. The life you want may be difficult, but you were trained to believe there is no way, so you do not even consider it. "You're just trained to bear the shocks of the default path," believing that the pain everyone goes through "is normal and not optional." He adds a qualified piece of good news: agency can be practiced and cultivated as a skill or trait — although, he warns, practical steps mean little without first understanding how the problem applies today. **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md.

**Contexto complementario:** The experiment almost certainly refers to Martin Seligman's research on "learned helplessness" in the late 1960s; the transcription does not confirm the name. The author uses the experiment as an analogy for social conditioning, not as evidence about humans in particular.

Agency, introduced here only as a hint, becomes the subject of Chapter 35.

#### Stagnation equals death

If the default path is so costly, why not simply do nothing and wait? The author's answer depends on the thesis of Chapter 1 that all human action is goal-oriented. You will keep moving because you must survive; if you do not choose the direction, the default one is chosen for you. So "choosing to do nothing makes you a **cog in the machine**": you go along the default path to a job you hate until you hit a low point and bounce out. Hence his compressed formula, "stagnation equals death," and the practical inference, "you might as well start now." He explicitly refuses to frame this as motivation; he describes it instead as a pull — "the universe is calling you to evolve" — and as the bad feeling of not doing what is obviously needed. **Fuente:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md.

In *The Art of Focus*, his own book, he raises the default path to the status of the central obstacle: it is "pronounced in our times; this is the main problem you must overcome." The present offers an abundance of information, opportunities and convenience, and most of it should be labelled distraction "except for the ones you filter with critical thought." "Distractions lead you further away from who you are and who you want to become. The solution to a mediocre life is focus." **Fuente:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md. The equation of escape with focus prepares Part III, where attention is presented as the only root control a person has.

#### The asymmetry of patience

One of the author's sharpest observations about the default path concerns time horizons. People spend twelve to sixteen years of school, degree and early career "working up to what you could make now," yet will not spend one or two years on their own path to make "much more than that." Four years for a degree are accepted without question; two years building something unknown, figured out along the way, feel unacceptable — even though, in his view, such a path can produce "incredibly rare results and irreplaceable results" within that window, while the default path limits you to the same salary and results as everyone else on it. "People just don't have perspective for pursuing their own dreams." **Fuente:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md.

He explains the impatience by the path itself. People raised on the default path are used to having things handed to them — a job, a paycheck. They worked, but they never worked on forging their own path. So when they try, they "can't zoom out and see beyond a week's worth of lack of results before they call it a scam or quit." The alternative he proposes is to stay with the problems and the lack of results long enough to identify them, and to use them to educate yourself further with the **specific knowledge** needed to overcome them. **Fuente:** Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md.

The time frames themselves are not stable in the corpus. The author has said that change "takes years of reconditioning," that one should commit "three months minimum," that a business can be started in "60 days," and, in titles, that one can "reinvent" oneself in six to twelve months or "fix your entire life in 1 day." The "1 to 2 years" of this argument is one point on that range. The figures depend on the goal (testing an interest, learning a skill, building a business, reinventing an identity) and are better read as rhetorical framings than as estimates; a plausible reconciliation, which the author does not make explicitly, is that a decision or an identity shift can be sudden while consolidating a new baseline takes years.

#### The retirement myth

The promise that holds the default path together is retirement. For people who hate their jobs — he used to hate his — the hope is retirement, "a promise that was marketed to you since you were a child, like a ClickFunnels landing page with a 40-year countdown timer." The metaphor is precise: a sales page with an artificial countdown keeps you moving toward a purchase by deferring the reward and manufacturing urgency. And the reward disappoints: "your idea of retirement is just another vacation that you will get bored of because that's how the mind works." The underlying claim is about the brain: brains "are pattern-making machines evolved to solve specific problems with limited resources, not to handle unlimited possibilities." A life with no problems to solve is not paradise for such a machine. **Fuente:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md.

**Contexto complementario:** ClickFunnels is a popular software for building sales funnels; countdown timers on landing pages are a common, often criticized, technique to create urgency.

What people actually want, the author argues, has been misread. They "misinterpret their desire for freedom as retirement or what the default path set them on. In reality, what they want is to grow. They want to evolve. They want to transcend and include their past self. They want to solve their own problems. Remove the limits on their potential." The answer to "what do I do with my life" is to progress in "the only areas that matter: mind, body, spirit, business." He then adds a characteristic twist: "Business is also partially spirit. It's value exchange. It's community. It's contributing to the world that you take resources from. You're giving back." **Fuente:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md. "Transcend and include" is Ken Wilber's formula, which the author adopts and which is developed in Chapter 38; the four areas reappear as the holistic development model of Chapter 40.

#### Biographical evidence: the anti-role models

The author supports the diagnosis with his own childhood, which he presents not as a law but as the observation that set him on his path. He calls the adults around him **anti-role models** — people whose lives showed him what he did not want. He describes "waking at 4 a.m., driving to work at 5, grinding, getting home at 7 p.m. to support a family, working double shifts, only to be met by a spouse ready to argue or tell him he could do more around the house." He admits this is "pent-up stuff" from negative parts of his childhood, not all of which was negative. As a teenager he became obsessed with what would make him the most money with the least work. **Fuente:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md.

In an earlier telling he generalizes the observation: everywhere he looked, people seemed unhappy with their careers, jobs, spouses and day-to-day lives. He hedges explicitly — this may have been his environment, plus the "negativity bias" by which our brains pick up on negative things most — but concludes that "that shouldn't be the default outcome of everyone's life so why would I try to emulate or follow that path." Personal responsibility, self-education and making his own decisions became priorities before age twenty. **Fuente:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

Other images reinforced the conclusion. From TV and films he took the picture of the repetitive life in *WALL-E*, where people in lounge chairs are drip-fed soda while watching screens all day. At the grocery store and at jobs he saw people doing what they did not want to do, who "rolled around like a tumble weed" into a career, relationship and lifestyle they did not care about. So "literally the only other option was to become an entrepreneur": however much he failed, there would be meaning in the failure, and at least it would not be boring. Like everyone, he had no idea where to start; the only options he knew were the ones his parents mentioned or those accessible to the general public. **Fuente:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md. A TV scene in which a boss refuses an employee time to see their kids "would hit hard": "the signs are all around you... there's clearly a different path." **Fuente:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

The economic version of the same rejection: "I realized pretty early in my life that I was not okay with breaking my back for 8 to 10 hours a day to make 80 to $150,000 a year in a job where I own nothing, doing work I don't care about, especially when I can see as clear as day that there are people doing meaningful work who make 100x that amount while working a fraction of the time." The point is not that he had the value to earn that yet — "I may not have had that much value to offer when I was young" — but that the possibility was visible: "I knew it was possible because I could just look." **Fuente:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md.

His family background frames these memories. He grew up in a strict LDS (Mormon) household — "do what you're told," church on Sundays, slacks and a white shirt, passing out bread — with parents who made a decent middle-class living and whose prescriptions were "save your money... invest... get a job... go to school... do exactly these things." He believes he was the first in his family to attend a four-year college (he did not graduate) and "the first to kind of do everything differently." From conversations with many people he draws a broader observation: many have a bad **relationship with money** — a common phrase he uses in a specific sense, the inherited attitude that prevents people from even thinking about earning more, or that makes them see billionaires and conclude "that's shallow, that's corrupt, that's evil," so that they write off starting a business "no matter how small scale it is." He admits: "I don't know how I overcame it really." **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md; Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. Money programming is treated in depth in Chapter 34.

In the same conversation with Dickie Bush, the author connects these early "signs" to a wider cultural shift he sees: more people internalizing the symbols that there is another path, and the internet-marketing world moving away from "crazy funnels flexing screenshots," with stigmas like "sleazy salesman" and "sleazy internet marketer" dying. People increasingly pay authentic creators with attention and money, and creators package genuinely helpful products — early YouTube fitness creators who once launched clothing brands "out of pure passion" now sell programs, some of which he has bought and enjoyed. The anecdote matters here because it shows how the author links personal rejection of the default path to the emergence of an alternative economy, the subject of Part X.

Two further details complete the picture. As a child he felt alone and was a rebel who questioned almost everything — church, after-school sports, even a four-year college — and he is "grateful for my programming or conditioning because it allowed me to get a leg up on my peers that just obeyed without question." **Fuente:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md. The remark is a useful nuance: conditioning is not always the enemy; a conditioning toward questioning was, for him, an advantage. And as a child he imagined he would write a book, then forgot about it for ten years "in exchange for what he was told: go to school, get a job." It did not sit well; an "inner voice" said "hey dude, like everyone's doing this, you can probably do something else," but the alternative was so uncertain that he followed the path anyway. **Fuente:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md.

Looking back, he summarizes the message of his upbringing: he was made aware of only one path — school, job, retirement "with just enough currency to survive until death" — reinforced by parents, teachers and friends, largely beyond a child's control. The injected dream was to do well in school, pick a major that raised earning potential, and become a doctor, lawyer or engineer. "I didn't realize that I was being trained as a **replaceable unit of society**." He knew that millionaires, digital nomads and creatives existed, but never registered them as a possibility; they seemed far away because the only message — "get a high-paying job" — was repeated by people who had never pursued their own dreams in the unknown. **Fuente:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md. The detail is a small illustration of the filtering mechanism described above: the alternatives were visible, but not perceivable as options.

The anti-role models are worth separating from later concepts. They are a biographical antecedent of the **anti-vision** (Chapter 7) — writing down the life you do not want — but here they work as observation, not yet as method.

#### College as a countdown

The author went to college anyway, and how he used it illustrates a stance toward the default path that is neither obedience nor outright refusal. College was, in his words, a default path he actually looked forward to; he saw its positives, but knew young that he did not need it to earn an income and that no one should rely on external circumstances, college included, to dictate their future. Internet education supplemented his learning. The main reason he went was to delay the 9-to-5, which at the time was "the bane of my existence" (he adds that he has nothing against such jobs). "As soon as I stepped foot on the college campus I knew that the countdown had begun": four years to build a name and a business, or "accept my demise" and get a job. He first wanted neuroscience — "every kid wants to make 300,000 a year at a job" — considered engineering, and ended up in business, where he met great people. **Fuente:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

In a 2026 retelling he coins the term **buffer period** for this use of college. As a teenager he looked at the older generation (he stresses that he loves his parents) and at people working at Walmart and thought, "that's not what I want my life to end up as." He then "reverse-engineered" what had led them there: a 9-to-5 takes most of your day, you come home tired, and you never work on something meaningful. He wanted to do something creative. So he treated college "as a buffer period to just experiment with things." The straight-A high-school student became "the C's get degrees dude," switching constantly — "a theme of my youth" — changing, making mistakes, pivoting, experimenting, accumulating experience and failures. **Fuente:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md.

The decision of what to study is one of his most emotionally charged stories. "I was lied to as a child and I feel like you were too." After his parents' divorce, at eighteen, he sat in his father's apartment in front of an old laptop with Arizona State University's website open, trying to decide what to study, where it would lead and what jobs would follow. "At the age of 18 I was faced with a near impossible decision": "I was forced to know what I wanted out of life without having ever lived my own life," and to choose the starting point of a career he would spend "the next 40 plus years" in. He was encouraged, "if not forced," to choose something that would set him up for the future "or else I would pay the consequences, literally, with a pile of student loans" that only a high-paying career could repay. Even with "nearly a full-ride scholarship," uncovered living expenses meant he "racked up $20,000 of student loans." **Fuente:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md.

The financial pressure eventually closed the buffer period. With about $20,000 of student debt and growing, "out of urgency and out of honestly just being scared that I wasn't going to be able to survive," he applied for jobs and was hired at a web design agency thanks to a skill he had already acquired. **Fuente:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md. The fuller version of the story: since he could remember he wanted to do what he loves for a living, but everyone "screams" the opposite — go to school, do what you are told, follow the **preset future** your parents thought possible and you thought possible as a child. He started down that path, went to college and "partied the entire time," while learning creative skills on the side, studying his interests (he went deep into fitness and nutrition, going to the gym with friends since high school) and starting online businesses "as much as I possibly could." In college he learned to code because he heard it was lucrative, got "sucked down that entire rabbit hole" of coding podcasts and community, loved it and built coding projects. As his online businesses kept failing, he decided to "call it quits": he had been in college too long, dropped out despite having been a straight-A student, and took a job as a front-end web developer at a web design agency paying $50–55k a year. **Fuente:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md.

The constant through all of this is what the author calls a **meaningful side project**: "I always had a meaningful side project that I wanted to work on from the ages of like 16 to 25" — coding, a business, learning a skill, digital art "for a long time." It was something to apply his learning to, and it made him happy. **Fuente:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md. In a 2023 telling he lists the sequence: "ever since I can remember I've had a project that I was building." First Photoshop art on Instagram, because he saw potential and wanted a large following; then web design and SEO, freelancing in many forms; then the agency job, while still building his own web design business first thing in the morning until freelancing worked; then Twitter, then Instagram, then "I need to actually create a product," and he kept creating: "I had like 10 different lead magnets." **Fuente:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md. A 2024 retelling summarizes it: family, teachers and even people he followed online "screamed at me to go down other paths"; he did not finish college after five years, started "every online business imaginable," dropped out because college was wasting his time, and took a $55,000 job as an entry-level web designer — but "the goal of doing what I love for a living stuck in the back of my head," and there was rarely a moment he was not working on a personal project, a habit he still keeps. **Fuente:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md.

The details of these retellings vary — "front-end web developer, $50–55k" versus "entry-level web designer, $55,000"; "partied the entire time" versus "didn't finish after five years"; $20,000 of student loans in some versions, $2,000 or $8,000 of debt in others (the $2,000 may be a transcription error; this cannot be confirmed). The core is stable: a preset future, a college stint used to delay and experiment, a job taken under financial pressure, and an unbroken habit of building something of his own. That habit is the seed of the **project** as the practical unit of change, developed in Chapter 9, and of the "one hour a day" approach to building alongside a job in Chapter 12.

### 2.2 School: The Education of Slaves and True Education

School is the first institution of the default path, the one where the hierarchy of assigned goals is installed. The author's critique of it is among the most developed in the corpus, and it is also the one where his rhetoric is harshest. It helps to know in advance how the critique evolved. In 2022 he called school "a blessing and a curse," necessary for society to function; in 2023 formal education "has its place," but schools "can't and won't" teach you to build your dreams; from 2024 the critique gains causal precision (who pays, whose pace sets the curriculum, what the institution protects) and rhetorical severity — "the education of slaves," the "bottom quintile," the "government-trained expert." Throughout, one concession remains: school gives socialization, literacy and "a base operating system to become a civilized human." The fairest reading, which the author's own concessions support, is that he accepts basic general education and criticizes specialized and professional education presented as the only route to a good life.

#### "If you don't create a purpose, you will be assigned one"

The central thesis comes from the author's book *The Art of Focus*: "if you don't create a purpose you will be assigned one because only slaves were taught to perform one task for the entirety of their life." Slaves were taught career-specific skills — "growing wheat, herding sheep or just riding a horse." "Our current education system reflects the education of slaves. Today we are taught to be useful workers, we are taught to obey the authority, we are conditioned to get good grades out of fear of punishment." By contrast, "a free man on the other hand is expected to have a purpose and do whatever it takes to achieve it." **Fuente:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md. The author's term for this is the **education of slaves**: education for a single task, for obedience, and for grades obtained out of fear.

The idea has a parallel source in Devon Eriksen, a novelist the author interviewed and whose name appears in transcriptions as "Ericson" or "Erickson." The author presents Eriksen's first point — "Only slaves are expected to perform one task for their entire life" — and expands it into a description of the whole trajectory since birth. We are taught to be useful workers; that is what school is designed for. We obey at school and do assignments we do not care about so that we can keep doing things we do not care about, "so that you never actually get around to the things that you do care about." Growing up, "you need to speak this certain way or else you're going to be punished"; you learn specific skills for specific tasks so that you are not punished with a bad life of little money; you learn to get paid for a specific skill by a specific person "so that they can become your master and you become a wage slave." **Fuente:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md. Here "wage slave" is a popular phrase the author redefines: someone who learned a narrow skill so that an employer could become their master. Section 2.3 develops it.

It is important to read "slave" as the author asks it to be read. He insists it is a mental metaphor, not a comparison with physical slavery, and his use of the word shifts over time from almost neutral to normative (see 2.3). In the school context, the contrast is between two kinds of preparation for life: preparation for a single assigned function, and preparation for self-direction.

#### Why school serves someone else

Why would a school system produce useful workers rather than free individuals? The author assembles several explanations, some his own and some from guests, that converge on one idea: a system serves whoever controls it, and the student does not control it.

The cleanest version is Devon Eriksen's, which the author adopts. Eriksen calls it the disease, as opposed to the symptoms "we could go on all day nitpicking." The chain runs: whoever pays for something is the customer; "it is the customer who gets served"; when the government pays for your education — with money taken from other people, through education grants or programs like the Stafford Loan — the government decides, at some level, what you learn. You do not even have veto power, because if you pack your bags and go to another college you get the same thing; the government is paying there too. "You're not writing the checks so you don't decide the curriculum." And whoever decides the curriculum gives you the education that makes you useful to them, not the education that makes you useful to you. **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md. The author's lexicon records this as a guest term, **third party is paying**.

The author repeats the point in his own words and adds a second layer of pressure: "Since you do not fund this school system you do not control what is taught. You're told to read this and memorize that, and if you don't then your **Industrial Age parents** will scold you and threaten to ground you, and then you can't pursue your own interest or go hang out with your friends or explore." The child receives "double the pain": from the institution and from parents who themselves were shaped by it and punish deviation from the curriculum. **Fuente:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md.

A third structural constraint is pace. "This education system has to bias the bottom quintile. In other words you are forced to learn at the same pace as the dumbest 20% of the people in the public school system, and this is extremely destructive." **Fuente:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md. **Bottom quintile** is the author's label for this levelling effect.

A fourth explanation applies a principle the author uses for every institution: systems behave like organisms. "Education is the first step in value creation" — without education or skills you have nothing worth packaging and distributing — but conventional education "won't cut it." The school system "is a system," with an end goal (go to school, get a job), variables, a path and a starting point. It is "intimately tied with government, politics and the economy, the old economy," so you will not find in it an education that threatens it, "because systems survive just like humans do," in the same way that we survive the concept of ourselves; if it starts to break down, its members fight to keep it together. **Fuente:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md. The parallel with "the concept of ourselves" is a preview of the **law of conceptual survival**, the author's coined principle that ideas and identities defend themselves like bodies (Chapter 3). His practical inference: to make it in the new economy, learn from the people who created it — courses, coaching, content from the brands and creators who invented the new jobs. The school system "aren't creators... they don't have the right to teach you that," whatever their curriculum, because they have not done it. The author's term pair **old economy / new economy** marks this distinction.

The same reasoning yields his bluntest formulation: "Schools can't and won't teach you how to build your dreams." Schools are bound up with government, politics and the economy, and their "conditioning process or education" is set up to keep those running; "anything that gives the individual power is a threat." Self-education therefore becomes a requirement, not an option. And it cannot be skipped, because "you can't reach your goals at your current level of development, or else you'd already achieve them": you need to develop skills, build things, fail and gain experience. **Fuente:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md.

#### The Prussian model and the question of intent

The author grounds the critique historically in the story of Horace Mann. In the early-to-mid 1800s, as America industrialized and needed to educate large numbers of immigrant children, educators such as Mann travelled to Prussia to study its school system. That system "was designed to create obedient soldiers, compliant citizens, civil servants and well-behaved workers," through mandatory attendance, teacher training, a national curriculum and testing, division of students by age, and grade levels. "Students were taught how to work, not how to think." Impressed by its efficiency and standardization, Mann and others brought many of these methods into American schools, especially in Massachusetts, which became a model for other states. **Fuente:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md; How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md. In a 2025 version he calls the Prussian model "not education at all, but a weapon of mass conformity," and concludes that "society wants you simple, predictable, and easy to categorize," because that best serves its interests and the profits of its organizations. **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md. One downstream effect he names is that "reading to most people is a performative act" — something done for others rather than for understanding — a theme he develops when he discusses reading (Chapter 16).

**Contexto complementario:** Horace Mann (1796–1859) was secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education and a leading advocate of the "common school" movement; his 1843 report praised aspects of Prussian schooling. Historians debate how far the Prussian model was meant to produce obedience versus broad literacy; the author presents his version as his reading of this history, not as a historiographical claim.

This history raises a question the author handles with deliberate care: was this done on purpose? He gives two kinds of answer, and the tension between them is never fully resolved. The strong, rhetorical version appears in some places — schools "were created to keep you narrow-minded and dumb," or, quoting his own book *Purpose and Profit*, "Schools were created to enslave the brightest minds," which he says was "taken quite a bit out of context" but is "largely true." The systemic version appears more often and is the one he explicitly favours when he stops to qualify. From his research, Mann did not want "the whole slave mentality" brought over, "but that's kind of a byproduct of the system itself... a **property of the system**" — the author's term for an unintended effect that emerges from a system's structure. He insists that he does not mean it "as a conspiracy theory." **Fuente:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md; How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md.

The 2025 formulation turns this into a general principle: "If you understand systems, you understand that the system takes the shape of that which most benefits the end goal, which in society's case is keeping you sick and dumb, whether it's intentional or not. It doesn't have to be a conspiracy theory for the system to naturally take shape of the subconscious desires of the humans at the top of the pyramid." **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md. The lexicon records this as **systems take the shape of the end goal**. The principle is important beyond school: it is the same reasoning by which a person's goal shapes their perception (2.1) and by which, in Chapter 6, a goal determines the system that pursues it. The reader should notice, however, that the claim that society's end goal is "keeping you sick and dumb" is an interpretation the author states, not something he demonstrates.

#### What school does to learning and thinking

Beyond who controls it, the author criticizes what school does to the student's relationship with learning. The earliest version (2022) is simple: people are forced to learn things they are not interested in, and as a result they "never go on to learn or pursue their curiosity again." He links this to the brain's reward mechanism, which exists to separate signal from noise; forcing learning against interest works against the very mechanism that makes learning possible. **Fuente:** Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md. The role of curiosity as a guide is developed in Chapter 14, and the reward system in Chapter 11.

A personal version uses reading. Before his arrest (the episode in 2.7), the last books he chose to read were the *Magic Tree House* and *Alex Rider* series, because they genuinely interested him. In high school you are forced to read books you did not choose; those who liked them probably already had the reading habit, and those who did not "probably just grew to hate reading." "School probably made you hate reading and thus they made you hate learning." The cost is large because "learning is a fundamental human drive": without it activities are less enjoyable, conversations less meaningful, days feel repetitive and lifeless, and you do not see your future changing much. Most people are still "**clones of the school system**... on a life trajectory that they didn't set." **Fuente:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md. In another formulation: humanity needs education, but not an idealized, regimented form "that antagonizes the wiring of our psyche and makes us hate learning" — and "learning is the one thing that fuels all areas of your life," from the corporation to the individual ("yes, public schools are corporations"). He adds that "reality is decentralizing," and that individuals who realize this can ride one of the most profitable shifts in history. **Fuente:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md.

The critique also concerns thinking. "In schools they don't teach you how to think; you learn how to memorize, you learn how to rewrite things in your own words, and as kids we kind of know deep down that this is silly... so we put in the minimum effort it takes to get good grades." He was an A+ student in high school, but "in college I was not; I failed and dropped out." "That's not how the real world works. You aren't rewarded for thinking someone else's thoughts." He offers his own credentials ironically — a writer "for almost 5 years, not an academic writer, no English degree, just a guy that decided to put his thoughts out on the internet." **Fuente:** You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md. Original thinking is the subject of Chapters 17 and 18.

And it concerns speed. Most people are wired to believe that success comes from "sticking your nose in textbooks for 12 plus hours at a time to study for an exam where 95% of the knowledge you learn goes out the window." When it comes to getting a job or results in the real world, "I would argue that less than 1% of it applies." The common stories confirm it: a degree followed by a job in an unrelated field, or a job in the field where none of the degree applied because everything had to be learned on the job. "You actually have to learn how to do things by doing them. Shocker." Meanwhile you put off your life and progress for four to eight years, delaying development until you reach the status point that lets you get the job you will then be stuck in. "Schools train you to learn extremely slow." **Fuente:** How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md. The percentages are the author's argued estimates ("I would argue"), not measurements. The constructive alternative — learning by building — is Chapter 14.

The author once condensed his complaints into a list of the traditional school system's problems. It is worth seeing whole, because each item becomes the negative image of a later principle in the book.

| Problem the author names | What the book later proposes instead |

| Convergent thinking | Original and divergent thinking (Ch. 17–18) |
| Compartmentalized learning | The generalist who learns across fields (Ch. 20) |

He adds that school has benefits, but the deficits outweigh them, and that "a new school system is emerging because it's demanded for us to move forward." **Fuente:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md. The right-hand column is this book's mapping, not the author's; the left-hand column is his list.

The deepest version of the critique explains how children lose what the author calls their generalist ability. As children we love to adventure, discover, figure things out, make mistakes and learn — touching fire, taking risks. "But then our learning stops being about real mistakes. It starts being about the traits our parents and teachers dislike in us" — traits they find annoying or uncivilized, or that will not lead to the version of success they were conditioned to see as "the only **one true path**." The default path is why so many people feel lost, confused, anxious and overwhelmed: "it was the path that was supposed to be safe and secure, but was the least safe and secure." You were placed in front of "a government-trained expert, trained by government-trained experts who are clearly not doing what you want to do in life," six hours a day, told what to learn and how to act, and prodded toward "the status symbol of a job and degree." The goals that compose your worldview — fancy degrees, high-paying jobs — then determine your mind's potential development and freedom: "Your aim determines what you see." His prescription: "give yourself permission to study and pursue multiple interests. You have the internet. You have AI." **Fuente:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md. The phrase "your aim determines what you see" echoes Jordan Peterson's "what you aim at determines what you see," which the author cites elsewhere; it is the principle of the goal as a lens developed in Chapter 6, here applied to childhood.

Devon Eriksen adds a psychological mechanism that the author adopts. In a government school, success means getting an A on the test: answering every question, at a difficulty "determined by the lowest common denominator," without making mistakes. A child in American public school spends "almost as much waking hours there as with your parents," so this shapes personality. The learned definition of success becomes "avoiding failure" — "except in the real world the real definition of success lies on the other side of a whole lot of failure." An environment that makes children risk-averse, especially high-IQ children, is "psychologically crippling what could be the next generation of hyperproductive people." The author's summary: "the greatest mistake is no mistake at all." **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md.

Eriksen also supplies the most important qualification in this part of the corpus. "Don't get me wrong, somebody has to do a lot of these tasks that anybody can do... we need certain things done that maintain civilization." The problem is not that such work exists. It is that some people "have the potential to produce amazing things and they've been **raised to be afraid**." Imagine Elon Musk raised timid and nervous — "it's very possible to do that to a child if you're not paying attention or if that makes you feel good or if you are a fearful person yourself." The loss to civilization would be real, and invisible: "we would never have understood what we lost, we would never have seen it." Many people with "wonderful things in them" are selling their time "for minimum wage making sandwiches." **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md. The qualification matters because it shows that the critique is not a condemnation of ordinary work but of a system that wastes potential by training fear.

#### The lies of career choice and the value of a degree

The school critique culminates in the choice of a degree, the moment when the assigned hierarchy of goals turns into debt. The author argues that "you're set up to fail from the start where you have to be motivated by money." Because the degree comes with student loans that only a lucrative career can repay, the choice is made under financial pressure, not from knowledge of what you want — unless scholarships pay for it, and even then, as in his case, living expenses create debt. **Fuente:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md.

He then names three lies of career choice. "For most people they choose the career that will make them the most money, but that's a lie. For others they choose the career that people will respect them for, but that's a lie. For the rest they choose the career that they think they will enjoy the most, but again that's a lie." The degree delivers none of these. "You were sold an illusion by parents and teachers who were sold an illusion": "their degrees or lack thereof had little to no impact on their income, status or enjoyment." A degree "may have opened opportunities," but "ultimately it was their personal choices that led to a higher income, status or level of enjoyment." **Fuente:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md.

The consequence he draws is categorical in tone: "There are plenty of rich dropouts and broke graduates." "A degree means absolutely nothing if you have the personal responsibility, work ethic and thirst for personal development and skill acquisition." The people most tempted by college are "the naive 18-year-olds who haven't even had a chance to do those things or prove anything to themselves," and "people are starting to wake up to the fact that the conventional path is not the smartest move for sensible people." **Fuente:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md. In another place he states, explicitly as opinion, that "to think that a college degree can teach you something other than getting a job or working for the school is a bit silly in my opinion. If a business professor had a business then why does he have a job?" He qualifies it — some professors are very passionate — and gives Jordan Peterson as an example of a professor who spread his ideas beyond the lecture hall because he had a "business mindset": his lectures went online and he built a personal brand, whereas otherwise "the only other thing he could do is be a professor," and the author is sure Peterson had bigger dreams. He also admits that his own unfinished degree, which he "used and leveraged," got him a $60,000-a-year job. **Fuente:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md.

The most balanced version describes a pattern rather than a rule. People spend four to twelve years and tens of thousands of dollars to land a low-level entry job paying barely enough for a decent apartment, then spend another five to ten years self-educating in line with that job to climb the ladder, "while barely using anything in the degree." He calls this an over-exaggerated case, says not every degree fits it, that "everything is context and situation dependent," and that he is not absolutely opposed to college. But for those who want fulfilling work and an open schedule, "a glorified piece of paper called a degree" will not do it: "it will be a stepping stone at best and a financial chain at worst." Later in the same video he offers the contrast: if you stick with forging your own path for two to four years you will be far ahead, and you will be monetizing along the way instead of paying for four to twelve years. **Fuente:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md. The phrase "stepping stone" is important: it is the same term the author will use, with the same ambivalence, for employment.

#### Education is not training

The positive side of the critique begins with a distinction the author takes from Devon Eriksen, who takes it from Cicero. The frame is set by the first post of Eriksen's that the author came across, after Naval Ravikant reposted one of his tweets. Eriksen was responding to a clip of Yuval Noah Harari saying that nobody knows what to teach because nobody knows what will be relevant in twenty years. Eriksen's reply opened with: "that's because this dude doesn't know what education is." The implied argument: the problem of not knowing what will be relevant exists only if education means learning specific content; if education means learning how to learn, uncertainty about the future is not a problem. The author says Eriksen "was saying the things that I knew deep down but did not know how to articulate." **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md. Note that Harari's position is presented through Eriksen's rebuttal, as a counterexample; the author does not discuss Harari's own argument.

The distinction itself: in Rome, job training was given to slaves. A slave was "essentially a human machine," property who would spend his whole life doing one task, so he was given job training. "Job training is not education, it is preparation to do a specific task." A free man — a Roman citizen with full rights — was expected to do many different things in life, because he acted in his own interest; he existed to benefit his society but also to serve himself, "and hopefully the two would align." Since neither teacher nor student could know what he would need to know, "you taught him how to train himself, you taught him how to learn." Eriksen's application to the present: "what we call education today is mostly actually training, and that is the kind of education that in Rome would have been given to slaves," because those who design it ask "what's going to make this person a useful worker for the moneyed interests that I actually serve." Eriksen notes that "we don't really have an organized version of what Cicero would have considered education" and recommends reading the relevant parts of Cicero's writings. **Fuente:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md.

**Contexto complementario:** The Roman distinction between the *artes liberales*, the "arts worthy of a free person," and servile or mechanical skills is the origin of the later "seven liberal arts" (grammar, logic, rhetoric, arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy). The author returns to the liberating arts in Chapter 14.

The author condenses the distinction into his own formula: "**education is not programming**. Slaves were expected to perform one task for the entirety of their lives; free men were expected to do many things throughout their lives because that brings power, flexibility, principles-based decision-making and the ability to change direction with a well-rounded skill set to nearly guarantee success." **Fuente:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. The formula connects directly with Chapter 1: "programming" is the author's word for conditioning, so education in the true sense is the opposite of the process that installs the default path. It also anticipates his defence of the generalist (Chapter 20).

#### True education and self-education

What, then, is education? The author gives three overlapping definitions of **true education**, and they are best read together.

The first, built on Eriksen's third point that the difference between slave and free person is "how to live, how to think and how to learn": "True education is not a clone-producing machine that we call the public school; true education is an orientation for how to live without being dependent on everyone but yourself for your survival." It does not teach only career-specific skills, which is what everyone worries about children learning; it teaches how to become resilient and creative, learn faster, be adaptable, and use technology's new tools "so that you are not used by them." **Fuente:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md.

The second defines it by discovery: "True education is about discovery, not memorization. You have to dive into the unknown, discover, make sense of the world. It's sense-making, it's clarity. It's not being told or forced to learn something that you don't care about that serves a goal that you didn't choose to pursue." And he names its setting: "with entrepreneurship as your vessel you set the scene for true education and sovereignty." **Fuente:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md. **Vessel** is one of the author's recurring words with a sense of its own: the container or vehicle through which something is pursued — here, entrepreneurship as the vehicle for learning and self-rule. Its fuller meaning appears in Chapter 9.

The third draws on Jiddu Krishnamurti, whom the author quotes at length: "Life is really very beautiful, it is not this ugly thing that we have made of it, and you can appreciate its richness, its depth, its extraordinary loveliness only when you revolt against everything — against organized religion, against tradition, against the present rotten society — so that you as a human being find out for yourself what is true. Not to imitate but to discover, that is education, is it not?" The author's takeaway is milder than the quotation: "this leaves us with one task: open our minds, take our own path, and discover our way into a life of meaning." **Fuente:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md.

**Contexto complementario:** Jiddu Krishnamurti (1895–1986) was an Indian philosopher and speaker who rejected organized religion and authority in spiritual matters; he wrote extensively on education, including *Education and the Significance of Life* (1953).

The link between "education equals discovery" and the critique of school is made explicit in 2025. The author grants school its perks — socialization, reading and writing, "a base operating system to become a civilized human" — "but if education equals discovery, it is doing the opposite of just that": everyone learns the same topics, chooses the same classes and looks up the same high-paying skills and jobs "as everyone else to enter a race to the bottom." This is the only path you know because you have not begun self-education, so your mind works only toward getting a job for money and survival. Yet "now you can learn and do anything without permission." **Fuente:** You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md. The lexicon records **education equals discovery** as the author's label.

The practical counterpart of true education is **self-education**, which in 2026 receives its most precise definition. "Schools teach general knowledge or knowledge required to do a job. They do not teach how to navigate your own path. They narrow you into one area of study, which by nature does not prepare you to direct your own work through the path of entrepreneurship or simply doing what you want to do." "Self-education is the process of learning what's necessary to achieve self-generated goals. Again, no hoarding general knowledge to feel like you're learning." **Fuente:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md. The definition is precise in two ways. It ties learning to a goal, which is why the author's theory of learning (Part VI) depends on his theory of goals (Part IV). And it excludes the accumulation of information for its own sake — what he later calls "tutorial hell" (Chapter 14). The default path, in other words, is not escaped by learning more of the same kind of thing that school taught, but by learning toward a goal one has chosen.

### 2.3 The Job: Modern Slavery and the Eternal Known

If school installs the hierarchy of assigned goals, the job is where it settles. The author's critique of employment is the most polarizing part of his output, and also the one where his position oscillates most visibly. The same video can call the 9-to-5 "the worst outcome" and, minutes later, "a stepping stone." Rather than resolve the oscillation prematurely, this section first lays out the components of the critique — control, the arithmetic of time, the metaphor of slavery, the psychology of a plateau — and then shows how the author himself qualifies it.

#### Conditioned to work for everyone but ourselves

The general thesis appears in an excerpt the author read from a book he was preparing: "We have been conditioned since birth to work for everyone but ourselves. We are given assignments, paths that have already been trodden. We work on these assignments without struggle or conscious thought, leading to a mechanical and replaceable role in society, filled to the brim with people who try to prove their happiness to hide their internal misery." The sequence begins at school — "we're told to read books we don't care about, to complete projects we don't care about, to prepare ourselves for a life we don't care about" — and continues into adult life: "before you know it you are trapped in a **dense cloud of responsibilities**," the time, energy and money that could change your life "are exhausted like clockwork," and "you have no choice but to remain a **productive robot** as society's plan for you intended." **Fuente:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md.

In the same passage he gives a deliberately unflattering definition: "A job is some unpleasant work you do for someone else for the sole purpose of making money. A job is a survival mechanism. A job is one milestone on the path to living up to those who shaped your mind." And he compares it to school: in both cases "good marketing can make up for a bad product." "After centuries of failing to get results, things like schools are still alive and well for the simple reason that very few people go through the trouble of thinking for themselves. Most people do what most people do. Most people aren't okay with the results of most people, but by that point it's much harder to escape." **Fuente:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md. The definition is rhetorical rather than neutral — it describes the job as experienced by someone who does not want it — and should be read alongside the concessions below.

#### The arithmetic of a working life

Much of the author's case rests on a simple accounting of time, which he repeats in several versions. The earliest (2022): working eight hours a day means roughly 70% of your life is not your own, leaving about 30% to live on your own terms — and you are only "possibly" set up for success after 65. This reflection on the **conventional career path** ("go to school, get a job, retire") made him ask why one would do all this "to *possibly* get a job in this market, to *possibly* enjoy life," and was a major reason he decided not to follow it. **Fuente:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md.

The most developed version divides life into thirds. "If you hate your work and it comprises one third of your life and it drains your energy to enjoy the other third, and you are asleep the other third, there doesn't seem to be a higher priority than to create, build, design, write, sell, invest, own, experiment and discover a way to control what you do with your day." **Fuente:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md. In 2026: one-third of life doing unchosen work, one-third mentally exhausted, one-third asleep, "for 40+ years." Elsewhere he says "work consumes about 25 percent of your life if you do the math," and asks whether you would rather spend that share making discoveries in the unknown, with meaning, or in predictable sameness, travelling only briefly because you must go back, with things out of your control. **Fuente:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md. The figures differ (70%, a third, 25%) because they count different things — waking hours, total hours, the share drained after work — and the author does not reconcile them. The argument does not depend on the precise number but on the structure: unchosen work consumes not only its own hours but the energy of the hours around it.

The thirds also appear in a personal story, presented as the origin of one of his "traps" to avoid: "do everything in your power to not get a job." Around age fifteen he had an insight he cannot trace to any source: "if I got a job like most people, I would end up like most people." The reasoning: a job takes a third of your time, which you could use to better yourself and create work you enjoy so that you do not hate a third of your life; another third is spent in a low-energy state after work, drained from putting energy into something you hate; and "you become what you focus on," so focusing on something you hate makes you become something you hate. **Fuente:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md. The phrase "you become what you focus on" links the critique of employment to the theory of attention in Part III: what occupies attention shapes identity.

#### Who controls what pays the bills

A second line of argument concerns control. "You only gain full control over your time and income when you realize that entrepreneurship is the only path to do so." At a job "you are not in charge of the product, the task you are assigned or the customer acquisition process"; "your employer is in charge of everything that pays the bills, therefore they are in charge of your entire life." The chain is causal: every action is tied to money, and money to survival. Whether you can go on vacation depends on time and money; your energy depends on fitness, rest and nutrition; if you cannot afford proper food, have no time to cook, or work so much that stress keeps you from sleeping, "it just gradually ruins your life." Hence "your employer controls your entire life." **Fuente:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md. In the same video he immediately qualifies that "there's degrees to this" — not every employer exerts the same control.

A related point concerns earning power. Having learned the skills that pay most, you "realize that you have a hard limit on your earning potential that your employer sets for you." "How much you earn depends on the vessel you choose for that skill, and a job usually isn't the best long-term vessel." The skill is the same; the container — job, freelancing, own business — sets the ceiling. **Fuente:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md. Here "vessel" again carries the author's sense of a vehicle or container, in this case for a skill.

Dickie Bush, a guest, describes the same frustration from the inside. On Wall Street he worked fourteen- to fifteen-hour days and learned as much as he could, yet "there was literally no way I could have done one tenth the effort" and earned differently: people who worked far harder or far less "made the same." That never sat well with him. "I sought the ability to have control over my income again where inputs directly led to outputs" — in a sense, getting back to being a tutor. His original goal for Ship 30 for 30 was modest, $10,000 in a year, an extra thousand or so a month, which he calls "a dream," against the assumption that multi-seven-figure founders begin with grand ambitions. **Fuente:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. The principle — seek a model where inputs lead directly to outputs — is Bush's, offered as his own experience; the author uses it as corroboration.

The author's own version is based on observation rather than experience. "I could just observe. I could just see that other people had a life that I could potentially want. And if other people had done it, then that means there is a series of steps that I can personally take," especially with technology, the internet and social media, "to achieve close to the same thing; not even the full thing, but I could at least have some control over my money. Therefore I could have some control over my day, my routine, my mind, my emotions, and especially my time." **Fuente:** What It Takes To Get Rich In Your 20s.md. The reasoning is simple but foundational: an outcome that someone else achieved is evidence that a path exists, and control over money is the lever for control over everything that money conditions.

From these premises he draws a strong conclusion: "If you truly think about what you want in life, starting a business is not optional. A business is the only thing that gives you control over your time." With a job, "you're going to be destined to pursuing the goals that are assigned to you for the rest of your life." He then immediately clarifies: "I'm not saying jobs are bad, I've never said that." A job is "a stepping stone to learn and acquire skills to eventually be able to go and do your own thing and survive along the way." The problem is getting trapped because it is comfortable, then locked in by responsibilities — family, bills, mortgage — with no way out, and then justifying "that that's what they wanted their entire life." What people really want, by nature, is "autonomy, work you enjoy and to have an impact on the world," to fulfil "your human need to be a valuable contribution to humanity." And a business, he insists, "is not some capitalistic pursuit to own a private jet and mansion; a business is how you solve problems, how you build something of your own, how you contribute to humanity through value exchange, how you take back control of your life, how you develop self-confidence in your interest and expertise." **Fuente:** It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md. **Value exchange** — giving something of value and receiving value in return, "the lifeblood of any society" — is a recurring term of the author's, developed in Chapters 31 and 34.

#### Modern enslavement: the metaphor of the slave

The author's most provocative claim is that most employment is a form of slavery. He builds it in two distinct registers, and it matters to keep them apart.

The first, from 2023 (repeated in 2024), is structural and is named **modern enslavement**. The hierarchical structures of society "present a hierarchy of goals that help frame our attention." In a world of overwhelm, uncertainty and insecurity, the masses flock to whatever most likely heals uncertainty or orders their mind — usually an external, ready-made hierarchy of goals such as the corporate ladder — "because they don't have clarity on anything else." They have not been exposed to another path that makes sense, with clarity about how to reach it, so they believe it impossible. "This is the definition of modern enslavement." The author explicitly rejects a conspiratorial reading: "I don't believe that like someone at the top is just planning being a puppet master." It is human nature, the hierarchical way nature is structured, and the way we focus attention — all normalized and conditioned over time "to the point where we are born as slaves unless we take it on ourselves to free or break those chains," because social conditioning since birth narrows the mind to see the world as everyone else does. Investing our "psychic or mental energy" into these hierarchies of goals becomes automatic, partly to maintain them: "if society collapses then we all collapse." To the objection "someone has to do this job," he replies that this is what creative problem-solving is for; if enough people decide not to take the traditional route and solve problems instead, "the universe will correct itself," perhaps through bad times — which, from a universal perspective, are "part of the process of life, birth and death." **Fuente:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. The video opens with a pull-quote of uncertain origin: "Nobody: I want to be trained into a job I hate, work for 40 years and get angry at a TV until I die. Ninety percent of the population does exactly that. Wake up, it's not too late."

In this first register, "slave" is almost descriptive. "You can opt for doing nothing, you really can, you can be a sheep, you can be an NPC... but you have to understand that you are a human and that you are not going to escape survival. If you opt for doing nothing you are automatically a slave." "You don't have to tie a negative connotation with slave... that's not necessarily a bad thing, in certain cases it can be." And: "don't take what I'm saying as absolutely literal... like a slave from a thousand years ago; we're talking metaphorically here, in terms of the mind, not physicality." **Fuente:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. The logic follows from Chapter 1: since all action serves some goal and survival cannot be escaped, declining to choose one's goals means serving goals set by others. **NPC** (non-player character), the video-game term for a character who follows a script, was introduced in Chapter 1 as the opposite of the "main character."

The second register, from 2023 onward, is financial and more precise. "You are a slave and I'm a slave, and slaves don't know that they're slaves if everyone else is too." "Just because the slavery that everyone knows and hates of the past isn't a thing anymore doesn't mean that slavery doesn't still exist; I'm talking about mental and financial slavery here." He reads a dictionary definition — "slave, noun: a person who is forced to work for and obey another and is considered to be their property" — and singles out its key word: "force." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md.

He then redefines **force**, a common word that he uses in a specific sense, from the dictionary entry "strength or energy as an attribute of physical action or movement": "there is something that is pushing you to do one thing over another thing, and the stronger that something is that is forcing you in the other direction, the more of a slave you are." The historical analogy he offers is central to the argument: a slave of the past "could, if they wanted, run away, but they would be forced back because of death: they would run out of shelter, they would run out of food, they would run out of water." "They were slaves not only because they were chained and held there by force, but they were also forced because their survival depended on it." So "survival plays a huge role in modern slavery." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. The step is subtle: by locating the essence of slavery in dependence for survival rather than in chains, the author can apply the word to anyone whose survival depends on a single employer.

From there he arrives at an operational definition. "Most workers are the literal definition of a slave: if you cannot stop showing up to work for your survival, and have no skills to open other options immediately, and you are the property or employee of a certain company, that's a slave." He puts a figure on it — "95 percent of nine to five workers are slaves, and your feelings do not matter here" — which is his own claim, with no source given. And he anticipates the reaction: "If you do feel threatened by someone talking to you through a screen, then maybe there is some truth to what I'm saying; you would only feel threatened if your survival were at stake; so look inward to see what you're hiding from." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md.

The 2026 formulation compresses all this into one sentence and adds the identity mechanism. "**Wage slavery** is doing meaningless grind work you didn't choose for someone else just to survive." "If you cannot stop showing up to work without catastrophe and if you have no skills to create alternatives, you meet the definition of a slave, no matter if your feelings tell you otherwise." The problem extends beyond wages: "we are all slaves, usually to ideology and belief systems." And because "slaves don't know they're slaves," a person who has identified with their job will take the argument as an attack: their identity, "your **mental body**," tries to preserve its survival, producing a threat response with no physical threat present — and wanting to argue "only proves my point further." **Fuente:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md. "Mental body" is the author's term for identity as a non-physical body that defends itself, the core concept of Chapter 3; here it explains why the critique of employment provokes such strong reactions.

The same pattern of reasoning appears in a story about his own reaction. On a YouTube community post asking "what is one thing that you find overrated but most people love," the top comment said entrepreneurship: great if you have the skills, but "work is work"; "I'm sick of being told I'm a wage slave for choosing to work a nine to five; most of these entrepreneurs are really just unemployed." He found it "very loaded, very assumptive," though understandable given the people who flex big numbers online. "This irked me at first... my defense systems immediately started going off," producing objections in his mind. He hates getting trapped in social comments, "but our brain loves it," so he turned it into a video. **Fuente:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md. The anecdote is instructive because the author applies to himself the same mechanism he attributes to the defensive employee: a threat to one's identity triggers defences.

His replies to the comment are two objections. The first: "people hate what they don't understand." The commenters have not been entrepreneurs and do not understand "the nuances of the brain that you pick up through daily experience." Online you see maybe 1% or less of someone's life, or 5% of their business, and take that as enough to judge without experience. Observing society should serve to correct your own actions, not to reinforce what you were told and justify it as "I chose to do this" when the choice is tied to money. "They think entrepreneurship is overrated because they aren't entrepreneurs." The second reply repeats the financial definition — "your feelings aren't reality: if you got let go and you could not pay the bills then you are by definition a modern slave" — and adds a historical prediction: "slave doesn't have to be good or bad"; we agree past slavery was bad, but "we will look back on the Industrial Age and realize that slavery was the norm, and that is what AI is coming in to fix." Being forced to work for survival removes choice: your choices align with unconscious survival goals. Most people are also unconscious of what happens behind the scenes at their company — exploitation, paying pennies to sweatshops, "a much worse form of slavery" — and becoming conscious of this and continuing anyway is "a terrible thing." He qualifies: not all companies, though mega-corporations and chains like McDonald's serve "the worst food for health." You cannot control any of this at the start; that is the self-actualization journey, and helping create a better world is what makes life meaningful. **Fuente:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md. The theme of unwittingly contributing to harm returns in 2.5 as "unconscious destruction."

The author's use of the word changes over time. In 2023 "slave" is "not necessarily a bad thing." By December 2024 it is normative: "if we want to define what a slave is, it's being average." He notes that about 1% of people get upset when he uses the language, which he takes as proof of his point, since they close their minds. He includes himself: "we're all slaves in our own regard; it's not physical, it's mental." He says he is not trying to make anyone feel bad — "you make yourself feel bad by how you perceive it" — and that his intention is awareness; he admits he is exaggerating, since no one is in physical chains, though metaphorically anyone could be in some area. "Most slaves don't know they're slaves because it's just the norm." Awareness of what you do not want — being average and its ramifications — is necessary because "you need to move away from something in order to move towards something." **Fuente:** How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md. The shift is from a descriptive definition (the consequence of not choosing) to a normative one (the average, to be moved away from). The second sense anticipates the **anti-vision** of Chapter 7: knowing what you are moving away from as fuel for moving toward something.

Finally, the author repeatedly positions himself against both camps of the online debate. "I'm not anti-nine to five, I'm not anti-working a job; I think it's very useful; I think it's an absolutely incredible path to go down if you want to climb up the ranks and get a high-paying job and you love that job." He "worked a nine to five for a good amount of my life." His perspective "always splits people into two camps," but "you don't have to fall within either of those camps; you can agree with both sides." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. And later in the same video: "I'm tired of both sides of the argument: one side is like all nine to five workers are slaves, and then the other side is like no no it's okay, you're not a slave, don't worry, you're perfect the way you are. Both are wrong and both are right in some cases, because when people say this online both are trying to maximize the engagement they get; they don't zoom out and think if people are actually slaves." His principle: "a published idea without depth is a disservice to humanity." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. The reader may notice that the "95% are slaves" claim and "I'm not anti-nine to five" come from the same video; the author does not reconcile them, though the definitions suggest a reconciliation: the objection is not to employment as such but to dependence on it without alternatives.

#### The 90/9/1 estimate

A more granular version of the "95%" appears in 2024 as the **90/9/1** split, which the author explicitly presents as opinion. "I would argue that 90% of people are in jobs that they hate, and I would argue that statistics back that a select 9% of people are in jobs that they think they enjoy and they're okay with it and they're not trying to get out, but they would be better off if they got out. The last 1% are in the select few jobs where all of the **psychological markers** match perfectly and they thoroughly enjoy their job." The exception is explicit: "if you are in that 1% this doesn't apply to you; if you are absolutely happy and have the autonomy to do what you want in your life, keep the job." No specific statistic is cited. **Fuente:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. A month later he gives a slightly different version: 90% "publicly hate their jobs," 9% lie to themselves that they love their jobs or lack awareness of anything better because they never explored the unknown, and 1% have jobs that let them create the conditions for flow — and not everyone can have those jobs. **Fuente:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md.

These figures should be read as what they are. Across the corpus the author's percentages for "the masses" — 90%, 95%, "95 plus," 99%, 75–80% conventional, 50% conformist — measure different things (employment, thinking, childhood conditioning, developmental stages) and do not add up to a consistent dataset. In one place he says statistics support him without naming them; elsewhere he calls the figures impressions from observation. They are rhetorical estimates, not data. "Psychological markers" — the author's term for the conditions that must coincide for someone to enjoy a job — connects with the flow conditions of Chapter 10.

#### The psychology of employment: from the edge to the eternal known

The deepest part of the critique is not about money or control but about the psyche, and it is where the author's ambivalence becomes most explicit. In a 2023 video he admits that through questioning and observation he "conditioned myself to believe that getting a 9-to-5 job was the bane of my existence," "honestly the worst outcome." The reason: once people accepted a job offer — leaving college, in or near their late twenties — they seemed "doomed to a life of comfort," "obviously not all of the time but 95% plus of the time." They filled their plates with never-ending responsibilities — mortgage, spouse, kids — before having the financial capacity to devote time and energy to them; "they were quick to become an adult but were never able to reap the rewards of being one"; they "chained themselves to a paycheck" because they never made the choices that lead to more money and time. **Fuente:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md. He frames this as a self-conditioned belief, partly a personal stance; the "95%" is again an impression.

He then shows a graphic he calls **the psychology of employment**, repurposed from an earlier graphic about flow. The reconstruction from the transcript is as follows; the axes are not stated beyond skill and challenge (and, implicitly, meaning), so the details are partly ambiguous.

| Element of the graphic | What it represents |
|---|---|
| Solid line (employment) | At first, skill and challenge rise together; life is fairly meaningful because you are "branching into the unknown," "at your edge where meaning and fulfillment are." The longer you stay without seeking more challenge or skill, the line flattens. |
| Flattened region: "the eternal known" | Life becomes boring; you chase cheap dopamine; an "endless cycle of comfort." |
| Dotted line (entrepreneurship) | "The complete opposite," "the natural progression": skill and challenge keep rising, with highs and lows (revenue, growth) that force you to evolve in an ever-changing marketplace. |
| Circle at the intersection | "A great time to actually switch to entrepreneurship." |

The author's own career illustrates the intersection: he took a job at a web design agency while trying to freelance in web design, in order to learn the skills that would let him do it himself. He adds that entrepreneurship "flows in alignment with nature" because its ups and downs force adaptation, whereas a job with set tasks does not. **Fuente:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md.

**The eternal known** is the author's coined term for this flattened region: the repetitive comfort zone where "you can't make new discoveries." It is the negative image of the edge of challenge and skill where, in his theory of flow (Chapter 10), meaning lives. A related, looser claim describes **the known** neurologically: at any moment one brain hemisphere "controls the known" — "it's either the right or the left, it's one of them" — and that known is all you operate within when you are calm, at home, going about your routine; in that state you rarely become aware of new potentials. The author himself presents the neuroscience loosely, and it should be read as an image rather than a finding; its role in his argument is to set up why a problem-solver mindset is needed. **Fuente:** Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md.

Immediately after the graphic, in the same video, the position changes: "I see 9-to-5 jobs as a **stepping stone**; my problem with them is that they breed complacency." A job is not inherently evil; the problem is that "challenge is what makes life fun and interesting," and if you halt your personal evolution by never pursuing more, you lose purpose and fulfillment. Hence, in this video, "entrepreneurship is the only logical option for long-term thinkers." **Fuente:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md.

A 2025 version (repeating the same graphic) locates the problem in time. When the novelty and challenge of school and job advancement stop in the mid-twenties, meaning goes with them. School did not feel purposeless because it offered progression; a new job brings challenge and skills to learn (some of them annoying — "that's life"), and that is where novelty, challenge and fulfilment come from. Once it dies down and you plateau, it disappears and you are stuck in a "repetitive and mundane robotic life." We do not take that as the sign to branch off and self-educate further, which would be the natural path. "When that mundane and robotic life starts to set in, that's the best time to pivot off." He specifies: not "never work a job," but if you want control over lifestyle, novelty, challenge, meaning and fulfilment, "entrepreneurship is the only logical option." **Fuente:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md. The **plateau**, in his specific sense, is the end of novelty and progression in a job, and the moment to change course.

The fullest psychological explanation comes in 2025, prefaced by a disclaimer: it is easy to lump him into the "all 9-to-5s are bad" camp, which is not his belief, but he understands psychology "to a reasonable extent." As a child "there is a magic to life because everything is new." "Schools simulate this desire to hunt for good dopamine by providing a video-game-like progression to learning" — grade one, grade two, grade three. Your first job is challenging; "you develop skill to cure your anxiety," get promotions and raises. "Then when you reach as far as that career ladder will allow you, the challenge and novelty cease to exist. Your days turn into a blur of repetition. This is incredibly damaging to your psyche." You get stuck in the defined and known "because a monthly paycheck is addictive and you max out your lifestyle to fit what that paycheck allows." Since most people will not get the elite jobs that keep the ladder going, his advice is to take your skills and interests and do your own thing, "so you're in control of the novelty, meaning, fulfillment of your life." **Fuente:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md. "Good dopamine" — dopamine earned through effort and discovery — is the subject of Chapter 11; the point here is that school and early employment provide it artificially through a ladder, and the supply stops when the ladder does.

The "addictive paycheck" has a counterpart in a concept Dickie Bush describes from his own life: the default track of working the job, slowly increasing income, and "slowly inflate your lifestyle so you're always stuck... you make more but you accumulated more recurring costs... boom, you're 35, you're 40... **golden handcuffs**." Bush is grateful he joined Twitter two and a half years earlier, because exposure to other ideas reshaped his trajectory, and he insists "it's by no means too late to start." The author adds a caution against passive consumption of such conversations: listening "is not going to do much aside from prime you to start... it's up to you to start and then figure out the rest as you go and learn from the people that you resonate most with." **Fuente:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

#### The path of uncertainty

Against the eternal known the author sets **the path of uncertainty**, a passage he reuses almost verbatim in 2023, 2024 and 2025. "When you get stuck in a nine to five or freelance work or agency work or any of the new digital nine to fives, you start to live in the eternal known. You can't make new discoveries in the known; you can't hunt; you get bored, depressed and see life as meaningless, because the only dopamine you get is from superficial sources." "You never take risks, push into the unknown and discover new knowledge, tools and potentials that raise dopamine levels in your brain. You must evolve." Note that the critique extends beyond employment to freelancing and agency work — what the author calls the **new 9-to-5**, models that reproduce the logic of employment online (Chapter 30). Then: "Entrepreneurship is the path of uncertainty, like slashing your way through the jungle: you are required to learn skills that aren't taught in schools; to be okay with failure, rejection and slow progress; to learn from your mistakes, show up again tomorrow and push until you strike gold." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md.

The 2024 version adds that this is "required in other words to be human," and that as an entrepreneur you "hunt for survival" by gathering knowledge, creating a valuable product and giving it to people who would benefit. It also addresses readers with a bad relationship with money or business: people actually want to buy products that benefit their lives; if a product hurts people, obviously do not sell it, "but that's not all that business is." **Fuente:** Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md.

The paradox at the heart of this section is that uncertainty is presented as more secure. Education businesses, he argues, are the future of schooling and a good place to bank one's future: "more secure, though uncertain." Entrepreneurship is uncertain, but "that's what meaningful living is, being constantly exposed to novel experiences; entrepreneurship is the vessel to do that." "Your ancestors were entrepreneurs serving their community." Meanwhile "robotic living in a cubicle," the same day in and out, is "the least safe and least secure path you can take, especially for your psyche and your mental health." **Fuente:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md. Safety, in this argument, is measured psychologically rather than financially — a move developed in 2.6 as "the illusion of certainty."

The 2025 version links the path to the mind's need for challenge. People climb ladders — the corporate ladder among them — because the mind craves challenge, but they get used to it and it becomes boring; so do vacations and retirement. You need the next challenge, which usually involves work; but most people hate their work, so they do not believe good work exists, or that they can create it, pursue their life's work and enjoy and control it. "Entrepreneurship is the only logical option for long-term thinkers. It's the path of uncertainty requiring skills not taught in schools. You must be okay with failure, rejection, and slow progress." "The secret is cultivating a skill set so impactful that you can't help but share it. You solve your own problems, sell the solution, and improve humanity. That's entrepreneurship." And: "Employment isn't our natural state. Your psyche is wired to hunt. But today's threats are psychological and spiritual, not physical. Nobody wants to be a monkey in a cubicle." **Fuente:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md. The hunting psyche is the subject of the next section; "solve your own problems and sell the solution" is the business principle of Chapter 28.

A small textual detail shows the evolution of the slogan. In 2023 the author said, referring to his "value creator" videos, that "entrepreneurship is the only long-term option for logical thinkers," without further argument in that transcript. **Fuente:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md. By 2025 the formula is inverted: "the only logical option for long-term thinkers." The inversion shifts the emphasis from logic to time horizon — from "if you reason well, you will choose it" to "if you think in decades, you will choose it."

#### The author's agency job: the ticking clock

The author's own experience of employment supplies the concrete texture of this section. He studied programming because he learned he could do it without a degree, as a backup plan, trusting that he was good at self-education. He got "a very base-level web design job at an agency in Arizona," and spent the first hours of each day on his own projects. He describes "the brutal reality of working a desk job": "most of your work is just procrastination." Most people, with exceptions, can work on their own things, procrastinate until the end, then copy and paste templates — in his case for the e-commerce stores the agency served — and get paid. He knew that if he stayed too long he would get comfortable — "the wife, the mortgage, the kids, the responsibilities," with nothing wrong in any of them — and that this would "pile-drive me into the state of not having any time, money, or energy," and life would "go on autopilot." **Fuente:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md. **Autopilot** in this 2025 sense means the state in which the comfort of employment and its accumulated responsibilities leave a life without time, money or energy.

When he got the job he felt that "the clock would start ticking down toward my demise." He had stayed for a fifth year of college, taking out loans to delay employment while frantically trying business models; from observation he believed that once employed it was downhill — responsibilities stack up and "I'd fall into the **conventional life path**." Through experimentation and curiosity he found his way. At the job he procrastinated on his assigned work until the last minute so he could focus on freelance web design, sensing he was close. After a few pivots it worked: typical websites first, clients from referrals, then a more specific system for a more specific target, built from skills he had meshed together — "similar to what he teaches now." Even so, as a full-time freelancer he hated working on projects that were not his own, which is when he turned to social media. **Fuente:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md.

The job also cost him something he valued. After a few months in any job, once trained, the challenge plateaus: clock in, do tasks, clock out — and somehow you are exhausted. At the agency, while desperately trying to get out, he stopped wanting to go to the gym, although health and fitness had been a lifelong love. The first time he skipped was after work, exhausted from sitting in a cubicle all day: "the fluorescent lights above me were just sucking the life out of me." You get bored, it is against your nature, and attention drifts to "what else could I be doing?", which for most people means opening the phone and "rotting your brain." Rarely, he adds, does a job require consistent skill improvement to match greater challenge. **Fuente:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md. Another telling gives the schedule that made the gym impossible: an hour's commute, leave at 8, arrive at 9, clock out at 6 (the lunch hour does not count toward the nine-to-five), ten minutes to the gym, an hour of training, an hour's drive home, then bed — because health was a value. It was the only time in his life he skipped the gym. "That sounds horrible." **Fuente:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md.

#### Reading the oscillation

Across 2022–2026 the author's statements about employment range from "do everything in your power to not get a job" and "95 percent of nine to five workers are slaves" to "jobs are an absolutely incredible stepping stone," "keep the job" if you are the fortunate 1%, "not never work a job," and, in 2026, jobs as "very worthy stepping stones for skill acquisition and experience" alongside the insistence that wage slavery is real. There is no clean chronological turn. The tone oscillates between total rejection and the stepping stone; there is a mild trend from "the only path" (2023–2024) toward admitting paths inside organizations (2025); and the absolute condemnation is presented, by the author himself, as a belief he conditioned into himself.

The reasons he gives point to a reconciliation he never states as such. The object of the critique is not the job as such but three things: permanence (the job as destination rather than stage), complacency (the plateau after which challenge and evolution stop), and dependence (no skills or alternatives if the job disappears). He addresses people who have "that desire for more"; he exempts those who are genuinely happy and autonomous; and his own life — an agency job used to learn skills while building something of his own — is the model of the job as stepping stone. The rejection is his personal rule and his advice to those who want autonomy; the stepping stone is his concession to everyone else. Both versions are his.

### 2.4 The Hunting Psyche in a Cubicle

The previous section explained why the author thinks employment harms the psyche: the plateau, the eternal known, the end of challenge. This section gives the underlying model that explains why a plateau should hurt at all. Its core claim is evolutionary in form: the human psyche was shaped for a way of life that modern work has removed, and entrepreneurship restores it. The reader should keep in mind that the author presents this as a framing — he insists his central image is "a simile, not an insult," and his appeals to science are general — rather than as a demonstrated scientific theory.

#### The monkey in a cubicle

The image appears as early as 2022 and recurs through 2025. Its most complete version is a chain of reasoning. First, "your psyche is wired to hunt." Second, the physical world has changed much faster than our minds can adapt: we have the same neurobiological processes as our ancestors. Third, therefore, if you stay in a 9-to-5 too long you become "like a monkey who lives in the jungle that is trapped in a fluorescent-lit box — is that healthy for the monkey?" The author stresses that this is a simile, not an insult. Fourth, stuck in a 9-to-5 or a "new 9-to-5" — an agency, freelancing — you live in the eternal known, where "you can't make new discoveries"; you get bored, depressed and see life as meaningless because the only dopamine comes from superficial sources. Fifth, you never take risks or push into the unknown to discover the new knowledge, tools and potentials "that raise dopamine levels in the brain." Sixth, as an entrepreneur you "hunt for your survival" by gathering knowledge, creating a valuable product and putting it in front of people who could benefit. **Fuente:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md.

The **monkey in a cubicle** is one of the author's most persistent coined images. A 2024 version sharpens it with a thought experiment: "If you were to put a chimpanzee in a fluorescent-lit cubicle for 8 hours a day, do you think it would thrive? Of course not. It lacks the natural environment that contributes to its psychological well-being. You don't need a scientific paper to assume this." "Most people are monkeys in a cubicle: they have the mental wiring that our ancestors did, but they're in a new environment and nobody understands how to act within that environment." **Fuente:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. The phrase "you don't need a scientific paper" is revealing: the author offers the image as intuitive, not as research.

The same 2024 passage adds the mechanism that explains why people do not notice. "The psychological effects that come with boredom and a repetitive string of tasks slowly builds and builds, and because it slowly builds... you don't even notice it, so you're operating at this **Lower State of Consciousness** for so long that you just think it's normal." You no longer believe you could feel better, or that any opportunity is worth pursuing. At such a job "there is no more learning, there is no challenge, there is no growth" — and he qualifies: "of course this is for some jobs." **Fuente:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. The gradualness matters. It is the same structure as the conditioning of Chapter 1 and the "trained to bear the shocks" of 2.1: harm that accumulates slowly is perceived as the norm, which is why awareness (2.7) has to come before change.

The earliest, 2022 version states the positive side: we were not meant to be "metaphorical monkeys in cubicles," deprived of what we are wired to do. "We are wired to be entrepreneurs; entrepreneurs are creators." Social platforms now let anyone connect with the specific community they resonate with, which requires developing communication, writing and persuasive skills. **Fuente:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md. This early statement already contains the link the rest of the book will develop: from the hunting psyche to the creator, and from the creator to writing (Part VIII) and audience (Part IX).

#### What hunting means today

If the psyche is wired to hunt, what does hunting mean when no one needs to chase animals? The author's answer shifts slightly across versions but keeps a common core.

The 2023 version (repeated word for word in a 2024 compilation): "Your psyche isn't wired for modern life... not wired for being under fluorescent lights going and sitting in a cubicle." "A monkey would suffer in a cubicle because its psyche is not wired for that." "We need to hunt, and in this age what I mean by hunting is novel dopamine sources or meaningful dopamine sources" — something new in your life, discoveries, creativity — "being able to find something new, take it, create something with it, and that feels so good," which many people lack. Then the link back to Chapter 1: if "the repetitive NPC automaton lifestyle" — an automaton being "a little robotic doll thing" — feels normal to you, "you are by definition trapped in The Matrix, which is a metaphorical term for you are just enslaved and a slave to routine." **Fuente:** Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. Here **hunting** is defined as the search for novel and meaningful sources of dopamine: discovering something, taking it and creating with it. The Matrix, as Chapter 1 explained, is the author's metaphor for the inherited network of beliefs; in this passage it is equated with enslavement to routine.

The 2024 version turns hunting into the skill to learn. "The skill that you need to learn is hunting. You need to learn to hunt for your survival. You can't find enjoyment in life because you don't know how to hunt." The contrast is with assignment: "you're assigned material to read to pass your exams, you're assigned a routine to complete your tasks at a job, you're assigned beliefs and values to survive in your culture." "The problem with assigned goals is that the path to achieve them is known: you are doing something that's been done before, or you're repeating tasks that a visionary assigned to you to build their dream instead of your own." Then the definition: "to hunt is to discover knowledge, skills, ideas and opportunities that help you achieve the goals you set for yourself, and once those goals become known you must have another one ready so you don't get lost." **Fuente:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md.

This definition is the bridge between this chapter and the rest of the book. It shows why assigned goals fail psychologically — not because they are imposed, but because their path is already known, and a known path offers nothing to discover. And it explains why the author insists on self-generated goals (Part IV): only a goal whose path is unknown keeps the hunt alive. The final clause — have the next goal ready once the current one becomes known — anticipates the idea that life is a chain of problems (Chapter 8) and the infinite game (Chapter 10).

A practical consequence is the advice not to outsource one's curiosity. "Don't ask me for recommendations, that's against the point, that's not hunting." Lean into "your psyche's nature of wanting to hunt for ideas and knowledge and information in order to reap that good dopamine and make it a sustainable habit. Don't outsource your curiosity to me." He adds, while promoting his own programs, that courses are not necessary if you make time to study and practice, but they help those who know their goals, know the course aligns with them, and find it streamlined while it still emphasizes free and critical thinking. **Fuente:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md. The point is that finding the knowledge is part of the reward; being handed a reading list removes the hunt.

#### Become your own dopamine dealer

The author derives a practical method from the hunting model. He paraphrases an unnamed friend who said to him: "It's wild, I remember asking you why none of your friends take action or follow in your footsteps; I get it now: people are dopamine junkies, even businessmen. The people that don't take action don't know how to replace the dopamine they are getting from their current habits with business, so they either don't start or quit after a week because they couldn't get their hit." The author recognized what had worked for him: "I was constantly chasing the good old dopamine, but in the right direction." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. The observation about "dopamine junkies" is the friend's; the coined prescription — **become your own dopamine dealer**, replacing the hits of current habits with those of building and learning — is the author's.

The method has four parts. "Hold a loose idea of what you want in life: money, success, peace, fulfillment. Start building something, anything, that will make that a reality" — he recommends a one-person business "because it is long term." "Learn what you want to learn the most to help build it: skills, courses, websites, logos, whatever, so you get your dopamine hit." And "don't be afraid to change what you're doing if you get bored." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. The logic is that motivation does not have to be summoned by willpower if the activity itself produces the reward; the task is to arrange things so that building toward a goal is the source of stimulation.

One detail of this advice changes later. In 2023 he recommends learning "websites, logos" for the dopamine hit; in 2024 he calls focusing on website and logo among the things "you don't need when starting out." He gives no reason for the change. The two can be read as answers to different questions — what keeps a beginner engaged versus what a beginner's business actually needs — but the author does not draw that distinction himself. The relationship between dopamine and goals is treated fully in Chapter 11.

#### Entrepreneurship is modern survival

The hunting model culminates in one of the author's central coined formulas: **entrepreneurship is modern survival**. Its first version (2023) is an argument from ancestry. Our ancestors were, metaphorically, entrepreneurs who served their communities. In the past you belonged to a small, physical niche community, not an unlimited digital one, and you had a specific thing to provide: cook, naturopath who helped people heal, doctor, hunter, fire-maker, caretaker of the children. Things have shifted in tangible terms, but our psyche remains the same; therefore we have to fulfil the same needs through entrepreneurship. Later in the same video: "your psyche is wired to hunt; you are neurobiologically rewarded for hunting for your survival, not for being a monkey in a cubicle"; so hunt for money to ease survival-induced stress and open room for creativity. **Fuente:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md.

A month later he adds the dimension of purpose. "We have the psyche of our ancestors." The external world changed, but ancestors were "metaphorically entrepreneurs": entrepreneurship is a new form of contributing to a community, providing value in exchange for value. In tribes each person had a specific value skill — cooking, cleaning, childcare — that served the community. "If you're just doing the same repetitive task day in day out then life loses its weight; you have to have purpose, and entrepreneurship and modern business is the vessel for purpose." **Fuente:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md.

The compact definition: "As an entrepreneur you hunt for your survival by gathering knowledge, creating a valuable product and putting it in front of people that could benefit from it." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. The three components — knowledge, product, distribution — map almost exactly onto the structure of the later chapters: learning (Part VI), product (Part XI) and audience (Part IX).

In 2024 the image gains a territory and an instrument: "The internet is one big community." Your ancestors were entrepreneurs within their communities, each fulfilling the role they were best suited for. "Entrepreneurship is modern survival and the internet is your **hunting ground**." You hunt for deep knowledge by pursuing curiosity; "**dopamine is your compass**," but for this to make sense you will need to break free of your "drip-fed dopamine sedation of quick pleasures." **Fuente:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md. The compass image says that the reward signal can guide the hunt — but only once cheap, effortless sources of stimulation stop drowning it out. Another 2024 formulation uses the jungle metaphor of 2.3: "You have to branch out, you have to dive into the unknown, and entrepreneurship is the only way to do that: to slash through the jungle, to make new discoveries, to hunt for ideas and income, to fulfill the needs of the psyche that is wired the same as your ancestors." **Fuente:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md.

The 2026 version adds a warning about the present. "Entrepreneurship is in our nature. It is modern survival. We are wired to create and distribute value to a tribe of like-minded people. Now, that tribe is on the internet and you have to find them or attract them. We are wired to hunt, to explore the unknown, to seek novelty, and never stagnate. And psychologically, this is the most enjoyable way of life" — a claim he grounds in the psychology of flow. "The barrier of entry has collapsed," but it is not easy: you will not have AI "spit out 30 posts and go viral"; "it's all skill-based," though people with multiple interests have an advantage because they love learning. "I don't care how fast AGI is going to speed up things. It still takes time to get any form of result in life. If you were to just get it immediately, then it would become a commodity" — and, like scrolling, it would bring no meaning. **Fuente:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md. The final point is a subtle defence of difficulty: meaning depends on effort, so a result obtained without effort would lose the very thing that made it worth pursuing.

#### Even the perfect job is not yours

The last piece of the argument addresses the strongest objection: what about someone who has found a good job? The author's answer appeals to creation and to the intrinsic drivers. Humans have a unique ability to create — "the gift of God within us to create," which "feels amazing" — and creating for someone else, with a purpose assigned by them, is different. He claims there is "so much scientific literature... about how the psyche is wired that all point to entrepreneurship," but names no source. The perfect gig is increasingly hard to find, and "even the perfect gig isn't perfect": there will be something you hate, and because it is not yours you cannot change it, nor can you leverage the **intrinsic drivers** that make life good — curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy, mastery — "not extrinsic, not something being assigned to you." **Fuente:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md.

The list of five intrinsic drivers comes from the psychology of flow; in early versions the author attributes it to Steven Kotler, later he uses it without attribution. **Contexto complementario:** Steven Kotler, a writer on flow and peak performance, describes curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy and mastery as intrinsic motivators that can be "stacked" to drive flow; the model also draws on Daniel Pink's work on autonomy, mastery and purpose. The drivers are developed in Chapter 10.

This argument complements, rather than contradicts, the 1% exception of 2.3. There the author conceded that a few people have jobs where all the psychological markers match; here he adds that even then the job is not theirs to reshape. The two together define the author's position: a good job is possible, but ownership — the ability to change the game you are playing — is what employment structurally cannot give.

### 2.5 Pyramids of Domination, Pyramids of Attention and Status Games

School and the job are particular institutions. Behind them the author sees a general shape: the pyramid. This section collects his account of hierarchies — the kind that dominate and the kind that develop — and two extensions of it: society as a pyramid of attention, and status as a game everyone plays. Together they explain why climbing an existing ladder is a poor strategy, and what the alternative looks like.

#### Two kinds of hierarchy

"Everything is structured as a hierarchy," the author says, and hierarchies come in two types. The first are **dominator hierarchies**, "which give the word hierarchy a bad reputation": pyramid-scheme-like structures whose main feature is that "there are more people with less power at the bottom and then there are less people with more power at the top" — and, in a true pyramid scheme, the investors at the bottom are what enable this distribution of power. The second are natural or **actualization hierarchies**, ordered by "increasing wholeness," where the whole at one level becomes a part of the whole at the next: particles, atoms, cells, organisms; letters, words, sentences, paragraphs. **Fuente:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md.

The distinction is adapted from Ken Wilber, and the author uses it as the lens for society. Society mimics hierarchical structure, but — "context dependent" — it mostly falls under domination. Its parts — public schools, government, corporate jobs, even some religious institutions (which he considers more a matter of culture, but still parts and wholes) — are "hierarchies within hierarchies," each built as a pyramid. **Fuente:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md.

**Contexto complementario:** Ken Wilber, in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality* (1995) and *A Brief History of Everything* (1996), distinguishes healthy "actualization" or "growth" hierarchies, in which each level transcends and includes the previous one (a "holarchy"), from pathological "dominator" hierarchies, in which one part tries to dominate the whole; he credits the terminology largely to the cultural historian Riane Eisler. The concepts of holon and "transcend and include" are developed in Chapter 38.

The author's lexicon records two variants of this pair. In one, dominator and actualization hierarchies are described in Wilber's terms (pyramid of power versus growing wholeness). In the other, which is more the author's own, a "dominance hierarchy" is someone else's power structure and an "actualization hierarchy" is one's own series of goals-as-problems increasing in creativity, fulfilment and impact. The second sense is the one that matters most in what follows.

#### Climbing the ladder: bad odds

Inside a dominator hierarchy, the standard advice is to climb. In corporate jobs or school "you're taught to climb the ladder through hard work and usually manual labor." But "it's close to impossible to reach the actual top of the pyramid if you go about it this way"; you usually get stuck at one level. "If there's 10 people at the top and a million people at the bottom... those are just bad odds." Not everyone even tries to climb every ladder — most people in a church are not trying to become pastors. Those who want out "have to create their own": start their own business, "create their own actualization hierarchy to escape a dominance hierarchy." And such hierarchies "are created every single day" — "they transcend and include after creative emergence." **Fuente:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. "**Climb the ladder**" is a common phrase the author uses in a specific sense: rising by hard work inside someone else's pyramid, with poor odds.

The 2026 version adds a historical claim and a clarification. "Civilization was quite literally built by tribes enslaving other tribes. That dynamic never disappeared. Instead, it abstracted into employment, law, and culture." "Society has essentially become a pyramid scheme because there are more people at the bottom than at the top"; it is mathematically impossible for everyone to be at the top — one boss, several employees who depend on the boss for survival. You must leave this pyramid behind — but not hierarchies altogether: "you're going to create your own." **Fuente:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md. The clarification matters. The author is not an anti-hierarchy thinker; he argues for changing which hierarchy you are in, from one you climb to one you build. The historical claim about tribes is stated as background, not argued in detail.

The objection "someone has to do this job," and the author's reply that this is what creative problem-solving is for — "if enough people do this then the universe will correct itself," with bad times being, from a universal perspective, part of "the process of life, birth and death" — was presented in 2.3 as part of his account of modern enslavement. **Fuente:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md. It is worth noticing here that the reply moves into a second register that coexists with the critical one throughout the corpus: a cosmic acceptance in which "who's to say that Mother Nature is wrong for it being what it is today," and in which, in 2026, "I'm not against capitalism. I think it's going to evolve into something better." The critical register motivates individual change; the cosmic register removes drama and blame. The author does not reconcile them.

#### The collective societal vision and the two-step exit

What keeps people climbing a pyramid whose odds are bad? The author's answer is a vision that no one in particular chose. "The collective societal vision for your future is to get good grades, maybe get a high-paying job, watch TV to ease your mind and maybe retire happily at 65," all driven by "the unconscious seeking of status, survival and approval." Through conditioning it becomes habit. "We go on to mimic others... we become a product of others," chase **status games**, buy fancy cars, invest our attention all day to make the money back, and "never think to consult or create and then consult our own values, vision, wants, needs and potential impact on the world." **Fuente:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. The **collective societal vision** is the author's name for this inherited picture of a good life; it is the social-scale counterpart of the default path.

The exit has two steps, which the author states as a process. "We escape this modern enslavement, one, by becoming aware" — which is what the video itself tries to do — "and then the second thing is by creating your own actualization hierarchy to order your mind and create a better future for yourself and those around you." An actualization hierarchy "will present a series of goals, which are problems, increasing in creativity along the way, and fulfillment and impact," potentially spreading to others. You work your way up: goals imply problems; you solve them over and over; that is where you invest your attention, changing your life and affecting others. **Fuente:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md.

This two-step process is the structural outline of the whole book. Step one — awareness — is the subject of 2.7 and closes Part I. Step two — creating one's own hierarchy of goals that orders the mind — is the work of Parts III and IV, where goals are explained as lenses (Chapter 6), as problems (Chapter 8) and as the structure of a game (Chapter 10). The phrase "to order your mind" anticipates the concept of order in consciousness (Chapter 5).

#### The system is rigged: learn the rules

A related principle, from 2022, frames the same situation as a game. In an open-world strategy game like *World of Warcraft*, players follow common patterns: stack gold, choose a profession, level up a character. Life has a "macro game" with similar patterns. "The system is rigged, you can't change it but you can learn it, that is how you rig yourself in the system's favor." The conventional path to success "has already been programmed into the collective psyche before we know it," due to what the author calls the **curse of knowledge**. He uses that phrase in his own sense, different from its usual meaning: the human drive to know and learn more — learning being the foundation of human experience — combined with not questioning what we learn. So before we know it we are pursuing a game others laid out, "usually in favor of maintaining the game itself." And "90% of the time it just doesn't work out that way": the path thought to be secure leaves "90% of people, or however many percent," with too much anxiety, stress and overwhelm, "trapped in this jail or prison of their own doing." **Fuente:** How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. The percentage is self-hedged.

**Contexto complementario:** In standard usage, the "curse of knowledge" is a cognitive bias described by economists Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber (1989): once you know something, it is hard to imagine not knowing it, which makes experts poor at explaining things to beginners. The author's usage is unrelated.

The principle has a practical corollary: "You can't break free in an instant because that's just not how things work. First, you learn the rules of the game and then you kind of create your own rules of the game without breaking the other rules." You play the game others want for you, as everyone does, learning from parents, "but then eventually you reflect and you question": what can I actually do, how can I create my own game and get better results than those before me? **Fuente:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md. This is the author's answer to the question of how rebellion should proceed: not by smashing the existing game but by mastering it enough to design a better one. It connects with his later idea that templates and frameworks are training wheels (Chapter 23) and with the stepping-stone view of employment in 2.3.

He also sees signs that the old game is breaking down. "The flaws in the system itself are revealing itself and the programmers can't patch the game fast enough" — "who are the programmers? I have no idea." More people work remotely and do their own thing. School curriculums cannot keep up with the creator economy, which educates people to do their own thing rather than training them into the conventional employment system to which school is tied. In banking, whatever one thinks of Bitcoin or decentralized currency, someone saw a problem and individuals and small teams are building solutions. Freelancers with digital skills are hired by big companies and individuals as contractors rather than employees. These, he says, are the people "actively trying to solve the problem of this faulty system." **Fuente:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. **Patch the game** is his coined image for the system's inability to fix its own flaws at the speed of change; it is labelled opinion and is developed historically in 2.6.

The 2022 formula — "rig yourself in the system's favor" — stands in tension with a 2025 formulation: "society is rigged against you," followed by a list of the ways (your boss, marketing, food, health, social circles, dating, politicians, therapists). The shift is from a strategic, playful attitude to an enumeration of how the system works against the individual. The two are compatible — the system is against you by default, which is why it pays to learn it — and the author's "first learn the rules, then create your own" is the bridge, but he does not make the connection himself.

#### Society as a pyramid of attention

The most original extension of the pyramid image appears in 2023: "Society is a pyramid scheme of attention." The premise is metaphysical in tone: "If something is not given attention it does not exist, and if it does not exist it must be given attention, and should be able to be given attention, in order to not only come to life but to survive." The author connects it to spiritual teachings: "If you stop paying attention to who you are and feeding those thoughts then you'd experience death, ego death; that's what you hear if you're familiar with spiritual teachings and meditation; that's what they're saying: stop giving life to the negative aspects of what you don't want to pay attention to." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. The **pyramid scheme of attention** is the author's coined term; the idea of ego death is taken generically from spiritual teachings and adapted.

From this premise he draws two realizations. The first concerns organizations. "How do megacorporations grow and thrive? They start small and they form a power hierarchy over time: fewer people with more power at the top and more people with less power at the bottom." The top tier — the company, its executives and board — "can only do what they do because a plethora of people are giving attention to the tasks, visions and values from the bottom up." This "pyramid of attention" lets those at the top focus on the big picture without doing the technical work. "What do you have to do first in order to work? It's pay attention. Attention is the root of all of it, so the company would collapse if there was no attention to build that company." Yet "often people aren't paying attention to what they're actually paying attention to; they don't see the depth behind their actions." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. The idea that attention is the root resource is the thesis of Chapter 6 ("attention is the RAM") and of Chapter 25 (attention as power).

The second realization follows from the shape of the pyramid. "At the bottom of the pyramid it's low-skill grunt work, like a cashier at McDonald's, and by nature it is impossible for every single person that is at the bottom to rise up to higher and higher levels, simply because there's less people" at the top; "there has to be the attention filled from under." "This is why I see many nine to five jobs as a stepping stone to doing what you want, or even job hopping until you get to where you want." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md. Here the stepping-stone view of employment is derived from structure rather than conceded as an exception: since the base of the pyramid must always be filled, the sensible use of a position there is as a transition.

The first realization also carries a moral charge: "you are contributing to **unconscious destruction**." "The people at the bottom are unconscious of what they're actually contributing to." "McDonald's workers probably don't understand or care that they are poisoning millions of people across the country," because "they probably aren't giving attention to the nutrition knowledge" needed to know what it takes to be healthy. "If they did have that education or health knowledge then they probably wouldn't be working at McDonald's"; they would be working as, or under, a nutrition coach or in other health and fitness jobs, "because they paid attention to the things that gave them that knowledge." Other examples: "military programmers write the code that kills millions of people on the other side of the planet," or "a web designer that creates websites for a person that scams people." He cites the saying "forgive them for they know not what they do." **Fuente:** How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md.

**Contexto complementario:** "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" is from the Gospel of Luke (23:34), spoken by Jesus at the crucifixion. The author cites it without naming the source.

The examples are deliberately provocative and the claim about poisoning is stated rhetorically, not supported. What the passage contributes to the argument is a concept: one's attention is a contribution, and directing it unconsciously can mean contributing to outcomes one would reject if one saw them. That is why, in the author's system, awareness of where attention goes is a moral as well as a practical matter.

#### The status game

The pyramid image can make status sound like the enemy. The author's treatment of status, from 2024, corrects that impression and adds nuance. "You can reject the status game, but by doing so you choose to be low status" — decreasing your impact, increasing your suffering, and never realizing that you still chase status. Status-seeking is "an immutable principle in human nature" since the dawn of time. "To think that you aren't chasing status as a noble pursuit is still chasing status" — just as the desire to kill the ego is still a desire. That is not bad; it is natural, and once you accept it you begin to be rewarded. People judge you by perceived status; not realizing this means accepting a life of being treated unfairly, with close to zero impact. **Fuente:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md. The **status game**, in this sense, is the competition for status inherent in human nature.

Status operates in every circle — your friends, your job or career, your religious organization. People assign it when they first see you, which is out of your control, but you can position yourself to make a high-status perception more likely. On that perception people decide whether to engage with you, offer opportunities, hire, pay or help you. Even the leaders we glorify — the author names Jesus Christ, Eckhart Tolle, Naval Ravikant, Alan Watts, Jordan Peterson, Ken Wilber, any thought leader — "are playing a status game whether they say they are or they aren't"; he doubts it is unintentional given their awareness, since how else would they attract large followings perceived as high status and change the direction of evolution? He rejects the edge case of high-status people who neglect inner work: status is one part of a good life, not the whole, and should not be pursued at all costs. "Money doesn't buy happiness" does not mean you should not try; refusing would close you to the experience of playing the game of life and pushing further toward depth and meaning. **Fuente:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md.

Why does it matter to be conscious of this? Because "contributing to humanity is the prerequisite to getting something from humanity," and status-seeking is natural. If it remains unconscious, "you will manipulate and cheat without knowing it; you will be the cause of evil without knowing it," because you will not learn the inner workings of your mind that would let you turn the status you create into something beneficial for others. Even a "consciousness scale" — a hierarchy of levels of consciousness — is a status hierarchy: telling someone to reach the highest level of absolute love is still persuading them it is worth pursuing. He does not say the scale is wrong; it is a tool, an ideology, a construct for moving in a better direction — as is understanding status. **Fuente:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md. The parallel with "unconscious destruction" is exact: in both cases the harm comes not from the drive itself but from its operating outside awareness.

**Contexto complementario:** The "scale of consciousness" most likely refers to David R. Hawkins's "Map of Consciousness" in *Power vs. Force* (1995), which ranks emotional and spiritual states numerically from shame to enlightenment.

Status, however, is not self-sufficient. "To become high status you must become valuable, or else that status is just an illusion and you're deceiving more than you are persuading" people to give you what you want. The author quotes *The Art of Focus*: "the path of the problem solver or value creator is how you escape the world of replaceability. Fall in love with the challenge that problems present, from superficial to metaphysical, and your ideal future will create itself." Value is created by solving problems, creating solutions, forging your own path and creating something unique with the story you carve out. **Fuente:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md. "The world of replaceability" ties back to the "replaceable unit of society" of 2.1: the escape from replaceability is not status but value. The **value creator** is a central figure of Chapter 35.

A concrete example shows the author playing the game consciously. "I drive a fairly nice car; that's the one thing that I allow myself to like splurge on": a Mercedes GT63 S, "pretty fast, it sounds good, it's matte black so it's on brand, but it is a status game and I'm aware of that, and I feel like if people are aware that they're playing status games then it helps you not become a slave to those things." The car, the way you dress, nice locations, living somewhere nice give "access to this network and opportunities that are just given to you if you look good." The contrast: if you look "like a homeless guy because you don't care in your hyper-spiritual hippie" way, "but you're struggling to make money and survive and deep down you actually hate your life, nobody's going to come up and talk to you." If you are presentable and "show that you invest in how people perceive you," people approach — "if a billionaire comes up and talks to you and gives you a million dollar opportunity, that's available to you now." He calls it "more of a luck game, but it is a game, and it affects your mindset; it's abundance, being able to spend this money knowing that you have the resources and knowledge and education to make it back." **Fuente:** A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md.

The example closes the loop of this section. The collective societal vision has people chasing status games unconsciously — fancy cars bought with money earned by spending attention all day. The author's car is the same object chosen knowingly. The difference he insists on is not the object but the awareness with which the game is played: "if people are aware that they're playing status games, then it helps you not become a slave to those things." That principle — the same action, transformed by awareness — is exactly what 2.7 sets out to explain.

### 2.6 From the Industrial Paradigm to the Information Age

The institutions described so far — school, the job, the pyramid — did not appear from nowhere. The author places them in history: they are the products of an industrial worldview that made sense in its time and is now obsolete. This section reconstructs that historical frame, the cultural habits that keep people in the old paradigm (the glorification of the grind, the illusion of certainty), and the author's reading of the society that is replacing it. It also contains some of his most speculative claims, which are marked as such.

#### The industrial paradigm

The author's 2026 vocabulary names the source of the default path directly: the **industrial paradigm**, also called "industrial values," "industrial standards" or "industrial mindset." It is the worldview that says "becoming a specialist, getting a job that requires you to perform a mechanical string of tasks for 40 years, and retiring at an age where your money can't be put to great use outside of buying material objects because you were deprived for so long, is still the best route." Because "most people still have a worldview influenced by the industrial paradigm," society "has convinced you that that trait... of loving learning or having multiple interests is a weakness" — and you may believe it, because you spend all day in rabbit holes "and frankly you don't really have much to show for it. All you have is a bit of knowledge." The author "can confidently tell you that that is not the best route." **Fuente:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md. The term is new in 2026, but it formalizes a critique the author had been making since 2022 (the school–job–retire route of 2.3). The defence of multiple interests against the specialist ideal is the subject of Chapter 20.

The paradigm's central irony is that it believes itself safe. Most people are still conditioned by industrial values: they think that going to school, getting a job and retiring at some age is safe and secure, "when it's the least safe and secure route." The same holds, he adds, for building leverage and increasing one's ability to generate wealth. The author frames this as both the problem and the opportunity. **Fuente:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md.

Industrial standards also produce a specific blindness. Most people were raised to become specialists, study one domain hard, and get a high-paying job so that friends think them successful. So they stayed blind to most of the process: they understood their one skill, "but you didn't try to understand the system that paid you." Not having spent time on other domains, "all you know is how to fill a role in someone else's thing," and the ability to think is crushed. Even if you are smart in your skill and decently paid, you do not feel financially stable, so you get caught in a chaotic **stress loop**, and "stress narrows the mind," making a life of building your own thing seem unfathomable. Stuck in the thirds — sleep, work, exhaustion — you have no capital saved, no time or energy for personal development, and you are too tired, "spiritually, not just physically," to re-educate yourself, because most of your waking life fuels someone else's vision. **Fuente:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md. The stress loop is the author's term for chronic financial stress that narrows the mind; it explains why the people who most need to change course are least able to see the alternative — a version of the perceptual filtering described in 2.1.

The paradigm also supplies a strategy that no one chose. The average person does not solve problems; they "live the same day over and over again because that's what the conditioning process led to." Parents and teachers "injected values and beliefs into their head that put them on the default path heading toward the goal that society wanted for them, which is go to school, get a job, retire at 65... the industrial paradigm." "By the time they turn 20, it had become so conditioned... it's just habitual... unconscious." "Everyone has a strategy, but for most people, it's not one that they created. It's one that they were assigned": be a good student, make a decent amount of money, "be humble, don't stick your nose out. Your dreams aren't worth pursuing because they're risky — which doesn't make sense, because risk is the only way to get reward." **Fuente:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md. The **assigned strategy** is the strategic counterpart of assigned goals (Chapter 1). Strategic thinking, by contrast, moves you to "a more advantageous position where their problems aren't so painful... of a lesser magnitude... more fun and creative problems to solve. You start to see life as a puzzle rather than a death sentence." And the new paradigm of AI and information "requires a completely different strategy whether or not you agree"; the assigned one was "only somewhat beneficial to the old way of life, the American dream." Strategy is developed in Chapter 17.

The author finds an unexpected witness for the prosecution. He opens a 2026 video with Adam Smith, "who had a huge influence on the birth of capitalism": "The man whose whole life is spent in performing a few simple operations generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become." He finds it funny, because Smith "contributed to it, if not created it" — the industrial age, capitalism, free markets, employment. He immediately adds: "I'm not against capitalism. I think it's going to evolve into something better, hopefully." **Fuente:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md. He labels the idea the video opens with "the death of the expert."

**Contexto complementario:** The quotation is from Book V of Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). It appears in a passage where Smith argues that the division of labour, which he praises in Book I with the example of the pin factory, can degrade the intellectual life of workers, and that public education should counteract this. Smith's own position was therefore that specialization is economically productive and humanly costly — close to the tension the author identifies.

#### Mimetic grind culture

One reason the industrial paradigm persists, the author argues, is imitation. "Humans are mimetic creatures": we imitate to survive and to avoid being cast out of our family or of a productivity-obsessed culture; "our mind takes the shape of that which allows us to fit in." The problem is what we imitate. Everyone glorifies the **visible extreme** — the young startup phenom who built a $1M business in three weeks "just from grinding." It feels relatable, and it confirms what we have been told: harder and longer means more likely to succeed. But that is wrong; there is a deeper formula. A social-media **highlight reel** driven by **unconscious competence** — mastery so internalized that its possessor cannot see or explain it — is not reality. You can spend 10,000 hours writing ten books that never reach more than fifty readers, while someone who knows how to distribute a mediocre book sells millions because they focused on what produces results. People miss the context: the **invisible extreme** that actually led to the success of most creatives, visionaries and strategists. **Fuente:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md. The mimetic idea is used here without attribution; the author develops the conscious use of imitation in Chapter 1 (intelligent imitation) and the role of distribution in Chapter 25.

Beneath the cultural pressure the author identifies hidden fears and desires that tie people to the grind. They romanticize the long, hard route. Underlying trauma makes them want to prove themselves to people who will not or do not care. Raised in a frugal family, they delay success because deep down they are afraid to make money, so they work longer hours to feel they are doing something. And they think they do not deserve leverage yet, so they do everything themselves or everything at once. Meanwhile it is no longer socially acceptable to say you found and exploited a shortcut, and stopping overwork brings boredom and the feeling of falling behind. **Fuente:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md. These are the author's observations about motives, not a clinical typology; their value is diagnostic, as prompts for self-examination. The alternative — working less with more leverage — is the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12 and the "leveraged, not hard-working" thesis of Chapter 34.

#### The illusion of certainty

The deepest psychological reason for clinging to the default path is the fear of uncertainty. "It is impossible to know with absolute certainty what the future holds; this is why the masses flock to secure jobs and belief systems. It's an **illusion of certainty** to avoid struggle." In the earlier version he adds: "it's a trap." And because there is no way to know in advance what you will want, you cannot skip making mistakes: "mistakes are your light in the dark on this journey." **Fuente:** How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md; If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md. Note that the author pairs secure jobs with belief systems: both offer the same psychological product — a pre-made order that spares the mind the struggle of creating its own.

From this he draws a warning in the form of a list. "The fastest way to ruin your life": do what others want for you; never question what you are told; "take the route that seems safe on the surface but is the least safe of them all"; avoid "the essence of life, trial and error"; close yourself off to the profound, the deep and the meaningful. "The greatest mistake is not making any mistakes at all." "This is the life of the masses": they bounce around on the surface, live the same day over and over, learn only what they were taught in school, clock in, clock out, and fill the rest of the day "numbing your mind from the thought of what will happen" tomorrow. **Fuente:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md. "The greatest mistake is not making any mistakes at all" repeats, in the author's own words, the conclusion he drew from Devon Eriksen's critique of grades in 2.2. **The masses** is the author's recurring label for people living on the surface, the same day repeated.

A 2025 version explains why the trap is so easy to fall into. "The fastest way to ruin your life is to follow someone else's plan." The mind craves order; without thinking, we seek security, safety and comfort. When he was young his fate was in his parents' and teachers' hands; too young to trust his own thinking, he followed them, because it was the only path he knew — "and that's where things go wrong." "The path of least risk is the greatest risk of all"; "a life of comfort is the most uncomfortable thing in the world." So we loop: seek comfort, become uncomfortable, seek more comfort, become more uncomfortable, until we are deep in a hole that is hard to dig out of. "If it's so comfortable, why do you have to numb the pain with quick pleasure?" **Fuente:** The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md. The **comfort loop** — the cycle in which seeking comfort generates more discomfort — connects to the cheap dopamine of Chapter 11: quick pleasures are the anaesthetic for the discomfort that comfort produces.

The author finds the same idea in Krishnamurti, whom he connects to entropy. Tradition becomes our security, and when the mind is secure "it is in decay." The author's gloss: when you feel stable, "just chilling," you are slowly decaying — like a vacation that becomes normal — and you do not notice until you realize you are in a much worse situation, which is harder to dig out of the longer you wait. "You cannot make discoveries in the known." You cannot live a full, open, non-robotic life by operating according to what parents and teachers planted. You can be happy at times, but deep fulfilment is different; most people have not experienced it, so they do not search for it, and they project their insecurities by claiming fulfilment while working "as a robot would" — "the lines of code are engraved on their brain." **Fuente:** How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md. The quotation is Krishnamurti's; the connection with entropy is the author's adaptation, which anticipates Chapter 5's claim that there is no such thing as staying the same.

Even those who try to leave often fail at the threshold. The major problem is that it is too easy for modern man to get sucked into "a **mindless trap of mediocrity, degeneracy and roboticism**." When people try to break from the default path into the unknown, they get scared, dip a toe in, realize they are comfortable, and step out: the water is too cold or too hot, there is too much information to explore, too many opportunities causing overwhelm, too many opinions causing anxiety. "To find clarity you have to battle through the chaos"; you cannot shy away at the first sign of struggle. **Fuente:** Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md. The image of dipping a toe in water makes concrete the asymmetry of 2.1: years for the default path, a week for one's own.

The author's diagnosis ends in a claim about responsibility. Formal education "has its place," but it has been steadily losing trust in its ability to secure people's future (and, he argues, people never had absolute trust in it). Forging your own path is uncertain: you have to create your own way rather than have one "placed in front of you so that your mind can conform to that and have that clarity" — and only exploring the unknown leads to discoveries and insights. "People inherently know that life enjoyment is a choice, but nobody wants to face that reality, so they **outsource their quality of life** to the over-exaggerated promises of schools and jobs." **Fuente:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md. The phrase names the core move of the default path: handing the responsibility for one's own enjoyment to institutions that promise it in exchange for compliance.

#### A historical lens: technoeconomic bases

To explain how societies come to have the shape they have, the author gives what he calls "a brief history lesson," in a video about the "cheap dopamine epidemic." Societies can be classified by their **technoeconomic base** — "in a nutshell, their basic means of production," the things a society uses to meet its basic needs (the horse-drawn plow versus machine farming). He lists five, as follows.

| Technoeconomic base | What the author says about it |
|---|---|
| Foraging | Hunting and gathering; men hunted, women gathered. |
| Horticultural | Simple planting; women produced most of the food, and the society's deities became female. |
| Agrarian | Animal-drawn plows; women could not continue farming because of increased miscarriages, so roles changed and deities became male; 80% of agrarian societies relied on male slave labour. |
| Industrial | Named, not developed in this passage. |
| Informational | Named, not developed in this passage. |

The pattern he highlights: "whoever produced the most foods, or whoever produced the most in general, whether it was male or female, became the deities of that society." **Fuente:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md.

He then develops the agrarian shift as a chain. In agrarian societies women could not operate the horse-drawn plow because of too many miscarriages — "it was a Darwinian disadvantage for women to do that thing" — so roles shifted, men produced more of the food, and women took over more "quote-unquote traditional roles of tending to the house and the family." This marks "a massive shift in culture," "the rise of the patriarchy, so to say," notable because women had been the deities "for most of human civilization." And because the plow produced an abundance of food, men did not have to work as much; they had more time for thinking and contemplation, "and this is what led to the invention of writing and mathematics." **Fuente:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md.

The author does not name a source for this framework in the transcript. Its vocabulary — "technoeconomic base," together with other terms recorded alongside it such as "great chain of being" and "physiosphere/biosphere/noosphere" — suggests integral theory, but this is an inference.

**Contexto complementario:** Ken Wilber, in *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality* (1995) and *A Brief History of Everything* (1996), uses the classification of societies by "techno-economic base" — foraging, horticultural, agrarian, industrial, informational — drawing on the sociologist Gerhard Lenski, and discusses the plow, women's status and the rise of patriarchy along the lines the author summarizes. The historical claims (for instance, the role of miscarriages, or the 80% figure) are part of Wilber's synthesis, and are debated by historians and anthropologists; the author presents them as background, not as his own research.

The function of the history lesson in the author's argument is clear even where its details are uncertain: societies take the shape of their means of production, and so a change in the means of production — from industrial to informational — implies a change in the shape of society, including its institutions of school and work.

The same video series adds a claim about the mental world. We keep building the physical world, and our minds must adapt, evolve and transcend to have the ideas that create it; every physical thing created demands "ten, a hundred, a thousand ideas and iterations of the ideas in our mind." This makes the information landscape we inhabit drastically different from the old world. At the inception of human consciousness we "transcended and included" the physical and biological world — animal life; each stage of evolution holds everything beneath it, but what lies above it has many times the depth and span (the transcript's numbers are garbled). So the mental world is far larger and more chaotic than the physical and biological, but it gives much more creative ability. **Fuente:** Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md. The claim is adapted from Wilber's "transcend and include." Its importance for the book is that it locates the challenge of the information age in the mind: the environment that now needs to be navigated is mostly mental, which is why attention and focus (Part III) become central.

Two other historical-cultural remarks complete the frame. The first, stated as opinion: "In today's world postmodern thinking has largely gone pathological. This has resulted in things like DEI or gender politics and attempting to dismantle all hierarchies, which is stupid and impossible, because by saying all hierarchies are bad, you still form a value ranking. You still form a hierarchy." (The transcript continues, "It's just not a hierarchy that you don't like," which appears to be a slip for "a hierarchy you do like.") **Fuente:** This mental model will make you think like a strategic genius.md. The remark connects with 2.5: the author does not oppose hierarchy as such, only the dominator kind, and he regards the attempt to abolish all hierarchy as self-refuting. The label **pathological postmodernism** is his; the reader should treat its political examples as the author's opinion.

The second is a framing quotation from Krishnamurti that the author uses to open the history section of a 2026 video, asking the viewer to "think about that as we go through this section": "It is no measure of health to be well-adjusted to a profoundly sick society." **Fuente:** How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md. In context the quotation recasts the default path: adjusting well to the industrial paradigm is not, in itself, a sign of health.

#### The age of intelligence: outdated software

If the industrial paradigm was a response to its era, what changes when the era changes? The author's answer from 2026: "Think of where we are right now in the **age of intelligence**. If you are still operating on industrial-age technology as a business, or you think that the right path... is go to school, get a job, retire at 65... you're running on this outdated software and you need to change it." "AI is on our doorstep and no one knows what to do next... That's the biggest problem." Looking back at history lets us "at least tell whether what we're doing is good or not." Running a business on industrial technology "is probably not a smart thing." (The transcript at one point says "if you are integrating AI in some way, transcending and including, then you're probably falling behind," which, given the logic of the passage, is almost certainly a slip for "if you are *not* integrating AI.") For an individual, the risk is keeping the industrial plan "when jobs are at threat of automation" without thinking creatively. He adds a disclaimer: "everything I say obviously isn't going to be correct, but I do think it's heading in the right direction. I'm open to being wrong... I'm not just telling people to sit around and just be bulldozed over." **Fuente:** How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md. The image of **outdated software** fits the author's computational vocabulary for conditioning (Chapter 1): the default path is a program written for hardware that no longer exists.

The argument rests on the rate of change. During the industrial age there was "not too much change. That's why it's so deeply ingrained to go to school, get a job, retire at 65, because the societal system was built up according to that paradigm." "Now with AI and disruption... everything is changing every 3 to 6 months, and the rate of change will only increase, and it's going to filter out so many people who get stuck with a rigid strategy." "The future belongs to the true strategist at this higher level of thinking... Masters can adapt on the fly because they have the universal principles that they can apply to all domains. They don't necessarily need to relearn everything. They don't need to upskill or reskill or re-educate, because they can do that extremely fast on their own." And: "Your job is to respond to what's actually happening then and there on the battlefield, not your mental fabrications of worst-case scenarios or your fantasies that are not yet real." **Fuente:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md. The "every 3 to 6 months" is an impression, not a measurement. The conclusion connects to two later ideas: principles over tactics (Chapter 15) and the strategist as a stage of development (Chapter 38). It also recalls Devon Eriksen's reply to Harari in 2.2: if what matters is learning how to learn, an unpredictable future is not a reason for paralysis.

#### The emerging society

The author's positive reading of the transition rests on one principle: society shapes itself around how people earn. "Everyone wants to be a content creator, and this isn't a bad thing, especially if you can predict where the future of work is going." Boomers will tell you to "get a real job" when those jobs will not exist in a few decades; they do not realize that the Industrial Age created those jobs, nor that the information age has created "new and more profitable jobs." "Profitability determines the direction of society and the economy; technology allows for more profitable lines of work." **Fuente:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md.

He found the same principle in *The Sovereign Individual*, a book whose authors he does not name. As he summarizes it, nations will fragment into millions of city-states and even individual estates with sovereign status, as information technology makes resources available to more people and changes society's political and economic structure. "How people make the most money impacts the evolution of society": society shapes itself around the most profitable jobs and businesses, and a new way of earning makes society adapt to increase wealth. His own mapping: the creator economy is composed of brands (city-states) and creators (the individual estates that maintain their own sovereignty). He says the book predicted what he had been preaching for a year, a conclusion he reached by pursuing his own goals. **Fuente:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md.

**Contexto complementario:** *The Sovereign Individual* (1997) is by James Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg. It argues that the information revolution will erode the power of nation-states and shift power toward individuals and small jurisdictions. The brands-as-city-states mapping is the author's adaptation.

The new society, in his account, does not arrive all at once. "The first thing is to join the new society, and when I say new society I mean emerging society, because that's what ideas do": they do not just pop up; "they evolve, they come from something, they emerge over time." "The new society is already here, but it's taking time to transition... and the old society is clearly dying and phasing out as it has been." **Fuente:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md. The **new society** — alias "emerging society" — is the author's term for the social order already forming while the old one fades; elsewhere he applies it to social media as "not an app but a new society" (Chapter 25).

The signs of the transition are those already described in 2.5 under the image of the programmers who "can't patch the game fast enough" — remote work, a creator economy that school curricula cannot keep up with, decentralized currency built by individuals and small teams who saw a problem in the monetary system, and agencies hiring skilled freelancers as contractors instead of employees. The image first appears in 2022, in "How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game," where it follows directly from the "system is rigged" principle: the game is rigged, but its flaws are now visible faster than they can be fixed. **Fuente:** How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md.

In 2025 he generalizes the reading of the present as a period of transition. We are in a very noisy time on social media and YouTube; "when there's a lot of conflict, a lot of chaos, that means that a **new ordered structure** is going to emerge," though we do not yet know what. "Thankfully, human nature remains pretty much the same." This period of rapid growth draws a clear line between machines and humans: "Those who lean into their humanity will thrive," while those who identify with mechanical living may not. **Fuente:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md. The idea that order emerges from chaos is the author's adaptation of a principle developed in Chapter 5 (order out of chaos, in the sense of dissipative structures); "mechanical living" — identifying with school, job and repetitive tasks — is the industrial paradigm seen from the side of identity.

The author's most compact argument for the new society is a chain of conditionals from 2023. "If humanity is only as strong as its weakest link, and 99% of people suffer from the same survival problems, and education is what allows the individual to solve their own problems rather than being prescribed temporary relief, and problems are what prevent personal to collective evolution, and if the education system is focused on training people into jobs for its own benefit, then the **new digital society of value creators** is one realistic path out of this mess." **Fuente:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md. The argument is worth reading as a structure, because it shows how the chapter's pieces fit together.

### 2.7 Awareness: The Healing Sixth Sense

Section 2.5 described the author's two-step exit from modern enslavement: first become aware, then create your own hierarchy of goals. Everything in this chapter so far has served the first step — making visible the institutions that install the default path. This section turns to awareness itself: what the author means by it, how it grows, why he believes it heals, and how it is practised through observation. It ends with the episode the author presents as his own awakening.

It helps to note first how the author frames the purpose of this kind of awareness, since his stance changed. In 2022 he spoke of "breaking free" of the Matrix and of the "red pill"; but already in late 2022, and again in 2024, he distances himself from the phrase — build your own thing, be independent, "break free of the Matrix if you want to frame it that way," and live an enjoyable life on your own terms. **Fuente:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. By 2025 he states outright that the goal is not to "break off from society": the only way out is to understand the system. Awareness, in the mature version, is not escape but comprehension — seeing the program clearly enough that one can stop being run by it.

#### Awareness as a sixth sense

The author's most ambitious definition comes from 2022. Most people are unaware of "the sixth sense, which is awareness. This is the foundation of creativity." Through awareness we perceive things beyond what the five senses make sense of — a feeling, a thought, something intangible that people operating from "the materialist paradigm" do not value. And this awareness is stored in the subconscious, letting you draw on more information when you create. **Fuente:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md. The phrase **sixth sense** here is the author's own metaphor, not a claim about a literal sensory organ; the "materialist paradigm" is his name for the worldview that only values what can be measured by the five senses. The link between awareness and creativity is developed in Chapter 18.

#### Awareness begets awareness

The second feature of awareness is that it grows in sequence. "Awareness begets awareness, thoughts beget thoughts, ideas beget ideas." You cannot become aware of something at a higher level if you were not first aware of something at the level below. The author compares the unknown to the map of a video game: areas you have not explored are not available to you; you are not conscious of them; you do not know what is there and can only guess. You have to reach one location before you can reach the next, and eventually you build a network you can tap into with your awareness — to draw from, to be creative, and to expand your mind toward new perspectives and "new realities in a sense." **Fuente:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md. **Awareness begets awareness** is the author's coined formula: progress in awareness is sequential, each step unlocking the next.

The same map image gives a measure of how little most people see. If you have not branched into the unknown, "you understand less than .1% of reality." The author doubts anyone reaches 100% — perhaps "the masters, the select few" — and argues that the average person in a repetitive routine, not pursuing their own goals, is "closer to 0.00001%." "It's not all money, girls, Lambos": people chasing those are still solving **survival needs** and "can't even access their **growth needs** yet, of challenge, skill acquisition and understanding." In a game, you cannot progress to new areas of the map until you acquire the skill and ability to survive there; levelling up unlocks quests, dungeons, special abilities, career choices, and the ability to ride or fly to new parts of the world. **Fuente:** Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md. The percentages are, explicitly, opinion and rhetorical. The pair survival needs / growth needs is the author's; it resembles Maslow's distinction between deficiency and growth needs, though the author does not cite Maslow here.

A third formula ties awareness to development. Opening the mind does not happen overnight; you will not have "a monk-like perspective" after being closed-minded for a while — "that's why it's called development, because it takes time." First you must understand the signs of closed-mindedness, so that you can become aware of them and improve. "Awareness begets improvement," because you need awareness to improve, "and then improvement begets awareness of the next but better problem to solve." **Fuente:** Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md. **Awareness begets improvement** turns the sequence into a loop: awareness enables improvement, improvement reveals the next problem, and the next problem is a better one. This loop is the seed of the author's later view of life as a chain of ever-better problems (Chapter 8).

#### Awareness is curative

The most practical claim the author makes about awareness is that it heals. "When you feel tension or stress or the need to reproduce or react, you have to train yourself to pause. This is what mindfulness is." When you notice a thought, you become aware of it, "and that's really the cure to it: just becoming aware of it over time and letting that correct your actions with time." He states the limit honestly: you may succumb a few times; "it's not going to be perfect the first time around." You may notice you are in an angry phase and see your body react in real time, doing things out of your control — but you have the awareness, and "that will fix itself over time." "Awareness is curative; it's a medicine." **Fuente:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md.

**Awareness is curative** (also "awareness is a cure") is the author's coined principle. In 2023 it means that noticing a thought or reaction is enough to correct it over time; in a 2024 formulation, developed in Chapter 7, it means that you do not need to force yourself to drop bad habits — it is enough to look at them through your vision long enough. The mechanism the author proposes is gradual: awareness does not stop the reaction the first time; it creates a gap in which, over repetitions, a different response becomes possible. This idea is developed as "interrupting autopilot" in Chapter 11.

The author places himself in a tradition here. Among books he recommended in 2022 is *Awareness* by Anthony de Mello, which JK Molina "turned him onto" — "the last spiritual one, kind of." It helps you navigate the world in a "calm, collected and aware way," gaining awareness of almost everything: your thoughts, your actions, other people, how they react and how you can respond. He describes it as a way to develop spiritual well-being and to navigate conversations, becoming aware of certain things so that you can "not only overcome them but kind of dissolve these negative traits and beliefs and mindsets that you have adopted over time through cultural conditioning." **Fuente:** 7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md. The phrase "dissolve... through cultural conditioning" connects awareness directly to Chapter 1: awareness is the solvent for the program.

**Contexto complementario:** Anthony de Mello (1931–1987) was an Indian Jesuit priest and spiritual teacher whose posthumously published *Awareness* (1990) argues that most people are "asleep," running on programming from society, and that simply observing one's reactions without condemnation is what dissolves them. The author's "awareness is curative" is close to de Mello's thesis, though the author presents his formula as his own.

#### Awareness before action

Awareness also has a methodological priority in the author's teaching, which explains the shape of his content and of this book. A section of one video is deliberately not "hyper-actionable," he explains, because you need awareness of a possibility in order to act on it. Steps without a reason, without a "why" that applies to your life and without intrinsic motivation, will not mean anything. Awareness of what is going on and of the opportunities comes first, then actionable steps for clarity — "and even then the actual steps are just a prescription... a temporary fix until you figure out how to do it on your own." His advice is to hold the ideas in mind as you go about life, so you can connect the dots. **Fuente:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md.

In 2026 the same principle is stated more bluntly. Before the practical part of a video, he insists on theory: "you have to change your mind, you have to understand what's going on. You don't understand by me giving you practical steps." If you just want the steps, paste the video into a transcription tool, try to follow it, "and fail." **Fuente:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md. The consistency between 2023 and 2026 is notable. It is the same reasoning that led him to say, about the dog experiment in 2.1, that practical steps toward agency mean little without understanding how the problem applies. It is also why this book places the diagnosis (Part I) and the model of the person (Parts II–IV) before the systems (Parts V–XII).

#### The conscious decision-maker

What does a person look like who acts from awareness? The author's answer is the **conscious decision-maker**, and he defines its core skill in 2024: "There is one extremely important skill, and that is figuring it out for yourself, not doing what people tell you to do, and doing what you want from a conscious mind" — not from a "mediocre mind" that says "I can do whatever I want — you want me to jump off a bridge? Okay." The conscious mind asks questions: what are your desires; what survival mechanism or force pulls you toward them; is it good or bad; do you actually want it; does it align with your values; do you have values at all? What people tell you will not be conducive to what you want. You may have to do it for survival, but in parallel you must pursue a project of your own that leads to autonomy and mastery, full-time and not for someone else. Many of his own aims were deemed impossible by closed minds projecting their opinions. Hence, to pursue mastery and autonomy, you need a skill that monetizes your interests and passions — which is where his argument for writing begins (Chapter 21). **Fuente:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md. The **conscious mind vs mediocre mind** contrast is the author's: the first examines desires, values and survival mechanisms; the second obeys. Note that the word "force" recurs here in the sense defined in 2.3: what pushes you, and how strongly.

The same idea appears in a conversation with Dickie Bush about high performers. The author attended a large gathering of entrepreneurs where some of the biggest names were passed out drunk, while other genuine high performers seemed to have enjoyable lives doing similar things. "It breaks your frame": "they're just conscious, they make conscious decisions, and they know themselves to the point where it's like, if I'm not making progress I'm changing something, and it doesn't matter how painful that's going to be, it's done." His own example: quitting *Call of Duty* was not even a struggle once he saw it was not worth it for his lifestyle. Bush's summary: "it's all self-awareness all the way down." The author adds that if you read the real backstories of the figures we praise — Alan Watts, Marcus Aurelius, the philosophers, Alexander the Great — the frame from which they are promoted "is like a highlight reel," which creates false expectations; "everyone's human, and it's really just a matter of making beneficial choices and self-correcting them over time." **Fuente:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. "Self-awareness all the way down" is Bush's phrase; the deflation of idols as highlight reels is the author's, and it echoes the visible and invisible extremes of 2.6. The definition of high performance as "making beneficial choices and self-correcting them over time" is a compact statement of the cybernetic model of Chapter 6.

#### Observation of the masses

How is awareness practised? The author's characteristic method is observation, and it starts from a diagnosis. "Sovereign living, or autonomous living, or just being independent in building your own thing, your own way of survival, is difficult; it's uncertain." "The mind craves order, it craves certainty, so if there is a path that is easier or more certain or more secure for someone to take, that's where the masses flock, that's where 99% of people flock." **Fuente:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. **Sovereign living** is his name for building your own way of survival — the hard, uncertain alternative to the default path.

Given that the masses take the certain path, observing them shows where it leads. To see where you will end up if you keep doing the same things, observe "the masses" and see where **mindless action** leads — "it's not pretty at all." The key condition is how you observe: sit with your thoughts, think them through, do not close your mind and accept what you see on the surface as reality. "That closed-minded surface-level living is what got you into this situation in the first place; you need to think deeper." **Fuente:** How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md. The method is the precursor of the anti-vision (Chapter 7), whose raw material is precisely the vivid image of where the current trajectory leads.

The method has an everyday form, which the author returns to again and again: the grocery store. Observe society and discern which daily actions lead to a good or bad life. At the store, see what someone puts in their basket and realize "that's how you end up like this person" — "not in a judgmental way." "You have to be able to make decisions through observation, and then you become less mindless when you put things into your own cart. Do I want to end up like that? No, I want to end up like this." Later in the same video he names the misuse: observing society only "to just reinforce what you've been told to do and then justify it." **Fuente:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md.

He traces the habit to childhood. Since he was a child he was "obsessed with finding a better way of living." Everywhere he went he observed people's actions — what they put in their grocery baskets, their daily routines (go to work, go home, watch Netflix, fall asleep, repeat) — to discern whether doing those things would lead to the life they had, and whether he wanted that life. He asked what, how and why: what were they pursuing, how did they get where they are, why did they look the way they did. One example he gives is a person picking up "a 1,000-calorie dessert drink for breakfast with a sandwich that leaked grease from its wrapper." He admits there was some judgment as a teenager — "we all judge people, it's okay" — but says he wants "to shift that from judgment to discernment." The method: with enough observational data, decide "do I want that or do I not want that." **Fuente:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md.

The earliest version (2022) adds the inward turn that makes observation a tool of self-knowledge. He describes himself as having always been a **silent observer** — not judgmental (he admits he was a bit judgmental at first, and that it is hard to escape) but "discerning." The practice has four steps: notice what others do (what they put in the cart, how they look); ask whether you want to look or be that way; notice "the reflection in yourself" — what reaction the observation causes in you; and ask what you can learn from that reaction. **Fuente:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md. The point is not to evaluate others but to use them as a mirror.

#### Judgment versus discernment

The distinction between judging and discerning runs through all these passages, and the author states it as a principle in *The Art of Focus*: "Observation and questioning are how you learn to navigate difficult situations with wisdom and grace." And: "There is a distinction between judgment and discernment: judgment leads to closed-mindedness, but discernment allows you to pull from others' experiences in a way that helps you make wise decisions for your own development." **Fuente:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md. **Judgment vs discernment** is the author's coined pair: judgment closes the mind around a verdict about the other person; discernment extracts information for one's own choices.

The same distinction governs comparison. At the grocery store he sees a very overweight person in a wheelchair with a cart full of junk food: he can "discern, not judge" that he does not want to end up like that, so he will not take those actions. "That's how you learn": "I don't want to be that guy, I want to be that guy." "Comparison can be a very good thing if perceived correctly," in every area of life. **Fuente:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md. Later in the same video he qualifies the claim. Comparison is good only if you understand that there is a gap between you and the other person. Seeing someone you admire as an "overnight success," because you cannot comprehend the gap, is the danger. Instead, "make a positive assumption": this person did this, this and this to get there, our goals align, so I will follow that path — "in that case it's not comparison but connection." He adds that the assumption can also be a very bad one. **Fuente:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md. **Comparison becomes connection** is the author's formula: comparison helps only when it recognizes the gap and makes a positive assumption about the path across it. The theme returns in Chapter 39 (comparison versus connection as part of happiness).

In 2025 the author adopts the very word he had avoided. His wife, then getting her Pilates certification, learned from her instructor that to fix your own posture over time, in public spaces "you just judge people's posture": noticing what bad posture looks like, you slowly correct your own. "The same holds true for me." Judging sounds bad, "let's call it observation and discernment." As a young man at the grocery store he saw people who did not look or act as he wanted to — what they bought, how they shopped, how they spoke — and corrected his own actions; at school and at church too. "You can see mediocrity all around you, just because that's the natural state of how things are unless people put effort into creating their own vision." "So judge, or observe society more. You don't need to tell them these things. You simply observe and correct your own actions." **Fuente:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md. The change is rhetorical rather than substantive: he uses "judge" where before he said "discern, not judge," but immediately redefines it as discernment and limits it to correcting one's own actions without saying anything to others. The Pilates image is useful because it makes the logic concrete: others' posture is information about one's own.

A broader tension sits behind these passages and the author never fully resolves it. On one side is a relativist empathy: "everyone is right and everyone is wrong, it's all relative"; transfer your awareness into the other person and assume a positive goal behind their actions; move "from judgment to discernment"; raise the collective consciousness. On the other, in parallel, is rhetorical contempt for "the masses": "if a boomer tells you to get a real job, you're on the right track"; the 99% who "blabber about how you should live your life when they wasted their own." The most coherent reading, compatible with what he says, is that empathy serves to understand perspectives, while the sharper dismissal serves to decide whose life advice to take. The discernment method lies between them: it observes without condemning, but it does draw conclusions.

#### The author's awakening: the arrest and *The Power of Now*

At eighteen or nineteen, in his first year of college, his roommate had just been left by his girlfriend (in Utah in one telling; for another man in another), and needed to "slow down"; someone told him "just go smoke some weed, you'll be fine" — the first option was "the magical Green Leaf." He forgot about it. Months later, in 2016, home for the summer and working as a lifeguard, he found a letter bearing a government seal. "Spirituality — that's for hippies. But I was desperate." Every morning for about an hour he sat on his patio with a "terrible cup of coffee" — his uncle's favourite gas-station coffee in the blue container, "kind of like Folgers" — and read. **Fuente:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md; Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md; 7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md.

He read it closely, practised the meditations, and opened his mind to the possibility of a higher power, which he had closed himself off to as a rebellious teenager forced to go to church. ### 2.8 Psychological Time

The awakening story of 2.7 turned on a book about presence, and on worries — about a court date, about parents, about the future — that "washed away." This section explains, in the author's terms, what those worries were made of. His concept of psychological time describes how the mind drags the past and the future into the present, and how that habit becomes personality. It is the inner, individual counterpart of the institutional conditioning described earlier: schools and jobs install the default path from outside; psychological time keeps it running from inside.

Most of the material comes from a single 2022 video, in which the author explains conditioning in neurological-spiritual language. In later years he explains the same phenomenon differently — as information repeated until it becomes automatic (2023), as Krishnamurti's "thought is always conditioned and therefore mechanical" (2025), and as self-replicating memes protected by institutions within the industrial paradigm (2026). The underlying idea does not change; the explanatory lens does. The 2022 lens, presented here, is best suited to explaining individual habit.

#### What psychological time is

**Psychological time**, or linear time, is in the author's account "one of the biggest problems humans face," and one that humans are "the only ones that can really face... because of our advanced cognitive abilities." It means that you can visualize a past experience you were made aware of — a time of emotional turmoil, say — and if you do not order your consciousness or focus on that moment correctly or positively, "you are quite literally pulling that experience into the now": you feel the same experience, the same stress. Reminiscing about a bad breakup, you begin to experience the same feelings and emotions you had then. The same applies in the other direction: if you project into a future you can imagine clearly, and it is negative, you pull that experience into the now with its feelings, thoughts and emotions. **Fuente:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md.

The phrase "order your consciousness" connects this to Part III: the problem is not memory or imagination as such, but memory and imagination without direction — what Chapter 5 calls psychic entropy. And the claim that only humans face this problem is consistent with the author's view, in 2.9, that human cognition is both a superpower and a hazard.

**Contexto complementario:** The term "psychological time" (as opposed to "clock time") is central to Eckhart Tolle's *The Power of Now*, the book of the author's awakening, and to Jiddu Krishnamurti before him; the author uses the term without attributing it in this passage.

#### The familiar past and the predictable future

The author then explains how psychological time shapes personality, borrowing from Joe Dispenza. As you repeat experiences in psychological time — "the familiar past, possibly negative, and the predictable future," predictable because you have already lived those experiences and project them forward — and allow this "possibly unconscious behavior," "you are quite literally hardwiring neural networks in your brain related to those experiences, so that becomes your personality." He recommends Dispenza's *Becoming Supernatural*, which "does a much better job at explaining the science behind this," and states his own position with unusual candour: he is "not too interested in the science"; it is "not very congruent" with him; he prefers the spiritual aspect, which he can apply easily, while others find the scientific view of the same topics more congruent — "it's just a different lens of viewing it." **Fuente:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md. The pair **familiar past / predictable future** is Dispenza's, adapted. The author takes the idea and explicitly leaves the science to its source; the reader should therefore not treat the neurological language here as the author's own empirical claim.

**Contexto complementario:** Joe Dispenza is a chiropractor and popular author whose books (*Breaking the Habit of Being Yourself*, *Becoming Supernatural*) combine neuroscience vocabulary with meditation and spirituality; his scientific claims are widely regarded as speculative, and the author himself elsewhere lists the book as "potentially pseudoscience." The general principle that repeated activation strengthens neural connections ("neurons that fire together wire together") derives from Donald Hebb's work on learning (1949) and is mainstream; Dispenza's further claims are not.

From this the author derives his definition of conditioning in this period: hardwiring neural networks "is what conditioning, programming is." The mind "kind of mimics a harmonic oscillator or a system": hardwiring makes it habitual to tap into an experience. His example is an ordinary morning. You wake up and immediately think "stressful boss at work"; you pull that stressful experience into the now; you have "those chemicals throughout your body"; and this "can quite literally cause you to be sick by **living as your past self**," or by dwelling on the predictable future ("this is going to happen to me"). Then a further step: because you imagine it first, and awareness begets awareness, "what do you know, it happens" — you actualize the vision. **Fuente:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md.

**Contexto complementario:** A harmonic oscillator, in physics, is a system that, when displaced from equilibrium, is pulled back toward it by a restoring force and oscillates around it (a spring, a pendulum). The author uses it as an image of a mind that keeps returning to the same states.

This last step deserves attention because it is the hinge between the diagnosis and the method of Part IV. The same mechanism that makes negative imagination self-fulfilling makes a positive vision self-fulfilling. If imagining the stressful boss each morning tends to produce the stressful experience, then imagining a chosen future, repeatedly, tends to produce that. The author's later practices — anti-vision and vision (Chapter 7), bringing the ideal future into the present (Chapter 8) — rely on this symmetry. Here, it is stated as an observation and an interpretation, not as a proven law.

Why would the mind cling to familiar experiences, even painful ones? The author offers an explanation he explicitly marks as speculation: our love of familiarity is a survival mechanism. The brain registers "this past experience happened, it caused a negative experience, it threatened my survival, I need to remember this for later," and builds a system in the body so that the trigger produces a reaction. The result is "living in this **stressful internal environment** because my body and my mind think it's being threatened on a physical survival level." (In the transcript he says "possibly don't take this with a grain of salt, this is speculation" — an evident slip for "take this with a grain of salt.") **Fuente:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md. The explanation links psychological time to the survival theme that runs through the whole chapter: just as the default path persists because survival depends on it, the familiar past persists because the mind treats it as survival-relevant information.

#### The only solution: awareness and the unknown

The author's remedy has two parts and he presents them as inseparable. "The only way to solve this problem is, one, becoming conscious of those negative thought patterns and experiences, but at the same time it's diving into the unknown." Awareness alone is not enough, because the pull of the familiar is strong: humans love familiarity, habits, automating thought processes and taking shortcuts. So you must "think new thoughts," "have new ideas," and "gain awareness that we didn't have before by doing the hard thing and kind of leaning into the discomfort of life that will threaten our survival." **Fuente:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md.

The two-part solution maps exactly onto the two-step exit of 2.5. Becoming conscious of patterns is step one, awareness. Diving into the unknown is the beginning of step two: building a hierarchy of goals whose path is not yet known, which is where, in 2.4, the hunt takes place. It also explains why the author treats the unknown as both frightening and necessary: the discomfort that "threatens our survival" is precisely the signal that one is leaving the familiar past behind.

#### Mental health as making sense

A related video from 2022 extends the idea to mental health, with a caveat the author states first: "I'm not a doctor here." He acknowledges how defensive people can be about their mental health and "how they kind of hide behind that," and adds: "I'm not saying that you're wrong, I'm just saying why not exhaust every single solution available to you" — offering a solution "that has worked for me and many others." **Fuente:** How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md. The caveat is important for reading what follows: it is a personal and philosophical perspective on mental distress, not a clinical theory, and it is offered as one option among others.

His definition: "In this sense mental health is about making sense, understanding and clarity." If you let a compulsive negative thought hijack a situation — you are talking to someone and suddenly think "I have to get this done" — you feel the stress associated with "that alternate situation that you are projecting into," and "you can't make sense of the current one because your mind is influenced by something that is not even relevant at that point." **Fuente:** How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md. **Making sense**, in this sense, is the author's term for mental health as clarity about the present situation. The connection to psychological time is direct: the to-do thought is a small piece of the predictable future pulled into the now, displacing the conversation actually happening.

He frames the idea with a quotation from Anthony de Mello and extends it: "Remove the obstruction from your mind and you have peace; remove the obstruction from your heart and you have love." The author adds: "I would go as far to say remove the obstruction from your business and you have money; remove the obstruction from any given system and you have result." **Fuente:** How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md. The first two clauses are de Mello's; the extension to business and to any system is the author's. The principle — that the desired state is the default once an obstruction is removed — is characteristic of his thinking: clarity, like peace, is not built but uncovered.

The video's central example makes the idea concrete. "Does a bodybuilder care if he brings chicken and rice as his own meal to dinner?" Yes, "but only if he **projects outside of his reality**, which means he loses control of his attention." If he remembers what matters to him and what his goals are, and perceives the situation — including "the little jabs of insecurity that other people are throwing at him" — accurately, he will not care, "because he is focused." If he were operating robotically, a comment like "oh, you're bringing chicken and rice to dinner" would provoke a defensive or flustered reaction, rather than a joke, or an explanation of his perspective and goals — "and if other people don't accept that, then what can you do?" The lesson: "we have to exchange robotic living for intentional living." And an extension: if he adopts the others' goal — just having a very pleasurable meal — which lies outside his reality, and sacrifices his values to pursue it, he will feel bad and create problems he could have avoided by asking "what am I stretching towards?" **Fuente:** How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md.

The example is a miniature of the whole chapter. The dinner table is a small society with a default (eat what everyone eats); the bodybuilder has a self-chosen goal; the jabs of insecurity are the social pressure that keeps people on the default; and the difference between being hurt and being unbothered is whether attention is anchored in one's own goals or projected into others'. **Robotic living vs intentional living** is the author's pair for reacting automatically versus living with one's goals in mind. And the closing question — "what am I stretching towards?" — anticipates the author's definition of intention in Chapter 7, which he derives from the Latin *intentio*, "stretching toward."

### 2.9 Why Waking Up Matters

The chapter has so far treated awareness as a personal matter: how an individual sees the default path and begins to leave it. This final section widens the frame. The author gives three kinds of reasons why awakening matters beyond the individual: a metaphysical one (perception itself is a survival interface, which limits what "waking up" can mean), a social one (conscious individuals hold society up), and a civilizational one (the metacrisis, borrowed from Daniel Schmachtenberger). The section is the most speculative in the chapter, and most of its frameworks are taken from others; the author's own contribution lies in how he applies them.

#### Perception as a user interface: you can't escape the Matrix

In a 2023 video titled "Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix)," the author offers what he calls a metaphysical perspective: we live in "a survival-based simulation." He draws on Donald Hoffman, a cognitive psychologist, and his book *The Case Against Reality*, quoting: "If you play a video game on your computer such as Doom or Uncharted, you see compelling 3D worlds and 3D objects, yet the information is entirely 2D, limited by the number of pixels on the screen. The same is true when you look away from your computer to the world around you; it too has pixels and all information is 2D." In the book and in podcasts the author has heard, Hoffman argues that human perception is a user interface for survival that hides the true nature of reality, because "if we saw the truth we wouldn't survive": in simulations, "natural selection does not favor those that see the truth, or the ones that see reality as it is"; organisms that saw the truth went extinct. Hoffman's examples include an ant trying to mate with a glass bottle because its perceptions were distorted, and frogs that see a completely different range of colours. Hoffman also argues that space-time is a visualization tool — space and time are "compression algorithms," "the operating system of the user interface." **Fuente:** Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md.

**Contexto complementario:** Donald Hoffman is a cognitive scientist at the University of California, Irvine. His "interface theory of perception," set out in *The Case Against Reality* (2019), argues on the basis of evolutionary game simulations that perception is tuned to fitness rather than truth. The jewel beetle that tries to mate with discarded beer bottles is one of his standard examples (the author's "ant" may be a slip). The theory is debated in philosophy and cognitive science.

The author then extends Hoffman's metaphors. Subjective reality is like a desktop screen programmed to help us see what we need to see and do what we need to do. Every material object is like an icon you can move around, play with, delete or lose. The screen is attractive, has properties and qualities, "and we should take them seriously but not literally," as Hoffman says. You click around and perform tasks for desired outcomes, which Hoffman calls **fitness payoffs**; these are "like points in a video game": through trial and error we learn to win and evolve, and by winning, your offspring goes to the next level. "Everyone thinks they're seeing the truth, but... it's like we're all playing a game of Grand Theft Auto": consoles are packaged nicely; we do not see the wires and diodes; and even if we did, it would not serve survival — we could do nothing with the jumble of electronics and could not comprehend the code running the game. **Fuente:** Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md. The game metaphor connects Hoffman to the author's own central metaphor of life as a video game (Chapter 10); "fitness payoffs" become, in effect, the points of the survival game.

The author's own contribution is a limit. If you removed the user interface — with a tool such as psychedelics — you would eventually go insane. "I'm not saying that insane is bad necessarily; maybe that's the truest thing there is, maybe that's ultimate reality, maybe that's ego death." But for operating in the real world, "this survival game," it would not serve you; you would probably die sooner because you would lack the survival instinct. "There is a reason why people aren't on psychedelics 24 hours, seven days a week: we aren't at that phase of evolution yet." That does not mean, he adds, that you cannot have awareness of this, or grounding in it, to make better decisions and live a meaningful life. **Fuente:** Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md.

This passage matters for the chapter because it qualifies what "waking up" can mean. The author's use of "the Matrix" evolves. In 2022 it is "what you think you know," and the red pill and a "glitch" in robotic cycles can break you out. By September 2023, with Hoffman, perception and language form a closed system, a survival interface that would drive you mad if removed: you cannot escape the Matrix in that sense. By 2025 the Matrix is a structure of "circular validation" among institutions, and "we're not talking about some kind of red pill... break off from society — no": the only way out is to understand it. The shift is from a problem of individual habits that can be broken to a structural and epistemological condition that can be understood but not exited. Both versions can apply at once: "breaking" applies to conditioned expectations (the psychological level), and is a practice within the larger frame; "not escaping" applies to perception itself (the metaphysical level) and to society (one should not isolate oneself from it). The awareness of 2.7, then, is not a departure from the interface but a way of using it more consciously.

#### Conscious individuals hold up society

The second reason is social and is stated with more urgency. The fate of humanity is not guaranteed: every life form is fighting to survive in "this little competition," and humans may or may not stay on top. If we do not make intelligent, conscious choices we may eradicate ourselves — a worst case, but possible. Species go extinct all the time because they do not make conscious decisions or decide their fate; they live by instinct. Humans are different, but a majority of them also live by instinct, in a "narrow-minded, mechanical state of stress — robotic, unconscious, mindless," the same as animals. If "95 plus percent of society" operates robotically, doing what they are told, following the path laid out by society, "being a slave to their programming and conditioning," those masses are "leading us off of the cliff." Hence: "society, by my standards, is held up by the **conscious individuals**; it's like they have the world on top of them, carrying it towards something better" — by building businesses that contribute to humanity, writing, creating, and putting things out that affect others and wake them up. **Fuente:** Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md. The percentage is again an estimate; at the end of the same video he says "90 to 95%." The image of individuals bearing the world is the figure of Atlas.

From this follows a duty that the author states in terms of persuasion. "Wake up, learn to persuade." If you do not, "you are actively choosing to let unethical people win this **spiritual war**." You are not conscious by opting out of the game of life; you are arguably less conscious than the "sleazy businessman" who at least provides a fraction of value with a product that genuinely improves some people's lives — others not so much, "and that's okay." **Fuente:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md. The argument is aimed at a specific reader: the person who, having seen through the default path, concludes that the right response is withdrawal. For the author, withdrawal is a failure of consciousness, not its expression, because it leaves the field of persuasion to those with fewer scruples. The ethics of persuasion is developed in Chapter 32.

The third piece explains why individual creation can change society at all. The more attention a creative person attracts to a theory that leads society in a new direction, and the more supporters it gains, the more it tends to create culture and society. "The more attention that is invested into a creative idea, the more likely it is to come true than not. Things just don't happen. Humans build what they want to see in the world." His example is post-labour economics, a theory that could become real if enough attention is invested in it. **Fuente:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md. The principle closes a loop with 2.5: if society is a pyramid of attention in which what receives attention exists, then directing attention toward a better idea is a way of building it. It is the social version of the self-fulfilling vision of 2.8.

#### The metacrisis

The widest frame comes from Daniel Schmachtenberger, whom the author calls "one of the greatest thinkers of our time": someone who rarely posts on social media (and has not in years), appears occasionally on podcasts, and is calm, nuanced and non-polarizing, and who has dedicated his life to what he calls "the metacrisis" — a subject, the author notes, much deeper than his own video's focus on posting content and writing essays. The framing quotation: "As technology is empowering our choices and we are getting something like the power of gods, you have to have something like the love and the wisdom of gods to wield that, or you self-destruct." **Fuente:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md.

**Contexto complementario:** Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher associated with the Consilience Project and the "Game B" discussions. His central thesis is that humanity's many crises — ecological, technological, geopolitical, informational — share underlying causes, which he calls generator functions, and that addressing them requires improvements in collective sense-making.

The **metacrisis**, as the author presents it, is not one crisis but a convergence of three, driven by three **generator functions** — "a deep structural pattern that creates surface-level problems," that is, root causes. Treating symptoms without addressing root causes is, in the author's own gloss, "like mopping water while the faucet runs." The author explains the three in 2025 and again, almost verbatim, in 2026.

| Generator function | What it is | Examples the author gives |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalrous dynamics | Win-lose games in which one party's gain requires another's loss | Arms races, corporate competition, social media content, academic publishing (hoarding data to publish first) |
| Substrate consumption | Systems consuming their own foundation — what they need to exist — faster than it regenerates; "when systems consume their foundation faster than they can regenerate, that's bad" | Soil for plants (depleting topsoil that took millennia to form), attention for media (the attention economy consuming human cognitive capacity faster than it recovers), trust for markets |
| Exponential technology | Tools and systems that improve themselves at accelerating rates, "outpacing human wisdom" — "that's the key point" | AI doubling in capacity, automated weapons, social media algorithms evolving faster than their psychological impact can be studied |

**Fuente:** The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md; I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md.

When the three forces converge, they can produce outcomes that Schmachtenberger calls **attractors** — basins into which many complex societies already fall. Two are to be avoided: civilizational or catastrophic collapse (nuclear war, unaligned AI, ecological destruction, engineered pandemics) and dystopian control (total surveillance, digital authoritarianism, the elimination of agency and of the ability to make one's own choices). The third attractor, the good outcome, is "a world where sense making, shared understanding, and aligned incentives exist." **Fuente:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md. In the 2025 version the author does not detail the third attractor; he recommends studying it himself — "binge-watch 10 hours of his lectures" — and pivots to the question of what social media can do about it and how that aligns with getting civilization back on track. **Fuente:** The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md.

The author's use of the framework is specific. One generator function — substrate consumption, with attention as the substrate of media — describes precisely the information environment that Chapter 1 identified as an amplifier of unconsciousness. And it gives him a reason for one of his practical recommendations. He quotes Schmachtenberger: "The written word as the primary type of media was probably required for democracy to work," because people had to think well enough; reading meant a longer attention span for "non-dopaminergic stuff" and enough working memory to hold multiple perspectives. **Fuente:** The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md. The author uses this to introduce his "level three creators," who produce long, thoughtful content, and to argue that writing essays is part of the response — a theme developed in Chapters 21 and 24.

The author's view of the internet across these years is not linear, and it frames how this section should be read. In 2022 the internet was "a giant mind" documenting collective consciousness, but also a "socialization chamber" that made conditioning "10 times easier." In 2023–2024 he emphasized the user's responsibility and the potential of social media — "not an app, it's a new society," "devil or vessel," the engine of most of his own success. From 2025 he shifted toward a diagnosis of systemic contamination, using Schmachtenberger's frame: the attention economy consumes cognitive capacity faster than it recovers, and social media and AI amplify unconsciousness. He gives no explicit reason for the 2025–2026 turn. Throughout, one idea remains: that creating good content, especially long-form writing, is part of the solution.

#### From the institutions to the self

The chapter has moved from institutions to awareness and from awareness to civilization, and the argument can now be stated as a single chain. The default path is a hierarchy of goals assigned by school, the job and the culture of the industrial paradigm; once installed, it steers perception and behaviour toward its own fulfilment. Its costs are psychological as much as economic — the plateau, the eternal known, the hunting psyche trapped in routine — and they are hidden by gradualness and by the illusion of certainty. The exit begins with awareness, practised as discerning observation, which the author found through his own crisis and *The Power of Now*, and which extends to the inner habit of psychological time. And it matters beyond the individual, because conscious individuals, in his view, carry society and shape culture by what they choose to build and to give attention to.

One question remains open, and it is the question of Part II. Every step of this chapter has assumed someone who becomes aware, discerns, chooses and builds. Yet the same chapter has shown that this someone was itself produced by the conditioning it now observes — that identification with a job can make criticism of employment feel like a threat to survival, and that a "mental body" defends itself. Before the author's method for creating direction can be understood, it is necessary to understand what that self is, why it defends itself, and how it changes.

### Ejercicios

1. **Diagnosis of your own default path.** Write down, as specifically as you can, the default path that was handed to you: the goals, the timeline, the definition of success, and who conveyed them (parents, teachers, peers, culture). Then apply the author's cybernetic explanation from 2.1: identify one "error correction" you have made in the last few years that kept you on that path, and one opportunity you suspect you could not perceive because your current goal did not demand it. How confident are you in the second answer, and why is it inherently hard to be confident?

2. **Testing the slavery metaphor.** The author defines wage slavery operationally: "if you cannot stop showing up to work without catastrophe and if you have no skills to create alternatives, you meet the definition of a slave." Apply the definition to three cases — a salaried employee with six months of savings and a portable skill; a freelancer with one client who provides 90% of income; a business owner whose company cannot survive a month without them. Does the definition sort them in a way you find illuminating or misleading? What does the author's emphasis on "force" and survival capture that the ordinary meaning of "slave" does not, and what does the metaphor risk obscuring?

3. **Reconciling the author's two voices on employment.** Section 2.3 shows the author calling employment both "the worst outcome" and "a very worthy stepping stone." Construct the strongest argument that these are compatible, using the concepts of the plateau, complacency and dependence. Then identify at least one statement in the corpus that your reconciliation cannot accommodate. What does that residue suggest about the difference between rhetoric aimed at motivating change and analysis aimed at describing reality?

4. **The psychology of employment graph applied.** Sketch the author's "psychology of employment" graph (challenge and skill over time, with the employment line flattening into "the eternal known") for your own working life or studies so far. Where are the plateaus? Where was the "intersection" at which, by the author's logic, a change would have been well timed? If you have never experienced a plateau, explain what has kept the challenge rising, and whether that contradicts or confirms the author's model.

5. **Observation without judgment.** For one week, practise the author's "silent observer" method in one setting (a store, a commute, a workplace): notice what people do, ask whether you want to end up as their actions suggest, and — the step most often skipped — record what reaction the observation provoked in you and what it reveals. Afterwards, evaluate the method critically: where did discernment slide into judgment? Does the author's 2025 move from "discern, not judge" to "just judge people's posture" clarify or blur the distinction?

6. **Conspiracy, property of the system, or neither.** The author oscillates between "schools were created to enslave the brightest minds" and the claim that obedience is "a property of the system" that "takes the shape of that which most benefits the end goal... whether it's intentional or not." Choose one institution you know well (a school, a company, a profession). Identify a feature of it that serves those at the top, and argue for which explanation fits better — design, emergent property, or something the author does not consider (for example, a feature that serves its members or users). What evidence would distinguish the explanations?

7. **Psychological time in practice.** Identify one recurring thought that pulls a "familiar past" or a "predictable future" into your present (the author's example is waking up and immediately thinking of a stressful boss). Using the bodybuilder-at-dinner example, describe what it would look like to stay anchored in your own goals in that situation instead of "projecting outside of your reality." Then assess the author's claim that negative imagination is self-fulfilling by the same mechanism as a positive vision: what in your own experience supports it, and what would count as evidence against it?

8. **When would the author's prescription stop applying?** The author argues that "entrepreneurship is modern survival" and "the only logical option for long-term thinkers." Identify at least two conditions under which this principle would fail or become harmful — for a particular person, a particular economy, or a society in which most people followed the advice. Use the author's own concessions (the 1% who should keep their job, Devon Eriksen's "somebody has to do a lot of these tasks," the pyramid whose base "has to be filled") as material, and decide whether they are exceptions to his thesis or evidence that it needs reformulating.
