## Chapter 12 — The 4-Hour Workday

Chapter 11 explained why attention leaks away: cheap dopamine, an environment designed to capture it, and a mind that, without a goal of its own, lets the algorithm choose for it. This chapter is about the other half of self-government, the positive half: how Dan Koe organizes the hours he does work. Its organizing idea is a phrase he arrived at by misreading a book title, the **4-Hour Workday**, and the chapter follows that phrase from its origin as a personal identity to the full daily architecture he built around it.

The chapter draws on three earlier ones. From Chapter 6 it takes the claim that conscious attention is the scarce input of all creative work, so that the value of work depends on where attention goes rather than on hours logged; that chapter ended by promising that the distinction between *tasks* and *levers* would become a daily priority system, and this chapter delivers it. From Chapter 8 it takes the hierarchy of goals and its lowest rung, the one to three daily levers (lever-moving tasks) that move a project forward. From Chapter 11 it takes the diagnosis of distraction, which explains why the morning, the phone and the order of tasks matter so much. Chapter 5's entropy also returns, because Koe's later descriptions of his routine are built on the vocabulary of low and high entropy.

The argument moves in seven steps. Section 12.1 presents the 4-Hour Workday as a philosophy and an identity rather than a rule, with Koe's reasons for the number four, the evidence of his own hours, and his account of deep work as a necessity. Section 12.2 answers the most common objection, lack of time, with the idea of building one's own project in one protected hour a day, even while employed. Section 12.3 explains how to choose what goes into that hour: focusing on few things, and the **priority ladder** that orders a day by challenge and distraction potential. Section 12.4 covers the mechanics that narrow attention inside the day: time blocks, breaks, deadlines (pseudo, public and true) and what Koe calls **mindful procrastination**. Section 12.5 explains why the morning comes first and how the day is ordered by energy and, later in the corpus, by entropy. Section 12.6 shows how work is kept within four hours over the long term (systemizing, eliminating, delegating) and presents **Koe's Law**, the author's own extension of Parkinson's law, together with the three-stage business roadmap he derives from it. Section 12.7 closes with the least glamorous claim in the chapter: that roughly 95% of results come from repeating boring fundamentals. Chapter 13 will take up the other side of the polarity: rest.

### 12.1 Work Less, Earn More, Enjoy Life

#### A misread book title

The 4-Hour Workday begins with a mistake that Koe tells about openly. In college, while trying to become "a fitness YouTuber of all things," he came across the title of Tim Ferriss's popular book *The 4-Hour Workweek*. "I've never read the book but when I first saw the title I interpreted it as the 4-Hour Work Day." The idea stuck "in the back of my head" through college, through several failed businesses, and through everything that followed. All along, he says, his subconscious mind "was kind of munching on creative problems because I perceived anything more than 4 hours of work a day as a problem," and "this alone changed the direction of my life and really everything I do and all of the decisions that I made" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; the same passage is reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

**Complementary context:** Tim Ferriss's *The 4-Hour Workweek* (2007) is a lifestyle-design book about escaping the office through automation, outsourcing and "mini-retirements." Koe stresses that he took only the title; nothing in his framework should be read as a summary of Ferriss's book, and he never claims otherwise.

The story is worth keeping for what it shows rather than for its details. A held idea, even one born of a misreading, functions as a standing definition of what counts as a problem, and the mind keeps working on whatever it has been told is a problem. This is the mechanism of Chapter 6 (the goal as a lens) applied to a work norm: once "more than four hours" was registered as a deviation, his attention began to notice ways of reducing it. The details of the story are not quite stable. In the 2023 video he places the encounter "in college"; in the official summary of his book *The Art of Focus* (2024) he says he heard of the book "as a kid" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). The inconsistency is minor and does not affect the argument, but it is a reminder that the origin story is a remembered anecdote, not a documented fact.

#### What "problem" means here

Koe uses the word **problem** in a sense of his own, and he takes care to define it. Calling more than four hours of work a problem does not mean "it was the end of the world." It means that he "knew that I was able to achieve some form of success with only four hours or less worth of work per day, and so my mind naturally wanted to come up with solutions relating to that whenever my work extended beyond 4 hours of work a day" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01). A problem, in this usage, is a deviation from a standard the mind accepts as achievable, and its function is to trigger a search for solutions: systemize something, eliminate something, delegate something, turn a service into a product. The four-hour line is a reference point, not a punishment.

He illustrated the difference with his own situation in January 2023: he was working about six hours a day. It was not affecting him negatively, he said, but he still saw it as a problem he would systemize at some point. The rule, in other words, is a direction rather than a ceiling. Later the same year he gave the idea its most useful name: the four hours are a **baseline**. "Most days now I work 4 to 5 hours, and even with my excessive workload I'm still close to that 4-hour workday philosophy." Work occasionally goes over (launching a book, recording the audiobook of *The Art of Focus* over three days), "but I'm talking baseline, what I'm continuously working back to, and using that philosophy as a way to identify problems, like working over four hours, in order to create systems and solutions that allow me to do what I do better in that time block" (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19). A baseline is a level one returns to, not a level one never leaves. The term connects to his broader use of "raise the baseline" in Chapter 5's discussion of entropy management, where peaks of intense work are followed by systemization so that the same hours yield more.

#### A mindset, a philosophy, an identity

By August 2024 Koe had become explicit about how the idea should be held. Many people, he says, misinterpret his "4-Hour Workday philosophy" as "work 4 hours a day for the rest of your life." Instead, "the 4-hour workday is a mindset, it's a philosophy, it's an identity": something that lets you identify problems so they can be solved so that you can work less. If he identifies as someone who works four hours a day (an **operating mindset**, made believable through self-education and acquired skill), then going over four hours is registered by his mind as a problem. When he first read the title and still had a job, he "constantly saw that job as a problem to be solved," because his mind wanted the four-hour workweek, later the four-hour workday. Most people, by contrast, "don't see their current lifestyle as a problem or can't identify problems in it," and so they never notice opportunities, such as a business model, that could change it (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

This reformulation is the key to the whole chapter, because it explains why a work-hours norm belongs in a book about identity and perception. The argument runs in three steps. First, perception is filtered by identity and goals (Chapters 3 and 6). Second, a problem only exists relative to a standard; without a standard there is nothing to notice (Chapter 6, section 6.6: without a goal there is no problem). Third, adopting the identity of "someone who works four hours" installs a standard against which one's current workday becomes visible as problematic. The four hours are therefore less a schedule than a perceptual instrument. The difference between the reader who thinks "eight hours is just what work is" and the reader who thinks "eight hours is a problem I have not solved yet" is not effort but what each can see.

Several of the commandments are treated in other chapters of this book; the table shows where each is developed.

The first commandment operates at the conceptual level, and it reuses the law of conceptual survival from Chapter 3. Because humans survive not only physically but also as concepts, if you hold the idea of a four-hour workday and associate yourself with it, "you're going to try to survive that identity, you're going to feel threatened when something threatens that belief or that system of yours." The result is better decisions: you will look for ways to "productize, systemize or just maintain 4 hours of focused work a day" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The move is characteristic of Koe. The mechanism that Chapter 3 presented as the reason identity resists change (the ego defends its concepts as if they were its body) is here put to work deliberately: choose the concept you want defended, identify with it, and let the defensive machinery protect it for you.

#### Why four hours

Why four and not six or eight? Koe gives three reasons in 2023, and they are of different kinds, which is worth noting.

The first is about the cost of conceptual systems. The beliefs and systems one tries to "survive" require mental energy and attention. At the start this is "very draining," because you do not yet have a way of organizing your mind to act on the new system consistently; you have to test, refine and iterate. The second is an empirical claim: "From personal experience and anecdotes from friends and just scientific studies it seems like... three to five hours of focused work a day is what humans can have before they need to just give their focus a break." The studies are not specified, and the claim should be read accordingly, as a personal estimate with vague support rather than as an established finding. The third is a metaphor that becomes one of his most repeated phrases: "**focus is a muscle**." "You're not going to be able to do it very well at the start, you're going to be able to focus for like 20 minutes," take a break, come back, and train it until it becomes habit. Refined, repeated systems "form grooves in the neural networks" and become automatic (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The book version, in the official summary of *The Art of Focus* (January 2024), adds three further arguments. The first is a comparison: "Four hours of focused work is better than 8 hours of distracted work." The second is a reframing of the four hours as a game. "4 hours as a deadline creates the frame of a game that you can play to win": if you **play the character** of someone who only works four hours and internalize that trait, you feel pressure to finish within that time; chunking the session into smaller time blocks then "creates a powerful environment to narrow your focus into a productive state of good stress." The third is a correction aimed at readers who think four hours is too little. For most people the four-hour workday is not possible right now, he writes, "not because you'll have to work longer at the start but because you'll have to work shorter": with a job, a spouse or a family, you start with one hour a day and increase toward four as results come. The same passage gives the "sweet spot" version of the empirical claim (three to five hours "is a sweet spot for mental energy expenditure," according to personal experience, studies and "anecdotes from respected creatives") and recalls that his early perception of anything over four hours as a problem led him to notice business opportunities that allowed two hours a day while others worked eight to twelve doing the same thing (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The fourth argument is an analogy from the gym, which he uses repeatedly. "You wouldn't go to the gym for 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week and expect to make any progress, so the same is true for work or study." That, he says, is why he is "so big on the 4-Hour Work day": you cannot train that long and sustain it with meaningful results; "you aren't going to build muscle, you aren't going to lose fat, you're just going to feel like a sack of [expletive]" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). A variant of the analogy appears the same month as a coined phrase: "**productivity is like fitness**, and you wouldn't train 8 hours a day with no food or sleep and expect to make progress" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The analogy carries a specific claim: that cognitive work, like training, produces adaptation only when it is intense, limited and followed by recovery. That claim is the bridge to Chapter 13, where rest is treated as half of the work.

The 2023 claim that three to five hours is what a human can sustain has a later counterpart in tension with it. In October 2024 Koe published a video titled "It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day," describing twelve-hour days that come naturally during a phase of intensity when a goal is meaningful. Chapter 6 traced that evolution; here it is enough to note that the "why four" argument was formulated for a baseline phase of work, not as a physiological limit valid for every phase.

#### The cutoff as a forcing constraint

There is a further reason for the number, and it is the most structural one: the cutoff forces evolution. In 2024 Koe explains it with the example of a client business. "With a client business you can only take on so many clients with four hours of work a day... and so without the 4-Hour Work Day cut off you're not going to have a time where you're like, okay, I need to evolve, and if you work 8 to 9 hours a day doing client work you're not going to have any time to evolve, so you need that buffer." Looking back over the whole progression from clients to products, he adds: "by working 4 hours a day you forced yourself to change how you work in a more leveraged fashion; you didn't add on more work and ruin that possibility" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28). The **4-Hour Work Day cut off**, or **buffer**, has two functions in this argument. It is a limit that makes the current way of working fail visibly, and it is a reserve of time in which a new way can be built. A person who absorbs growth by adding hours never meets the moment of failure, and never has the spare time to build the alternative.

Koe's illustration is a third-party case. If you commit to four hours a day instead of the twelve of "hustle and grind entrepreneurs," he says in 2025, you would end up like JK Molina, who left an agency (run with another person) where he worked twelve hours a day and made a lot of money, reasoning that he had the knowledge and skill "to make a million bucks working 4 hours a day"; he is actually working about three. The constraint makes "your mind notice higher leverage business opportunities that you wouldn't have noticed when you're pursuing another goal." His offer and delivery are valuable enough that he charges higher prices, works with fewer people and spends less time (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The case is reported as Koe tells it; the numbers are his summary of Molina's account, not independently documented. What it illustrates is the mechanism of the lens again: a different goal (a million working four hours rather than more money working twelve) makes different opportunities visible.

#### The motto: work less, earn more, enjoy life

The 4-Hour Workday sits inside a larger formula that Koe calls his motto and the value proposition of his website: **work less, earn more, enjoy life**. He frames it as an instance of a principle he calls **success is counterintuitive**: forcing himself to try to work less and to build solutions so that he could work less, rather than accepting that "you can only work 8 hours a day," is what brought him the most growth (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06; first version in `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md`, 2022-10-23). Each of its three parts has a definite content.

*Work less.* "I can work as much as I [expletive] want to if I build the systems that allow me to do that." The emphasis is on building: working less is the result of systems, not of willpower or luck. The mechanism, which he presents as "just obvious," is a causal chain: "working less leads to higher quality rest or more rest, which leads to higher quality work." Hence his claim that people "on this whole 4-Hour workday kick" usually produce better work than people working twelve to sixteen hours a day as the "hustle and grind entrepreneur," a figure he finds "a bit too promoted on social media."

*Earn more.* This part is "subjective, up to the individual." Most people want to earn more doing what they enjoy, and Koe has a rule for himself: he must build or create a way to do that rather than expect it to "fall into his lap." Over time, he believes, anyone with dedication, will and consistency can do what they enjoy for the rest of their life and make a good income.

*Enjoy life.* This is "usually a byproduct" of the other two: of working less, resting more, earning more, and having the time and location freedom to do what you choose. To skeptics in his comments he replies with a question: do you believe you have no control over your life? It takes time, but believe in yourself and work toward a better future.

The order of the three parts is not arbitrary. Enjoyment is not pursued directly; it is what remains when time is freed and income is no longer tied to hours. That is why the 4-Hour Workday, which governs the first part, is the operative piece of the motto.

#### Optimize for creativity, not productivity

In December 2024 Koe opened a video with a line he had, ten months earlier, attributed to Alex Soojung-Kim Pang's *Rest*: "the clever man may work smarter not harder, they say, but the creative man doesn't work at all." In the later video it appears without attribution (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29). The change of attribution does not change the position, but readers should know the line is borrowed.

**Complementary context:** Alex Soojung-Kim Pang's *Rest: Why You Get More Done When You Work Less* (2016) argues, from the work habits of scientists, writers and artists, that deliberate rest is a condition of creative productivity. Chapter 13 discusses what Koe takes from it.

What follows the quotation is Koe's own. He finds it "a shame that the average American has equated overwork with a status symbol, or worse, they use it as a way to escape their responsibilities." "If you were serious about your work you would **optimize for creativity, not productivity**": walk, train, exercise, eat well, "all of the things that lead to a quality mind that leads to quality work," get enough sleep, socialize, "push your limits in calculated ways," and understand "that 4 hours of focused work when done correctly leads to much more results than 12 hours of scattered work." The phrase "quality mind" names the intermediate variable: the hours outside work are not a deduction from productivity but the production of the instrument productivity depends on.

Two arguments explain why long hours undermine that instrument. The first is psychological. "Often times the people that feel like they have to work a long time and try to force themselves to work a long time, and they don't see that work as play, it's often at their own psychological expense; they are lacking quality in their work because they are working too long, and therefore if their work were higher quality they would see better results in less time with less work, and their life would just be all around better" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). Note the condition: the cost falls on those who *force* long hours and do not experience the work as play. This qualification will matter when we see that Koe does not oppose long hours as such.

The second argument is about the mind's shape. Hustle culture, working twelve to fourteen hours a day "in a consistent state of stress," is a problem because of "stress being the antagonist to open-mindedness." When you are closed-minded the whole time and cannot zoom out or have any creativity, "you can't change your work so that you work less and make more" (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). This argument closes a loop. The 4-Hour Workday requires redesigning work (section 12.6), redesign requires creativity, creativity requires an open mind (the zoomed-out mode of Chapter 6), and chronic stress keeps the mind narrow. Overwork is thus self-perpetuating: it consumes exactly the faculty that would be needed to escape it.

#### The "lie" in the four hours

If creativity is the input, what counts as work? In the same December 2024 video Koe describes his day (writing for the first two hours, then administrative tasks) and then admits: "There is a lie somewhere in there. I've never really considered reading, walking, napping or training as a part of my work. I read to hunt for ideas, I walk to make sense of those ideas, I nap because I like to and I train because I enjoy energy and health. The quality and quantity of my work would not be possible if I did not do those things. I'm not optimizing for more, I'm optimizing for the right amount that brings the results I need to actualize my goals." Since "creativity is the fuel for productivity," counting those activities means that "technically I work all day, 24 hours a day, because even my sleep fuels my work. My work is the most enjoyable thing in my life, it is my mission, it is my entire day" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29).

This is a refinement, not a retraction. The four hours measure focused output; the creative inputs run all day. The phrase **optimizing for the right amount** states the goal precisely: not to minimize hours, and not to maximize them, but to find the quantity of focused output that, with its supporting inputs, produces the results a goal requires.

A month later, in a conversation with the founders of Stan, Koe gave the same idea a structural form. When the value you provide and what you do to provide it align with your life's work, "everything else kind of falls around that" and "it's all considered work." His videos on the 4-Hour Workday carry "a lot of philosophy": "it can go over four hours or under, it's just like a constraint to determine, okay, what's the most effective amount of work I can do in this time and what's moving the lever." Outside that constraint, walks (research by listening and writing things down), the gym and the rest were "all done to fuel... my best work." Creative people may not seem to work much, but they must structure the day to deliver their value; others sit longer at work they would not swap for anything else; lives are structured differently (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md`, 2025-01-28).

In the same conversation Vitali, Stan's co-founder, quoted back the lesson that had impacted him most in Koe's course *2 Hour Writer*: "you have to set up your life in a way that enables what it is that you actually want to accomplish." For a writer living a creative lifestyle, "you can't hustle for 14 hours and be creative" (maybe some can, he allows, but conventionally not). Exercise, blood flowing, being present somewhere with sunshine, being mentally uncluttered, meditation or social activities: "whatever gets you in that state, it's all part of the process," and "everyone needs to find for themselves what that lifestyle is." The idea is Koe's, relayed by a guest. *2 Hour Writer* is the name of Koe's course and system for producing all of one's content in under two hours a day from a weekly newsletter.

Vitali also offered his own definition of work, which should be kept distinct from Koe's because it is presented as an "unpopular opinion" by someone who works differently. "I work all the time," days, nights and weekends, but with an "enabling detail": he holds himself accountable "to where the value actually is." Work is "not just a 15,000 items checklist, it's like making concrete, measurable and tangible progress in the areas that matter, and everything that you do in your life should be supporting your ability to do that." His example: waking before a 5:30 alarm and driving around Los Angeles at sunrise for an hour, "**morning dreaming**... thinking and envisioning" the future, which built clarity. "Do you call this work? I call this work." Productivity, for him, is measured "not of how much things you complete but how much progress you make in the direction that you've established"; the tangible work is "a couple hours per day," the rest is working with people and thinking about the world (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md`, 2025-01-28). Vitali's view and Koe's converge from opposite starting points. Koe says he works four hours and then admits that everything else is work too; Vitali says he works all the time and then admits that the tangible part is two hours. Both define work by progress in a chosen direction rather than by time at a desk, and both treat the rest of life as its support.

#### Not against long hours

A recurring misunderstanding forced Koe to clarify what the philosophy is *not*. In 2024 he set out to "smash the bug" that his message is anti-long-hours: people who read only the titles of his videos or the headlines of his newsletters ("4 hours a day," "work less") misinterpret him because they do not watch or read the whole thing. "I'm not saying don't work long hours." His actual claim is that he wants to propose a different way of working and of thinking about work. Working long hours is not inherently bad; what is outdated is working forty to eighty hours a week as a "repetitive and eternal lifestyle," rather than as an occasional thing (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

He was also candid about his rhetoric. "The entrepreneurs that decide to work more usually enjoy working more." "I preach the 4-Hour Work Day as if it's a fit for everyone because that's how you get the point across: you be confident and somewhat polarizing so your words don't get watered down and people actually get use from them." By the same logic, "I understand why entrepreneurs that work a lot of time tell people to work a lot of time; this is the birth of hustle culture" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The admission is important for reading everything else in this chapter. The universal formulations ("you don't need more than four hours") are partly rhetorical; they are calibrated to be heard against a dominant culture of overwork, and Koe explicitly grants the symmetrical legitimacy of the opposite rhetoric. This is consistent with the two registers Chapter 6 identified in the corpus: a general critique of hustle culture and respect for individuals who genuinely enjoy long hours.

Against the image of the overworked entrepreneur he also offers an observation from 2023: "I've never seen an entrepreneur go back to a nine to five; I've only seen nine to fivers leave to become an entrepreneur." To the objection "you're busy all the time," his answer is "no, you aren't": he works about four hours a day, sometimes more, sometimes less, and "you control your lifestyle"; you can work as much as you want if you build the things that allow it (**Source:** `Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md`, 2023-04-16). The observation is anecdotal (he reports what he has seen, not a survey), and its rhetorical function is to reverse the burden of proof: if entrepreneurship were as consuming as its stigma suggests, one would expect some traffic back toward employment.

#### How many hours the author actually works

Koe supports the philosophy with his own numbers, and the numbers change. Presenting them together shows what the "baseline" means in practice.

| 2023-06 | "At this point in my life... about one to two hours a day"; about 90% of days under four | "The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More)" |

The 2023 explanation of the variability invokes what he calls the **principle of impermanence**: people don't believe him when he says he works less than four hours a day, but even people who work twelve to fourteen hours do not do so every day of the year for life. "Sometimes it's four, sometimes it's six, sometimes it's eight, sometimes it's ten, sometimes it's more, sometimes it's less, sometimes it's one, sometimes it's zero." On about 90% of days, when he is not focused on a particular project and has not "procrastinated to the point of needing 10-hour days," he works one to two hours (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). In 2024 he gives the structural explanation: he made the most money of his life working two to four hours a day "because he had leverage locked down," and he was quick to identify his highest-leverage tasks. When building something new (Cortex, new products) he works longer until the system is built; then he maintains it, and "it takes less time to maintain the system rather than build the system" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`, 2024-06-02). The distinction between **building** and **maintaining** a system resolves most of the apparent inconsistency in the table: the four-hour baseline describes maintenance periods; building periods run longer.

The August 2024 version adds a choice. He works four hours "the majority of the time, over 50% right now"; building a startup, things pick up randomly, and with the launch the following month he will work much more. "But I'm not going to unnecessarily fill my plate with things that don't need to be done just to say that I've worked more." When people ask whether there are not other things that could get him ahead, he says yes, but he has other areas of life to get ahead in: the gym every day and cardio four or five times a week, nutrition, mental health, meditation, naps, walks, reading. These "only benefit his work." So which makes more progress: working more on things he does not care to do, or resting and caring for areas of life that benefit his work? (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

What does a "maintenance" day contain? In October 2024 he describes it. His work mostly comes down to writing ("building the audience is all writing": YouTube scripts, newsletter, content), plus administrative tasks, working with the Cortex team and similar maintenance. The first thing in the morning is experimentation: starting a new book or a new product. "Usually those things would kind of fizzle out, but then when I come back to them, or when I start something that actually starts to take off, that brings me into an **intensity phase**" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13). The intensity phase, in which twelve-hour days come naturally, is the season-based exception that Chapter 6 traced and that Chapter 39 treats as part of the cyclical model of life. The morning experiment is its seed: the four-hour baseline contains, at its start, a slot designed to generate the projects that will occasionally break it.

He also answers the "crazy look" he gets when he says he works four hours by listing his output: weekly videos and newsletters, three posts a day on all platforms, managing and building a software company, one book written and two more in progress, more than ten products. "What's so crazy about that?" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29). And in January 2024, filming on the third of the month and getting back on his routine, he described the four-hour constraint as a source of enjoyment rather than a restriction: although he feels he "should have to work longer," "trying to jam everything into 4 hours in the morning is just one of the most fun games for me to play": wake up early, do the routine, do all the high-lever work; it takes four hours to build anything he wants, and the feedback of playing that game feels good (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07). The game framing connects back to *The Art of Focus*'s "frame of a game that you can play to win" and forward to Chapter 10's account of life as a game: a constraint with clear rules and feedback is what turns work into play.

The table and these quotations together establish what the evidence for the 4-Hour Workday actually is: the self-reported practice of one person over several years, varying by phase, with a consistent pattern (a baseline at or under four hours in maintenance periods, longer days when building or near deadlines). It is testimony, not a study, and Koe does not present it otherwise.

#### The arithmetic of a million dollars in four hours

The most ambitious claim attached to the philosophy is financial. "As a one person business it is more than possible to make a million dollars plus a year working 4 hours a day; people don't believe me when I say that, but it just takes simple logic." The logic: it does not take long to write three tweets a day or one thread a week; "it can take less than an hour a day, probably way less than that, to write a week's worth of content." If you are growing and generating "millions of impressions per month," where do they go? Spend fifteen minutes a day promoting your work and forty-five minutes writing a newsletter, and "you now have all of this traffic going to your product or service, that at a specific conversion rate... that's a lot of money if you do the math." Why do people disbelieve it? "Your entire life you've been conditioned to: I work 8 hours a day, I make this amount of money for that amount of work." But "entrepreneurs don't work on an hourly basis; they work based on the value that they provide," and some do not work at all because automation and a team run the business. In the same passage he admits that he personally works "a lot more" at the moment, because he is building Cortex (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28).

The argument has a clear structure: output that can be produced in under an hour, multiplied by distribution (impressions) and by a conversion rate. Its premises are the ones Parts IX and X of this book examine (that an audience can be grown, that a product converts); the four hours are possible only because income has been decoupled from time through media leverage. Without that decoupling, the arithmetic does not hold, which is why the 4-Hour Workday cannot be separated from the business model Koe advocates.

He condenses the path into a five-step formula, repeated almost word for word in two 2024 videos: "(1) Do not work more than 4 hours. (2) Start with client work. (3) Get results, make money. (4) Build your audience. (5) Productize your work. Repeat steps three through five until you reach 1 million. Then don't stop" (**Source:** `Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md`, 2024-02-25; `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28). In one transcript the numbering of "steps three through five" is ambiguous, depending on whether "do not work more than four hours" counts as the first step; the sequence itself is clear. Note that the time constraint is listed as step one, before any business activity: it is the condition under which the other steps must be carried out, and section 12.6 will show that this placement is the point of Koe's Law. A year earlier he had described the two routes to the million: high ticket (coaching, consulting, sometimes agency work; he does not like the agency model, "but it works") or low-ticket products. Either way it is a process in which leverage builds over time in the form of an audience or personal-brand traffic source that compounds. "Eventually one day you run a promotion, you make your first 100 or 200K a month," you realize you could hit the number, and then you hit it. "It's that simple. I mean, it's difficult, but it's that simple" (**Source:** `The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md`, 2023-03-19). The final sentence is a fair summary of how he wants the formula to be read: simple in structure, not easy in execution.

#### Deep work: no longer an option

Koe's name for the focused hours is borrowed. He quotes Cal Newport, "who you are, what you think, feel and do, what you love, is the sum of what you focus on," and notes that Newport wrote *Deep Work*: "I have it somewhere out in my living room, I've never read it, but I have been doing my own version of deep work for the past 10 years, really I'd say probably six years actually" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The term and the opening quotation are Newport's; the practice and the definitions that follow are Koe's own. The self-correction (ten years, then "probably six") is his, mid-sentence. Chapter 8 introduced Newport and his distinction between deep and shallow work; here the point is what Koe builds on the word.

His definition of productivity is the first thing he builds: "Productivity is about getting the most effective work done in the least amount of time possible. Productivity is not about seeing who can work the longest and then wear that as a badge of honor that they can show their friends, co-workers and peers; that's just a status game." The definition has two parts that should be read together. "Most effective work" excludes busy work (Chapter 8's distinction between tasks and levers); "least amount of time" makes time a cost to be minimized rather than a contribution to be displayed. The **status game** he contrasts it with is the competition for who works longest, a display of effort rather than a production of results.

His second move is to argue that deep work has changed status. "In a world of notifications, ample options and overwhelm, deep work is frankly no longer an option, it is a necessity." Block out time to work on your future or your business, and avoid conversations, notifications and distractions. Then the purpose: "channel the creative firepower that you've gained through your consumption or your education or your skill acquisition," and focus it "into a vessel, AKA a business," to reap money, connections and resources, "and then be able to have that as currency that you can trade up towards a better life." "Deep work is how you build out your dreams in record time" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued 2024-03-21). Three of Koe's coined terms meet in this passage. **Creative firepower** is the stock of knowledge, interests and skills accumulated through consumption and learning; the **vessel** is the vehicle into which it is channeled (here a business; Chapter 9 treated the business as the vessel of a life's work); **currency** is what the vessel returns, which can be traded up for a better life. The image makes deep work the conversion step between input and outcome: consumption that is never channeled into a vessel stays potential, which is the problem Chapter 14 will call tutorial hell.

The same video adds the time objection's first answer, which section 12.2 develops: "If you don't have the time right now to write for 15 minutes every day, you're not going to have the time in the future, if writing is going to be what you're doing for work." Deep work, he says, is about "actually executing on the tasks that you're going to be doing, but slowly tapering up until you can do them longer" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26). **Taper up** is his term for this gradual increase, and it is the training logic of "focus is a muscle" applied to time: start with the load you can handle and add to it.

### 12.2 Start With One Hour

#### The objection and the comparison that dissolves it

The most common reaction to the 4-Hour Workday is not that four hours is too little but that the reader does not have four hours at all. A job, a commute, children or school already consume the day. Koe's answer is that this objection, far from refuting the philosophy, describes the situation in which almost everyone who succeeded actually started.

He locates the source of the objection in a distorted reference point. The main problem, he says in 2024, is that everyone is immersed in **hustle culture** and thinks that becoming an entrepreneur requires eight to twelve hours a day, as people claim online, or compares themselves to people far ahead of them, such as Sam Altman or Elon Musk ("they were in the factories working 12–16 hours a day, sleeping there"). You do not have that time: you have responsibilities, schooling, or whatever already takes eight to ten hours a day, and after that you are drained. "They don't have 12 to 15 hours a day to do that, they have 1 hour a day." Anyone building a business from scratch, he insists, has one hour, not twelve or fifteen. The video's purpose is to teach how to use that hour and how to tap "the universal principle that is leverage"; it is, he notes, not exactly a deep work routine video (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`, 2024-06-02). The comparison he warns against is an error of reference class: the schedule of a founder running a large company is not evidence about what it takes to *begin*.

The positive principle is stated a few months earlier as a near tautology, deliberately: "You don't need more time than you already have; that's the realization you need to make if your excuse is time... it's impossible to have more time than you have." "Everyone starts somewhere. People don't start with a **fake millionaire routine**, they start with what they have." Though he has mostly worked four hours a day, "when I was just starting out I didn't have 12 hours a day, I didn't have 4 hours a day, I worked a job, I had other responsibilities, I was building something on the side; during that time I worked for 1 to 2 hours a day, and that's what led to this" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The "fake millionaire routine" is the idealized day (early rise, cold plunge, eight hours of uninterrupted creation) that circulates online and that nobody actually begins with. Measuring oneself against it turns a real starting position into an excuse.

The sharpest form of the answer is a comparison that opens his 2023 deep-work video and reappears in 2024: "**If you can spend eight hours building someone else's dreams, you can spend one hour building your own**" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; `Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md`, 2023-09-17; the book version in `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). The objection "I have no time" is met not with a productivity technique but with a ratio: the hours already given to an employer show that the capacity for sustained effort exists; what is missing is the allocation of a fraction of it to one's own project. The 2023 version adds the ladder that follows: if you don't have one hour a day to invest in having two hours a day, "and so on," you need to get your priorities straight. The phrase "and so on" contains the growth model of the whole chapter: one hour invested now buys more hours later, which are invested again.

#### The author never had twelve hours

Koe supports the claim with his own history, told in several versions. In college he went to class and worked part-time, spending two to three hours a day building side projects and learning through Udemy courses or YouTube. At his first full-time job he learned during his one-hour commute with audiobooks, podcasts or YouTube, and procrastinated work tasks for an hour or two to build side businesses. "I had 1 to 4 hours, if I didn't lie to myself about how I could prioritize my time." When he went full-time on his own thing, he worked about the same amount (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). The qualifier "if I didn't lie to myself" moves the question from the availability of time to its honest accounting: the hours were there, hidden inside commutes and slack periods.

His start on social media is the most concrete example. He began "by writing posts and replying to people in between video game matches. I would play Teamfight Tactics or League of Legends and reply in between." Adding up the hours that got him started: "probably around an hour a day." The lesson he draws is blunt: "you're drastically overestimating the amount of work you need to do to just start"; "people don't start with 12 hours, they start with 1 hour in the morning. You just love to make excuses about how your situation is special" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29). The anecdote is personal and should be read as such; its value is in correcting an overestimate, not in prescribing video games.

He also recalls what the alternative felt like. Coming home from work and trying to go to the gym afterward, when he normally went in the morning, the workouts "just sucked, and it pissed me off even more," because he then had to drive home in rush hour, arrive with no time left, and "life just sucked all around" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`, 2024-06-02). The story illustrates the reason the protected hour goes first in the day, which section 12.5 develops: whatever is attempted after an eight- to ten-hour obligation is attempted with what is left.

#### The minimum you can, and tapering up

For readers with heavier constraints, Koe lowers the entry point further. "I'm not asking you to spend four hours a day on this, or six hours a day, or eight hours a day. I'm asking you to spend the minimum amount of time you can, because if you don't, then you aren't creating your future life." Without blocking out and focusing time to move levers toward your ideal future, "you're not going to create it, it's that simple." You should not feel you need two or three hours at the start "like a young dude who has no responsibilities"; "if you have a wife and kids, then yeah, you're gonna have to start with 15 to 30 minutes and work up from there, and it's going to take a bit longer" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The passage contains two claims that should be distinguished. The first is a concession: the amount of time can be very small, and the cost of a small amount is only speed ("it's going to take a bit longer"). The second is non-negotiable: *some* time must be blocked and focused, because the future life is not created otherwise. The minimum is flexible in size, not in existence.

The heuristic that goes with it was quoted at the end of section 12.1 and reappears in the 2024 compilation: "If you don't have the time right now to write for 15 minutes every day, you're not going to have the time in the future, if writing is going to be what you're doing for work" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). Its logic is diagnostic. Time does not appear later by itself; a person who cannot find fifteen minutes now will, in the future, face the same competing demands with the same habits. Starting small and **tapering up** is therefore not a compromise but the only route: the capacity to spend more time on a project is built by spending a little time on it now, as a muscle is built by the load it can currently bear.

**Complementary context:** "tapering" is a term from sports training, where it usually means *reducing* training load before a competition. Koe uses "taper up" in the opposite sense, for a gradual increase. The usage is his own.

#### One hour, every morning, for life

Where should the hour go, and when? Koe's instruction from 2023 is unusually absolute. Your life must change fundamentally, and the hour must be fitted in and prioritized as "arguably the most important thing in your life": it has to be the first thing every morning, or so habitual later in the day that distractions and responsibilities cannot get in the way. You will have to wake up earlier. Its content depends on your most pressing problem: "if you need money, dedicate the first hour of your day to education and action around money"; if you need to lose weight, to fitness; if you need to decrease stress, to mental health. "Don't look for a shortcut... this is a lifestyle"; you will do one hour of focused work every morning "for the rest of your life" (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11).

Two features connect this instruction to earlier chapters. The content of the hour is defined by the most pressing problem, which is Chapter 8's definition of purpose ("your purpose is the most pressing problem in your life right now"). And the hour combines **education and action**: not only learning about money, fitness or stress, but doing something about them, which anticipates Chapter 14's argument that information without application is false progress.

The same year Koe described how this looked in his own life, under the name of his "four-hour workday philosophy." The first block of every morning went to the **high-lever project**, the project leading to the life he wanted. Even with a nine-to-five, he had an hour a day to build, "making progress however slow." He then defines the word: "when I say project, I mean something that is measurable and documented." It need not be physical ("hammer, nails, wood"); it can be a weight-training log, food and weight tracking, plus research into fitness used to correct one's actions. The reason the project matters is that "without doing, you can't register a problem when consuming education; it goes in one ear and out the other." In business, building a product or brand involves levers you move daily (the transcript here reads "removing," which makes the exact phrase ambiguous), milestones, and components to piece together (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30). The definition of a project as *measurable and documented* is what turns an hour of effort into a feedback loop; Chapter 9 developed the project as the practical unit of change, and this is its daily slot.

In 2024 the instruction was reduced to its core: "wake up 1 hour earlier... focus on one to three lever-moving tasks toward a goal that you set for yourself." He assumes, for that video, that the goal is about making more money (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`, 2024-06-02). The title of the video carries the arithmetic that the body leaves implicit: one hour a day for a year is **365 hours**. A month earlier he had stated it directly: "nobody has 12 hours a day to start; you have to start with 1 hour, and you can drastically change your life in 365 hours." The same passage gives the complement that keeps the hour productive: "schedule walks in between work blocks, go to the gym, block out reading time, go to dinner with your friends, and just do anything that isn't work, especially thinking about work; that's the hardest part" (**Source:** `How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md`, 2024-05-26). One focused hour and real rest around it are presented as a pair.

In 2024 he also condensed the practical advice into a sentence that removes any expectation of ease: "Nobody is going to give you the time, you must take it." Set aside thirty to sixty minutes in a non-distracting block of your day and build the habit. "It will be difficult at the start, as with anything new; don't expect otherwise" (**Source:** `The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md`, 2024-03-24).

#### Why the person with one hour often wins

Koe goes further than defending the single hour: he argues that people with little time often succeed *faster* than people with a lot. Those who challenge his four-hour claim, he says in 2023, are usually "kids who don't know what they're doing" or people just starting, "brainwashed" by business ideology: with so much time, they think they must fill it with work. Most of the people who will succeed are forced to focus on levers and high-value activities, for example people at a nine-to-five they hate, who feel the pressure to get out and dedicate "one hour, that's it, every single morning" to making the business work. They will make it work faster than the kid working fourteen-hour days who "just procrastinate[s] the entire time and think[s] they're making progress." If nine-to-five workers can build a side income with one hour a day and scale it as leverage and audience compound, "what is so crazy about working one hour a day?... it's crazy to work longer than that." His diagnosis of the long-hours beginner: "most people aren't increasing their skill level, they are increasing the amount of time that they spend at their current skill level," "for absolutely no reason" (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18).

The argument has the same structure as the forcing constraint of section 12.1. Scarcity of time forces selection, and selection forces attention onto levers; abundance of time permits everything, including endless low-value activity that feels like progress. The final sentence names the real variable. What produces results is skill, and adding hours at a fixed skill level adds volume without adding capability. This connects forward to Koe's Law (section 12.6), whose core claim is that income grows when the *work evolves* through skill, not when the hours grow.

A caution is in order. The claim that pressured workers "often" outperform full-time beginners is an observation from Koe's experience, not a measured rate, and he uses "procrastinate" here in a pejorative sense that section 12.4 will contrast with his positive notion of mindful procrastination.

#### With a job: one hour a day and four on the weekend

Koe is explicit that his four-hour framework was designed mainly for "people that are creators... and they have more time on their hands." With a job, "this is going to have to be limited to like 1 hour a day and maybe 4 hours on the weekend, if you have that time." If you do not care about starting a business right now, you can use the framework for your job itself. His own example comes from his time at a web design agency: he "would usually knock out my work in like 2 to 3 hours and then I would just screw around or work on my business for the rest of the time." The general purpose, he says, is "saving time on the things that you're working on to free up more time for the more [important] priorities in your life" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The passage shows that the framework has two uses. As a creator's operating schedule it governs the whole working day. As a method it can be applied to any work, including employment: the same compression of tasks into focused blocks frees time, which can then be redirected. His agency story is a personal anecdote and depends on a job whose output was measured by deliverables rather than hours at a desk; readers in jobs that measure presence will find the second use more limited.

#### Build the good life while you are still in the bad one

The deepest justification for the single hour comes from a guest, and Koe adopts it. In a 2025 conversation, Koe asks Sahil Bloom how to cope when you are not in the situation you want (school, a part-time job) and "get trapped in your head." Sahil's answer: "The way that you escape the bad is by doubling down on more of the good." The path is not "an **eject button** out of the bad life, and then you go build the good life. It's: you find a way to build the good life while you're still here. Then you hit the eject button on the bad part, because you've already built the good." He did exactly that: "I had started sowing the seeds of this entire new world... before I hit the eject button on the thing that was sucking the life out of me" (in his case, a career in finance). It is "the common thread... among people who have changed their life: they find a way to create structure around building the good while they are still living the bad" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30).

Sahil names three mechanisms, and they explain why the order matters. First, building alongside creates "evidence of your ability to live and build a life on the good," which replaces hope with proof. Second, it makes the jump "much easier and more comforting," because you "feel security to hit eject." Third, "you have more energy around that whole experience when you're working on the thing that's going to change your life alongside it": the good part of the day subsidizes the bad part's energy, rather than the other way around. The **eject button** is Sahil's image, and it is the reason this book treats the one-hour practice as more than a productivity tip. The protected hour is the structure in which the good life is built before it is needed.

Koe's own version of the principle predates the conversation by more than two years and is framed in the vocabulary of Part I. "One cycle that I would encourage everyone to break is the conventional work or employee cycle": at least try building a **hobby project** and potentially see it grow into some kind of business or something you have more control over (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20). The recommendation follows from the principle, stated in the same video, that staying in the same cycles as others yields the same results as others (Chapter 1's autopilot). The modesty of the ask is deliberate: not quitting a job but adding a project that might grow.

#### The inconvenient truth

The guest who made the cost of this path most visible is Dickie Bush. In a 2023 conversation with Koe he describes what building on the side actually required. While working at BlackRock and scaling his writing program Ship 30 (all of 2021, working from home), he wrote purely from 4:30 to 7:30 a.m. for more than a year, because a 7:30 a.m. meeting was required nearly every day for three to four years. Earlier, in New York City, he had also been up at 4:30 to exercise (trying everything for weight loss, from 280 to 220 pounds: fasting, keto) and to do anything productive before reaching his desk at 6:50 or 7, then spent the day under fluorescent lights until 5:30 or 6 or later, walked home, ate, and panicked about 4:30 the next morning, for a year and a half. His summary: "The **inconvenient truth** is I spent from 4:30 to 7:30 and 7 p.m. to 10 writing, learning, building, doing things for two years to unlock the ability to not have to spend that middle 12 hours of my day working for someone else" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

The story belongs next to Koe's own because it qualifies it. Koe's version of the one-hour start emphasizes how little time is needed; Dickie's emphasizes that, for some, the hours around a demanding job were long and the period lasted years. Both describe the same structure (building the good inside the bad until the eject button can be pressed), but they disagree, implicitly, about how hard it is. The honest reading is that the minimum is one hour, that more is often needed, and that the amount depends on the gap between the current situation and the goal. What neither of them suggests is that the middle twelve hours can be escaped without first investing the hours at the edges.

### 12.3 Prioritize: Focus on Few and the Priority Ladder

The protected hour solves the problem of *when*. It leaves open the problem of *what*. One hour spent on the wrong task is an hour of busy work, and Chapter 8 already established that the bottom rung of the goal hierarchy is a small number of daily levers. This section adds two things: an account of how to choose the few things that receive focus at all, and Koe's first full scheme for ordering a working day, the priority ladder.

#### "Focus on one thing" and its hidden premise

Koe accepts the common advice to focus on one thing and then points out what it leaves out. "In order to focus on one thing you have to focus on the things, the multitude of things, that allow you to focus on that one thing." If you cannot start a business because you lack time or money or something else, then identify the problem in the way, bring focus to *it*, so that you can later bring focus back to the main thing you want (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). The point is a correction to a slogan. Singular focus presupposes conditions (time, money, health, a minimally ordered life) that are themselves objects requiring attention. A person who ignores them in the name of focus will find the one thing perpetually interrupted by the neglected others. The qualification matters because elsewhere Koe promotes singular focus strongly (Chapter 9's "one meaningful project"); here he states the precondition.

#### The single bottleneck

In 2026 Koe turned the same thought into a sharper principle. "Most people try to change their life and place 10% of their attention into diet, sleep, exercise, meditation, relationships, their side hustle, and more, when they would get better at all of them faster if they focused on the **single bottleneck** holding them back the most." The claim is counterintuitive: "Focus does not decrease your progress in all areas. It increases them in all areas." "By focusing on one thing, you increase every other area." "For most people, the single bottleneck is money, because money buys anything." He traces the cascade: solving finances frees resources for diet (higher-quality food); more money produces a less stressful state, which lets you think more creatively, which benefits creative work and even spirituality; a clearer mind, a better diet, more energy and more time to exercise in turn benefit relationships (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

The two passages, three years apart, fit together. The 2023 point says that the conditions of focus must be addressed; the 2026 point says which condition to address first: the one that constrains the most others. The argument is a systems argument (a bottleneck limits the throughput of everything downstream of it), and its empirical part, that money is the bottleneck "for most people," is an opinion offered without data. Readers for whom health or a relationship is the true constraint can apply the principle while rejecting the example. The **single bottleneck** also gives a concrete answer to the question section 12.2 left open: the protected morning hour goes to the bottleneck.

#### Three things at a time: "not never, but not now"

The question of how many things one can hold at once received its clearest answer from Dickie Bush in the 2023 conversation. "You can only focus on, I think, three things at any given time, three kind of larger-picture goals," with the evidence, from his own experience, that whenever he has more than three on a day, week or six-month horizon, it fails. So each time he slots something new in he asks: "what am I removing to add that thing in... at what cost... what am I willing to sacrifice?" He admits he has been guilty of adding more and more without asking what he was sacrificing. His priorities at that time (physical fitness and a financial foundation) likely came at the expense of relationships, traveling and hobbies: "**not never, but not now**." "I'm going to be able to build anything I want, but not everything, and only a couple things at a time," which means extending the time horizon (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

"Not never, but not now" is the formula that makes saying no psychologically tolerable. A deferred goal is not abandoned; it is scheduled behind the current ones. The number three is Dickie's heuristic from personal experience, and it coincides with Koe's own count of daily priority tasks in the same period (three, in Commandment 3). Chapter 8 showed that Koe's count later became a range (one to three for beginners, five to ten in his own "Lego day"); the principle that every addition has a cost does not change with the number.

#### The priority ladder

In March 2023 Koe presented the scheme that, by his own account, organized his days for years. He calls it the **priority ladder**. "With the priority ladder you can drastically change your life in six months with one hour a day of focused work, one hour, that's it; do not think that you need anything more than one hour, one meaningful project, one day at a time." The ladder has four priority time blocks, "all not only decreasing in challenge but also increasing in the amount of **distraction potential**." Priority 1 is "absolute laser focused on building out your vision... through a project"; Priority 4 is maintenance (emails, messages), where being distracted matters less "because there's nothing after that... there's no creative work that you have to do" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The organizing idea is that two properties of a task vary inversely across the day. The most challenging work is the work most damaged by distraction; the least challenging work tolerates interruption. **Distraction potential**, Koe's term, is the degree to which a block can absorb distraction without harm. Ordering blocks so that challenge decreases as distraction potential increases means that the day's inevitable interruptions fall where they do the least damage.

| Block | Content | Challenge | Distraction potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| Priority 1 | One new project, first thing in the morning; the levers that create the vision | Highest | Lowest (must be none) |
| Priority 2 | Lever-moving tasks from a project already producing results ("how I feed my system") | High | Low |
| Priority 3 | More lever-moving, creative or meaningful work you won't outsource; keeps momentum | Moderate | Moderate |
| Priority 4 | Maintenance: messages, email, community | Lowest | Highest (tolerable) |

Each block deserves a closer look, because Koe gives a reason for each.

#### Priority 1: the hour that built his life

"This pretty much built my entire life": since his younger years he "always, always had one project that I was working on pretty close to first thing in the morning," and now always first thing. At the time of recording, that project was writing his book, chosen because it was new, "not something that I have to consistently work on... maintain or update." If you have built nothing yet and have only one hour a day, "this is the one hour time block that you use," focused on "the levers that are going to lead to creating your vision for the future." For beginners, he suggests, it is probably starting a social media brand or learning to write (he mentions his courses *2 Hour Writer* and *Digital Economics*) (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued 2024-03-21).

The defining criterion of Priority 1 is novelty: it holds a project that is being *built*, not one being maintained. This is the same distinction between building and maintaining that explained the variations in his working hours in section 12.1, now applied to the order of the day.

#### Climbing the ladder

The ladder is not static; work moves up it. Once the first hour yields results, "you move that into the second priority time block so you can start working on something new." Priority 2 holds lever-moving tasks; for him, replying on social media or, mostly, creating content: "how I feed my system," creative work he does not want to outsource. Starting something new in Priority 1 "is what's going to keep **meaningful dopamine** flowing and... maintain momentum along this path, until you systemize that and then you bump it up another priority." He gives a typical progression: start a social media brand; once it produces results and becomes efficient, build a service or product to monetize it; move that up and start reaching out to people; then build a funnel or something else; "all of these things slowly, like, build on each other over time" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued 2024-03-21).

The mechanism of promotion connects three chapters. From Chapter 11 it takes **meaningful dopamine**: the dopamine earned through progress and effort on a goal, as opposed to the cheap dopamine of consumption. Something new in the first block of each day supplies a steady source of it, which is what sustains momentum. From Chapter 5 it takes systemization as the conversion of a demanding activity into a routine one: a project moves down in challenge (and therefore up the ladder in number) only when it has been systemized enough to tolerate more distraction. And it anticipates section 12.6, where Koe's Law describes the same promotion at the scale of a business: client work becomes a hybrid model, which becomes a product, each stage freeing time for the next.

#### Priority 3: momentum and work you won't outsource

"The third time block is also for lever-moving tasks or creative tasks or meaningful tasks, right, so the things that you don't want to outsource. I will never outsource my writing, at least I hope I won't, maybe I will, um, but in terms of, like, just writing in my day, I'm not going to outsource it as a whole." Its function is "just to continue keeping the momentum going on whatever you're building." He adds that "all of this can also apply to, like, a corporate job, or just any work that you have; you have to structure your days with this method" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued 2024-03-21).

The hesitation in the quotation is worth keeping, because it is honest about the criterion. What defines Priority 2 and 3 work is not that it can never be delegated but that one does not *want* to delegate it, because it is the creative core of what one does. The criterion is preference and identity, not technical possibility. The remark about corporate jobs echoes the second use of the framework from section 12.2: the ladder orders any working day, not only a creator's.

#### Priority 4 and the rogue thought

The last block holds maintenance: checking and responding to messages and emails, checking and interacting in his community (Modern Mastery, at the time). "This is where I expose myself to people." The reason it comes last is the most psychologically specific argument in the ladder. If you expose yourself to messages, phone or notifications too early in the morning, "that one **rogue thought** that you get from it, that you think isn't of importance when you first see it... it's gonna pop up, and then that alone is going to split your focus, and then that opens up room for entropy, where your mind starts going to all of these different thoughts without you knowing it." "You probably think it's normal right now... and you think that you're doing the best, most efficient work... but you're not." So push all conversational and maintenance tasks to the fourth priority. To the objection "oh, I can touch my phone in the morning and work just fine," his answer is "no, you can't" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued 2024-03-21).

The **rogue thought** is one of Koe's coined terms: a stray thought opened by early exposure to messages or notifications, which seems unimportant when it appears but then occupies part of conscious attention for the rest of the session. The argument has three steps, each from an earlier chapter. Conscious attention is scarce (Chapter 6's RAM). An unresolved input becomes an open loop that keeps running in the background (the same chapter's open loops). And a split mind is, in the vocabulary of Chapter 5, a mind with higher entropy: attention dispersed among many objects rather than ordered toward one. The specific claim that people *believe* they are unaffected while being affected is a claim about the invisibility of the cost, and it is the reason the rule is stated so absolutely. Chapter 6 discussed the same phenomenon as "the phone in the morning"; here it becomes a rule of ordering.

#### Block length and the self-made lifestyle

Koe structures the four priorities as "anywhere from 45 to 90 minutes long time blocks," with breaks between them: making breakfast, lunch, a thirty- to forty-minute walk listening to something, usually closer to the last blocks. He refuses to list "every little hack" because "the entire joy of all of this comes through experimentation, creating a way that you like doing and just testing different techniques": pull the good parts of others' methods into your own day and refine over time "until you live a completely **self-made lifestyle**" (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; reissued 2024-03-21).

The closing remark changes the status of everything above it. The priority ladder is offered not as a protocol to be copied but as a starting structure for experimentation, and the goal of the experimentation is a lifestyle designed by oneself. This is the same stance section 12.7 will find in his treatment of prescriptions (follow them as a beginner, then build your own), and it explains why later sections show the ladder changing names and shapes over the years. Koe himself kept experimenting with it, and section 12.5 shows what it became.

### 12.4 Time Blocks, Deadlines and Mindful Procrastination

Choosing the right tasks and ordering them is necessary but not sufficient. Inside each block, attention still has to be held on one thing. This section covers the mechanics Koe uses for that purpose. They share one principle, stated in Chapter 6's terms: focused work is *narrow* conscious attention, and narrowing can be engineered. Time blocks narrow attention in time; deadlines narrow it further by adding pressure; public and true deadlines add consequences. Koe's account of procrastination, which closes the section, turns out to be an account of the same mechanism operating without design.

#### Commandment 5: time blocks and breaks

The fifth of the Ten Commandments is "time blocks and breaks." Koe acknowledges the Pomodoro Technique as "very good, but I found something different that works well for me." Before this becomes a habit, "very heavily consider writing this on your calendar": "you need to make it real, you need to take it out of your mind and you need to put it somewhere physical" (calendar, notebook, a sticky note on the computer), because you are forming a new habit. His protocol in 2023: schedule in ninety-minute blocks; sit down with a priority task (for example, writing content); set a small physical kitchen timer for forty-five minutes; work without any distraction, with no tabs open, only what is needed; when it rings, re-evaluate ("did I complete this, what's next?") and set another forty-five minutes; after ninety minutes, get up and walk outside for thirty minutes, or ten if he does not feel like thirty. "The main thing is to just get up and realign and bring your intention and attention to a new priority task" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

**Complementary context:** the Pomodoro Technique, developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, divides work into twenty-five-minute intervals separated by short breaks, with a longer break after four intervals. Koe's 45/90-minute structure is longer, and he presents it as a personal alternative rather than a criticism.

Each element of the protocol has a function. Writing the block down externalizes an intention that would otherwise compete with everything else in the mind for attention (a habit not yet formed needs a physical anchor). The physical timer separates timekeeping from the device that carries distractions. The re-evaluation at forty-five minutes is a small cybernetic correction: compare progress with the target and set the next one. And the break is defined by its purpose, which is not only recovery but *realignment*: moving intention and attention from one priority task to the next.

#### Focus is a muscle: active rest

In February 2024 the same practice reappears as "Guideline 4" of a productivity routine, with its rationale stated through the muscle metaphor of section 12.1: "**Focus is a muscle**, and success is reserved for those who train it." He places time blocks at the end of a sequence of narrowing devices: "Identity, vision, projects and deadlines all narrow your attention on the present moment, from the big picture to microscopic action; time blocks take that a step further, narrowing your attention and making it easy to get into flow." The method: set a phone timer for forty-five to ninety minutes; "you can start with 45, you can start with five, you can start with 10, start with whatever... like building a muscle in the gym, it's going to take time for your focus to actually grow." When the timer goes off, take a break, go on a walk, "engage in **active rest**," and do not think about work, or alternatively "set an intention for your next work block and allow your subconscious mind to munch on potential ideas" while walking or cooking; get out of the chair and "stop burning your eyes with blue light." "It's like a rest between sets at the gym... once you hit failure you can't go past failure" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18).

The analogy to sets in a gym is precise in one respect: rest between sets is not time off from training but part of the training, because the next set depends on it. **Active rest** has two forms in the passage, and they are different. In the first, attention leaves work entirely. In the second, a conscious intention is set and then left to the subconscious, which is the open-focus mode of Chapter 6 used deliberately. Chapter 13 develops the second form at length, as the default mode network and Koe's polarity of work and rest.

#### The "Lego" day

By October 2024 Koe's own blocks had grown. He describes liking "two hour work blocks spread throughout the day with breaks in between": "it's like Legos throughout the day, where I can schedule one to two hour work blocks" to complete the five to ten priority tasks for that day, depending on how long he works. Between focus blocks he walks, goes to the gym, eats, "or whatever" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13). **Legos** is a metaphor Koe uses for several things (notes, elements of a lifestyle, ideas); here it means blocks of time that can be arranged and rearranged across a day.

The duration of the blocks changed several times across the corpus, and the changes follow a pattern.

| Date | Structure | Source video |
|---|---|---|
| 2023-01 | 90-minute blocks made of two 45-minute kitchen-timer sprints, then a walk | "The 4-Hour Workday" |
| 2023-03 | 45–90-minute blocks for each priority, breaks between | "Change Your Life In 6 Months" |
| 2024-02 | 45–90-minute phone timer, or start with 5–10 minutes; active rest | "Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day" |
| 2024-10 | 1–2-hour blocks spread "like Legos" for 5–10 priority tasks | "It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day" |
| 2025-05 | "Challenging time blocks" of 60–90 minutes with a non-work task between them | "The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life" |

The reason Koe gives for the changes is personal experimentation. The two ends of the range can coexist: short blocks for someone still training focus, long ones for someone who has trained it. That reading follows directly from "focus is a muscle": the appropriate load depends on current capacity.

#### Commandment 6: deadlines narrow attention

The sixth commandment is "manipulate deadlines," and its principle is the theoretical center of this section. "Deadlines narrow your attention, and the more narrow your attention is, the less likely distractions are going to register in your awareness." Koe explains it through the **Focus Matrix** of Chapter 6 (conscious or unconscious focus, crossed with narrow or open focus). The same mechanism operates on the negative side of the matrix: in unconscious narrow focus, when you are locked on a problem, nothing else distracts you, unless one problem leads to the next ("oh no"), your focus opens up, and you are "drowning in all of these problems." On the positive side, with clarity on what you are doing and narrowed on one specific task, no distractions penetrate because you are immersed, "and that's when you start to kick into flow." "You can see the deadline as a challenge as well" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The passage makes a subtle point about narrowness. Narrow focus is not good in itself; it is the shape of both stress (unconscious narrowing on a problem, which cascades) and flow (conscious narrowing on a chosen task, with clarity). What distinguishes them is consciousness and clarity, which is why the deadline must be applied to a task already chosen and defined (sections 12.3 and Chapter 8) rather than to a vague obligation. The remark that a deadline can be "seen as a challenge" connects to Chapter 10's account of flow, which requires a challenge matched to skill.

He then reveals that everything described so far in the chapter was already a deadline. The four hours in total, the ninety-minute blocks and the forty-five-minute timers are all **pseudo-deadlines** that narrow attention. The question he leaves with the viewer is practical: "how can you add a deadline to your important work?"

#### The pseudo-deadline and Parkinson's law

The four-hour limit's role as a pseudo-deadline is explained through a law Koe borrows. Limiting yourself to four hours a day "kind of acts as a pseudo deadline." It is not as powerful as a real-world deadline that you must meet, but "just limiting yourself to four hours and knowing that you don't have to work more than four hours, it will shrink time, and according to Parkinson's law, where work matches the time or whatever it is, you're going to try and fit your work within those 4 hours" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The paraphrase is loose ("whatever it is"), and in a different video he states the law correctly: "Parkinson's law, which states that work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion: so if you give yourself 10 minutes to do a task it's going to take 10 minutes, if you give yourself a week to do a task it's going to take a week." The law, he says there, "stresses the importance of deadlines and how to set a true deadline that actually forces you into action," and he calls his own concept of tactical stress "quite similar" to it (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

Chapter 6 gave the origin of Parkinson's law (C. Northcote Parkinson's 1955 essay). What matters here is how Koe uses it. He reads the law not as a satire of bureaucracy but as a lever: if work expands to fill time, then restricting time contracts work. The four-hour workday is the restriction at the scale of the day; blocks and timers are the restriction at the scale of a task.

The same law reaches Koe through Sahil Bloom, who uses it for a different purpose: **batching**. Batching engages "what's called Parkinson's Law... the idea that work expands to fill the time allotted for its completion. So if you give yourself four hours to do emails, you'll take four hours. If you condense it into a single hour block, you'll crank through the entire inbox in an hour." His application is to "condense some of these energy drains into shorter windows than you previously would have been comfortable with," to "free up space and actually... breathe life into your calendar" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). Sahil's use complements Koe's. Koe compresses the important work to sharpen it; Sahil compresses the draining work to shrink it. Section 12.5 returns to Sahil's framework of energy drains.

Koe's own most concrete pseudo-deadline is physical. Going to the gym, at one or two in the afternoon or whenever, is "the Parkinson's law of my work": he must finish his work before then because he will not want to do it afterward, being tired. Sometimes he works afterward, mainly when pressure is high, "like working like a lion to go and hunt when you're hungry." If he is not forced to do work in the afternoon, he will not; he usually procrastinates until he has to, and then the work gets done pretty well (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`, 2024-06-02). The lion image is Naval Ravikant's "work like a lion, not a cow," which Chapter 6 discussed; here it describes how a fixed afternoon commitment converts the morning into a hunt with a deadline. The admission that he "procrastinates until he has to" anticipates the last part of this section.

#### Level two: a challenging deadline with a reason to obey it

In May 2025 Koe rebuilt the deadline principle into a sequence of narrowing "levels." Level one, covered in Chapter 8, is limiting the day's priorities to two or three most important tasks. "Level two, to narrow it even further and really get into deep work, is to create a deadline, preferably in the form of a time block for each task." Its full statement: "Step two is to set a challenging deadline with a reason to obey it." He disclaims originality: "This is nothing novel. We all know that a deadline can make even the world's most intense procrastinators get the work done. Like when your essay is due tomorrow and you just get it done and you usually do it pretty well." And then the promise: "You can replicate this to an extent every single morning to make more progress in the next month than you have in the last year" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

The new element is the phrase **a reason to obey it**. A pseudo-deadline that one can ignore without consequence loses its effect; the 2025 version asks what makes a self-imposed deadline binding. Koe gives two mechanisms. "There are two ways that I like to ensure deadlines. The first is working in **challenging time blocks**, and these are usually 60 to 90 minutes. And then number two is having an important non-work-related task in between time blocks." The first gives each task a deadline; the second gives the deadline teeth (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

What makes a block "challenging" is that it is deliberately too short. "If I'm writing a thousand words for my newsletter, then I'll give myself something like 60 minutes to achieve that. And that usually isn't enough time. But that's for a good reason, because I don't have time to think about what I'm going to write. I don't have time to allow other distractions to get into play. My mind, when it's in that narrow state, is going to pull the necessary resources to do what it's meant to do at that time." The claim about the **narrow state** is a claim about how a constrained mind allocates resources: with no slack, it stops deliberating and executes. Koe immediately marks its limit: "If you can't write just by doing that, it's because you're missing another piece of the puzzle. It's because you don't have ideas to write about" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). Pressure can extract what is already available; it cannot create what is missing. Section 12.5 develops the consequence (deep work uses only the knowledge you already have), and Chapter 13 treats the "mental metabolism" that supplies it.

The same short block has a second effect, which Koe attributes to a psychological phenomenon. "The other thing about 60 minutes not being enough time in a time block is that a lot of the time it invokes the Zeigarnik effect, right? Which is like open loops. If you have an open loop, your brain wants to go back and complete that thing, or it can't stop thinking about that thing. So if you stop your work early, or you cut it short, your brain is going to have that much more ideas and drive to get back into the work and finish it during the next time block" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

**Complementary context:** the Zeigarnik effect is named after the psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik, whose experiments in the 1920s found that people tend to remember interrupted tasks better than completed ones. Later research has produced mixed results on the memory effect itself; Koe uses the idea in its popular form, as the mind's pull toward unfinished business.

The use is an adaptation worth noticing, because open loops appeared in Chapter 6 and in the rogue thought of section 12.3 as a *cost*: an unresolved input that occupies attention. Here the same mechanism is turned into a resource. An open loop created deliberately, around the task one wants to return to, carries momentum across the break. The difference is whose loop it is: a loop opened by a notification pulls attention away from the work; a loop opened by stopping the work early pulls attention back to it.

#### Breakfast as a deadline with teeth

The second mechanism, the non-work task between blocks, is illustrated with a personal story that also records a change in Koe's habits. "Where the power of the deadlines come into play is in between these time blocks." Between them he puts "small but extremely important tasks, like, for me, eating breakfast." He did intermittent fasting "for a long time" ("you do you"), but when he began prioritizing health and the gym more, he noticed his gym sessions suffered without eating. "I know you can perform well on a fasted stomach. That's not what I'm saying"; for him it matters to eat so that workouts are fueled with "food, carbs and the rest," and the effect compounds over days. Eating right before the gym makes him groggy compared with eating about three hours before plus a small snack right before. "Because that breakfast time, something so simple, is tied to another value in my life... then I kind of have to stop work." "So if I don't stop my work, then my health takes a hit... in a domain that's important to me" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

The mechanism is borrowed from no one; it is Koe's own discovery about how to make a self-imposed limit binding: attach it to something you already value. The deadline is no longer "I said I would stop at nine" but "if I don't stop at nine, my training suffers," and training is part of his identity. This is the principle of Chapter 8's "a responsible life has deadlines built in" turned into a deliberate design: commitments outside work are used as the deadlines that work itself cannot supply. The story also documents a reversal in practice. Elsewhere in the corpus, in late 2024, he describes a modified intermittent fast (only fruit until midday); by May 2025 he had abandoned fasting for the reason given. He presents both as personal choices ("you do you"), not as nutritional advice.

#### Public deadlines: the procrastinator's edge

The strongest self-imposed deadlines are the ones other people can see. In 2024 Koe makes this the third guideline of his routine: "The third guideline for focused work, or the 4-Hour Work day, is deadlines, which are the **procrastinator's edge**. You aren't productive because you aren't public, which is another point for building an internet business on social media. The flow state requirement of challenges and deadlines is baked in: when I set a launch date for a lead magnet or a product or something that I'm going to do, I better finish it before that date, because people are expecting it, and people probably paid for a pre-order" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18).

The phrase "you aren't productive because you aren't public" reverses the usual causality. One normally imagines that people become public once they are productive; Koe argues that publicity is a cause of productivity, because it converts private intentions into commitments with witnesses. It also gives a second, less obvious reason to build an audience (Chapter 25 gives the main ones): an audience is a permanent supply of real deadlines.

His method, described in 2023 and repeated in 2024, is the **pre-sale**. You can "put something tangible on the line when you really need to get something done"; what "just works miracles" for him is public deadlines, "another reason to build an audience." When launching a product he usually has it mapped out, puts out a promotion, and "when I get the first sale, that means, like, okay, it's [expletive] go time." If the deadline is a month out, he has a month to get everything done, and close to the date he is "spending like 12 hours a day, just unbroken focus." The compilation version ends with the instruction that generalizes it: "get creative with this... how can you add a deadline to your important work?" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The twelve-hour days near a launch are an explicit exception to the four-hour limit, stated in the same video that sets the limit. The exception does not contradict the philosophy as section 12.1 reconstructed it: the four hours are a baseline in maintenance periods, and a launch is a building period with a true deadline. It does show that the 2023 framework was never as rigid as its headline.

#### True deadlines

Koe distinguishes deadlines that merely feel urgent from deadlines that are real. "It creates a deadline, a **true deadline**, and everyone knows that deadlines are useful in productivity, but most deadlines aren't real." "When you fly across the country or invest your life savings in coaching, the deadline is as real as it gets: you only have so much time to make it work." If you fly across the world to become a digital nomad, "how much time do you have until your bank account hits zero and you become homeless in a country where you don't even know the language? You either make it work or you let it fall apart" (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02).

The passage belongs to Koe's discussion of tactical stress, which Chapter 10 treats in full: the deliberate decision to put oneself in a high-stakes situation, knowing one has the skill to meet it. Its place in this chapter is as the far end of a spectrum of deadlines that the section has climbed step by step.

| Type of deadline | Example in the corpus | What makes it binding |
|---|---|---|
| Pseudo-deadline | The four-hour limit; 90-minute blocks; 45-minute timers | Identity and the frame of a game |
| Challenging time block | 60 minutes for 1,000 words | Deliberate shortage of time; the narrow state |
| Deadline with a reason to obey it | Breakfast three hours before the gym | Attachment to another value (health) |
| Public deadline | A launch date with a pre-sale | Other people's expectations and money |
| True deadline | Savings running out abroad | Material consequences |

The table orders the deadlines by the strength of the consequence, which is also, roughly, the order of their cost and risk. Koe recommends the lower rows for daily use and the higher ones for particular projects; the highest (staking one's savings) is presented as a strategy for getting out of a rut, not as a daily routine.

#### Mindful procrastination

Koe's treatment of procrastination follows from everything above, but it is easy to misread. In 2023 he says it is human nature, and more people should accept it, that we move better under pressure: procrastination "is kind of a good thing, but **mindful procrastination**." You should not really care about your work until closer to the actual time; then you are consciously and narrowly focused on the task and knock it out "in a 10 times better fashion than if you were to spread that work out." He limits the claim to his own experience and that of people he knows. Being stressed in this mode "isn't necessarily bad, it's a mode of being" (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). "Ten times better" is a rhetorical estimate, not a measurement.

In 2024 he states it more provocatively: "Procrastination isn't bad, it's human nature. Most successful entrepreneurs are lazy; they wait until the last minute to get work done, but during that last minute they enter the most enjoyable season of their lives. Their mind becomes a magnet for ideas, their best work happens in these **periods of obsession and intensity**; they don't have a choice but to get it done, because their survival is at stake" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The gym-as-Parkinson's-law passage quoted earlier belongs here too: he usually procrastinates until he has to, and then the work gets done pretty well.

These statements seem to clash with the criticism of procrastination quoted in section 12.2, where the fourteen-hour beginner who "just procrastinate[s] the entire time" is the negative example, and with another 2023 passage in which people who say they have no results are "procrastinating for three hours." Koe uses the same word in opposite senses, and the distinction he draws is the adjective "mindful." Read in the light of this section, the two senses can be separated precisely.

| | Mindful procrastination | Unconscious procrastination |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Present and chosen | Absent or vague |
| What fills the waiting time | Rest, other life areas, idea accumulation | Low-value activity that feels like work |
| What ends the waiting | A real deadline | Nothing in particular |
| Mode of the work itself | Narrow, conscious focus (flow side of the Focus Matrix) | Scattered attention spread across the day |
| Koe's verdict | "A mode of being"; the source of best work | Fake progress |

Mindful procrastination is the deadline principle operating on a longer time scale. The waiting period is the open phase of the lion's cycle ("sit, let vision accumulate, wait until you see the prey," as Chapter 6 quoted it); the deadline closes it into a sprint. What it is not is a license for avoidance. Its condition is that the work is clearly defined and the deadline real; without those, the same behavior is just the procrastination everyone means by the word. The idea also connects to the seasons model treated in Chapter 39: the "periods of obsession and intensity" are short seasons of twelve-hour days that the four-hour baseline makes room for.

### 12.5 The Morning, Energy and Ordering the Day by Entropy

Sections 12.3 and 12.4 described what goes into the day and how attention is held inside each block. This section concerns *when*: why Koe puts the most important work at the start of the day, how he matches tasks to energy, how guests he interviewed audit energy across a week, and how his scheme for ordering the day evolved from the priority ladder into an explicit vocabulary of entropy. Its anchor is a single sentence from 2025, which summarizes the whole chapter's daily architecture.

#### "Do your work when entropy is low"

"We're here to learn about my deep work routine. So in a nutshell, what it is, is to do your work when entropy is low" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). The rest of that video explains what entropy means (Chapter 5's measure of disorder, applied to the mind as dispersed attention) and how to schedule a day from low to high entropy: the quiet morning first, deep tasks in it, shallow work afterward. **Do your work when entropy is low** is the most compressed formulation Koe gives of the morning-first principle, and it explains the principle rather than just asserting it. Early in the day, before messages, conversations and demands arrive, the environment is quiet and attention has not yet been split; later, every input adds disorder. Deep work goes where disorder is lowest because deep work is the activity most damaged by disorder, which is the same reasoning as the priority ladder's distraction potential, now stated in terms of the environment rather than the task.

Koe introduces the routine with an apology that is also a statement of method. "This is nothing revolutionary": the routine is simple, "and it's what more people need, because I've tried all of the productivity hacks. I've tried the Pomodoro timers. I've even tried putting on a hat... to simulate a narrow focus, and it kind of works." But "like your favorite Notion productivity template, all systems fall apart or they evolve to fit your new needs": the template you downloaded, or the Cortex workspace you built, "isn't going to last forever." So he focuses on "the **core principles** that you can do all the time so that you can accomplish 8 hours of work in four" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

This is a change of emphasis, and it should be stated as one. In the 2024 compilation of his earlier material, Koe had endorsed the hat trick ("which actually does help") and recommended building one's first productivity system in Notion and refining it. In 2025 hacks and templates are demoted: they work for a while and then break or must be rebuilt, whereas principles (narrow the focus, add a deadline, protect the low-entropy hours) survive every change of tool. The two positions apply to different moments, as the corpus itself suggests: a first system helps a beginner start; principles are what last. The same video makes the four-hour promise in a new form, "8 hours of work in four," which restates the argument of section 12.1 (four focused hours outperform eight distracted ones) as the goal of the routine.

#### In deep work you are not thinking

The same 2025 video adds a condition that limits what any routine can do. "Usually in your deep work session, you're not thinking. You're not thinking of the strategy. You're just doing. You're in the flow state. You're at one with the task. The knowledge you have available is what you're using. So if you don't have the ideas to write about already, or the strategy to test with marketing, then your deep work session isn't going to be that deep" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

The claim explains the limit of the challenging time block in section 12.4 (if you cannot write under pressure, you lack ideas). Deep work is execution, and execution draws on a stock of ideas, knowledge and strategy built elsewhere. That is why, in the same routine, leisure and what Koe calls "mental metabolism" (the intake and digestion of ideas during the rest of the day) are part of the deep work routine rather than separate from it. It also explains the "lie" in the four hours from section 12.1: the reading, walking and napping that he did not count as work are where the material for the four hours is produced. Chapter 13 develops this half of the polarity.

#### Wake up before distractions

The eighth commandment, "wake up before distractions," was Koe's favorite in 2023. He acknowledges two camps, those in favor of waking at four or five in the morning and those who say "I get up whenever I want," and allows the choice, but "unless you try waking up early, I wouldn't talk smack about it." His reason is the quality of the hours: "The hours of, like, 5 to 7 in the morning are sacred; there is nothing that can distract you, it's very peaceful." No one calls or texts, and if they do it is so early that you do not feel obliged to respond; the day has not started, so there are no lunch invitations and no pressure to stop and eat ("I haven't eaten breakfast yet"). He adds a provision for those who are not morning people: even if you are a night owl and your sleepy brain is "in more of that default mode network, open focus state," you can assign priority tasks related to creativity rather than to hyper-productivity in those hours (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; reissued in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The **sacred hours** are defined by the absence of social demand, not by the clock. The provision for night owls uses a neuroscientific distinction from Chapter 6 (the default mode network, active in rest and associative thought, versus the task-positive network of directed effort) to suggest that sleepiness is not a reason to avoid early work but a reason to choose the right kind of early work.

In October 2024 he restated the idea with a new image and a looser rule. Waking at 4 a.m. gives "3 to 4 hours of experience that others just don't get to have": a completely different, quiet reality, in which people are not awake and "**distraction lack[s] gravity**." He is not saying you must wake at four: "create a glitch in the Matrix, do something different," perhaps stay up two hours later at night, when you may be more creative. Whether the explanation is spiritual, esoteric or scientific ("I'm sure there is" science behind it, he says, without citing any), in the early morning and late at night the world is quiet, and your mind can have creative thoughts to channel into work. He personally prefers waking earlier, because of "obvious health deficits to going to bed very late" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13).

"Distraction lacks gravity" reuses a word Koe gives a technical sense elsewhere: **gravity** is the pull a goal exerts once energy has been invested in it (section 12.6 returns to it). At 4 a.m., the competing pulls (messages, people, obligations) are absent, so the goal's pull dominates. "Create a glitch in the Matrix" links the practice to Part I: doing something at a time when nobody else is awake is a small departure from the default schedule, and its value lies precisely in the departure. The shift between the two versions (from sacred hours at five to seven, to four in the morning or two hours later at night) shows that the principle is the quiet, not the hour.

#### Night owls: an unresolved contradiction

On whether night owls exist, the corpus contains opposed statements that Koe does not reconcile. In March 2025 he says that execution should happen in the morning "because I personally don't think that anyone is a night owl; everyone is bound to their circadian rhythm, and by staying up extremely late, that disrupts your health more than you could ever know." If you woke up and went for a walk in the sun every day and got more sunlight, "you would be a morning person." He invites viewers to "go down the circadian and light health rabbit hole" (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). He flags the statement himself as personal opinion ("I personally don't think").

**Complementary context:** research on chronotypes generally finds that people differ, partly for genetic reasons, in their natural sleep timing, and that morning light exposure can shift that timing earlier. Koe's claim that "nobody" is a night owl is stronger than that research supports; his advice about morning light is consistent with it.

The opposed statements are on record. The 2023 commandment explicitly accommodates night owls; the 2024 version allows staying up two hours later as an alternative; and in May 2025, two months after the "nobody is a night owl" claim, he says that some creatives worked late at night while others preferred mornings, and that one should work according to one's energy cycles. He gives no reason for the changes. The practical core that survives all versions is narrower than either extreme: protect a block of quiet, low-entropy hours for the most important work; the morning is his recommendation and his preference, the night a tolerated alternative.

#### Frontload the morning

Koe's fullest description of his morning comes from 2023, in a video about developing body, mind and business together. He says he outpaces "95 percent of people" by **front-loading** his mornings, between 5 and 11 a.m., with needle-moving tasks: run three or four days a week, and walk on the other days to keep the habit of going outside; write the newsletter and content for one hour, which "sustains 95% of my business"; build a new project (at the time, his book launch and software company). Also: the gym six or seven days a week; walks between activities; nutrient-dense meals that take ten to fifteen minutes to prepare; eating out for dinner quite often; about 4,500 calories a day (he shows a physique photo as proof). Not everyone can do this, because of time constraints, but you can change some part now in order to do what you want later. As you get results you become efficient and time frees up, and freeing time "should be a sub goal for almost every goal you set." "When in doubt, waking up an hour earlier unlocks the distraction-free time that will solve most of your problems" (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30).

Two claims in this passage are general and deserve to be pulled out. First, **freeing time should be a sub-goal of almost every goal**: this generalizes the 4-Hour Workday from a business norm to a design principle for any project; whatever one pursues, part of the pursuit should be making it take less time. Second, the earlier hour as the default fix: a restatement of section 12.2's one-hour start as a universal remedy. The "95%" figures (outpacing 95% of people; one hour sustaining 95% of the business) are rhetorical estimates, not measurements.

The point of front-loading appears more clearly in a 2024 video, where Koe explains what it buys. Because his maintenance intake is around 4,000 calories, "I'm allowed to have nice dinners with my friends and family and loved ones, because I live this lifestyle early in the morning": writing and work done, multiple walks, the gym, micronutrient-dense, high-protein food in the morning, "so that later in the day I can live a normal lifestyle with the people that live that lifestyle, and it doesn't take anything away; in fact it actually benefits my life, because I'm not so stressed and neurotic about going to the cold plunge, going to the sauna, eating extremely strict." "I'm solely focused on doing what I need to do in the morning and then doing what I want to do in the afternoon" (**Source:** `A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md`, 2024-04-02).

**Frontload**, in Koe's usage, is a common word with a specific sense: do what you *need* in the morning so that you can do what you *want* in the afternoon and live a normal social life. The rationale is not only productivity but freedom from neurosis. A person who completes their essentials early has nothing left to protect in the evening, and can therefore join others without anxiety; a person whose essentials are still pending must either refuse or feel guilty. The critique of health neurosis (cold plunges, saunas, rigid diets) is developed in Chapter 40.

#### Match tasks to energy

A second principle runs alongside the quiet: match the hardest work to the time of highest energy. In 2023 Koe instructs viewers to schedule lever-moving tasks in a **high energy time block**. Midday or afternoon is fine if you want, but for most people the right block is the first hour of the morning, when they have the most time and energy. Then he adds a twist: even night owls operate from the default mode network when sleepy, and "that's when you're the most creative anyways"; "go to work tired, you'll do better work." Energy may not peak in that hour, he concedes, but you have a limited amount of energy each day, and working after draining it all yields low-quality work. It is difficult: wake up early for a week until it feels somewhat normal, for a month until you enjoy it (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11).

The passage contains an internal tension that Koe himself half-acknowledges: the first hour is called "high energy," and in the same breath one is told to "go to work tired." The resolution lies in the reasoning he gives. The relevant quantity is not momentary alertness but the **daily energy budget**: whatever one's alertness at 6 a.m., the budget is full then and depleted by evening, and every demand of the day draws on it. Early work, even sleepy, is done before the budget is spent. The sleepiness itself is then reframed as an asset for creative tasks, via the default mode network. The reframing is Koe's interpretation of the neuroscience, not a finding he cites.

A guest gave the most precise version of energy matching, and Koe adopted its spirit. In a 2021 interview, Justin Welsh admitted that he had chosen his schedule "just based on... what I've done historically... old habits are hard to break." Talking with his wife, he noticed that while drinking coffee in the morning he is "at my highest point... I can think most clearly," and "towards 2:00 p.m., I'm almost worthless." His planned experiment: flip the order, coffee then writing rather than going straight to the gym, and move the gym to 2 or 3 p.m., "when I'm a little down on energy and kind of getting ramped back up through... some workouts." He would measure "the quality of my day... the quality of my content, what I write, how my business reacts" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20). The method has three parts: locate the peak of clarity and put the most demanding cognitive work there; locate the trough and use physical activity to recover energy in it; treat the change as a test with an outcome measure. It is Justin Welsh's method, offered as his own plan.

Koe's response in the same conversation is a confession. He has been "the type of guy where it's like, 'I just have to go in the morning.'" He gets up at 5:36 and feels "we need to get the day started right now," but "my mind isn't primed for that at all." The result: "I'll sit there for an hour doing practically nothing, just scrolling social media on my computer," and only then write or post. That hour, he concludes, "could probably be better used just relaxing, prepping my mind for the day, and, like, warming up in a sense," inspired by Justin's slow coffee hour. He then describes the whole question as an open experiment: in close to three years of self-employment, "the entire time it's just constant testing of, like, 'Okay, what's the perfect schedule for optimizing my energy throughout the day?'" "That's just a perk of self-employment and being able to **create your own structure**" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20).

The confession is valuable because it predates every rule in this section. Before the sacred hours, before the commandments, Koe was waking early and wasting the first hour, and the solution he reached for was not more discipline but a warm-up. The episode also shows that "wake up early" and "work first thing" are separable: an early start without a primed mind produced exactly the scattered, high-entropy hour the later rules were designed to prevent. And it frames schedule design as an ongoing experiment, which is the stance the self-made lifestyle of section 12.3 asked for.

#### Energy creators and energy drains

The most systematic method for auditing energy in the corpus comes from Sahil Bloom, in the 2025 conversation, and Koe engages with it closely. Its first step is the **energy calendar**. Take a day you have just lived (say, Monday) and color-code every activity: **green** for energy creating ("it lifted you up"), **yellow** for energy neutral, **red** for energy draining (it "actually made you feel physically drained"). Do it for the coming week. At the end of the week, "zoom out and look at this. You will have a very clear visual picture of the types of activities that are creating energy in your world versus draining energy from it" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30).

The principle behind it is Sahil's claim that outcomes follow energy. "Your goal as a human being is to spend more time on energy creators and [less on] energy drains, because your outcomes will follow your ability to do that." (The transcript literally reads "spend more time on energy creators and energy drains"; the context makes clear that less time on drains is meant.) "When you are leaning into things that are truly creating energy in your life... you're feeling a pull towards [them], your outcomes go ten, 100x. When you're trying to spend time on things that are draining your energy, your outcomes are always going to be one x": "1 to 1 trades that are going to keep you on that **life treadmill** that everyone wants you to stay on" (**Source:** same; "trades" is a probable reading of a garbled transcript word). The multipliers (10x, 100x, 1x) are Sahil's illustrative figures, not measurements; the structural claim is that energy-creating work compounds and energy-draining work does not.

Since some drains are unavoidable, Sahil adds two layers for dealing with them. The first is to question the necessity and the format. "Question the idea that you have to do anything. Just ask the question. I'm not saying it's going to change the answer," but many defaults can be "slightly change[d]." In his eighty-to-hundred-hour-a-week finance job, his first energy calendar showed that "phone calls and Zoom meetings were the most energy-draining thing in my life," and his calendar was full of back-to-back thirty-minute calls. He asked: "do I really need to do these things in the current format that they exist in?" For some, no: he did them on walks. "That slight adjustment... made them energy creating, because I love being outside; I'm much more creative when I walk." The calls themselves improved: he was "significantly more attentive... more present... more thoughtful." The general rule: ask whether a task "truly need[s] to exist in the current format, where it is energy draining," because a slight adjustment can "change its **energy profile** in your life." Koe names this move **alchemizing** a draining task (**Source:** same).

The second layer is batching. "You are never going to remove energy-draining tasks from your life. What you can do is batch them more effectively in your calendar." The reason is that drains "have a tendency to **bleed into** the energy creators": if a fun podcast conversation sits between four energy drains, "you're going to experience the good thing as a lot more negative than it actually is." So drains go into windows: "My Thursday afternoon is going to be my call day... I'm going to batch all my calls into a Thursday afternoon, or I'm going to batch my email processing from 4 to 5 p.m. every single day" (**Source:** same). This is the place where Sahil's use of Parkinson's law, quoted in section 12.4, belongs: the batch window should be shorter than feels comfortable, and the work will shrink to fit.

| Step | What to do | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Energy calendar | Color activities green, yellow, red for a week; zoom out | Makes the energy profile of a life visible |
| 2. Question and change format | Ask whether the task must exist in its draining form; alter it (calls on walks) | A small change can turn a drain into a creator ("alchemizing") |
| 3. Batch what remains | Group drains into short windows | Drains bleed into neighboring creators; Parkinson's law shrinks them |

The framework is Sahil's, with Koe's name for step 2. It complements Koe's ordering principles in a useful way. The priority ladder and the entropy schemes ask *in what order* tasks should come; the energy calendar asks *which* tasks deserve to be there, judged by their effect on the person rather than their place in a hierarchy of goals.

Koe had expressed the same audit years earlier, in his conversation with Justin Welsh, whose Monday and Friday "systems" days were dedicated to it. Justin's summary of the purpose: "Trying to understand what's taking a lot of my time that I don't like doing and that doesn't give me energy. Trying to do more things that do give me energy." His tools were three: eliminate, automate, delegate. Koe singled it out as "a big thing of mine, too: doing things that give you more energy and taking away the things that don't" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20). The triad returns in section 12.6, where Koe's own version (systemize, eliminate, delegate) is the means of keeping work inside four hours.

In 2025 Koe gave his own form of the audit, with a different target: enthusiasm. "**Reverse engineer** where **enthusiasm** comes from in your life, and then eliminate, outsource, or accept all of the things that don't align." Notice which activities make you feel energized, which distort your sense of time, which make focus effortless; what makes you excited, curious and open-minded; "especially notice when you can't sleep because you are having too many ideas." Fill your day with more of these and remove what prevents it. "Literally block off one to two hours a day to pursue your enthusiasm" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The signals he lists (time distortion, effortless focus, ideas at night) are the markers of flow and obsession that Chapter 10 describes, used here as diagnostic instruments. The triad changes too: "accept" replaces "automate," acknowledging that some misaligned tasks can be neither removed nor handed off and must simply be borne. And the closing instruction returns to the one-to-two-hour block of section 12.2, now defined by its content (enthusiasm) rather than by its position in the day.

#### Ordering the day: from the priority ladder to entropy

The priority ladder of 2023 was Koe's first scheme for ordering a day. In later years he redescribed the same ordering several times, and following the redescriptions shows what remained constant.

In April 2024, describing his routine as a writer and founder, he called it "option B" and divided the day into three blocks. The **long-term block** comes first, usually for someone working full-time on their own business: for him, writing the book and building Cortex, "very long-term projects... day after day for months and months, until finally it launches"; then the project moves to the short-term or immediate block, and something new fills the long-term slot, "because if you're not building something new always, I've found that you get into this repetitive routine and you kind of put yourself in a **second 9 to 5**." The **short-term block**, after a walk, holds "things that don't pay off immediately but pay off in the near future," such as his newsletter, which turns into a YouTube video; it was his second priority after the book. The **immediate block** is like maintenance: responding to emails and DMs, "all of the little things that... check the to-dos off of your day" (**Source:** `A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md`, 2024-04-02). The structure maps onto the ladder directly (long-term = Priority 1, short-term = Priorities 2–3, immediate = Priority 4), and so does the rule of promotion: a project moves down the time horizon as it matures, and the first slot is refilled with something new. The new element is the warning about the **second 9-to-5**, Koe's name for a self-built job that has become a repetitive routine; Chapter 30 treats its business form (freelancing that recreates employment). Always having something new in the long-term block is the daily-scale defense against it.

In August 2024 he named the criterion. If you have more than one hour, order the rest of your work "from highest to lowest leverage, or lowest to highest entropy." His day: (1) writing first thing, because it is the entire fuel of his business: content, newsletters, landing pages, promotions; "the business model is typing at a computer"; (2) a walk, trying to learn something new for the day (filling the mind); (3) administrative work for Cortex, opening up to conversations and responding to people. A new project may demand more time until it is systemized back to about four hours. The last tasks, when the day is ordered by priority, are probably optional or easily automated or outsourced. He gives this a name, **nature's filter**: you naturally gravitate to the highest-leverage tasks in the morning, and what you do later, with little energy, just because you have to, is what can be automated or outsourced (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). Nature's filter turns the ordering into a diagnostic. The tasks that drift to the end of the day are, by that very drift, candidates for elimination or delegation, which is the bridge to section 12.6.

The same video gives the macro-level rationale. "This style of deep work on a macro level gains its impact from your ability to **release and constrain entropy**." The first one or two hours must be absolutely distraction-free: no calls, no phone, no conversations, "just you and the task." Once the tasks that make progress are done, you can allow more chaos, because the following tasks maintain what you have built and require less focus. He adds a condition: without a successful business, there is no maintenance work, and you only need one hour. If you write content and get it shared in one hour, you build an audience; with a built product, you make income, scalable "to infinity" as the audience keeps growing (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). The idea of releasing and constraining entropy is Chapter 5's entropy management at the scale of a day: order is imposed where it is most needed and disorder is permitted where it is harmless.

In December 2024 the scheme reached its most explicitly entropic form. "I like to slice my days into three parts": "**low entropy**, so little room for uncertainty or chaos: clear mind, deep focus, high intention"; "**mid entropy**, so allowing myself to interact with people and my phone"; "**high entropy**, so controlled chaos that results in a plethora of ideas I can utilize the next morning" (**Source:** `A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md`, 2024-12-29). The third part changes the meaning of the afternoon. In the priority ladder, the end of the day was maintenance, the place where distraction was tolerated because nothing important remained. Here the end of the day has a function of its own: **controlled chaos**, open exposure to people, conversations, reading and stimuli, which generates ideas for the next morning's low-entropy work. The day is now a cycle: chaos in the evening produces material, order in the morning converts it.

The May 2025 routine gives the final form, as a summary of the steps covered so far in that video. "You structure time blocks from low to high entropy. The world is quiet first thing in the morning. No emails, no communication with others, no phone, nothing that can send your mind spiraling and silently impact your work. Then, there's two to three most important tasks over two to three time blocks. It doesn't matter if you finish one task per time block. As long as you feel the pressure from the time clock, that narrows focus. And then last, shallow work only once deep work is complete. Do not, and I repeat, do not check emails, social media, or the rest until deep work is done." He allows one exception: "You can go on social media to post if that's what you do, but only if you are posting and leaving to get back to work" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18).

| Version | Date | Ordering criterion | First block | Last block |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Priority ladder | 2023-03 | Decreasing challenge, increasing distraction potential | Priority 1: new project | Priority 4: maintenance |
| Long-term / short-term / immediate | 2024-04 | Time horizon of payoff | Long-term project | Immediate to-dos |
| Leverage / entropy | 2024-08 | Highest to lowest leverage, lowest to highest entropy | Writing | Admin and conversations (nature's filter) |
| Three slices | 2024-12 | Entropy level | Low entropy: deep focus | High entropy: controlled chaos for ideas |
| Deep work routine | 2025-05 | Low to high entropy | 2–3 MITs in 2–3 blocks under the clock | Shallow work after deep work |

Across five versions, the criterion is renamed (priority, horizon, leverage, entropy) but the shape of the day stays the same: the newest, most demanding, most leveraged work first, in a protected low-disorder window; progressively more open, social and maintenance-oriented work later. The one substantive development is the reinterpretation of the end of the day, from a place where distraction is harmless to a place where controlled chaos is productive. Koe does not explain the renaming; the reasonable reading is that the entropy vocabulary, which he developed in other contexts (Chapter 5), proved a better explanation of a practice he already had.
