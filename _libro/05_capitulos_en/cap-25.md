## Chapter 25 — Why an Audience: Attention, Distribution and Leverage

Part VIII taught a skill: writing as the base of all media, the choice of what to write, the structures that capture and hold attention, and the weekly process and ecosystem that turn one idea into many pieces. Part IX asks what that skill is for once it leaves the notebook. This chapter gives Koe's answer at the level of principle, before Chapter 26 turns to the mechanics of growth and Chapter 27 to the personal brand. The answer can be compressed into a chain he repeats in many forms between 2022 and 2026: attention is the scarce resource on which every form of success depends; whoever can capture it and deliver value on it has distribution; distribution is what turns the ability to make something into the ability to live from it; and an audience is the form of distribution that an individual can build alone, without capital and without anyone's permission, and that accumulates as trust.

The chapter proceeds in six steps. Section 25.1 presents attention as power: what people at the base of social hierarchies actually invest, why Koe calls attention "the root of existence," why he refuses to treat the capture of attention as unethical, and why, in an age of artificial intelligence, he regards attention as "one of the last moats." Section 25.2 explains the formula "distribution equals freedom," adapted from Jocko Willink, and its companion from Jack Butcher, "build distribution, then build whatever you want," with the cases (Devon Eriksen, the founders of Stan, Koe's own software plans) that show what happens to builders who have no distribution. Section 25.3 distinguishes the forms of leverage: the built, borrowed and bought distribution of Koe's own taxonomy, the forms of "digital leverage" an audience unlocks, and the line that runs from Naval Ravikant's leverage of capital, labor and products with no marginal cost of replication to what Koe calls "permissionless leverage" and "new leverage." Section 25.4 treats the audience as an asset of trust: why it outperforms cold outreach, what "1,000 true fans" means in his adaptation, why "people follow people" and not glorified search engines, and the story of how Koe himself moved from cold outreach to inbound. Section 25.5 places all of this in Koe's larger picture of social media as a new society, a new town square and a new medium in which "you are the media," together with his changing judgments about whether that society is a socialization chamber or a vessel. Section 25.6 closes with the practical consequence: choose one platform, concentrate force there, and branch out only with validated ideas.

The chapter depends on Part VII, because the reason anyone would follow a person rather than a topic is the thesis that "you are the niche," and on Part VIII, because the instrument that captures attention is writing. It also draws on Part III, where attention was introduced as the root control of the mind; here the same concept reappears turned outward, as the resource a creator competes for in other minds.

### 25.1 Attention as Power

#### What the base of the pyramid actually invests

Koe's argument about attention begins not in marketing but in his critique of society as a set of "pyramid schemes," developed in Part I. In a 2023 video on that theme, and again in a 2024 compilation, he makes a claim that reframes what those at the bottom of social hierarchies give up. "Money isn't the biggest problem," he says: "the people at the bottom of these societal pyramid schemes are not investing money... they're investing attention."

The claim rests on a chain of steps, and the chain matters more than the slogan.

1. Humans survive on a conceptual level, not only a physical one. This is the idea of **conceptual survival** developed in Part II: a person defends their identity, their beliefs and their categories with the same machinery they use to defend their body.
2. If what you know and believe about yourself is, for example, your job title, then "you're going to work to survive that identity with your attention."
3. Things "don't exist unless there is enough attention to give it life." Koe's examples are a religious ideology and a business model: there could be a business model out there that nobody knows about, because no one has done it or spread the word, and since no attention has made it tangible, it effectively does not exist.
4. Identities such as a job title or a religion are "upheld through attention." If the attention that sustains them is threatened, their existence is threatened, and "you are going to feel threatened." His example is deliberately trivial: someone says "I don't like your sports team," and you have "a physical survival response." You defend the belief, and every defense hardwires it further.
5. Zoom out to the larger picture, and "those problems mean absolutely nothing."

From this chain he draws the conclusion: "it's safe to say that power in our modern world comes from attention." He specifies that the power is double. There is external power, which comes from capturing other people's attention, and internal power, which consists in "being able to control your own attention."

**Source:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md

This distinction is the hinge between the earlier parts of the book and this one. Internal power is the subject of Part III (attention as the only root control of consciousness) and Part V (dopamine, focused work). External power is the subject of Part IX. Koe treats them as two faces of the same resource: the person who cannot direct their own attention is the person whose attention is harvested by others, and the person who can capture others' attention responsibly is, in his view, usually someone who first learned to govern their own. The chain should be read as Koe's interpretive framework rather than as an empirical claim: the statement that things "don't exist" without attention is a claim about social and psychological existence (what is real to people and in culture), not about physical existence, and he offers it as an argument, not as a finding.

#### Consumers and creators

In a 2024 video on internet business models, Koe restates the same idea as a choice between two roles. "Are you a consumer soaking in value or memes while giving nothing in return, or a creator contributing to humanity with the value you produce?" He insists that the distinction is not an internet phenomenon. The masses, in his description, are consumers: they "leech off" the government, their parents, the school system, and "blow around like a bird in heavy winds." Life happens to them and is assigned to them, and they do not realize that they are "fueling society with attention." A select few are creators: they contribute more than they take, "they control their destiny by controlling their attention," and they encourage the destiny of others "by capturing their attention and expanding their consciousness with valuable content."

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

The image of the bird in heavy winds connects this passage with the default path of Part I: the consumer is the person living by assigned goals. The definition of the creator, in turn, contains both powers at once. Controlling one's own attention is internal power; capturing others' attention in order to expand their consciousness is external power put to a specific use. The qualification is important for everything that follows. Koe does not define the creator as someone who captures attention, full stop, but as someone who captures it in order to deliver value on it. The ethics of the whole chapter are contained in that clause.

#### "Attention is the root of existence"

Immediately after the consumer/creator distinction, Koe poses a question he asks the viewer to "mull over": "how are you going to expand someone's consciousness if you don't first capture their attention?" Attention, he says, is the first step and "the root of existence"; it must be captured for any form of success.

He then addresses the objection he expects: "I don't want to capture attention, it seems unethical or salesy." His reply has two parts. The first is a diagnosis of the objection itself: it is a word "put into your mouth" that people regurgitate without questioning, an example of the unexamined programming discussed in Part I. The second is a series of counter-questions. Are you angry that you are using your attention to watch this video? Is it unethical that your attention goes to important things, or to your phone when a notification arrives? Do you want nobody to capture your attention, to sit and avoid life, with no meaningful experiences, no highs, no lows and no story? "That's what capturing, holding and delivering value on attention is."

**Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md

The argument works by generalization. If every meaningful experience involves something capturing attention, then capturing attention cannot be bad in itself; what can be good or bad is what is delivered once attention is held. The triad Koe uses, "capturing, holding and delivering value," maps onto the structure of a piece of writing described in Chapter 23: the hook captures, the structure holds, the substance delivers.

#### Knowledge is king

A year earlier, in 2023, Koe had given the same idea an epistemic version. In the age of information and of the attention economy, he argued, knowledge is how you catch attention: "knowledge is how you communicate information. Information is how you capture attention." In "this modern business landscape," therefore, knowledge is king. He immediately mocks his own formulation: "I sound like fucking Tai Lopez," a reference to an internet marketer known for that kind of slogan.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

The point is less trivial than the joke suggests. It explains why the one-person business described in Part X is built on education rather than on, for example, entertainment: knowledge is the raw material that can be turned into information, and information is what captures attention in a market where people are looking for answers to their problems. Section 25.4 will show how Koe later qualifies this: information alone is now cheap, and what people follow is perspective on information.

#### Playing the engagement game

The most direct statement of Koe's position on the ethics of attention comes from a 2022 video on cheap dopamine. There he stresses that "this isn't a bad thing": you "have to play the game if you are going to thrive in this attention economy." Everywhere is a place where attention can be caught, so "you have to make yourself look good." His reasoning is practical: if you cannot catch attention but have a well-developed personality, "how in the world are people going to see that personality?" He observes that some of the best people he knows are "talked bad about because they play the engagement game online," and that they see "10 times more success than everyone who thinks that that isn't a game that they should play." Later in the same video he adds the formula that summarizes the stance: "you're not going to be able to change the game but you can change the player." Changing the player, in that context, means being a critical thinker who does not outsource their thinking.

**Source:** Why Cheap Dopamine Prevents You From Becoming High Value.md

In Koe's usage, the **attention economy** and the **engagement game** are common expressions used with a specific sense: the game in which every surface is a place to capture attention, and which one must play in order for others to see one's depth. The "10 times more success" figure is his impression of people he knows, not a measured result.

A 2022 method video makes the same point operational. Capturing attention is "the name of the game" in an "information and idea age" in which everyone competes for it. If you cannot stop someone long enough to read your post, they will not like it, retweet it, comment, or click your profile. And the task does not end there: you then have to catch their attention on your profile, lead it to your lead magnet, then to your emails, then to your product or service. You must "guide their attention throughout all of this." He adds that "you don't have to become a sleazy used car salesman to catch attention"; the alternative is to study psychology.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

This turns attention from a single event into a path. The post, the profile, the lead magnet, the email and the offer are successive points at which attention must be recaptured, and the funnel structure described in Chapter 24 ("short form attracts, long form converts") is a design for that path.

#### The only way to redirect attention

A 2023 video on why "people dumber than you are making millions" adds the principle that completes Koe's defense of the attention game. Following his argument that unethical businesses win because nobody competes with them, he states: "the only way you can stop people from paying attention to one thing is to give them something more persuasive to pay attention to." Criticizing a harmful product does not redirect attention; out-persuading it does. "The issue is not as simple as just starting a business to make money; everything is connected."

**Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md

The principle explains why Koe regards refusal to play the attention game as a moral failure rather than a moral achievement. If attention is finite and someone will capture it, abstaining does not protect anyone; it simply leaves the field to whoever is willing to play. The same structure appears in Part V, where the only reliable way to leave a cheap dopamine loop is to have a more compelling goal: attention moves toward what is more persuasive, inside one mind and across a market.

#### Attention as the ultimate leverage

In a 2026 video on the future of work, Koe combines scarcity and leverage into a single principle. Attention is scarce: there are limited people and limited attention to go around. Whoever learns the mechanics of capturing and delivering value on attention "can demand a premium." The flood of mediocre content produced with AI does not change this; on the contrary, there is not enough attention for all of it, mediocre content is not wanted, and people shift their attention to what matters. Hence his formula: "It's not only production but it's distribution. It's not only creation because anyone can create anything. It's curation."

"Attention is the ultimate leverage," he continues. Elon Musk attracts capital, talent and resources through his followers; his tweets move markets. MrBeast reinvests the proceeds of attention into production and is becoming very powerful. Koe presents both as extremes and states that he does not want to be either: the path is risky and stressful, and he does not even watch MrBeast.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md

The two examples should be read as illustrations of the mechanism, not as models. What they show is that attention converts into other resources (capital, talent, influence) and therefore functions as leverage in the sense Section 25.3 will develop: a multiplier of the effort of one person. Koe's personal distance from both figures is consistent with the "enough" position discussed in Part XII: he wants the leverage without the scale and stress that those cases imply.

#### When AI floods content: attention as the only differentiator

From 2025 onward, Koe places the attention argument inside a new context: artificial intelligence filling the internet with content. In a May 2025 video he states: "As the world is filled with more AI, trust, attention, and signal become more scarce." The "dead internet" is growing; anyone can ask an AI for a tweet or produce a YouTube video with an AI voiceover and B-roll. But that, he argues, does not mean much: such people will not get good results, nor will they be able to pivot and iterate to compete. What it does mean is that "the level of market sophistication will continue to increase rapidly." People get bored of the standard formula, "the cookie cutter will change every month," people lose trust in most content, and one must still know what one is doing to make AI produce something unique and compelling. The phrase **attention is the only differentiator** is his name for this situation.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md

**Complementary context:** "market sophistication" is a term from Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*, where it describes how many similar claims a market has already heard, and therefore how much more specific or novel a new claim must be to work. Koe uses it here in a loose sense: the speed at which audiences tire of a format and demand something new.

In a February 2025 video, he addresses the same phenomenon through a third-party idea. Citing Wikipedia, he describes the **Dead Internet Theory** as a conspiracy theory according to which the internet "died" around 2016–2017 and is now largely dominated by bots and AI-generated content rather than people. He doubts it should still be labeled a conspiracy theory, because "most of the internet is made up of bots and AI content and it will only get worse." But he does not see this as a problem: it "could present one of the greatest opportunities for creatives and entrepreneurs and founders and everyone else to stand out."

**Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md

The reframing is characteristic. The more of the internet is filled with content no one is behind, the more valuable a recognizable human perspective becomes, because it is what the flood cannot supply.

#### Engagement versus transformation

Koe's most critical treatment of the attention economy comes in April 2026, and it introduces a tension with his earlier defense of the engagement game. Adapting the framework of Daniel Schmachtenberger on "rivalrous dynamics," he argues that content creators compete for attention and therefore optimize for engagement rather than transformation, because "the algorithm doesn't measure transformation"; it measures views, likes and shares. Creators are therefore more likely to abandon truth or transformation in favor of whatever makes people react. Content optimized for engagement requires no thinking or understanding from the viewer, so the viewer's "thinking muscle atrophies." AI accelerates content production, but it also accelerates imitation, and when the content available to imitate is destructive, "you can see where that goes." AI itself is not the issue: the problem is that "it can mimic what looks like real thinking without requiring any cognitive effort from either the creator or the consumer." The default outcome is that the **epistemic commons** (the shared pool of knowledge and reasoning a society draws on) is poisoned quickly, because it is optimized for content that "looks like it should shift your thinking, but structurally it cannot."

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md

**Complementary context:** Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher associated with the analysis of the "metacrisis," the idea that many global risks share common generator functions such as rivalrous (win-lose) dynamics under exponential technology. "Epistemic commons" is a term used in that circle for a society's shared capacity to make sense of the world. Koe adopts the framework and applies it to the creator internet and AI; the application is his.

How does this square with the 2022 claim that the engagement game "isn't a bad thing"? Koe does not retract the earlier defense; he never says that capturing attention is wrong. The change is one of emphasis. In 2022–2024, attention was primarily something to be captured so that depth could be seen; in 2025–2026, his judgment of content optimized only for engagement becomes much harsher, and he contrasts it with slow content such as essays. The two positions can be reconciled if the hook and the engagement mechanics are understood as the door to a piece that actually transforms, rather than as the whole piece. A piece that only looks like it should change your thinking is the case he now condemns; a piece that uses engagement to bring someone to real thinking is the case he defended. He does not state the reason for the shift explicitly.

#### People follow lore and perspective

In a December 2024 video on daily routines, Koe gives the positive counterpart of the AI argument. What most people are missing, he says, is "a forward moving lifestyle designed to achieve your vision from which you can extract and connect ideas to create value. You're missing the context. People don't follow you because you have information, they follow you because you have lore, because you have perspective. When AGI has all perspectives it effectively has no perspective unless it's the one you give it or assign it."

**Lore**, in this sense, is the accumulated story of a person: the context of a life in motion from which ideas are extracted. The term is borrowed from fiction and gaming, where it means the backstory of a world; Koe uses it for the backstory that makes a person's ideas recognizable as theirs. The claim connects directly to Chapter 19: if you are the niche, then what an audience follows is precisely what cannot be copied, namely your situation, your goals and your way of connecting ideas.

He adds a speculative note: even granting artificial general intelligence, he sees no difference in kind between its capabilities (which he lists as compute, transformation, variation, selection and attention) and a human's, "and you still have it in your pocket. You aren't going to be replaced by AGI, you're going to be augmented by AGI." This is presented as speculation, not as a forecast, and it is consistent with his later description of such remarks as "fun speculation."

**Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md

The same conviction has a practical side. In a March 2026 video on building a one-person business with AI, Koe describes two ways of using AI for a personal brand. "The first thing is you can curate expert information and have a conversation with it": find YouTube videos or books from certified experts and add them to a chat, rather than asking a general AI for its opinion, "because AI works best when you know what you want." "The second and more complex one" is to take that expert information and break it down into a simplified guide: for example, take an expert's personal brand strategy, ask the AI to turn it into a guide (which "removes all of the fluff"), and then turn the guide into whatever kind of prompt you want. He did this, he says, for one of his own prompts, using his own personal brand strategy. The resulting prompts are of two types: a **blueprint prompt**, which produces a plan, and a **coach prompt**, which acts as a coach.

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

The method is consistent with the lore argument: the AI does not supply the perspective; it organizes expert material for a person who already knows what they want. That person's direction is what remains scarce.

#### Distribution and persuasion: what separates the human from the AI

The last group of ideas in this section explains why Koe treats attention as the factor that keeps human creative work valuable. In a March 2024 video he starts from the premise that pay depends on the quality and results of what is created, and on getting people to care about it. "This is what separates AI from human": it takes "a lot of knowledge and skill to get people to care about what it is that you actually created." An AI can write a short story or an article, but "you are the one that is going to deploy that writing in a place and in a way that gets people to read and care about it and pay you." What is missing from the AI's side "is distribution and persuasion." An AI that writes a short story cannot get it into other people's hands or get paid for it: for that you need an audience on social media, or advertising in which you spend your own money, money "that AI doesn't have," on Facebook ads.

From this he draws a blunt opinion: "people that are worried about AI taking their creative jobs are the ones that weren't going to make it anyways because they don't know how to monetize." The worry, on his reading, comes from lacking distribution and persuasion, not from AI's ability to replace them.

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

A July 2024 video gives the argument its most memorable example. "AI can write a book but that book is only valuable if it's marketed to, distributed to and written for an audience that perceives that book is valuable because of persuasion." "Try to have AI write a bestseller for you and I'll wait a long time." A bestseller, he points out, "is not just about the writing": it requires hooks, connections to companies, brands and creators with distribution, the execution of a marketing strategy on Amazon, getting into bookstores, "and that's just the tip of the iceberg." His recommendation for the meantime is practical: learn AI and use it "to get out of your work faster so you can start working for yourself."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

In January 2026 the same idea receives a name. "Attention is one of the last moats because it's very scarce." AGI, he speculates, may provide a basic foundation for survival, and many scarce things may become commodities, "but our attention, there's only so much to go around." So what will be worth paying for in the future? "As a creative, you may be surprised to hear this, but it's creative work. Stop worrying about AI copying your image or copying your writing." His reasoning: "Anyone's going to be able to create anything. Which ones are going to win? It's going to be the ones that people know about." You can have the greatest mobile or SaaS app, "but if nobody knows about it, it just ends up like every single other product." "It's never only just been build this thing."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

**Attention as moat** is therefore Koe's term for the claim that, as production becomes abundant, the scarce input that decides which products win is the attention of the people who know about them.

The tone of these passages changed over time. In 2024 Koe's judgment of those who fear replacement was harsh: the worried "weren't going to make it anyways." By 2026, in other videos, he speaks of AI "coming for the meaning" and not only for jobs, acknowledges the livelihoods at stake, and specifies that he is speaking to an individual with some control over their situation, "not people in circumstances with less control." He does not explain the change. The structural argument (distribution and persuasion are what make creative work pay) is stable; the moral framing of those who lack them softened.

The conclusion of this section prepares the next. If attention is the scarce input and creation is becoming abundant, then the decisive question for anyone who makes things is not "can I build it?" but "will anyone know about it?" Koe's name for the answer is distribution.

### 25.2 Distribution Equals Freedom

#### Anyone can build the thing

The second principle of the chapter begins from an observation about builders. In a February 2025 video on multiple interests, Koe states it plainly: "Anyone can build the thing. You can build a note-taking app or a writing app right now if you wanted to, but not everyone can attract people to that thing or allow them to care about it. That's where most coders, creatives or authors fall flat. They build a cool app or they write a book or they create art but they can't get people to care. They can't get customers." The good news, he adds, is that you can build an audience: "social media is the New Media," and you no longer need radio, television or a billboard.

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md

The same argument appears in October 2024 aimed at artists. Musicians who chase record labels miss the opportunity "right under their nose": "you can just build an audience based around your interest and then your music becomes your product," and the music is also how you promote it. The same holds for writers: to sell a book you no longer need a large publisher, because you can build your own audience. And the same holds for any independent work.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

Two earlier statements extend the argument to software. In January 2023, discussing his "$1 million skill stack," Koe calls coding a great high-value skill, especially when paired with marketing knowledge: if you can create software and know how to market it, "which most startup people don't," you are far ahead. Many coders passionate about their app or startup launch with no audience, no distribution, nothing behind them; investors, he notes, are what would otherwise substitute for the audience. He mentions that he is building a note-taking SaaS and claims that, with his marketing knowledge, his distribution and his understanding of what will benefit his audience, he will have a more successful launch "than any other tech founder could," because "most coders only know the technical side, they don't know the human side."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The claim should be read as what it is: a 2023 prediction about his own project. The last subsection of this section, and later the case chapter of Part XIII, show that the software company he built did not succeed in the way this passage anticipates, and that Koe himself later located the main problem in the product rather than in distribution.

In January 2026 he restates the argument as a matter of self-sufficiency and adaptability. For self-sufficiency you need a business, and every business must attract customers. You probably do not care about paid ads or SEO, and "this is what trips many people up because they are only used to doing one specialized task within a business as an employee." For adaptability, an audience lets you build and launch new products as fast as you can make them. His own situation illustrates the point: if his next product failed, he has people willing to invest, work on the team or support the next one. "It's not just one and done." Moreover, "it doesn't even cost you money to post content," whereas blowing your life savings on paid ads may be a dead end. A "vibe-coded" SaaS (software built quickly with AI) may be valuable, but without distribution or an audience "you're going to be putting in like a marathon's worth of effort into getting capital for it, into getting customers or users, into finding talent." His conclusion: "No other job or career or path allows people with multiple interests to do all of those things."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

Read together, the four passages establish the asymmetry on which the rest of the chapter rests. Making a thing is increasingly easy and increasingly common; getting people to care about it is hard and, for most builders, the step at which they fail. The 2023 software claim was a promise; the 2026 passage is a report: what an audience provides is not a single launch but the capacity to launch repeatedly, and to recover from failure, without starting each time from zero.

#### The case of Devon Eriksen

Koe's most developed illustration of this asymmetry is Devon Eriksen, a guest on his podcast (whose name the transcripts spell in several ways: Devon Eriksen, Devin Ericson, Devon Ericson). Eriksen had been a programmer who wanted his whole life to write a science fiction book, and he wrote one, *Theft of Fire*. Koe does not know whether Eriksen looked for a publishing deal, but he decided to sell the book himself, "because even if he got a publishing deal he would still have to sell the book." Publishing helps with distribution, Koe acknowledges, but with Amazon and other technologies "you don't need a publisher, you need to go out and sell, you need to go out and express your opinions and interests and beliefs online to attract people to your work."

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md

In the December 2024 interview, Eriksen himself explains why he declined the deals that came once "people figured out I could sell." His reasoning: "what use are you to me? I can have a direct relationship with my audience where I can be more responsive to them and I can be more appealing to them and I can get better feedback about what I'm doing wrong and what I'm doing right directly from them, not only from what they say but from their actions." The value, he concluded, was not there; the publisher was "this useless buffer in the relationship between author and audience that actively disrupted the quality of the feedback loop." He tells of the reaction he provoked at science fiction conventions: when he said that publishing houses add no value for new authors, a man threw a tantrum and called him a sociopath. The man turned out to be an editor at a major publishing house. "I was standing between this man and his bread," Eriksen says, and the man was operating from the self-limiting belief "I am an editor from a major publishing house."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

This argument is Eriksen's, not Koe's, and it adds something Koe's own formulations do not stress: an audience is not only a channel for selling but an instrument for learning. The direct relationship gives better feedback, both from what readers say and from what they do, and an intermediary degrades that feedback loop. The connection with Part VI (learning through feedback) and with Chapter 24 (the post as a test) is direct: owning the relationship with readers means owning the signal that tells you what to improve. The anecdote about the editor is Eriksen's illustration of conceptual survival from Section 25.1: a threat to an identity built on a role produces a reaction proportionate to a physical threat.

In March 2025 Koe returns to Eriksen to explain why his approach works where other self-published authors fail. Follow him on Twitter, Koe says, and look at how he posts. You may not like what he posts about (politics, among other things), but he is very good at writing his opinions in long form in a way that is unusual on that platform. Other self-published authors post things like "is anyone making sales on Twitter for their books?" They are thinking about it wrong: "it's not about Twitter or Instagram or YouTube... it's about your message and how people care about you." Their profiles are only book promotions. Eriksen's profile contains fleshed-out thoughts worth following, and people buying his book is a by-product, although he also promotes it. His book is "an extension of him."

**Source:** How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md

The distinction between a profile that is only promotion and a profile in which the product is a by-product anticipates the principle of Section 25.4 that "people follow people." The book sells because readers already care about the person who wrote it; the person is not a delivery mechanism for the book.

#### "Distribution equals freedom"

Koe gives the principle its canonical name in a December 2022 video on the one-person business roadmap, repeated in a 2024 compilation. "Build distribution, then build whatever you want in life." Adapting Jocko Willink's maxim "discipline equals freedom," he says that "in business, especially this one-person business, distribution equals freedom." (In the 2022 transcript the source of the maxim is garbled as "Draco willing"; the 2024 version and another 2023 passage name Jocko.) He calls it a very important concept that few people talk about. It falls, he says, under **digital leverage** (the subject of Section 25.3), while most people focus instead on generic social media tactics.

He then gives the definition that he will keep for years: from the lens of a creator business, **distribution** is "the potential traffic that you have to send to a product, service, or just like some crevice of the internet or some internet content."

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

**Complementary context:** Jocko Willink is a former U.S. Navy SEAL officer and author whose phrase "discipline equals freedom" (also the title of one of his books) expresses the idea that self-imposed constraints produce freedom of action. Koe transposes the structure of the phrase from personal conduct to business: what discipline is for a life, distribution is for a business.

The January 2023 version of the formula makes the transposition explicit and adds a technical note: "in life, discipline equals freedom, shout out Jocko, but in business distribution equals freedom." With an internet business, distribution comes from media and code, which Koe describes as the "back end" (code) and the "front end" (media) of internet business. That pairing is developed in Section 25.3.

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

Two passages from 2024 add the second half of the formula. In February, describing the stages of the one-person business, Koe says that the main thing across all stages is audience growth, which is why content creation is so important to master as a skill. The biggest problem in the space, he adds, is that people cannot stay consistent or cannot generate good ideas that result in good content. "If you aren't growing on social media then this isn't an opportunity for you, that's your number one lever, stop focusing on other [stuff]." And then: "Distribution equals freedom, audience equals distribution." He notes that client work can be turned into a digital product at any time for extra income, and that a product also serves clients who want to learn more before hiring you, but that the product will not be the main income at first.

**Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md

In May 2024, describing the "value creator" (Koe's name for the person who builds a one-person business on their own development), he explains why such a person creates social media content: "to become a one-person media company, to build distribution under your name, because in business distribution equals freedom and audience equals distribution." He then adds a sentence he asks the viewer to "hammer" into their head: "you will not make money that you control if you don't have people who give you money for the value you offer in the form of a product." Without that, "you have no other option but to work a job where your employer does this for you for the rest of your life."

**Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md

The two halves of the formula should be distinguished. "Distribution equals freedom" is a claim about consequences: whoever has distribution can choose what to build and how to earn. "Audience equals distribution" is a claim about means: for an individual without capital, the audience is the form distribution takes. The **number one lever** is Koe's name for audience growth as the priority that dominates all stages of the business. And the **one-person media company** is the identity this implies: the individual who, by publishing under their own name, owns a small media channel. The sentence about the employer shows what "freedom" means here. The alternative to owning distribution is not a smaller business but dependence on someone else who owns it: the employer, whose marketing department does the work of attracting customers.

#### "Build distribution, then build whatever you want"

The first half of the 2022 formula ("build distribution, then build whatever you want") is not Koe's. It belongs to Jack Butcher, a designer and creator, and Koe's handling of it over time is a good example of how he moves from quoting to adapting.

In May 2022 he quotes it directly and gives the definition of distribution he will reuse: distribution is "just potential traffic": an audience, an email list, communities, your network's audience, "everyone that can send traffic to something." When you have enough traffic, you really can build whatever you want; people with distribution sell merchandise and exclusive products to their audiences. His example is a physical planner or journal: sell it to an audience of 100,000 followers, ask five friends with 100,000 followers each to post about it, and you have reached roughly 600,000 people.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

In April 2023 he credits Butcher again ("shout out Jack Butcher") and gives the reason the formula holds: "you have infinite monetization options in this day and age." Once you build an audience, opportunities are thrown at you: people asking to hire you, people offering $3,000 for coaching, people asking for a product so they can learn more, people wanting to sponsor your newsletter. "You literally just have to put yourself on the market." He then compresses the whole business model into one line of his own: "give value, build distribution, ask to get paid, that is it."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

In December 2023 the quotation becomes the frame for a full process. The Butcher line, he says, has lived "rent free" in his head for two or three years. How do you start a billion-dollar company? Not by starting as one, but by starting as one person leveraging technology. The steps he gives are:

1. Deploy digital real estate (profiles, posts and other digital assets) until you have ample distribution.
2. Write posts every day that live on your profile.
3. Network so that your posts get shared and you grow without depending on the algorithm.
4. Get on podcasts to build authority.
5. Plug your newsletter so that you own your audience.
6. Give away small free products to grow the newsletter.
7. Sell a low-ticket product to get people dedicated to your brand.

He then gives a threshold: "an audience of 10,000, newsletter of 1,000 and network of 100." With that, he says, you can make a full-time income, and it takes about a year if done correctly. After that, extra cash can be funneled into whatever you actually want, even a grand idea.

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md

The sequence is a compact map of much of this book. Digital real estate and daily posts are the subject of Chapter 24; networking and podcasts are developed in Chapter 26; owning the audience through the newsletter is Section 24.4; the free and low-ticket products belong to Part X. The 10,000/1,000/100 threshold is Koe's rule of thumb for a full-time income, not a guarantee; elsewhere in the corpus his benchmarks for growth vary with skill and stage, and later he moves away from follower counts as the main metric (Section 25.4). What the threshold does show is the relative weight he gives the three elements: the network is the smallest number but, as Chapter 26 explains, it is what lets a beginner grow "without the algorithm."

#### Why he went all in: the infinite readership

A 2023 passage explains the personal origin of this conviction. After seeing some success with freelance web design, Koe wanted to go all in on social media, "because I saw the potential to infinitely grow my readership over time: if I could get one follower then I could get a million, and if I could get a million then I could get 10 million, and so if I had 10 million or 1 million followers I was kind of set for life if I had a product that sold." "Building an audience with valuable writing that they wanted to read would allow me to build and sell anything I wanted to make a living, as long as the customer wanted it as well." This, he says, "is the driving force behind why I created 2 Hour Writer": writing and building an unlimited readership lets you sell according to your interests, and for people who want to "monetize what they enjoy learning about and improving themselves with and improving others with, that's how you make money."

**Source:** A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md

The reasoning is a personal version of Butcher's line, with one condition attached that is easy to overlook: "as long as the customer wanted it as well." Distribution creates the option to build anything; it does not make anything sell. That condition is formalized below in Koe's three things needed for success.

#### What changes slowly and what changes constantly

In December 2022, describing the seventh step of building a one-person business, Koe distinguishes the parts of the business by how often they change. The brand, once solidified, does not change much. The product is updated and iterated a few times, but "few and far between" compared to content and promotions. Content builds distribution: an audience, an email list, a community. Marketing distributes the product through promotions: plugs in social posts, newsletter promotions, changes of angle and messaging. "The only way you're going to succeed in this great online game is through testing, persistence and iteration."

**Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md

The distinction clarifies what "build distribution" means in practice. Distribution is not built once; it is built by the parts of the business that move constantly (content and promotions), on top of the parts that move slowly (brand and product). Systemizing promotions is the step that connects the audience to the product.

#### Distribution is not optional: the personal distribution center

In an April 2023 video on "solving your own problems and selling the solution," Koe describes the third component of the one-person business as a **personal distribution center**: traffic, people who will buy the product. "This is where most people get stuck": "I have a freelance service but I don't have any clients," "I have a cool planner that I made but I don't have any customers." "It's because you have to build and prioritize distribution. This isn't optional."

How is such a center built? "You build a personal distribution center by passing down the lessons you learn pursuing your goals, writing out your thoughts, beliefs and opinions to separate yourself from others, and teaching what you learn in a persuasive manner. That's really it." He notes that his tactical content on writing and structure exists elsewhere, and that this passage is "more big picture": the philosophy behind building a personal brand and generating independent income.

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

The method contains three elements, each of which links to an earlier part of the book. Passing down lessons learned while pursuing your goals is the "build, write and sell to yourself" principle of Chapter 19: the content comes from the path you are on. Writing out your beliefs and opinions to separate yourself from others is the conviction and differentiation of Chapter 22. Teaching persuasively is the structure and attention work of Chapter 23. The personal distribution center is therefore not a separate activity added to the writing; it is what the writing becomes when it is done consistently and in public.

The same month, describing his "micro education" business model, Koe makes the link between distribution and daily work explicit. The three-point model he has just described is "for building distribution or traffic to send to a product or service, that's how you monetize." Everything in it "will form the levers for what you do on a weekly and daily basis": writing newsletters, threads, tweets, and repurposing them if you want. As distribution grows, you start to think about how to monetize it.

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

#### Owned distribution and the arithmetic of 100,000 followers

In a September 2023 video, Koe reports a pattern from conversations with high-level entrepreneurs in private equity, agencies and software. In almost every conversation, he says, "the point of owned distribution comes up." Among the things said to him directly: "I don't know why I didn't start posting content sooner," "it's insane how much the marketing world has changed in a few short years," and "a personal brand is becoming less and less optional." Business owners, he observes, know they need to post content, or, in his phrase, "get into virtual reality by displaying their character as a personal brand."

**Owned distribution** (or owned audience) is distribution that belongs to you and accumulates, as opposed to attention that is rented each time. The evidence here is anecdotal and Koe presents it as such: it is what people he knows tell him.

He follows it with a thought experiment. Imagine he spent two years building an audience of 100,000 followers. If he promoted his product every day for a month "in a smart way," he would get at least a million impressions. How long would it take to send a direct message to a million people? "A long time." How much would it cost to reach that many people with ads? About $7,000 a month, assuming an average cost per thousand impressions (CPM) of $7. And owned distribution, unlike either alternative, compounds.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

The numbers are illustrative assumptions, not measurements: the CPM is an average he states, and "a million impressions" depends on the promotion being done "in a smart way." But the structure of the comparison is the argument. Manual outreach scales with your time, ads scale with your money, and an audience scales with neither once it exists; each month of building makes the next month's reach cheaper.

#### The most accessible start

Several passages explain why Koe treats audience building as the natural first step for someone without capital. In May 2024 he argues that for an independent income you can no longer rely on your boss's marketing department to generate attention; you must do it yourself. Building an audience is the most accessible way for beginners: there is no capital to invest, you start from zero, and you start with writing.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

In March 2026 he makes the same point with a concrete target. The traffic needed for, in his example, 720 daily visitors can come from social media, Facebook or Google ads, SEO, influencer partnerships, podcast sponsorships or newsletter sponsorships. "But you are one person, and if you're like I was when I first started out, you don't have that much money and you don't want to spend that much money. So what are you going to do? You're going to do social media, and you're going to get really good at capturing attention, delivering value, delivering substance, not just copying templates, not just following the trends, but getting very good at what you do and showing other people that you're good at what you do."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

The qualification "not just copying templates, not just following the trends" ties this back to Section 25.1: in an environment where formats are copied and AI accelerates the copying, the beginner's only durable advantage is substance.

A March 2024 video gives the method and its benefits in one sentence: "Building an audience with education, inspiration and entertainment through content is the best way to start a business these days." The benefits are three: "you get to prove your value in public, attract a following you don't have to pay for, and put a product in front of them so you can get paid to do what you want." It adds a heuristic for anyone who doubts it: "Study those who are doing what you want to do. I guarantee they are posting on social media, building a name for themselves and selling something, because they aren't selling something for someone else anymore."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

**Prove your value in public** is the key phrase. The audience is not only a list of potential buyers; it is the place where the evidence of your competence accumulates before anyone pays you. The triad of education, inspiration and entertainment is the one developed in Chapter 23.

#### Three things you need for success

In an October 2023 video on building an audience from zero followers, Koe reads a note he wrote after an idea came to him at the gym. "Three things that you need for success are: one, what you do — what do you make, what do you do, is it valuable; two, how many people know what you do; three, is the thing that you do valuable to the people that know what you do, and if it's not, how do you make it valuable to the people that know what you do — that's where marketing and sales comes in." And then: "With consistency and effort and improving the thing that you do and increasing the amount of people that know what you do, that's success."

**Source:** How To Build An Audience With Zero Followers (What They Don't Tell You).md

The framework is small but it organizes the chapter:

| Component | Question | Where it is developed |
|---|---|---|
| What you do | Is the thing you make valuable? | Parts VI, X and XI (skill, product, value) |
| How many people know | How large is your distribution? | This chapter and Chapter 26 |
| Value to those who know | Is it valuable to the people who know about it, and if not, how do you make it so? | Marketing and sales (Part XI) |

The second and third components are separate for a reason. Distribution alone (many people knowing) does not produce success if what you do is not valuable to them; the third component is where the fit between product and audience is made, and Koe assigns it explicitly to marketing and sales. This is the formal version of the condition in his 2 Hour Writer story: "as long as the customer wanted it as well."

#### The personal brand as backbone

Koe's most consistent argument for the audience as distribution compares it with the alternatives. In April 2023 he reports having tried cold outreach, LinkedIn prospecting and paid ads. All are useful and serve their place, "but you need the backbone, you need leverage, you need something that compounds over time, you need a personal brand," to display authority and have clients come to you rather than "shooting blanks into the cold email space." The comparison that follows is the argument: if you send cold messages while giving out value and growing an audience, you end up with the same number of messages sent and the same number of clients, plus 50,000 followers, "but now you have 50,000 people more that are waiting to pay you."

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

The **backbone**, in his usage, is the element of distribution that accumulates while the other methods produce one-off results.

A March 2023 video gives the same idea its most strategic form. "Your personal brand should be considered the foundational traffic source for your offers." Many people worry about selling a business before they have even started; "stop worrying that late into the future." Koe says he plans to keep his personal brand for life; he does not plan to sell himself. With that **foundational traffic source**, he can build another business, fuel it with the brand and, provided he is not too closely tied to it, eventually sell it, making it successful ten times faster than someone without a personal brand, because of loyalty and a nurtured audience. The businesses reflect the values of his brand and are an extension of it; previous customers and his following fuel them; his network can send massive traffic and promote them without paying for influencer marketing, practically for free.

In the same video he explains how a brand multiplies other traffic sources. Paid ads are another source, but if more people know your name and you run ads to your audience, conversion goes "through the roof." With cold outreach, if he messages his audience or people who have seen him around because he produces content, they are "ten times more likely to reply"; with 100,000 followers, "they're going to feel obligated to [...] reply." He says he does not like to think of himself that way, but that is what happens.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

A November 2023 video adds a reason why the brand must be personal rather than corporate. A personal brand is "arguably the most potent lead generation, just front end of a business right now, because people follow humans, they don't follow company accounts." Companies do not know how to run social media, and they do not have individuals as the face of the brand. He mentions Red Bull not posting pictures of its product, as an aside whose point is unclear in the transcript; it seems to illustrate that successful brands avoid showing the product, but the passage does not make the inference explicit.

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

In July 2024 he ranks the options. Online, the ways to attract an audience are paid ads (unsuitable if you have no startup money), SEO and similar methods, and social media. "Building an audience under your personal brand is the most profitable, the most trustworthy." With content, "you don't need to worry so much about sales calls and how optimized your landing pages are and things like that; you just talk to your audience, people like who you are, and then you can build whatever you want."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

In June 2024 he goes further and attacks the advice to "build something offline, then start online," which he calls narrow and dumb. Social media, he argues, is a viable and in fact the best way for beginners to start, for two reasons: no experience is needed, and you learn the skills along the way. You build a profile, and its success depends on learning the only business skills you need (design, writing, marketing, sales, branding, product, distribution), each learned in sequence: start writing to build an audience and traffic, then build a product, then learn promotions, copywriting, sales pages and web pages. "Becoming a creator is like guiding your learning in the most effective way possible towards the skills that are going to make you the most money." It is not wise, he argues, to suffer through brick-and-mortar or even e-commerce when you can build a personal brand ("the highest form of leverage") and use the audience and cash flow to start whatever business you want. Offline businesses should be on social media anyway, so it makes sense to do it under a personal brand, which then becomes **business agnostic**: you can launch services and products under your own name as businesses of their own.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

The 2023 case of Sahil Bloom, a friend of Koe's, illustrates the same point from the outside. Bloom posted that personal brands are "the ultimate business hack": having built several businesses, he now knows when he builds one that it will succeed, because many people are already going to be customers. Koe's explanation is that with an audience you know what people want and create something valuable for them. "It's not like you're just milking your audience for money by scamming them"; they pay because they want what you made, and people with "a bad psychological relationship with money always get this twisted."

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The cluster of terms these passages introduce (backbone, foundational traffic source, front end of lead generation, business agnostic) describes one asset from several angles. The personal brand compounds while other methods do not; it is the base on which several businesses can be launched; it multiplies the yield of ads and outreach; it works because people follow humans; and it is not tied to any single business. Chapter 27 treats what a personal brand is in itself; here it matters as the form distribution takes for an individual.

One tension should be registered. In October 2024 Koe published a video titled "The Death Of The Personal Brand," in which he criticized the term and the money-obsessed, template-driven model it had come to denote, and recommended being "you, in a place where your work can be discovered" rather than "a personal brand." By 2026 he had recovered the term ("get over" the cringe of the word; "it is the future in many ways, especially as a value creator"). What he criticized was a drift (the brand as an empty funnel), not the practice described in this section: publishing under your own name to build owned distribution. Chapter 27 develops that change.

#### Distribution through content: Stan and the education-first strategy

Two cases close the section by applying the principle to software, the field where builders most often assume the product will speak for itself.

The first is told by John Hugh (also transcribed as John Hu), a co-founder of Stan, a platform for creators, in a January 2025 conversation with Koe. "If you build it they will come is not necessarily true for most people," Hugh says; "no one cares... you have to make people care." From a business-school standpoint, Stan was "such a bad idea": 500 competitors, legacy incumbents that had raised hundreds of millions, and software in the market that was "shitty and clunky and so overpriced." Stan was not initially ten times better, only "two or three times better." Yet hundreds of competitors flatlined while Stan became "a rocket ship." The key, in Hugh's account, was distribution. The competitors "were just tech bros trying to make money on a hot economy"; Hugh "bled this," posted content all day by vlogging in public, and his customers became his friends. His formula: "The best way to talk to your customers is through the content that they consume and the place that they're already at"; "distribution should come inbound through content." Scaling that means hiring more creators to make content and "blow out your content."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

The case is Hugh's, and the account of Stan's success is his own. What it contributes to Koe's argument is a counterexample to the assumption that a superior product wins: here a product only marginally better won because its founder had **inbound distribution through content**, a second sense of "distribution" that complements Koe's definition as potential traffic. Distribution is not only traffic you can send; it is the conversation with customers that happens inside the content they already consume.

The second case is Koe's own plan for a software company, described in September 2023. The common route, he says, is to build the product, test it and market it to a specific audience; "this works," but he wants to "skip the sink or swim phase." He is aware that churn, developer costs and ingraining software into people's daily lives will be difficult. Instead, he plans to take the company to $5–10 million in its first year ("a lofty goal") by leveraging what he has learned in modern business: the power of a personal brand and of digital products. The plan has four steps:

1. Build the company's social media "like we would a personal brand, with an avatar as the face of the brand."
2. Use that audience, plus "a plethora of creator audiences," to fuel a cohort-based course on the subject of the software.
3. Make that education product, operating at 95% profit margins, the main revenue driver in year one.
4. As a result, it does not matter whether the software is profitable in year one; they can "play long term and slowly inject it into the creator economy as the one true software."

He notes that most businesses have no idea what it takes to build a social media audience, so they rely on creator sponsorships; "we want to create a Creator." He says he cannot reveal details yet for legal reasons.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

The **sink or swim phase** is his name for the risky initial period of a software product, and **create a Creator** (with an **avatar as the face of the brand**) is his name for building a company's distribution the way an individual builds a personal brand.

This plan must be read alongside its outcome, which the corpus records two years later. In November 2025 Koe described the software company, Kortex, as "the $3 million mistake": a series of necessary errors by first-time founders, in which the product was not compelling, he spent too much time justifying it, and they were "forced" to stop and pivot to a different product. The 2023 plan located the solution in distribution and an education product; the 2025 assessment located the central problem in the product and its fit with the market. The corpus does not connect the two moments explicitly, and it does not say whether the education-first strategy was executed as described. What can be said is that the case qualifies the principle of this section rather than refuting it: distribution is necessary for a product to win, as Stan's story suggests, but it is not sufficient, as the third of Koe's "three things you need for success" already stated. Part XIII returns to this case as a limit of the one-person model.

### 25.3 Forms of Leverage

#### The people side of the equation

Section 25.2 established that distribution is the condition for a product to reach anyone. This section asks a more analytical question: what kinds of distribution are there, and what does each one multiply? Koe's answers come in several versions, and the differences among them are instructive.

The simplest statement of the problem comes from a September 2024 video on wealth, where building an audience is the third step of a sequence. "After you have understood someone's psychology or your own psychology and you've created a solution, you need to build, borrow or buy an audience comprised of the people with the same psychology and problem that you're solving." Having made "something that people want," you "need the people side of that equation." He then warns against the typical trap: "most beginners fall into the trap of building a website, perfecting their product, forming an LLC and the rest, but never get around to the most important part: customers." For those who want to skip that process, he points to another of his videos, on "the minimalist offer or the micro offer": "how to start selling immediately without really building anything aside from just finding a way to get people to want the knowledge in your head."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md

The warning identifies the opposite error to the one Section 25.2 criticized. There, the builder who has a product but no audience fails; here, the beginner busies themselves with the apparatus of a business (website, polished product, legal entity) and postpones the only part that produces revenue. Both errors put the thing before the people.

#### Built, borrowed and bought (2022)

Koe's main taxonomy of distribution appears in the December 2022 roadmap video and is repeated almost word for word in the 2024 compilation. "There are three types of distribution: there is built, borrowed and bought."

**Built distribution** is growing on social media to gather an audience or readership for your value, retaining it, and then, in his word, "deplatforming" it. The reason is that social media can disappear at any time, especially if you are polarizing or controversial: you could be canceled and lose the audience. So you send people to an email list ("nobody can take emails away from you"), a community or a Telegram channel, and keep delivering deeper value there. Built distribution is the most important of the three, he says, because you are neither borrowing nor paying: it is in your control.

**Borrowed distribution** is appearing on someone's podcast to promote yourself and your products, being mentioned in someone's newsletter, writing a guest post, or being retweeted or shared by a large account. Borrowed traffic "spills over" into your built traffic. In the 2024 version he adds that the best way to borrow is to have what he calls a "mental monopoly," an idea developed in Chapter 27.

**Bought distribution** is paying for ads (Google, Facebook, Instagram) or for shoutouts. Paying for a podcast sponsorship also falls here. He notes that the categories overlap: paying for a shoutout is also a way of borrowing someone's audience, with spillover.

His conclusion: of the three, focus on building your own.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

**Deplatform**, in Koe's usage, is a common word given a specific sense. In general usage, to deplatform someone is to remove them from a platform; in his usage, you deplatform your own audience, moving it from a rented space (a social network) to an owned one (email, community, channel). The idea connects directly to the newsletter as "the Hub" in Section 24.4.

#### Built, borrowed and bought (2024), and the case of Cortex

In the September 2024 video, Koe returns to the same three categories with somewhat different contents. **Built**: "you build distribution on something like social media and an email list; this is something you own and can promote your value to at any time; it is the highest leverage of the bunch." **Borrowed**: "networking with other people, showing up as a podcast guest and getting thousands of eyes on you, writing guest newsletters for people with large lists, posting on forum sites or communities." **Bought**: "sponsorships and advertisements: podcast sponsorships, newsletter sponsorships, Google ads, Facebook ads, YouTube ads."

"In an optimal world you would do all of them," he says, but time is limited, money for hiring is limited, and so is money for ads "at a positive ROI." He illustrates with his software company, which he calls Cortex in this passage (elsewhere Kortex): they already have the audience (built), they are working with larger creators to build up to a larger launch (borrowed), and "eventually we need to buy distribution in order to reach the scale that we want."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md

The Cortex example shows the three categories not as alternatives but as a sequence: built first, borrowed to amplify, bought to scale. This ordering is consistent with how Koe's view of paid ads evolved. Early on (2021–2022) he treated ads as something not worth splitting focus over, or as a way of "throwing money into an oven" while testing ideas. From 2024 he describes ads as "steroids" that come after "natural bodybuilding," and organic posts as free tests of angles that later make ads profitable. The change is a refinement: ads move from "later, maybe" to a stage fed by organic validation. He attributes it to the scale of his later projects and, implicitly, to his own failure with ads in 2022.

The same video gives the recommendation for the beginner: "In my eyes the best option for a beginner is to build an audience, because it's completely dependent on your skill and how much you invest into learning that and actually doing it on social media." "It's free to start, it's accessible: you can post a TikTok tomorrow and get 2 million views and 50,000 followers; that's a very rare case, but it's more than possible and people do it every day." He adds immediately: "I'm not promising anything"; "even if you get a fraction of that, that's enough to make a full-time income"; and "I don't want to set false expectations: it's going to take time; expect it to take time."

**Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md

The three reasons for the recommendation (it depends on skill, it is free, it is accessible) and the one cost (time) define the beginner's trade. Built distribution is the only kind that a person without money or connections can acquire by effort alone, and the price is patience.

#### Low and high leverage; manual, bot, borrowed and owned

Between those two versions, in 2023, Koe used two other ways of dividing the same field.

In an August 2023 video on high-income skills, he frames step three of a career path as "build distribution," quoting Jack Butcher again, and calls distribution "the most important concept you can understand in the digital race to riches." Distribution methods, he says, vary in leverage. Freelancers, agencies, local businesses and even startups often focus on **low-leverage**, instantly gratifying methods: cold email, paid ads, direct messages. They remain viable, but "you are often left with nothing if you stop putting effort into" them. The better approach is to build long-term distribution while mixing in the other methods to see profit quickly. The **high-leverage** forms he lists are: building an audience (for example, 100,000 followers on X); promoting to your network (an internet friend with 100,000 followers promotes you to their followers); and **renting others' audiences** (newsletter or YouTube sponsorships to reach millions). "You must learn social media as the skill to tap into the global distribution network." And because audience building takes time, set aside time along the way for the quicker low-leverage methods, such as cold email and DMs.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md

A month later, in September 2023, he proposes a fourfold taxonomy, introduced with the remark that a personal brand does not eliminate other kinds of distribution. **Manual distribution** is cold email, cold calls and cold DM campaigns. **Bot distribution** is buying Facebook or Google ads. **Borrowed distribution** is paying for podcast, YouTube or newsletter sponsorships. **Owned distribution** is building an audience, newsletter, community or list of product buyers. "The lines are blurred between them all," he concedes, and he admits leaving out things like SEO. All have their use cases: the first three "are incredible for testing," are good for beginner business owners, and give "some form of control over your outcomes." Owned distribution, by contrast, "is a long-term game that scares most people away." His recommendation is to systemize audience growth over time and use the other methods to reach the revenue you want.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

The three schemes are compatible in substance but not in their labels, and the placement of paid sponsorships shows it most clearly:

| Scheme (date) | Categories | Where paid sponsorships go | Organizing criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Built / borrowed / bought (2022, repeated 2024-02) | Built; borrowed (guest appearances, mentions, shares); bought (ads, shoutouts) | Bought | Who owns the channel, and whether you pay |
| Low / high leverage (2023-08) | Low (cold email, ads, DMs); high (own audience, network, renting audiences) | High leverage ("renting others' audiences") | Whether results persist when effort stops |
| Manual / bot / borrowed / owned (2023-09) | Manual (cold outreach); bot (ads); borrowed (paid sponsorships); owned (audience, list) | Borrowed | Mechanism and ownership |
| Built / borrowed / bought (2024-09) | Built; borrowed (networking, guesting, guest newsletters, forums); bought (sponsorships, ads) | Bought | Ownership and cost |

Koe does not explain why he switches schemes or reclassifies sponsorships, and he acknowledges that the lines blur. The inconsistency is classificatory rather than substantive: one scheme measures who owns the channel, another measures cost and control, another measures whether the effect lasts. What is constant across all four is the hierarchy. Owned or built distribution, an audience and a list in your control, is always the highest-leverage form, the one to prioritize over time, and the one that compounds. Everything else is useful for speed, for testing or for scale, and is to be mixed in, not relied upon.

#### Instant versus long term: the arithmetic of impressions

Koe often justifies that hierarchy with rough arithmetic. In the same August 2023 video he reports getting about 12 million impressions a month on X alone, probably quadruple that across platforms. From a Google search ("I don't know how accurate this is"), he takes a low CPM of about $3 per thousand impressions, so his X impressions alone correspond to about $36,000 a month in ad spend; if the content and ads were profitable, that should produce well over $100,000 a month. You can pay $36,000 a month for the impressions he gets after three to four years of building an audience. "It's instant versus long term, but that doesn't invalidate either one: it's do both but aim for the long term." Twitter, he adds, is now one of his smaller audiences; across all platforms his impressions are worth roughly $100,000–150,000 of ad spend a month. If he cannot out-earn $150,000 a month, he is not getting a return on that asset; and since his audience is nurtured, understands what he talks about and is more likely to buy, he believes he should be able to make $300,000–600,000 a month as one person with growing distribution.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md

In November 2023 he makes a similar comparison with video. With a YouTube following of a million, "every YouTube video is as powerful as a webinar, and if your webinar makes you seven figures then do the math." You can spend hundreds of thousands of dollars a year on ads, or build an audience of 100,000 over a year and get the same impressions. But he adds a caveat: building 100,000 followers in a year is "extremely good," achieved by people with a lot of experience doing it "almost near perfect, so don't expect that much but aim big."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

These figures should be handled with the same caution Koe applies to them. The CPM numbers come from a quick search he himself doubts ($3 here, $7 in the 2023 thought experiment of Section 25.2), the revenue he "should" make is an aspiration rather than a report, and his benchmarks for growth vary across videos (elsewhere he gives 10,000–20,000 followers in the first year as a reasonable target). What the arithmetic illustrates is the structure of the comparison: the same reach, bought monthly or built once, and the built version compounding into an asset.

The earliest version of this reasoning is a 2022 method for the "product route." If you sell products, "you either need paid ads or you need an audience"; if you do not know how either works, educate yourself. To build an audience (traffic to send to a product), write three tweets a day and engage with ten people a day to get eyeballs on your profile, or network correctly so that people share your content and you grow from their audience. Once you have enough followers "or enough of a distribution network," promote once a day, test and observe, and, in his phrase, "actualize the vision of your conversion rate."

**Source:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md

The 2022 quotas (three tweets a day, ten engagements a day, one promotion a day) belong to an early stage of Koe's thinking about volume. Later he moved toward one weekly piece of content broken down into the rest and toward adding scarcity to the brand once an audience exists; Chapter 26 develops this change. The structure of the method, however, is the one this chapter has been describing: build traffic first, then promote into it, then test.

#### Digital leverage: access, network effect and control of income

What does an audience actually unlock? Koe's most concrete answer is the concept of **digital leverage**, presented in the December 2022 roadmap video and repeated in the 2024 compilation. What most people skip, he says, is leverage, "specifically digital leverage." He "paints a picture," acknowledging that it may sound self-aggrandizing, from his own situation at the time: more than 500,000 followers across platforms, with YouTube among the smallest. The picture has three parts.

1. **Access.** Several celebrities follow him on Instagram; he can reach out to almost anyone and get a reply, which an average freelancer cannot. Even if he never sold anything through his audience, he could get in front of a celebrity and persuade them to buy web design in the DMs.
2. **Network effect.** A promotion of his reaches about ten times his follower count, because "my promotions and my content are not only reaching my followers but their followers as well." If each follower has, say, 500 followers, the potential reach multiplies; and many of his followers are creators with audiences of 100,000 or more, since creators follow each other. Potential traffic compounds well beyond his own number.
3. **Control of income.** He can increase or decrease his income depending on how much he wants to work and on new projects. "If I really wanted to I could make $50,000 in 24 hours," or more than $100,000 if he "stepped moral boundaries," which he will not do because "it doesn't feel good." On Black Friday, "when it's justified," he made $139,000 in three to four days.

Hence, he says, he finds it funny when social media marketers advise people not to bother gaining many followers. In the 2024 version he adds a preface: this is aimed at one-person creator businesses (talking about your interests, few or no employees, freedom enabled by technology), not at freelancers unless they want an audience; and if it does not align with your goals, you should not let flashy screenshots distract you.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The Black Friday figure is not consistent across the corpus: elsewhere in the same compilation the same event appears as $130,000 in three days. The discrepancy is probably between different tellings of the same event and does not affect the argument.

The concept of **network effect** here is a common term used in a specific sense: the indirect reach of content through the followers of one's followers, summarized elsewhere as "your audience is not just your audience." It is distinct from the economist's usage (a product becoming more valuable as more people use it), though related to it.

In a June 2023 video on the four-hour workday, Koe adds a fourth dimension: audience size multiplies output. If a brand with 50,000 followers can make $50,000 a month ("the low end if you know what you're doing"), he can make the same with a tenth of the effort with 500,000 followers; with two million, which he says he has, he can make it "in an hour a week." He attributes the ability to make ten times what others make to "the focus that I've invested over the years," and he rejects the "status game" of who works the longest hours ("hustle, grind... I deserve more than you"). He then adds an observation about skill rather than audience: "me writing one tweet is the same as a beginner writing ten thousand, simply because I've iterated so many times." He understands what a high-performing idea is, and there is far more knowledge and experience behind each of his tweets.

**Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md

The last remark distinguishes two kinds of accumulated leverage that are easy to confuse. One is the audience, which multiplies the reach of any piece. The other is iteration, which multiplies the quality of each piece. Both are the result of time invested, and both explain why an experienced creator can work far fewer hours, which is the bridge between this chapter and the four-hour workday of Chapter 12.

In January 2025, Koe states the consequence for what comes after the one-person business. Once people have an audience, "we're in this space, this digital space where distribution is extremely important, and if you have distribution you have a lot of leverage, you can do something more if you want to": startups, software, physical products, "something where you need a team." He frames the one-person business as a stage he himself stayed in, partly because hiring was his constraint.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

This is the second meaning of "build whatever you want." Distribution does not only open monetization options within the one-person business; it is also the platform from which larger ventures can be launched. Part XIII examines what happened when Koe did so.

#### From Archimedes to Naval: the forms of leverage

The deeper source of Koe's concept of leverage is Naval Ravikant, an investor and entrepreneur whose ideas on wealth Koe adapted more than any other source in the corpus. In a May 2022 video, he opens the topic with a quotation attributed to Archimedes: give me a lever long enough and a place to stand, and I will move the earth. Then he cites Naval: "fortunes will require leverage," and business leverage comes from capital, from people, and from products with no marginal cost of replication, namely code and media. He says that he has been dissecting Naval's philosophy and that it "has become my own philosophy without even realizing that it was from Naval." In the same passage he distinguishes wealth from riches: wealth is "having more than enough money to meet your basic needs and do what you want."

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

**Complementary context:** Naval Ravikant's ideas on leverage were popularized mainly through a 2018 series of tweets titled "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" and the podcast and book that followed. In that framework, leverage is what multiplies the output of one's judgment; labor and capital are "permissioned" forms (someone must agree to work for you or give you money), while code and media are "permissionless" because they can be created and replicated without anyone's consent. The Archimedes quotation is a traditional saying about the lever, preserved in ancient sources in several versions.

In June 2024 Koe revisits the list, describing Naval as someone who has had a major impact on his worldview. The three forms are now (1) labor, (2) capital and (3) products with no marginal cost of replication. Why care? Because, in an idea Koe takes from Naval, "getting rich is about knowing what to do, who to do it with and when to do it"; and if you only have an hour a day, it is even more important to know what to work on, with whom, and how both fit into the puzzle of the digital world. His own framing in that passage is that digital products are **the highest form of leverage**, which is why he created his course (the passage is part of a promotional segment).

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

In the same video he develops the second form. Capital is a more recent invention than labor. Capital as leverage means spending money to make more money: investing in businesses, real estate, stocks or crypto; investing in resources that take work off your hands; investing in information that helps you avoid costly mistakes, make better decisions and work more efficiently. The problem is that most people are "broke in more areas than finances": they lack the money to invest in assets that pay back a reasonable amount.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

The phrase "broke in more areas than finances" suggests, without fully developing it, that the beginner lacks more than money: time, knowledge and attention are also missing. The practical consequence is that capital leverage is mostly closed to the person this book addresses.

In May 2025 Koe revisits the three forms again, this time with the drawbacks of each and a new name for the third.

1. **Labor leverage**: increasing time on tasks or outsourcing them to others. Its drawbacks are management overhead, complexity, and the fact that you do not have much time.
2. **Capital leverage**: money works for you through investments. Its drawback is that it requires existing wealth or something worth investing in.
3. **Permissionless leverage**: the most powerful modern form. Its drawback is that most people look as if they are doing the same thing ("everyone's a coach, creator, teacher now"). What people miss about the creator economy, he says, is that your specific interests and stories make you unique.

"We live in the most permissionless time in history. You can just do things." The forms of permissionless leverage are code, media, books, podcasts, tweets, newsletters and courses. They have zero marginal cost of replication: build once and sell as many times as you like; one digital product can serve a million users. Its importance, he argues, is that never before has so much power been in the hands of the individual: one person or a small team can reach global scale without gatekeepers; digital products need no permission to be built or distributed; and they often have exponential rather than linear returns on time.

**Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md

In June 2026 he gives the third form its most compact formulation, quoting Naval directly: "Code and media are permissionless leverage. They're the leverage behind the newly rich. You can create software and media that works for you while you sleep." He explains that media used to mean radio, billboards, books, physical mail and newspapers; "today, it's content," and content can work while you sleep: the very video he is recording links to his product Eden, some viewers will pay, and it will keep growing and driving traffic. Two things are needed: (1) a source of traffic, which is media created on a consistent schedule with a strategy; and (2) your offer, what you sell independently instead of selling your boss's product.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

The successive versions can be set side by side:

| Date | First form | Second form | Third form |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022-05 (citing Naval) | People | Capital | Products with no marginal cost of replication (code and media) |
| 2024-06 | Labor | Capital | Products with no marginal cost of replication; digital products as "the highest form of leverage" |
| 2025-05 | Labor (management overhead) | Capital (requires existing wealth) | Permissionless leverage (code, media, books, podcasts, newsletters, courses) |
| 2026-06 | — | — | "Code and media are permissionless leverage"; media now means content |
| 2026-07 | Old leverage (physical, linear, local) | — | New leverage: "media, data and code" |

The renaming is not explained, but the direction is clear. "People" becomes "labor"; the third form is renamed twice and widened, with "data" added in 2026; and a historical axis appears in which the first two forms belong to an older world. Koe's stated reason for the shift in emphasis is that leverage today no longer requires capital. The concept of permissionless leverage connects with the critique of gatekeepers in Section 25.2: the publisher, the record label and the investor are exactly the "permission" that this form of leverage dispenses with.

#### Media and code

The pairing of media and code, which Koe takes from Naval, has its own development in the corpus. In May 2022 he presents it as the two things one must understand to make curiosity practical, full time or otherwise. **Media** "is the message, media is communication": how people communicate, especially online. Television commercials and shows were media; now "the entirety of what we see on the internet is media," the front end. **Code** is the back end of the internet, which houses everything; advances in technology and code allow more creativity, replace some jobs and create new ones (creators, freelancing). Media is how you capture attention and potentially sell something of value; code is the infrastructure.

**Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md

In August 2023 he turns the pairing into a career path. Step one is code and content. Quoting Naval ("Naval popularized"), he repeats that "code and media are permissionless leverage, they're the leverage behind the newly rich." Most people start with one or the other, as a programmer or as a content marketer; with time you can learn both, "one of the most potent skill stacks": "that's a **full stack creator**." Programmers can work for creators, startups or any modern employer, as freelancers or employees, and so can content marketers. Every business has a content, marketing, sales or media team; every business needs content and, depending on the business, programming. "That's where jobs, work and customers will be."

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md

In August 2025 the pairing becomes a broader ideal, the **philosopher builder**. We respect two types of people, Koe says: programmers and marketers, big-picture thinkers and technical-detail people, the spiritual and the practical, philosophers and scientists. "The future demands that we merge these polar ends, because a builder has always needed a thinker." A programmer not paired with a marketer almost always gets disappointing numbers of users. "A hippie stuck in the sky and a scientist stuck in an atom are both missing the truth." Embodying the duality, he says, makes you unstoppable. His evidence: job titles shifting from "designer" and "engineer" to "design engineer," a more complex merged skill stack; creators founding and fueling companies by pairing with programmers; coders who understand distribution and generate millions of views themselves, drastically increasing their earning potential, working on their own projects and possibly out-earning any job. AI, he says, is not directly responsible; the general technological shift is.

**Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md

In June 2026 Koe takes a further step and ranks the two. He first quotes Naval again ("code and media are permissionless leverage") and adds that a beginner does not realize how much power one person has, especially with AI used at its higher level (not just asking ChatGPT questions, and not the anger of artists at AI), and the realization that you can build almost anything. Members of his Eden community, he says, build things they always wanted without knowing they could exist, and critics complain that they did not learn to "actually build it," as if that were not building. He cites a meme in which a corporate worker asks a "vibe coder" how his "little app" is doing, and the answer is that it made $2 million that month. The point is not the status of having learned a specific skill: **skill gatekeepers** (his term) play status games about who has "really" learned something. All of this, he adds, was possible before AI; the core problem is that people do not understand what is possible "with a long enough time horizon and the five ingredients of success" (agency, taste, persuasion, persistence and iteration, developed in Part XII). AI only lets you do more, faster, and gives access to things, such as software, that once would have taken much more time or money.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

The ranking follows in the same video. Koe believes the media side (content: posts, videos, podcasts, writing published once and seen by thousands or millions) is more important, especially as more people try to do everything with AI. With content you need to know what good looks like, and that requires an education AI cannot give you, because until you have started trial and error "you don't know what to ask." "The value of content is subjective": every reader interprets each sentence differently, so there is "no one right way." The value of code, by contrast, is "relatively objective": if it works and users do not notice problems, does it matter that the code is messy? (He grants that this is debatable, since messy code may break later.) "With content, there is no works. There is no set result." Two consequences follow. First, the **skill cap** of content (the ceiling of achievable skill) will keep rising. Second, people with high content skill can put code to much more use, because they have distribution: without distribution, how do you get anything you build (code, woodworking, your life's work) into people's hands and get them to care? "That's the skill of media."

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

The positions in this subsection did not form a straight line. In 2022 Koe said one needs to understand both media and code; in 2023 he said to start with one and learn both over time, while in other videos of the same year he said "you don't need to learn to code"; in 2025 he asked for the poles to be merged; in 2026 he ranked media above code, while still building software himself with AI. The stable element across the oscillation is his reason: code becomes increasingly accessible (first through no-code tools, then through AI) and is "relatively objective," while distribution remains the bottleneck, and distribution is a function of media. The 2026 ranking is therefore the 2024 argument of Section 25.1 (AI lacks distribution and persuasion) applied to the choice of skills.

#### The permissionless launchpad

A May 2025 passage corrects a misreading of all this. The path, Koe says, is not reserved for anyone: any interest or skill can become a digital product or content that resonates with people of similar interests, and it is one of the best beginner routes to initial leverage. The mistaken objection is "everyone's going to become a creator." That is not how it works. Many large YouTubers and creators build leverage, get cash flow and then move on, and they do not need to be big creators to do it. "They use it as a launchpad, a **permissionless launchpad**, into what they want to do." Anyone can start that way and learn the skills: on social media, with a digital product business, you can learn every skill needed to make a business profitable, and then work for someone else, start an e-commerce business, build a software startup (as he did), become an author, or anything else, once you have started and have other forms of leverage to bring in.

**Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md

The launchpad idea is the leverage version of Koe's argument, in Section 25.2, that becoming a creator "guides your learning" toward the skills that make the most money. Content and digital products are not presented as a final destination but as the cheapest place to acquire distribution and business skills, from which other paths become possible.

In June 2024, introducing digital products as leverage, Koe adds two quotations from Balaji Srinivasan, a technologist and investor, without elaborating on them: "it's a digital first world, physical is now a premium product," and "physical, then digital, then native digital." He asks the viewer to think about them.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

Since Koe does not interpret the quotations, any reading of them is ours. Read in context, the first suggests that in a world where most interactions happen digitally, physical goods become a premium rather than the default; the second describes a sequence in which activities first exist physically, are then digitized, and finally appear in forms that exist only digitally. Both support the claim that the leverage of the present lies on the digital side.

#### Old leverage and new leverage

Koe's latest synthesis, in July 2026, opens with a quotation from Daniel Priestley, an entrepreneur and author on personal brands (the transcript spells the name "Daniel Presley"): "The biggest leverage is no longer capital. The biggest leverage is enterprise, the ability to run a great business, and in particular, the biggest leverage is personal brand, having a big audience. As soon as you've got enough people paying attention to you, you have this enormous form of leverage." Koe uses it to introduce what he calls "the secret to modern success": **old leverage versus new leverage**.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

The rules of wealth creation have changed, he argues. The assets of the industrial age (property, factories, machinery, physical goods) are being replaced by digital assets (audience, personal brand, intellectual property, content, data, software) that are incredibly cheap to build, more scalable, harder to tax, and that compound while you sleep. "The only requirement to building leverage for yourself in today's world is a good idea and the confidence to post it on the internet."

**Old leverage** consists of physical, tangible assets: a house, a factory, a Rolex, a fleet of trucks, a warehouse of inventory, gold, bought in the hope that they will appreciate or generate income. Such assets are linear and local: a factory produces so many units a day; a house is rented to one tenant at a time. Old leverage can be good, Koe concedes, but most beginners lack the capital for it (they have thousands in student debt; they will not own a Rolex until their business does well). Today, leverage is no longer reserved for those with capital. **New leverage** consists of intangible digital assets (audience, personal brand, intellectual property, data, systems, software), "the leverage of the newly rich: media, data and code." Content written once can be seen by millions; software built once can serve millions.

He adds an argument specific to an age of AI. Physical assets, he says, will be the first thing governments find, tax and seize if wages collapse, which he considers "more likely today than yesterday" because AI could bring it about quickly, leading governments to expand social safety nets. Governments are much less likely to seize an email list of 1,000–10,000 people, a library of content or a best-selling book. "You can sell your house for a hundred thousand dollars, but you can also just send a well-written email to your email list and make ten to a hundred thousand dollars if you know what you're doing."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

This last argument is a speculative scenario, not a prediction Koe supports with evidence, and it creates a tension he does not resolve. In 2024 he had recommended "build the digital, invest in the physical": build cheap digital assets first, then invest the proceeds in scarce physical space. The 2026 passage treats physical assets as the most exposed in a future of collapsing wages. The two positions might be reconciled as different time horizons or different risk scenarios, but Koe does not reconcile them, and Part XII registers the tension. What is unambiguous is the place of the audience in the new picture: the email list, the owned distribution of Section 25.2, is here the paradigm of new leverage, an asset that costs little to build, compounds, and is difficult to take away.

### 25.4 The Audience as an Asset of Trust

Sections 25.2 and 25.3 treated the audience mainly as distribution: potential traffic, compared with ads and outreach by reach and cost. This section turns to what Koe regards as the substance underneath the traffic. An audience is valuable not because of how many people it contains but because of what those people already believe about you. The argument proceeds from the comparison with cold outreach, through Koe's (and Devon Eriksen's) definition of an audience as trust, to the claim that "people follow people," and ends with the story of how Koe discovered all this for himself.

#### Where do the non-buyers go?

In a September 2024 beginner's guide, Koe frames the comparison with a question. You can send 10,000 cold emails and spend $10,000 on ads, "but where do the people go after that?" Those who do not buy from your ad "disappear." You can retarget them, he concedes, "but still the point stands." Building an audience on social media, by contrast, has six benefits:

1. You keep access to people, because they follow you.
2. It is free to start, so you need neither much skill nor anyone's permission.
3. Every piece of content warms your audience up to buy.
4. You can build new products and increase profitability, because the audience is something you can "resell to time and time again."
5. People follow you for you, so you are not trapped in a specific business model or skill and can pivot.
6. When the audience is large enough, you can leave client work or build the startup you have dreamed of, with customers and potential team members already present, without spending hundreds of thousands on marketing and hiring.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

The first benefit is the decisive one, and the others follow from it. An ad or a cold email is an event; an audience is a relationship that persists after the event. The fifth benefit anticipates the theme of "people follow people" developed below: if the audience is attached to the person and not to the offer, it survives changes in the offer.

A June 2022 passage, from a video on lessons from seven failed online businesses, adds the idea of scale. In person and in cold email, you are not reaching a community or a network; referrals exist, but they are limited. When you build an audience to 20,000, 30,000, 50,000 or 100,000, "that's a hundred thousand people that know exactly what you do and they can refer people to you." With funnels in place, he adds, you are in full control of your income.

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md

An audience, in this sense, is a referral network at scale: every member is a potential source of introductions, which no individual outreach effort can replicate.

#### Cold outreach builds no leverage; with content, nothing goes to waste

In a July 2023 video, Koe explains from his own experience why outreach does not accumulate. With cold emails and DMs, "nobody knew who I was, there wasn't any prior trust, I didn't have a way of showing my authority or results from the start, I had to prove myself." As one person, keeping the messages personal rather than robotic and automated meant a great deal of manual work. "Every pitch I sent either led to a customer or nothing at all. I wasn't building leverage along the way."

He then describes the contrasting situation, with social media content, warm outreach to his audience and promotions on his timeline: "I create a reputation over time. I build trust with span and depth, short form and long form content. I can build trust in my audience without having to explain myself to them. I can generate interest in my products without trying. Nothing goes to waste: all content either contributes to data failures or growth successes. As I grow larger I can evolve my brand into something I enjoy doing. I gain rather than lose freedom with time and effort."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

Three terms in this passage carry specific meanings. **Span and depth** is Koe's pairing of short-form content (which reaches many people) with long-form content (which builds trust with fewer); it is the "one organism" of Chapter 24 seen from the side of trust. **Data failures** and **growth successes** classify every piece of content: a post that fails is not wasted, because it produces data for iteration; a post that succeeds produces growth. And **warm outreach** is outreach to people who already know you, as opposed to cold outreach to strangers. The phrase "nothing goes to waste" is the core of the contrast. Each cold pitch is binary (a customer or nothing); each piece of content leaves something behind, whether reputation, trust or information.

#### The final kicker

A December 2023 passage draws a consequence for the rest of the sales apparatus. An audience trusts you more after being exposed to much of your content, so "you don't need to have the perfect conversion-centered landing page and copywriting, because people already know if they are going to buy." Your content has already raised their awareness of their problems; the landing or sales page "is just the final kicker that provides clarity."

Koe contrasts this with his own past. As a freelancer relying on cold DMs, cold emails and referrals, he was "pretty neurotic" about optimizing his website and funnel; every email felt as if it had to be maximized; "I didn't have any breathing room," because every action had so much riding on it. An audience solves this: "my marketing is done every single day without me even marketing; it's just content that educates others, that builds trust." Optimization after that is a bonus.

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

The **final kicker**, in his usage, is the landing page reduced to its proper role once trust has been built elsewhere. The point does not deny the value of good sales pages, which Koe teaches in detail in other videos (Part XI); it reorders their importance. Where the audience exists, persuasion has largely happened before the visitor arrives.

#### Testing with content instead of a furnace

The same logic applies to the testing of ideas. In June 2022, recounting the failure of an early business selling blue-light products, in which he lost about $3,000 on paid ads and inventory, Koe draws the lesson: with an audience, "you don't have to rely on shoveling money into a furnace in order to actually test things out." You test ideas through content, see what people actually want, and then create a product or service for them.

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md

In March 2024 he gives the relationship between audience and ads its metaphor. Paid ads are "more an extension of social media," with social media as "a testing ground for ideas and understanding what works so that you can amplify it with paid ads." "Paid ads are like steroids for business and building an audience is like natural bodybuilding; you have to do one before you do the other."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md

The metaphor encodes a sequence, not a prohibition. Natural bodybuilding (building the audience organically) produces the base and the information; steroids (paid ads) amplify what the base has already shown to work. As noted in Section 25.3, this is a refinement of Koe's earlier stance: in 2022 the audience was mainly a way of avoiding ads; by 2024 ads are a later stage fed by what the audience has validated.

#### Start posting: escaping the cycle of manual labor

From these comparisons Koe draws a standing recommendation. In October 2022, citing his own experience and failures, he says: "I always recommend people start posting content." His reason is that many people who start freelancing or coaching get stuck in a cycle in which they must do manual labor to get clients (cold emails, cold DMs), because they lack a brand that educates people and builds trust and authority without their having to do it manually in DMs every time. So you build an audience along the way. Once you reach a certain amount of traffic, brand loyalty and authority, clients start coming to you. As the audience grows, you productize your service, reduce the time you spend on client work and sell a digital product, or, in self-development spaces such as mindset or health coaching, even a physical product. His own example at the time was a planned coffee brand with L-theanine.

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md

In January 2023 he repeats the recommendation and calls the personal brand "the future." A personal brand lets leads come to you and gives you an authoritative profile, so that when you do send cold DMs, "it is much better than a random email showing up in someone's inbox." He does not say cold email is bad ("it's a very good strategy"), only that for most people it makes "a thousand percent sense" to start delivering value through a personal brand.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

An October 2023 video reconstructs the reasoning as a sequence of realizations from his own time as a freelancer. The problem: "Why was I doing so much manual work to land clients, that was taking me more time than the actual client work?" The chain:

1. Posting content and building a following would give him "unlimited clients at my fingertips."
2. Even without an audience of his own, if he wrote a good, persuasive post promoting his product or service, and through networking or paid growth got someone to share it with their audience of 100,000 to a million, people would click through; knowing "how to capture and guide attention," he could "generate leads 10 times faster and easier than just a manual effort."
3. Building his own audience would save him 10–20 hours a week on client acquisition.
4. A following past 10,000 or 50,000 would let him pivot out of client work altogether, saving even more time.

This, he says, is how the "4-Hour Work Day" is real: "I noticed what was taking up my time and then used the thing that is called creativity to create a solution." And he adds a jab: "you call yourself an entrepreneur but you can't spot a problem and solve it in your life."

**Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md

The passage links this chapter to Chapter 12. The four-hour workday is not, in Koe's account, a productivity technique applied to an unchanged business; it is the result of replacing a time-consuming acquisition method (outreach) with a compounding one (audience), a creative solution to a problem in his own life.

A September 2024 passage shows what outreach becomes once an audience exists. Online business tutorials usually advise cold emailing or cold DMing with a skill you barely have. The difference in Koe's approach is that you build an audience with a focus on content and "only reach out to people that engage with your content," who know you and see you as an authority, which increases your chances of success "by a lot." People trust you because you are not "a paid ad showing up trying to sell them immediately." Whom to message first: people who comment on posts relevant to your offer; people who repost or share them; people who message you first, with questions or just "hi"; and people who fill out your questionnaire.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Warm DMs** are therefore not the opposite of outreach but its transformation: the same act of contacting someone directly, aimed only at people who have already signaled interest through your content.

Finally, a 2024 passage answers the objection that keeps many beginners from posting at all. People feel like impostors posting content; Koe's example is someone who knows a lot about web design but is trying to land a first client. His rebuttal: if you are willing to reach out to someone with a freelance service in order to make money from them, why be scared of posting free content? "The free content online should come first," as a way of distilling what you have learned and organizing your thoughts so that others benefit, and of building authority to the point of landing a client.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

There is a tension between this advice and Koe's own path, which the corpus registers without resolving. He recommends posting content before having results; but he himself obtained results with freelance work, through cold outreach, before he started posting. He does not address the discrepancy, beyond presenting himself with some humility as "just a regular dude." A reasonable reading is that his own route showed him the cost of the outreach-first path, and his advice is meant to spare others that cost; but this is an interpretation, not something he states. Chapter 26 develops the beginner's situation in detail.

#### What an audience is: trust, not a follower count

The most precise definition of an audience in the corpus comes not from Koe but from Devon Eriksen, in the December 2024 interview. "An audience doesn't mean how many people follow you on Twitter." His example is an account that posts cat pictures under a name like "why you should have a cat" and has two million followers: "they're not following him, they're following the cats." "Building an audience is about trust. An audience is a set of people who trust you," so that if you release something, they will say: "well, I'm going to spend 20 hours reading this book because I trust Devon," or devote 45 minutes to a video about success and "some obscure philosophy of Marcus Aurelius" because you have created enough value for them in the past. What you are creating is "this group of people who know who you are and they trust you."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

Koe adopts this sense of **audience** (a common word used with a specific meaning): a set of people who trust you enough to give their time to what you release. The definition separates two things that follower counts confuse. The cat account has reach but no trust in a person; an audience in Eriksen's sense is measured by the time people are willing to invest on the strength of your name.

Koe's own vocabulary shifted accordingly. In October 2024, describing what a creative business needs, he says the second need is a way to attract an audience, and then pauses on the word. "The term followers has lost its punch": someone can go viral on TikTok, make a million dollars, repeat it, and have only 500 followers (the subject, he says, of a future video on why followers do not matter). He prefers "readers," but since not everyone identifies as a writer, "let's just call it an audience," because it is a first principle of business: you need people to pay you for the value you provide. "If you don't have an audience you don't have a business."

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

In June 2026 he adds a third-party motto. He is not talking about the kind of content where someone gives an AI agent access to Instagram and gains 100,000 followers overnight; that is "practically worthless unless you have authority and loyalty and trust." (His product Eden can automate posting, he notes, "but that's not what we're pushing"; you still have to learn.) He cites JK Molina's motto, "likes ain't cash": followers and likes do not matter by themselves. But he immediately qualifies it: that does not make large numbers of followers or likes useless if trust, authority and loyalty stand behind them "to any degree"; "it's not binary." "Intelligent content creation is much more than just posting rage bait."

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

How does this square with Section 25.3, where Koe measured his leverage in followers (500,000 in 2022, two million in 2023) and mocked marketers who said follower counts did not matter? The corpus records a genuine change of position. In 2022–2023 Koe treated follower count as a measure of leverage, and in one 2023 passage claimed that people do not follow accounts with few followers. From late 2024 he says that "follower count doesn't really matter anymore," that the email list is the only real measure, and that "followers has lost its punch." The reason he gives is a change in the platforms themselves: the move from chronological feeds, where followers saw your posts, to interest-graph feeds, where content is shown to whoever the algorithm predicts will be interested, regardless of whom they follow. The 2026 qualification ("it's not binary") reconciles the two positions. Follower counts still matter as a proxy, and Koe keeps using them as benchmarks, but only insofar as trust stands behind them.

A March 2023 story shows that the opposite effect, visible authority, is also real. The first thing that made the power of an audience click for Koe, he says, was being messaged by someone with 50,000 followers at a time when he himself had barely any. He thought, "Oh my God, I'm talking to this dude with 50,000 followers. No way," and he bought the man's services. You are more open, he concludes, to people who display authenticity and value online.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

The story is the personal origin of the claim, in Section 25.2, that cold outreach from a profile with an audience gets ten times the replies. Read alongside Eriksen's definition, it suggests a nuance: the follower count worked on Koe as a signal of trust that others had already extended.

#### To build trust, be trustworthy

If an audience is a set of people who trust you, how is that trust built? Eriksen's answer, in the same interview, is moral before it is tactical. "In order to build trust you have to be trustworthy, you have to act with integrity, and you have to act with these people's best interest at heart, because they are not stupid." They know when you are only interested in what is good for you, when you are trying to scam them, "when you're trying to do something that 60 other people have done better and you're just going to try to jump on the bandwagon." So you must "be trustworthy and create real value, and some of it you have to give away for free so that they sample it and they say, aha, this guy makes good stuff, I'm going to pay him because I want there to be more of this stuff that I give a [damn] about."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

Later in the conversation Koe observes that every piece of Eriksen's content, "even something that could be as short as a one-sentence tweet," shows a great deal of quality and thought, and asks whether that drives his decisions. Eriksen answers that "it goes back to the integrity thing: you can't just try to cheat people... you can't just take pictures of your food and put it on Instagram and pretend you're a success; you have to offer them real value, and I try to proceed from the assumption that people are smart, because I want an audience of smart people, because smart people have more money," the last part said laughing.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

These are Eriksen's principles, which Koe elicits and endorses rather than formulates. They add three conditions to the definition of an audience: integrity (acting in the audience's interest), real value (not imitation of what sixty others did better), and generosity (giving part of the value away so that it can be sampled). The last condition is the logic of free content that Koe's own model relies on, and "proceed from the assumption that people are smart" is a standard of quality applied even to the smallest unit of content.

#### A thousand true fans, or a few hundred

If trust matters more than numbers, how many people does one actually need? In a May 2025 video Koe calls this point "arguably the most important." Most people do not want to be famous; even if you do, save that goal until you are making a living. You do not need millions of followers, but "about a thousand true fans," and honestly fewer if you are good, because of price points: $5,000 per client for a service; $10 a month for a paid newsletter (he says he is "on the Substack kick" and finds it promising); $50 to $150 for a product; and spin-off products such as a book or software, since anyone will be able to code an app that solves a specific problem for existing buyers.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md

**Complementary context:** "1,000 True Fans" is the title of a 2008 essay by Kevin Kelly, co-founder of *Wired*, arguing that a creator can make a living from about a thousand fans who will buy anything they produce, rather than needing a mass audience. Koe uses the concept without naming its author; in the corpus Kelly is named only when a guest, Justin Welsh, cites him. Koe's adaptation adds the price-point calculation, which makes the number of fans required depend on what is sold.

In February 2025 he lowers the number further. "You don't need a lot of followers, you need a few hundred people who support your work and need what you offer" to replace your income or live a decent life; "if you want to take it further then you have the agency to do so, but you don't have to." Not everyone will gain "a million kajillion subscribers," but everyone can have enough to sustain the life they want. He adds an observation about how audiences behave: they rotate. A few hundred people follow you; after four or five months they take a break; by then a few hundred more have arrived. "My audience probably isn't 4 million plus people, it's probably maybe 100,000 people that listen to me consistently, and that's more than I need personally." "You evolve, your tribe evolves; the internet is a constant flow of attention, and if you aren't in it your creative work doesn't make it."

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md

The estimate of 100,000 consistent listeners out of more than four million followers is Koe's guess, not a measured figure, but it illustrates the difference between reach and audience in Eriksen's sense. The observation that audiences rotate adds a dynamic element: the trust relationship is not permanent, and an audience must be continually renewed, which is why "the internet is a constant flow of attention" and absence from it means one's work "doesn't make it."

In July 2026 he gives concrete cases. Beginners think it is millions of followers or nothing. Koe replaced his freelancing income at around 5,000 followers. His friend Randy, a fitness personal trainer, made well over $100,000 a month with 10,000 followers, because he knew what he was doing: he studied, invested in his education, and learned business, marketing and sales. People find $100,000 a month absurd, Koe says, but "that's on the low end for business"; they react that way because they are in the employee mindset of being paid for time rather than value, with a poor relationship with money that distorts how they perceive it. Simply replacing your income is "not far off": three, six to twelve months of focused learning, skill acquisition, showing up daily and putting your thoughts out. "People drastically overestimate how much is actually required for them to win," psych themselves out and fall into "paralysis by analysis," because all they see are out-of-touch celebrities with millions of followers, "because that's all they follow."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

A July 2023 passage, addressing the limiting belief that one cannot build an audience at all, makes the same point from the side of the network. Every person on social media follows 300 to 1,000 accounts, even creators: "more than enough to make a full-time income if you know what you're doing," because "your audience is not just your audience" (the network effect of Section 25.3). And you do not have to sell a $100 digital product: "you can sell a service for two thousand dollars and you don't even need an audience to do that," provided your profile shows that you know your stuff.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The cases of Koe at 5,000 followers and Randy at 10,000 are anecdotes, and Randy's result is attributed explicitly to his business education rather than to his audience size. That attribution is the lesson: the number of people required is a function of price and skill. With a high-priced service and a convincing profile, the required audience can approach zero; with low-priced products, it must be larger. The 2026 timeline for replacing an income ("3, 6 to 12 months") is Koe's estimate for focused effort, not a promise, and it should be compared with the more cautious benchmarks he gives elsewhere.

#### People follow people

Why do people trust a person rather than a topic? Koe's answer, developed in several formulations between 2022 and 2026, moves from novelty to perspective to the person and their worldview.

The earliest version, from May 2022, is a chain: "attention is the new currency, novelty is what grabs attention, novel perspectives are your unique way of grabbing attention." He applies it beyond entrepreneurship to everyday life: to be remembered, to stick in people's heads and to be seen as valuable, you cannot simply regurgitate what you imitated before; you must see things from a new perspective. This applies to content, to sales, to anything you put out. The process: consume according to your curiosity, connect the dots, and from your own perspective put out a fresh message "that makes sense to a group of people that want to follow you."

**Source:** Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md

In January 2025 he states the principle in a tweet: "Your audience doesn't want information; they want your perspective on information. 10 people can share the same idea; only one will say it in a way that changes minds." Sharing this opinion, he says, "helps a lot of people think things through," particularly writers and creatives who worry that "this isn't original from me."

**Source:** You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md

In June 2025 he moves the emphasis from the perspective to the person. "People don't follow ideas. They follow people who share ideas." Two people can write the same thing and be perceived very differently. If James Clear (the author of *Atomic Habits*) and a random person both tweet "habits are good for you," the reactions diverge: tens of millions of people know James Clear, his story and his book about habits, "a deep yet indirect relationship," so most will like and repost the tweet because it is his; his name brings with it other ideas they already know, through which they read the tweet. From a random person, people ignore it or reply "yeah, no shit."

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

In August 2026 he gives the fullest version, now explicitly tied to AI. For writers and creators building audiences, "people don't follow you for your content anymore. There's Google, AI, and plenty of information that can be found at the click of a button. People follow you for your point of view, your opinion, your interpretation, your personality, your story. And all of those things are downstream of the ideas that compose your worldview. The ideas you use to make sense of the world." His own example: in a conversation about the nature of reality, his mind jumps to history, integral theory and Spiral Dynamics, "because I've deemed these the most useful lens... It makes the most sense out of the other ideas I've heard that tend to fall flat."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

The four formulations are not identical, and the corpus records the slide between them. In 2024 Koe said that people follow "the content," not the profile; in 2025 that they follow perspectives on information, and then that they follow people; in 2026 that they no longer follow content at all but a point of view grounded in a worldview. The reason he gives for the latest version is that Google and AI make information available at a click. The formulations are compatible if "content" in 2024 is understood as opposed to the profile (bio, banner, picture), not to the person: what attracts is what is said, and what is said is attractive because of who is saying it and from what worldview. The lineage connects with Section 25.1 (people follow "lore" and perspective, not information) and with Chapter 19 (you are the niche). It also explains the James Clear example: the tweet is perceived through the network of ideas the reader already associates with the name.

**Complementary context:** Integral theory is the framework of the philosopher Ken Wilber, and Spiral Dynamics is a model of value systems developed by Don Beck and Christopher Cowan from the work of Clare Graves. Both are treated in Part XIV; here they matter only as examples of the "lenses" that, in Koe's account, make one person's interpretation of information distinct from another's.

#### First they care about you; your product is you

Two passages draw the commercial consequence. In March 2025, after the Devon Eriksen case of Section 25.2, Koe states: "You need to post your beliefs, opinions and teach people about your interests." People "don't care about your book or product at first, they care about you first," and then they buy because they like you. This differs from older marketing such as radio and billboards (though radio, he grants, had some personal connection). The advertising industry makes marketing feel "icky" when it does not have to be: "you can just talk about what you want and provide something valuable."

**Source:** How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md

In the December 2024 interview, Eriksen makes the same point as a warning. He sees many independent fiction authors complaining about their lack of success whose every Twitter post is "some iteration of buy my book," and none of whose posts say, in effect, "let me say something interesting right here, right now, to you, for free, to prove that I am an interesting person who can entertain you if you buy my paid products." "If you're on the internet and that's how you make your money, your product is you: your ability to be interesting, your ability to be informative, your ability to be entertaining," making something on which people spend time and "walk away saying, I liked that, that was a good use of my time, I want more of that." "There's no shortcut, there's no 'here's the six bullet points to a successful internet influencer business where you can make millions of dollars going to cafes and taking pictures of your food'": people may click on that, "but they don't care." His summary: "Oh, my books aren't selling? Write something interesting."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md

**Your product is you** is Eriksen's phrase, and it radicalizes "people follow people." The paid product is an extension of a free product that is the person's ability to be interesting, informative and entertaining; without the free one, the paid one has no market. This is the same distinction Koe drew between Eriksen's profile and those of other self-published authors.

#### Not a glorified search engine

The negative image of all this is Koe's coined term **glorified search engine**: a creator who only dispenses actionable advice on one topic, without depth or personality. In August 2023 he introduces it in a critique of niching. In business, niching down for too long or too far makes you "a glorified search engine," lacking depth and personality, which is exactly what the future requires. People have explored niche areas enough; you need not subscribe to a single path (biologist, chemist); readily available courses, content and books let you become "everything." All of this points to the creator economy: distributing value you have cultivated over time, though most people do not cultivate it.

**Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md

In July 2024: "nobody wants to follow a glorified search engine that just talks about the same thing all the time." Many creators fear branching into new interests. His test: "look at everyone you follow: are they talking about one thing? Some will say yes, but are they really, or are they talking about their opinions, their interests, their beliefs, what are they posting to their story?" A fitness creator also talks about family life and what he learned as a father. "They're talking about everything, and you think they're in one singular niche when they're not, because they're human."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

In August 2024: "Talking about one specific skill or interest won't make you more authoritative. No, people won't trust you more. At best, you'll look like a glorified search engine of actionable advice."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

A guest gives the idea its most balanced form. In the January 2025 conversation, Vitali, a co-founder of Stan, supports Koe's practice of mixing different kinds of content: if all you talk about is a single area and you position yourself as a "Book of Knowledge just about this one thing," that is "not very different from just picking up a textbook." "People follow people, in my opinion, not necessarily topics." The nuance of a personality "inspires followership": "this is my area of expertise and 70% of my content is going to be about that, but there's also some personal and there's also some observations." But Vitali adds a counterweight: stay consistent with your niche, because "people need to know what you're about" and "everyone needs to be able to describe what it is that you actually do"; "but if that's the only thing you do, then that's going to be boring." "We got to stick to it, but we got to do so much more."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

Vitali's proportion (about 70% expertise, the rest personal and observational) is his own rule of thumb, and it is more conservative than some of Koe's statements. In early 2023 Koe gave, within three weeks, two incompatible proportions: that if at most 20% of posts have nothing to do with what you sell, it will not affect your income; and that 80% of his own content did not revolve around what he sold and it did not matter. The corpus records this as an unresolved contradiction, perhaps explained by context: the first is a cautious limit for someone afraid of losing sales, the second a description of his own case, conditioned on his skill at introducing why each topic matters. Vitali's figure sits between the two. What all three share is the rejection of the glorified search engine: some part of the content must carry the person, because that is what is followed. Chapter 19 developed the theory behind this (the niche of one); Chapter 27 develops its consequences for the brand.

#### Trust is the business strategy

Koe's most general statement of this section's thesis spans four years. In May 2022 he observes that the online landscape is shifting. A few years earlier it was all funnels, sales strategies, marketing and clickbait; people have caught on. He draws a parallel with the contrast, from a previous video, between corporations and individuals: communities, unlike corporations, are made of individuals pursuing what they want under a shared hierarchy of goals. People who "double down on trust, simplicity and individuality" make more money and have more impact than those who double down on tactics such as urgency and countdown timers. He maps each term: trust comes from being authentic; simplicity from making sense to others through a novel perspective; individuality from taking responsibility for your own future. In short: "a personal brand with a personal perspective and time in the game."

**Source:** Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md

In June 2025 he compresses it further. "Forget about your niche for now. Forget about your bio and banner." They are important, he grants, but plenty of people with no bio and a blank profile picture do fine. "Your content and the quality of ideas you post over a long enough time period are what create a brand that people can't help but trust. That's your entire business strategy. Trust. Money is a measure of trust."

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

This, too, records a change. In 2022–2023 Koe treated branding as half of growth and asked whether one's profile "looks like it deserves a million followers," devoting a full day to the profile picture. By 2024–2025 the profile had become a detail of the top of the funnel that should not block the beginner, because "they're not following the profile, they're following the content." His reason is that many creatives get stuck on the bio because it is the first thing courses teach, while trust is created by the quality of ideas over time. The two positions apply to different moments: the profile matters when optimizing; at the start, it does not.

**Money is a measure of trust** is Koe's coined formula for the end point of this argument. It does not define money in general (Part XII treats Koe's broader ideas about money); it states that, for a creator, revenue tracks the trust accumulated through the quality of ideas over time.

In March 2026 he applies it to the age of AI. "In the age of AI, when content can be endless and it's going to be flooding social media, what are people going to turn to? They're going to turn to who they trust. Who is that? A personal brand who they can almost verify is a human, and they can tell whether or not they're using AI in a non-tasteful way."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

The passage closes the loop with Section 25.1. There, AI made attention and trust scarce; here, the scarce trust flows to whoever can be recognized as a human with a perspective, which is what the personal brand, in the sense of this chapter, is.

#### The Twitter epiphany: how Koe moved from outreach to inbound

The principles of this section were not deduced; Koe tells them as the conclusion of his own story, which he narrates many times with varying details. Before Twitter, he tried repeatedly to build a name for himself: first a fitness YouTube channel, then a digital art page on Instagram, a great deal of photography (still visible, he says, if you scroll to the bottom of his Instagram), then several agencies and freelancing businesses, while testing tactics for growing and selling on social media. Then he "stumbled across Twitter." He had downloaded it around 2011, thought it was "a place for like politics and half-baked memes," saw nothing he liked, and deleted the app "for like 10 years." He argues that most people still do not think of Twitter as a serious opportunity.

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md

In another telling he says he had deleted it for about eight years: he had downloaded it once at a movie theater, followed two people and thought "this app sucks." The two figures (ten years and eight) are compatible approximations, and the corpus treats them as variations in the narrative, not contradictions of substance.

The July 2023 version gives the most detail about his situation. When he logged back on, a tweet caught his eye: a man (Jose Rosado) who sold web design services, as Koe himself did (web design and funnels for service businesses), and who also posted about self-improvement, mindset and "harsh truths." At the time, Koe says, he was "conditioned into the mind that cold calls, cold emails, direct outreach was really the only way to grow" without paid ads. After years of trial and error he had quit his job and was working full time on his business, making "six, eight, ten occasionally" thousand a month, and wondering how others made $100,000 a month. He felt he could not scale beyond one person: outreach took two hours a day and client work took the rest. Seeing people land clients from their Twitter promotions made things click. He adds that personal branding and audience building are something of a necessity for his four-hour workday.

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

A January 2023 telling adds what happened next. Rosado's "harsh truth" tweet showed him that "Twitter isn't all memes... I can actually find people giving out value on Twitter." After following Rosado, more such people appeared in his feed and he was exposed to "Money Twitter," the community of accounts giving money and business advice, where people gave out incredible advice that he was "just taking left and right." Eventually it made sense to start a personal brand there.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

The October 2024 telling stresses what struck him about the medium. After months of scrolling in early 2019, he realized that people were just writing, with no images, designs or time-consuming video editing, "like sending texts to a huge public group chat." They wrote about things he already knew ("I could write that tweet"); they used their profiles to attract clients, some for web design; and they were relaxed, without the business speak and professionalism he despised, yet "still cashing out big." They were not sending cold emails or running omnichannel outreach on LinkedIn, the work that took him two to four hours a day, meaningless work he could not outsource because he was young, did not have much money and did not want a team. It clicked: people can attract a following and land clients or customers from that audience.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

The May 2026 telling gives the moment of decision. Scrolling Twitter ("I don't know why... of all places"), he saw web designers and people talking about his interests, giving "basic advice" such as "here's the top 10 skills that you need to learn if you want to quit your job." It resonated, and those people were landing web design clients and selling courses on web design and freelancing. "I can do the same thing. They're saying all of the things that I know how to say and they're doing really well. Why am I not in their position?" So he started posting.

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

He describes the result in two ways. In October 2024, introducing a video on growing from zero, he says that in the past he absolutely hated social media and did not want to be part of it; now it is one of the most meaningful and fulfilling things in his life, while most people still think it is toxic. It was "the key to my freedom": it got him out of freelance work, let him do what he wants full time, and gave him his time back. He started writing on social media because he wanted to do what he wanted; writing was not his first attempt at freedom, and before then he did not realize that writing, "the skill for English majors and academics and technical writers," was the ticket.

**Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md

In March 2025 he adds the learning dimension. "I was once a freelance web designer who failed at every business prior to that, and I started on social media because I was tired of doing cold outreach to land freelance clients." He discovered the power of writing for getting in front of more people, including on landing pages: people went to his profile, read his content and visited his website. "I didn't have to get rejected as much for reaching out to people cold; I just shared what I knew and what I was learning in web design and what I was building." Programmers and designers who share their work gain a following if it is good; if it is not, "you have direct feedback." And for him it was not only about freelance work: "every time I hit post it felt like I had learned something new."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md

The story gathers the threads of this section into a single trajectory: two to four hours a day of outreach that built no leverage; the discovery of people writing in public and receiving clients inbound; the question "why am I not in their position?"; and the result, freedom from outreach, a four-hour workday, and a public practice that doubled as learning. The final remark connects back to Devon Eriksen's argument about feedback in Section 25.2: whether the work is good or not, publishing it returns a signal. Chapter 26 follows the story further, as a case of the growth system, and Part XIII places it within Koe's whole trajectory.
