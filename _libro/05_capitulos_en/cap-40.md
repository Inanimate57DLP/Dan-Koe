## Chapter 40 — The Good Life: Holistic Development, Relationships and Spirituality

Chapter 39 described the shape of a life: happiness as resistance overcome plus connection, struggle as something to be chosen, the alternation of peace and progress, and the cycle of chapters and phases that every project and every person passes through. This final chapter turns to the contents of that life. It asks what a person should actually be building, day by day, if the aim is not only a business or an income but what Dan Koe repeatedly calls "the good life," and it follows his answer outward in widening circles: from the body, which he treats as the foundation of everything else, to the mind and the work, to the people one shares time with, to spirituality as a connection with something greater than oneself, and finally to the collective, the society of creators that he believes individuals build when they develop themselves and pass on what they learn.

Four cautions govern the presentation.

First, the list of "domains" of life is not stable in the corpus. Koe speaks of mind, body, spirit and business; of mind, body, business and relationships; of mind, body, spirit and finances; of business, health, mind and connection; and of "quadrants." Section 40.1 shows the variants side by side. The underlying claim, that the domains are interdependent and must be developed together, does not change.

Second, much of the practical material on health comes from Koe's personal routine and from guests (Dickie Bush, Justin Welsh, Sahil Bloom). Koe often says himself that he does not know the science well, that a figure is something he "read somewhere," or that he is not dogmatic. The chapter keeps those hedges. Where he gives a physiological claim without a source, it is presented as his claim, not as established fact.

Third, the material on relationships is largely Sahil Bloom's. The conversation in which it appears is a podcast with Koe as host, and most of the frameworks (the Time Billionaire, kairos time windows, the eject button, tourists and locals, the Life Dinner) belong to the guest or to sources the guest cites. The chapter marks what is Sahil's, what is Koe's response, and what Koe adds.

Fourth, the spiritual material (sections 40.4 and 40.5) is philosophy and metaphor. Koe draws on Alan Watts, Taoism, the Hindu concept of Brahman, Marcus Aurelius and non-dual teachings, and he explicitly describes philosophy and spirituality as "non-scientific psychology" grounded in direct experience. Nothing in those sections is offered as empirical evidence, and the chapter does not present it as such.

The chapter draws on almost the whole book: on identity and the default path (Parts I and II), on entropy, attention and goals (Part III), on vision, anti-vision, purpose and the game of life (Part IV), on dopamine, walking and rest (Part V), on the generalist and "you are the niche" (Part VII), on the one-person business as a vessel (Part X), and on the developmental maps of Chapter 38. Its last section closes the loop that the book opened in Chapter 1.

### 40.1 Multi-Dimensionally Jacked: Holistic Development and the Decade That Builds It

#### The term and where it comes from

The phrase that names Koe's ideal of the good life began as a tweet: "put in the physical, mental, spiritual and financial reps; become multi-dimensionally jacked." **Source:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md (2022-06). "Jacked" is gym slang for heavily muscled; the joke of the phrase is to transfer that image from the body to every other dimension of a life. Koe uses the tweet in that 2022 video to show how wide the range of his "creation pyramid" is (Chapter 7): the same structure of vision, outline, goals and next actions serves a new project for freedom and money, finding and navigating a relationship, a better life in general, or a better video.

**Multi-dimensionally jacked**, in Koe's usage, means developed in every domain of life rather than specialized in one. In a 2023 video built around the term, he traces it to a drive he always had "to max out every area of life." He saw life "as a video game where mind, body, spirit and finances were the traits to gain experience in": "I wanted to become multi-dimensionally jacked." **Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md (2023-08-30). He offers three possible reasons for the drive, which connect the ideal to the diagnosis of Part I. He questioned the default path early. He noticed how unhappy, overweight and miserable many people seemed. And he observed how people limited their own opportunities by committing to one specific path. His conclusion is the negative image that motivates the whole ideal: following what most people do leads to the life most people have, "and that isn't pretty."

In another 2023 video he gives the term a concrete progression, aimed at the self-improver who wants to turn interests into income and "be the niche" (Chapter 19) rather than stick to a static niche. Quoting "an older tweet," he lists three builds: build your mind with books, self-reflection and writing; build your body with the gym, long walks and nutrient-dense foods; build your business with skill acquisition, problem solving and self-promotion. "Becoming multi-dimensionally jacked is the path to the good life." **Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md (2023-08-08).

The video devoted to the term turns each domain into a question to set a big goal against. For the **mind**: how do you want to handle emotions and stress, and do you want the same mediocre mindset as everyone else? For the **body**: how do you want to look and feel, and how does that affect other areas, such as how others perceive you and the opportunities they throw at you? For the **spirit**: does life lack meaning, wonder or fulfillment? "Do you feel like life is happening to you or that you are flowing with life?" For **business**: how much money do you want, and why do you want it from a purposeful endeavor, "unlike ninety percent of jobs"? **Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md. The questions are deliberately open. They are not targets but prompts for the kind of vision work described in Chapter 7, applied to each domain separately so that no domain is left without a goal.

#### Why a philosophy that cannot be lived means nothing

The same video opens with an argument that explains why Koe insists on the practical domains even when he speaks as a philosopher. A philosopher may ignore practical life because he identifies as "an intellectual, not a warrior." But the main question of philosophy is "how does one live the best life"; if the philosopher cannot build a business, manage his emotions or create fulfilling relationships, "their philosophy means absolutely nothing." **Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md.

This is the same test Koe applies everywhere in the corpus: an idea is judged by whether it can be embodied. It is also the reason the chapter that follows moves without apology from strength training to the mystical. For Koe they are not two topics but two ends of one question, how to live well, and a person who can only handle one end has not answered it.

#### Holistic development: improving yourself and the impact you make

The second key term is **holistic development**. Koe introduces it in 2022 while explaining how to treat life as a project. Treating life as a project, he says, implies holistic development, because "personal development doesn't only come from improving yourself, it comes from the impact that you make on the world": what you teach other people, "in other words your business." For those who do not resonate with the word "business," think they cannot start one or are not ready, he offers substitutes: "your life's work or your calling or just however you plan to leave the world better than you left it." He calls this "the polarity between selfishness and selflessness": first improving yourself, then helping others improve. **Source:** Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md (2022-11-27). The image he attaches to it is climbing a mountain while carving out a trail behind you for others.

"Holistic," here, has two senses that should be kept apart. One is breadth within the person: mind, body, spirit and work developed together. The other is the movement from self to others: personal development is incomplete until it becomes a contribution. The second sense is what section 40.6 develops into the idea of raising the collective consciousness; the first is what the rest of this section develops.

Koe gives a negative image of what happens when the first sense is ignored. If you pursue goals but neglect the inner work required to reach the next ones, "you just end up highly superficial, like a businessman with no health or a bodybuilder with no philosophy or a pickup artist with no values." "The art of self-architecture demands holistic development." **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md (2023-05-19). Each of the three figures has maximized one dimension and is hollow in another, and the hollowness is visible to everyone except the person.

He also uses holism to justify one of his most recognizable practices, the short workday of Chapter 12. People wonder why he works three to four hours a day on average; he wonders why they don't, if they value self-actualization and reaching the next level of their life. "There's a glaring problem with the overworker's 14-hour-a-day lifestyle: overwork is the antagonist of holistic development, and holism is the catalyst to exponential growth towards self-transcendence." **Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md (2023-06-18). The argument is structural rather than moral. Fourteen hours of work leave no hours for the body, for study, for relationships or for contemplation, so the overworker develops one branch of the tree and stunts the rest. The phrase "exponential growth" anticipates the next idea: because the domains feed each other, progress in all of them compounds in a way that progress in one cannot.

#### The skill tree: a web of problems that depend on each other

The image Koe uses most often for the interdependence of the domains comes from video games. In that same 2023 video on the four-hour workday, he asks the viewer to think of a talent tree or skill tree, and shows a graphic with Mind, Body, Spirit and Business as columns and levels running up the side. (The exact layout of the graphic is not reconstructible from the transcript.) Development is not linear across domains: they form "a web of problems that are dependent on each other to be solved." To reach level four of business, you will need level four or more of mind, body or spirit. **Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md.

He then turns the argument against a specific audience: spiritual people who think business is not a necessary path to self-actualization "will fail without realizing it," because they neglect their financial development and the development of their life's work, "which is the vessel for creation, your divine gift." Business, in this passage, is "the vessel for contributing to humanity, which is something greater than you, that is bridged by spirit; it is fulfillment." **Source:** same. The sentence is dense, and its logic is worth unfolding. Spirit, for Koe, is connection to something greater than yourself (section 40.4). Contribution to humanity is one form of that greater thing. Business is the practical channel through which contribution happens at scale. So the spiritual person who refuses business has cut the channel through which his spirituality could reach anyone.

A 2024 version gives the domains as four "meaningful goals" and adds the reason they are meaningful. "There are only a few goals that can truly be meaningful; they correspond to the eternal problems that can't be solved by AI, prescriptions or technology":

| Domain | What it means in this version |
|---|---|
| Business | Meaningful work that lets you choose the problems you solve |
| Health | "You live in your body; it is your obligation to understand and improve it" |
| Mind | Pursuing curiosity, acquiring knowledge, expanding your sense of self |
| Connection | Creating shared experiences and expanding your circle of concern to transcend the self |

For connection he uses the game image of "partying up": "multiple minds achieve goals faster than one." These domains "are like a skill tree": improving the relevant skills unlocks new levels. But there is a catch: "you can only improve one trait as much as you improve another." You reach a certain level of mind only by reaching a certain level in creative work, and a certain level of health only through a certain level of mind, since mental and physical health are intertwined. He closes with a question: "If you aren't building your mind, body, spirit and business every single day, what are you doing?" **Source:** Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md (2024-07-21). Note that in this one passage the list of domains appears in two forms, business/health/mind/connection in the framework and mind/body/spirit/business in the closing question.

The term **eternal problems** in this passage is a close relative of the "eternal markets" of Chapters 19 and 28 (health, wealth, relationships and happiness): the same domains seen as problems that never go away rather than as markets that never saturate. The skill-tree image itself is developed in Chapter 10, which shows that Koe uses it in two directions, against the spiritual person who neglects money (2023) and against the business person who neglects mind, body and spirit (2025).

#### Solving one problem reveals the next

The 2025 version of the argument is the most precise about mechanism. Koe now speaks of "quadrants" and addresses the person for whom one quadrant, a nine-to-five job for instance, exhausts all focus and energy. "The ultimate goal is to create a lifestyle where all quadrants are accounted for through problem-solving." If your vocation consumes so much time that your mind is distraught, you are too drained for your body and cannot "entertain" your spirit, "that is an obvious problem that you must attempt to solve through knowledge and skill acquisition, creating the chance for a channel or flow state or season of progress." His example: study business opportunities for an hour every day and make some time for practice. It may take longer than it takes others, but once you move through the phases you feel more confident that you can reduce or eliminate the job that is taking your time. **Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md (2025-10-19).

Then comes the important observation. As you solve a problem that was taking from the other quadrants, "that shift may make another problem more apparent." If you are starting a business but your energy is still low, it is hard to land customers, write well or have great ideas. "Reaching the next level of business may not require more development in business, but in solving problems related to the body, mind, or spirit." And the reverse holds: even with an incredible business and all the money in the world, "you can have a hole in the middle of your chest due to a lack of meaning in your vocational pursuits." By the nature of problem-solving, and by staying vigilant of the distractions that keep you from noticing problems, "you create a lifestyle where work becomes play, health is your default state, meaning is abundant, and your mind is on your side." **Source:** same.

This is the skill tree restated as a sequence of bottlenecks. At any moment one domain is the binding constraint on the others; removing it does not end the work but moves the constraint somewhere else, often to a domain you were not looking at. Chapter 38 gives the developmental version of the same idea: the business owner whose plateau is caused by low emotional intelligence cannot see the problem from inside the business.

#### Standards cascade across domains

A 2023 argument adds a further mechanism: standards. What counts as a problem depends on your standards. "If you're okay with ten thousand dollars in your bank account, then you're not gonna see anything less than ten thousand dollars as a problem." Koe applies this to a familiar complaint. If you cannot afford quality food and complain that wealthy or healthy people are privileged, "it's because your standards across the board for your life are" very low, including your standards for your own work ethic and business. The mistake is to isolate health and affording food as separate problems when they work together. He therefore recommends **holistic self-improvement**: health, wealth, relationships, mind, body, spirit and business "all play into each other and come up together." To be healthy you may have to fix your finances; if you are doing nothing to raise your status at work, start a business or budget (there are "millions of different techniques to increase your income"), you have no room to complain. Both affect the relationships you can access: with low standards for food, health, business and finances, your relationships lower to that standard, and "the entirety of your life is going to suck." **Source:** Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md (2023-07-11).

The tone is harsh, but the logic is consistent with the rest of the section. Standards are the threshold at which a situation registers as a problem. Low standards in one domain do not stay in that domain; they set the level at which every other domain is tolerated.

#### The holistic goal: zoom out before you solve

The practical consequence of interdependence is a rule about how to set goals. In a 2023 video on behavior change, Koe gives as step one: "align your future with a holistic goal." He is explicit that this is not "a super scientific approach to habit formation," but "the only real way to make behavior change seamless with time." "Holistic" means "all-encompassing," viewing from the whole rather than a part, "big picture." "This requires you to zoom out and see beyond a narrow problem." If you have money problems, money is not the only domain that needs improvement, and "money is interconnected to all other domains of your life, so solving your money problems may not actually happen until you choose to zoom out and change every single domain of your life," which means "changing who you are on a fundamental level." **Source:** The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md (2023-11-19).

This connects the holistic ideal to the theory of identity in Part II. A narrow goal can be pursued by the existing identity; a holistic goal requires a different one, which is why it changes behavior more durably.

#### The four pillars of the good life

In the same video Koe gives the ideal its most operational form, which he calls **the four pillars of the good life**. The pillars are "how we reverse entropy," the downward spiral into chaos, overwhelm and anxiety (Chapter 5). "Becoming multi-dimensionally jacked is the path to the good life," and the path is built from habits: "You need four habits: one that builds your mind, one that builds your body, one that builds your business, one that builds your relationships." "The good life is the process of becoming everything you could be." **Source:** The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md.

He gives three reasons for choosing these four. They "are interconnected systems that create who you are and reverse entropy in the mind; tiny improvements are what create an ordered mind." They are "the main things within your control." And they coincide with "the eternal markets in business": health, wealth, relationships and happiness, "where most burning problems lie," and therefore the most profitable problems to solve. Solve your own problems in these domains and sell the solution: "that's value exchange." **Source:** same. The coincidence is not accidental in Koe's system. The domains in which you must develop yourself are the same domains in which other people need help, so the one-person business of Part X is, at bottom, the four pillars turned outward.

"By adopting a daily habit for each pillar of your life, success becomes inevitable." He closes with the question that recurs in 2024: "If you aren't building your mind, body, business and relationships every single day, what are you doing? Is everything you do now a distraction?" **Source:** same.

#### How the list of domains changes

The four pillars of 2023 replace "spirit" with "relationships"; the 2024 skill tree uses "connection"; the 2025 version speaks of "quadrants." The changes are not explained in the corpus. The table gathers the main versions.

| Date | Domains named | Source video |
|---|---|---|
| 2022-06 | Physical, mental, spiritual, financial ("reps") | How To Create A Better Life For Yourself |
| 2023-04 | Body, mind, business (three steps for the twenties) | Your 20s Are Meant To Build |
| 2023-06 | Mind, body, spirit, business (skill tree) | The 4 Hour Workday |
| 2023-08 | Mind, body, business (progression); mind, body, spirit, finances (traits); mind, body, spirit, business (big goals) | You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much; The Cure To A Mediocre Life |
| 2023-11 | Mind, body, business, relationships (four pillars), identified with the eternal markets | The Daily Routine That Changed My Life |
| 2024-07 | Business, health, mind, connection, and "mind, body, spirit and business" in the same passage | Life Is A Video Game |
| 2025-10 | "Quadrants": vocation, body, mind, spirit | A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life |

The most reasonable reading is that the lists are equivalent cuts of the same idea, chosen to fit the context: three pillars when the topic is a young person's plan of action, four when the topic is a daily routine or a general vision. "Business" replaces "finances" because Koe's view is that finances in the modern world come through problem-solving work. Relationships sometimes appear as a domain of their own and sometimes sit inside "spirit" or "connection," which fits his definition of spirituality as connection with something greater than oneself (section 40.4). What does not vary is the claim that the domains rise and fall together.

#### Koe's own definition of success

Koe connects the holistic ideal with a modest definition of success. He was a rebel as a teenager (he assumes his viewers were too) and noticed the opportunity of working for himself early. If he can do something he enjoys and control how much he works, that is success. "I don't need to make billions upon billions of dollars." He wants a good amount of money, control of his day, work he chooses, enjoyment of life, and attention to his health and relationships. What he does not want is to become "over-specialized" in one area, such as business, to the detriment of his health. **Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02). This is a personal statement, not a prescription, but it shows that "multi-dimensionally jacked" is, for him, an alternative to maximization within one domain.

#### Don't waste your twenties: the 2023 argument

The domains are interdependent, but they are not developed in an arbitrary order, and the period of life Koe singles out for building them is the twenties. His first full treatment comes in an April 2023 video, made when he was 26, and it was reused nearly word for word in a 2024 compilation. **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md (2023-04-20); Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md (2024-03-21).

The thesis: "The harsh reality is that how you spend your 20s will make or break your future. Of course there are outliers, but for the vast majority of people this holds true." The diagnosis: "Dopamine and modern comforts have become so normal that 99% of the population is docile, just way too comfortable and heavily risk averse." The promise: a solution that is "simple," "three steps to future proofing yourself," which he later qualifies as "simple but not easy." **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build.

Why the urgency? "By the time you turn 30, life hits you like a truck." He gives a chain of seven reasons:

1. "Your time dries up."
2. "Your habits compound; they're much harder to break."
3. "If you didn't take care of your health, it will catch up to you and you will lose more time."
4. "If you didn't start a business, you need more effort to get out of a job; it will take longer to get out."
5. "Your mental peak starts to decline in your 30s." (He offers no source for this claim in the video.)
6. "You don't have the knowledge and experience that comes from forging your own path," which, he says, people miss a lot.
7. Commitments: "a house, spouse, work and possibly children."

He then limits the claim. "This isn't the case for everyone." If you are in your thirties, "don't let this discourage you... face reality: you will have to put in more effort for a longer period of time than a 20-year-old would have to. It is what it is. Start doing it now." **Source:** same. An earlier video puts the same point as a comparison: it depends on how soon you start. Someone who starts at 16, realizing that they can do more with their life, will reach their ideal life much more quickly than someone who starts at about 30, even if they fall off here and there along the way. **Source:** How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md (2023-01-12).

Koe is also candid about the limits of his own vantage point. "I'm 26, I can only speak so much here... I'm not 30 yet, so why am I even talking about this." He can attest that "people have told me that I am more wise than 50-year-olds or other people that don't have the experience," and he explains why this might be: "how much can you actually learn and know if you just do the same thing every single day for 50 years of your life?" **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build. The claim behind the anecdote is that wisdom tracks the variety of experience that comes from forging your own path, not age as such. It is an interpretation of a compliment, not evidence, and he presents it with that self-awareness.

#### The three traps of being young

Why do so many people waste the decade? "Being young presents the most traps," and Koe names three.

The first is **social conditioning**: "you may not even realize the trap that you've dug yourself into by the time you're 25, 26, 27." You may never have encountered anything, a video for instance, "trying to make you aware of the life you have not lived yet, your life." This is the Matrix and the default path of Part I, seen from the inside of a particular decade.

The second is **dopamine-laden entertainment**: "the stuff that we naturally gravitate towards. When you're bored, where does your attention go? It goes to your phone. You open up Instagram, you don't even know it, five minutes later you're just scrolling and you're watching some random reel." (The transcript renders the phrase as "dopamine Latin entertainment"; "dopamine-laden" is the obvious reading.)

The third is **modern comforts**: "video games, Netflix and just other things that cross over with the dopamine cheap entertainment."

The shared consequence is that these traps "make people not want to branch into the unknown and discover new things and find new opportunities for themselves and become aware of their potential." "The solution is simple but not easy": build your body, build your mind, build your business. **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md.

The traps map onto the mechanisms of the earlier parts of the book: conditioning is the programmed mind of Chapter 1, cheap entertainment is the dopamine economy of Chapter 11, and modern comforts are the environment that makes the unknown unattractive. What the twenties add is that the cost of these traps compounds before you notice it.

In 2025 Koe returns to the second trap with a sharper metaphor. Most people "treat their youth as a currency instead of investment capital": they party, smoke, watch Netflix and lounge around. These are not necessarily bad, or the end of the world, but they destroy the benefits of being young, which are time, energy and focus. He is not telling you to stop partying or smoking weed entirely, but "it's wise to get your taste quickly" so that you recognize them as mistakes. **Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md (2025-07-27). The difference between currency and capital is the difference between spending a resource on present consumption and investing it so that it produces more. Youth, in this image, is a lump sum that can be either.

#### Body, mind, business, in that order

The solution has an order, and the order is part of the argument.

1. **Build your body.** First, because "you can start right now," and because its benefits (clear thinking, a strong mindset, more physical energy) transfer to the other two.
2. **Build your mind.** Raise your "level of mind," which Koe here equates with perspective, through study and by using the mind to make progress in reality.
3. **Build your business.** Channel your interests, skills and expertise to impact others at scale through a one-person business.

The video closes with the formula restated: "Don't waste your 20s: build your body, build your mind, build your business." **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build.

Why the body first? "We start with building the body because you can start right now. You don't have to wait until 'oh, I have this certain skill set in order to make income from my business.' No, you go to the gym right now." And "when you prioritize your body, your mind and finances follow, because you're improving the vessel from which those things come." **Source:** same. The **vessel** is one of Koe's most overloaded words (the business, the brand and the newsletter are all called vessels elsewhere in the book). In this passage it has its most literal meaning: the body as the container from which thinking and earning come. Chapter 9 uses the same sequence as an example of dependency between goals.

Koe adds an argument from experience. "Most successful people that I've talked to have always started with some form of exercise." His own case: "I started in the gym; that was my first obsession. I did it for vanity reasons. I wanted to get jacked, I wanted to look good so I could get women." This, he says, is what "almost every single, at least, dude that I know" who is now a successful businessperson started with. The mechanism he proposes is what the gym teaches: "The gym specifically teaches you that you have to put effort into reality in order to get something out. It teaches you that you are not entitled to [expletive] anything." In the 2024 compilation he adds a link to his theory of dopamine: "the gym is literally the opposite of cheap dopamine; it is meaningful dopamine that you see through progress, effort, hard work." **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build; Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. **Meaningful dopamine**, in Koe's vocabulary (Chapter 11), is the reward that comes from visible progress earned through effort, as opposed to the cheap dopamine of scrolling and comforts. The gym is the purest training ground for it, because the relationship between effort and result is direct and impossible to fake.

The argument about appearance is the most likely to provoke. "Looks matter, whether you think it's fair or not." He concedes that some people are "less fortunate... genetic outliers" ("do what you can"), but for those able to improve their looks "to the absolute maximum, why are you not doing that?" His analogy is to business: "Grabbing attention is the first step to being able to display your value. So you have to catch attention with content in order to convert into money. In the real world you have to catch attention with your looks, your demeanor, how you present yourself to the world, and then that's when they discover who you are and your skill set." If you don't look good, "you're taking a big chunk away from what can introduce you to new opportunities." He adds that people respect you more when you have put effort into your body "regardless of if you're still objectively ugly," because they recognize the effort, and that "you attract more attention from high-level people." **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build. The analogy ties the body to the attention economy of Part IX: appearance is to a person in a room what a hook is to a piece of content.

For those who are no longer young, the advice is not withdrawn but resized. "This is why young people are blessed: they don't have so many responsibilities yet; they can explore the world, they can do things, they can build for a number of hours a day the future that they want." But "it's not like you're doomed; it's just that you have to set aside one hour for building your future and actually build it every single day." **Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md (2024-03-10).

#### Foundations built early: Dickie Bush's preseason

A conversation with Dickie Bush in 2023 supplies an economic argument for building early that Koe does not make in these terms himself. Dickie says that everything he is doing now builds the foundation "to be a world-class father in the next 10 years." He focuses on two foundations, health and finances, because both "only get more difficult to build... as you get older," and because the "stored energy" in them makes them "more passive over time" once efficient systems are in place.

His core argument: "maintenance is the same level of difficulty wherever you're at." A billionaire and someone with $50 in the bank face the same difficulty in maintaining what they have (put it in a bank at 3%), but the billionaire's stored energy yields far more. Someone in ridiculous shape and someone in horrible shape face the same difficulty in maintaining their current state (keep doing what got you there). "The amount of effort required to get to that passive state is way more than most people think, and the level to maintain it is way less." With those foundations set, he can move on to relationships (half-joking: "an army of 10 children plus a giant ranch in Argentina"). Entrepreneurial primes come in the late thirties or early forties, so for him the present is "the preseason." He does not know where he will be at 65 or 35; at 30 he expects to be doing the same things at a higher level. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03-16).

The argument is Dickie's, but it explains why Koe's "life hits you like a truck" is not only about lost time. The cost of reaching a level is front-loaded and the cost of holding it is flat; whoever pays the front-loaded cost earlier enjoys the cheap maintenance for longer.

#### The 2025 reframing: the twenties as the tutorial phase

At 28, Koe returned to the subject with a different emphasis. He announced that "the nuance of my normal videos" was switched off in favor of brutal honesty aimed at a specific type of person, and he observed that those who get angry at such videos often have terrible lives and have not grown past 20 or 25. **Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md (2025-07-27).

The central formula is a paradox: "the best way to make the most of your 20s is to make them your worst." There are only two options: use your twenties to become the person you were meant to be, or "repeat the same 6 months for the rest of your life." If you do not nearly max out your potential in your twenties, look at society to see where your life will end up: stuck in a loop of anxiety and overwhelm, trying to relive past peak experiences. **Source:** same.

He explains the loop through **peak experiences**. At twenty you are old enough to try new things, so you get the best peak experiences of your life so far: the bars, the raves, the video games. What you have not realized is that "those should be and are the lowest peaks of your entire life." People register them as "the good days" and try to repeat them, and when they are not repeating them they crave them, because "they want the euphoria that they associate with happiness, when happiness is not euphoria." The result is that life and routine fill with extremely shallow activities that do not lead to a good life; eventually they become your identity and second nature, you shut your mind off, and escape becomes very hard. "Most people plateau around the age of 23." **Source:** same. The phrase "happiness is not euphoria" connects directly with Chapter 39, where happiness is defined as resistance overcome plus connection, and where Koe's 2025 definition of happiness as calm is contrasted with the Western notion of euphoria.

The metaphor that names the video is the **tutorial phase**: "your 20s are the tutorial phase. They're not the main game." They are the preparation, not the main event. He offers three traps to avoid and three things to do (of which the corpus excerpt preserves the trap of treating youth as currency, discussed above), and admits that he was himself the person stuck repeating six months at a time; his life got substantially better after he decided to stop. His aim is to shift beliefs so that you notice new opportunities, "not in a self-improvement dopamine junkie kind of way." And the promise changes: if you set yourself up in your twenties, quality of life only increases, "even when your youth is no longer on your side." **Source:** same.

The shift from 2023 to 2025 is a change of emphasis rather than of position. In 2023 the frame is loss: what you will no longer be able to do at thirty. In 2025 the frame is preparation: the twenties are a tutorial for a main game that keeps improving with age. Both versions can hold at once, and Koe gives his own reason for the newer framing (he was stuck, and stopping made his life better). The tutorial image belongs to the larger metaphor of life as a video game (Chapter 10).

A short 2026 passage adds a warning about the social environment of the decade. "At some point, usually in your 20s, you'll notice that the people around you stop believing in themselves. And no matter how hard you try, you can't save them. By all means, do not let it infect your mind. Stay on your path." Koe adds that the more he mulled over this thought, the deeper the problem went. **Source:** How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md (2026-05-02). The passage is an opinion and a piece of advice, not an argument; it connects with the anti-role models of Chapter 2 and with the Pygmalion effect discussed in section 40.3.

#### The limits of the advice

Koe marks the boundary of his advice explicitly in a 2025 video on the future of work. "You're probably going to have to change your life, and that's a very good thing." But he is in no position to tell people how to live, and he does not understand most situations: "I don't have kids to feed yet. I'm not working two jobs. I'm just a 28-year-old dude who likes to go on walks and write and eat dinner out." People with very busy lives can still learn, use some time, make habitual changes for more energy and slowly improve their position, but "I don't know how to help you. So, this next section isn't really for you." His advice is for the majority who have a few hours to spare, are somewhat financially stable and are generally comfortable. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md (2025-05-25).

This boundary matters for the whole chapter. The prescriptions that follow (hours of walking, daily training, monthly relationship rituals, daily meditation) assume a margin of time and stability. Koe's position is that most people have more of that margin than they use; he does not claim that everyone has it.

### 40.2 The Body as the Foundation

If the body is the vessel from which mind and finances come, then the practices that build it are not a side topic. Koe's body practice rests on two habits, resistance training and walking, supported by a deliberately simple approach to nutrition and an equally deliberate suspicion of health neurosis. This section takes them in that order. One general caution applies throughout: Koe repeatedly describes himself as non-dogmatic and experimental about health, and several of the physiological claims below are flagged by him as impressions or things he has read.

#### Resistance training: the downward spiral and the case for lifting

The most direct statement of why the body comes first is a confession. "When I look and feel terrible, I don't want to do anything with my life. I want to hide from the world. I don't feel confident in my actions. So, I stop caring, let myself go. I binge eat. I have zero discipline and simply feel worse." **Source:** A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md (2025-10-26). The passage describes a feedback loop in which physical state, confidence and discipline drag each other down. It is the same structure as the entropy spiral of Chapter 5, located in the body.

From this Koe draws a prescription: "every single person should do some form of resistance training. Cardio's great, but so is a bit of muscle mass." He likes the gym and walking, and in 2025 he had recently added 20 minutes of cardio first thing after waking. But he argues against making cardio the main form of exercise. "Frankly, nobody wants to look like a runner": the runners who look great built their physique by lifting first, and "it's okay to not lie to yourself." "Men, you want broad shoulders and a V-taper. Women, you want the hourglass figure. It's a survival mechanism," and both are created only through resistance training. Calisthenics or at-home workouts work, but "the most optimal way, and arguably the most enjoyable way, is to just go to the gym." And a reassurance aimed at a common fear: "Ladies, you will never get too bulky. Men have been trying to do that their entire lives." **Source:** same. The claim that these shapes are a "survival mechanism" is Koe's evolutionary interpretation of attraction, stated without a source.

In a 2025 video he opens his "one workout" habit with a line he attributes to Socrates, paraphrased: no man has the right to be an amateur in the matter of physical training; it is a shame for a man to grow old without seeing the beauty and strength of which his body is capable. **Source:** You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md (2025-06-29).

**Complementary context:** The line is widely attributed to Socrates; it derives loosely from a conversation reported by Xenophon in the *Memorabilia* (book 3, chapter 12), in which Socrates urges a young man who neglects his body to train. The popular wording is a modern paraphrase rather than a direct translation.

Koe's own training, as shown in a 2024 day-in-the-life video, is frequent and short. His day up to that point consists of his writing routine, breakfast, a walk with an audiobook, a call with "my book guy" about the hardcover edition of his book, more work, a sunlit walk, a noon mastermind call with students, and then the gym at about 1:30. "I do train almost every day. I like going to the gym every day to keep a routine," with occasional rest days. "I train four to six sets per body part per workout and I do each body part twice a week, so that's 8 to 12 sets per week, which is on the lower end of optimal volume, but I go to failure as much as humanly possible." Workouts last "usually like 45-ish minutes, because since I go every day my volume is split across those days." **Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md (2024-04-02). The logic of the design is worth noticing. Daily attendance serves routine as much as physiology; the volume per session is kept low so that daily attendance is sustainable; intensity (training to failure) compensates for the modest volume.

His relationship with the gym is old. He has loved it since about age 15, and it was his first obsession. **Source:** The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md (2024-06-16). (Other videos place the start of his gym habit after his first breakup or in the eighth grade; the variations are narrative rather than substantive.)

#### Walking: from "the definition of lazy" to "the king of walking"

The second habit has a more surprising history, because Koe used to hate it. He despised walking anywhere, not just intentional strolls. He was "the definition of lazy," though he prefers to think of himself as "efficient over lazy." When younger, outside the gym he would watch YouTube, work on side businesses, or sit on the couch playing video games (World of Warcraft, Call of Duty, League of Legends, Teamfight Tactics), doing the bare minimum. He would play a match of League or TFT between business tasks: wake up, work, play a match, work again; he only worked because he was already at his computer. He has not played a full video game in a few years; now he walks in the sun. **Source:** The Power Of Walking.

The story of how this changed is told in three videos, and its lesson is a technique he calls **stacking whys**. In 2022 he explains that his then girlfriend wanted to go on night walks and he would refuse or hate every second. The reason was that he "didn't have whys stacked behind it": "I didn't like going on a walk because I didn't understand why it benefited my life." He was not, in his words, "selfish with why I was going on walks." A few years earlier, during a cut (he lifts, bulks and cuts), he did not want to do cardio, so he started walking 10,000 steps a day; then he began listening to audiobooks, noting ideas, writing articles, researching the benefits, and conditioning himself to like walking. Now, if he is under 15,000 steps a day, say at 10,000 at night, he gets up and walks before bed. **Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md (2022-11-13).

The 2023 version lists the stacked reasons one by one, and it is the clearest reconstruction of the process:

1. **Weight.** He had bulked, got too heavy and wanted to cut, but disliked cardio. He read or saw online "if you want to lose weight, just pick up a walking habit." That was reason one; he started walking around his apartment complex.
2. **Calls.** He can take calls on a walk, so business can happen on a walk.
3. **Writing.** He can write content on a walk by looking at his phone.
4. **Learning.** He listens to audiobooks and YouTube lectures, which is how he gets through so many.
5. **Ideas.** Walking is "a creativity block": "it's where I'm just blocked out; it's not productivity where I'm actually doing the work, it's creativity where I'm generating ideas."
6. **Breaks.** He works in blocks of about 60 minutes and walks between them.
7. **Errands and boredom.** When bored, lacking creativity or needing groceries, he walks (20 minutes to Whole Foods and back; to the coffee shop).
8. **Where he lives.** The habit now shapes his choices: he will never live in a non-walkable city. He contrasts this with "trad red pill" men who want to live off-grid on a farm. It sounds nice, but he will not drive 30 to 60 minutes to the grocery store or to have contact with humans.

At the time he walked "like 20 to 25k steps a day" and showed a monthly average of 18,500. His point: actions shape your future decisions, even something as simple as walking, and the same holds for every habit in his lifestyle. **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md (2023-05-19).

The technique is general. A habit that has one reason behind it is fragile, because the reason can be argued away on a bad day. A habit with eight reasons behind it is overdetermined: it serves so many goals that skipping it costs something in every domain. And once a habit is overdetermined, it begins to make decisions on your behalf, as the choice of city shows.

The 2024 video on walking gives the numbers as they stood then. After a month of the "glorified 10,000 steps" (10- to 15-minute walks around his apartment complex), he had fallen in love with walking. "For 5 years there has never been a time where I have walked under 10,000 steps a day." He started at 10,000, increased to 15,000, averaged 20,000 to 25,000 while living in Austin, Texas, outside all day, and was then at about 15,000. His name became so associated with walking that a podcast with Danny Miranda in Texas was titled "the king of walking." **Source:** The Power Of Walking. (Later in the same video he says "four years in," so the duration is either four or five years.) In 2023 he described the habit more casually: "I walk like 20,000 steps a day, sometimes less, sometimes more... If I'm bored, go on a walk; if I'm not bored, go on a walk; if I have nothing to do, go on a walk; if I need to take a business call, if I need to write an article, go on a walk, pull out my phone. I literally write my newsletters on my phone." He adds that the habit is not instant: "this is going to take time; we're forming a foundation here for life." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md (2023-02-26).

On those walks, he says in 2024, "I like to listen to audiobooks and YouTube lectures and just educate myself on things that I want to learn that can fuel my work or just make me a smarter individual," which he finds "a lot more beneficial than not really doing anything or always working all the time." At that time he was listening to *The Way of the Superior Man* "once again; I listen to it often, maybe once a year," and he recommends it "for anyone looking to live a life of purpose." **Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe.

**Complementary context:** *The Way of the Superior Man* (1997) is a book by David Deida on masculine purpose, relationships and spiritual practice. Koe does not name the author in this passage.

#### Why walk: the holistic habit

Koe calls walking "the ultimate **holistic habit**": holistic in the sense of section 40.1, covering mind, body, spirit and business (business being exchangeable for work or life's work). An old tweet of his that became popular says that "long walks are the secret to a lean body and mind." Without walks, he says, his ideas, content, body and identity would not be the same, and you would probably not be watching him. **Source:** The Power Of Walking. The 2024 video aims to give "multiple whys" to keep the habit and to turn walking into a game.

It opens with a rule: if you are bored, go on a walk; if stressed, go on a walk; if uninspired, go on a walk; if overweight, go on a walk. "Most if not all of your problems can be relieved, if not solved, by going on a walk." He attributes his writing and creator success "to one thing," walking, and he walks even in Arizona heat of 100°F and rising. **Source:** same. The rule is a heuristic, not a claim that walking cures everything; the hedge "relieved, if not solved" is his.

Walking, he says, has become a **cornerstone habit** of his lifestyle, and "lifestyle trumps almost everything, even diet, I would argue, and certain work hacks." A healthy lifestyle covers so many bases that it sits at the top of what he calls the health pyramid. He does not worry about being overweight or unhealthy, or about lacking creative ideas for businesses and income, because walking is a pillar of his lifestyle. **Source:** same.

He adds historical company: he discovered that some of the greatest creatives, such as Charles Darwin and Steve Jobs, considered walks "a massively important part of their day," and he discovered the same thing himself. **Source:** same. (Darwin's daily walks on his "sandwalk" are a recurring example in Chapter 13.)

The physiological reasons appear in two places. In 2024 he says that walking increases calorie expenditure, and that he read somewhere (he says he does not know how true it is) that walking and running the same distance burn about the same calories, though walking takes longer. He would rather go on a 45-minute walk than a 15- or 20-minute run, because on the walk he can meditate, listen to a podcast or audiobook, jot down ideas, or write newsletters, books or outlines. If you struggle to lose weight or want sunlight, walking helps, and you need not worry about "not exercising today"; it is a good first step. **Source:** same.

In 2025 he corrects the most famous number in the subject. "10,000 steps a day was actually a marketing gimmick to help sell pedometers. The sweet spot for walking is actually 7,000 to 8,000 steps. That's where most of the benefits are received. 10,000 steps just sounds nicer." His reasons for walking more are listed together: for most untrained people it is low-intensity cardio; it improves digestion, blood pressure and insulin sensitivity, which helps you get and stay lean; it gives you sunlight "to unfuck your circadian health"; it gives you undistracted time to learn from an audiobook or lecture; it lets you resolve mental turmoil and reflect on your life; and it generates ideas for your deep work sessions. "The 10,000 steps is to just get you moving." **Source:** A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md.

**Complementary context:** The 10,000-step figure is usually traced to the marketing of a Japanese pedometer in the 1960s whose name translates roughly as "10,000-step meter." Koe's statement that benefits plateau around 7,000 to 8,000 steps matches the general direction of recent research summaries, but he does not cite a study.

#### Walking as a creativity block

The use of walking that matters most to Koe's work is cognitive. He contrasts two kinds of scheduled time. "We all have productivity blocks" at the computer, working with the ideas generated during the week. But few people sit and read or listen to long-form content, so "the depth in their lives is very little," and they miss much of life. "To me, walking is a **creativity block**": getting outside for 15, 30 or 45 minutes of dedicated, zero-distraction time, provided you stay off your phone or use it well (a podcast longer than five minutes; an audiobook finished over a month of walks). **Source:** The Power Of Walking. In that video he asks his editor to show brain activity while sitting versus while walking as an argument; the visual is not reconstructible from the transcript.

His uses of the creativity block are four: audiobooks and long YouTube lectures (Actualized.org is his favorite); physical books (he has taken them on 5:30 a.m. walks and read for thirty minutes, noting ideas to write about); walking meditation (section 40.4); and walking in silence so that his mind can solve a problem, relieve stress or bring clarity to his goals or vision. **Source:** same.

The mechanism comes from a 2022 video, where he recommends the creativity block as a step in learning skills fast. His favorite implementation, which he says the viewer should test, is to schedule a 30-minute walk. On it, listen to an audiobook or podcast related to your intention, contemplate your future, and let your mind work through problems, away from the distractions that keep you in a non-creative state. He recommends reading about the **default mode network** in psychology: shifting focus from external to internal is what turns on the part of the brain that allows creativity; it "opens and expands your mind," so ideas register in your conscious mind, where you can write them down and use them in productive work sessions. **Source:** Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md (2022-11-27).

**Complementary context:** The default mode network is a set of brain regions that is more active when attention is not directed at an external task (during mind-wandering, recalling the past, imagining the future) and less active during focused external tasks, which engage what is often called the task-positive network. Koe uses the distinction loosely, as an explanation of why ideas arrive during rest; Chapters 6 and 13 develop how he builds it into his work routine.

A 2022 video on solitude gives the most contemplative form of the practice, the **contemplative walk**, which he names as his favorite thing to do alone. On the walk, listen to an audiobook and "munch on the ideas," pause and think; contemplate your vision for the future, who you want to become, who you are or think you are. Contemplation and being with yourself is not easy, but "struggle is where you're going to receive the lesson": the mind is "primed with tension to be resolved" by giving you a lesson. It may take a week to figure out who you are, or a year to figure out what you want, but you will receive mini lessons and ideas along the way that contribute to progress toward your intrinsic goals. **Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md. The claim that unresolved tension primes the mind to produce answers connects with the idea, developed in Chapter 6, that a held goal or question directs attention even when you are not working on it.

A guest supplies a social variant. Justin Welsh, interviewed by Koe in 2021, does "phone-less walks" with his wife. For him it is "clear time," time with his wife and "really good time for me to think." On the walk he throws "all the things that I'm struggling with out into the space for my wife to hear." Because they are "very different people," she brings "a very different approach to solving those challenges," and "a lot of times when I get back home, I have a problem solved, or at least I'm moving in the right direction," so he returns to the computer with much more clarity. (She takes her phone; he leaves his, because he loves technology in a way she does not.) **Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md (2021-12-20). The mechanism combines three elements: removing the device, externalizing the problem aloud, and exposing it to a dissimilar perspective.

The walks last about 90 minutes. Roughly 45 minutes are about travel ("Where do we want to go next? Why do we want to go there? What do we want to discover?"); the rest is business talk, hers and then his. Justin calls it "quasi work and quasi play," and the point is "a great opportunity for me to get my eyes off the computer." Koe raises the objection a listener would have, that 90 minutes is a long time to be away from the computer, which leads into Justin's daily schedule. **Source:** same. The phrase "quasi work and quasi play" anticipates Koe's own later idea, discussed in section 40.4, that when work and rest collapse they become play.

There is a tension in how Koe classifies his walks. In some places they are learning blocks with rules such as finishing a chapter or writing a newsletter section; elsewhere he defines leisure as an activity not tied to a mandatory work outcome. His own resolution is that if walks counted as work, "then I work all day every day," which fits his ideal of merging work, rest and play. The tension is noted here and is not fully resolved in the corpus.

#### Gamifying the walk: vision, levels and rules

The 2024 walking video ends with a three-step method for turning the walk into a game, which applies the game theory of Chapter 10 to a single habit.

**Step one: clarify why, with vision and anti-vision.** Clarify why you walk, for life and business. "If you are not making decisions in alignment with a vision for the future, you're making decisions in alignment with someone else's vision for your future." The memes and content you scroll program your brain and move you toward a goal: watching self-deprecating memes all day is working toward "a vision of mediocrity and destruction." Then reflect. Do you want the benefits of quitting a bad habit (walking can replace it)? To be more creative, to come up with ideas? To drop ten pounds of fat, get a tan, reap the benefits of sunlight? If your mental health is in shambles, isn't it worth a shot? The exercise: list everything you do not like about your life now (the anti-vision); from that, list the opposite, everything you want or think you want one year from now (the vision); then contemplate how walking 10,000 or more steps a day contributes to it. **Source:** The Power Of Walking. This is a compressed version of the anti-vision and vision method of Chapter 7, applied to stacking whys.

**Step two: set a daily goal, start small, and break it into blocks.** All behavior starts with a conscious or unconscious goal, and every action maps to a goal; the question is whether you set it, or "were quietly programmed by the media, your parents, your teachers and your friends." Set a goal aligned with your vision and start small: the habit should be so frictionless that it is nearly impossible not to do. To the objection "I don't have time for 10,000 steps," he asks for honesty: given its importance, he thinks 10,000 should be a baseline; you can wake earlier, drop bad night habits, reprioritize. But if 10,000 makes you anxious, start lower. Think of 10,000 as level 100 and 3,000 as level 1; level 2 is 4,000, and so on, with sub-goals and possibly rewards. Then break the daily goal into blocks. All in one walk, or spaced out? What route? Walk to a landmark? To a local café for focused work? Stick to these until they are programmed into you. **Source:** same.

**Step three: create rules that narrow attention.** Flow, as Chapter 10 explains, is narrowing attention on a task until you become one with it. To narrow attention and increase enjoyment, create rules like a game's. Without rules there is no structure to refocus on, and the mind defaults to thinking about something better it could be doing, or to complaining: too hot, too cold, "I need my comfortable air-conditioned box," "I don't live in a walkable city." Koe calls these excuses: you go into a sauna and don't care that it's hot. "Your expectations and limiting beliefs are ruining your potential." His example rules, which he invites you to replace with your own:

- Never step on a crack.
- Come back with three ideas to write about from a book or audiobook.
- Get 15 minutes of sun on each side of your body.
- Alternate 5 to 10 minutes of walking meditation with 5 to 10 minutes of learning.
- Finish one book chapter or course module.
- Write one newsletter section or a few pieces of content.

**Source:** same. The rules do two different jobs. Some are purely attentional (the cracks, the meditation intervals); others tie the walk to an output (ideas, chapters, newsletter sections), which is what makes it a creativity block rather than only exercise.

In 2025 Koe gives a much simpler prescription for beginners, which he names the **3 by 20 method**: "You walk three times a day, 20 minutes after each major meal: breakfast, lunch, dinner." His reasons: twenty minutes is more than doable; three walks a day give you all the benefits, probably 7,000 to 8,000 steps, and you will likely hit 10,000 if you walk at any other time; and morning, afternoon and evening sunlight "drastically increase your sleep quality." If you have never done it, you will drop water weight and inflammation and notice "a decently drastic change in how you look and feel within the first 7 days," assuming the average American lifestyle and the rest of the protocol in which the method appears. **Source:** A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md.

The step targets Koe mentions shift considerably over the years, and the shift has a clear pattern:

| Date | Target or practice | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 2022-11 | Under 15,000 means a walk before bed | His own practice |
| 2023-02 | About 20,000 | His own practice |
| 2023-05 | 20,000–25,000; monthly average 18,500 | His own practice |
| 2024-04 | About 15,000 | His own practice |
| 2024-06 | 10,000 as a baseline, with levels starting at 3,000 | Recommendation to viewers |
| 2025-10 | 7,000–8,000 as the "sweet spot"; 3 by 20 | Protocol for beginners |

His own practice stays high while the prescription for his audience comes down. The two are compatible: one is what a person for whom walking is a cornerstone does, the other is an entry point designed to be frictionless. The only reason Koe gives for the lower figure is the one in step two, start small.

#### Sunlight and the solar callus

Being outside means sunlight and a lack of blue light, and here Koe enters territory where he is especially careful to mark his uncertainty. He says he cannot yet articulate his own health philosophy. He points to people he learned from, noting that many viewers are not ready to hear it and that many people take it too seriously, to their detriment. His approach blends flexible dieting, the Ray Peat diet, the ancestral diet and the carnivore diet, integrating patterns and principles from each; he describes them as lifestyles rather than diets, and in all of them sunlight and blue light are big topics. He claims that blue light is more dangerous than its effect on sleep suggests: it "destroys your mitochondrial health" and contributes to chronic disease. And he cites Andrew Huberman's advice to get sunlight first thing in the morning, which he says has more benefits than optimizing the circadian rhythm alone. To avoid hearsay, he names accounts to follow (as transcribed: Zade K. Dage, "Analyze and Optimize," "Grim Hood," and whoever they interact with). Then a warning: he is not dogmatic about any of their diets; if presented with different information he experiments and judges by direct experience, and if one of them tweets a belief, do not assume it is his. **Source:** The Power Of Walking.

**Complementary context:** Ray Peat (1936–2022) was an American writer on nutrition and physiology whose "pro-metabolic" ideas circulate widely online but sit outside mainstream nutrition science. Andrew Huberman is a Stanford neuroscientist and podcaster whose recommendation of morning light exposure is widely known. The claims about blue light and mitochondrial health are Koe's, relayed from these sources; he offers no study.

His personal story is the **solar callus**. He was extremely pale, very white in winter. Walking shirtless 15,000 steps a day, he can now be on a roof in Arizona heat for 45 minutes at a time without burning. He attributes this mostly to building his "solar callus, which is a tan, pretty much," by easing into it, plus diet and avoiding foods that contribute to inflammation. He admits he does not know the science well and is stating what could be possible with an open mind. **Source:** same. The term seems to come from the health accounts he follows, although he does not attribute it.

#### Nutrition: principles, an elimination diet and frontloading

On nutrition Koe holds two positions that look as if they pull in opposite directions, and the corpus shows both.

The first is that the details mostly do not matter. In the 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, Dickie says that fasting and maximizing muscle are "100% at odds": muscle needs continuous consumption, but he operates best mentally while fasted. Koe says he is switching back to intermittent fasting because he enjoys the structure ("I'm not eating until this time"), which removes decision fatigue. He cites Martin Berkhan, who popularized intermittent fasting with the Leangains method (high protein with every meal, fasting, and high-intensity compound-focused training three times a week), and who is extremely lean and natural-looking, but Koe wonders aloud whether that is "just despite everything he's doing or because of it." He also mentions Thomas DeLauer as a big proponent of fasting. Dickie's conclusion: "almost none of it matters except... calories and... you find a way to get enough protein... and the truth is most of that doesn't matter." What matters is consistency, sustainability and enjoyment; he keeps his 20-minute breakfast in the sun because he enjoys it. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

The second position, from 2025, is a concrete protocol. Health and fitness has been Koe's passion "for 15 years now, since I was 15. I just turned 29 yesterday." At 15 he tracked calories and macros obsessively; he has "tried every diet under the sun"; he understands that they are "all based on a few principles," and he tries to be non-dogmatic. Still, "I do think that everyone should do an elimination diet at least once in their life." His reasons: it "nearly guarantees a drastic boost in energy"; it lowers inflammation and drops water weight (you "look 10 lb lighter with a tighter jawline"); if you commit for 30 days or more you "rewire your eating habits and potentially become disgusted at the thought of eating hyperpalatable foods again"; and you may discover foods that cause digestive problems, skin problems or headaches, or that make you feel terrible without your noticing. To keep it simple he recommends "the vertical diet. It's by Stan Efferding": red meat, which is very nutrient-dense; white rice, which is easy to digest; and micronutrients from various other foods. "You literally just follow this graph," which he shows on screen and which cannot be reconstructed from the transcript beyond those three elements. It is what he returns to "when I need to go back to a baseline," for instance after going out too much, eating Chipotle after the gym, feeling worse, or looking puffier in the face in the morning. **Source:** A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md.

The two positions are reconciled by the word "baseline." The principles (calories, protein, consistency, enjoyment) govern ordinary life. The elimination diet is not an ideology but a reset and a diagnostic tool, used once to learn how your body responds and then again whenever you drift. The evolution of Koe's stance on health over these years runs from relativizing details to giving concrete protocols, while keeping the critique of dogmatism.

His routine shows the principles applied. After writing and before a walk, he eats breakfast while handling small tasks (checking his community, messaging): rice, eggs, spinach and sardines, cooked in coconut oil. The reason is to "knock out as much of my protein and micronutrient intake in the morning so I don't have to worry about it too much later. As you can tell, I **frontload** everything in the morning, from my work to my nutrition to the gym." Eggs are nutrient-dense, spinach provides greens, and sardines are "extremely nutrient-dense: omega-3s, heart health, blood pressure, cholesterol." Then come morning supplements, including desiccated beef liver ("nature's multivitamin"). The breakfast provides about 50 grams of protein (he used to add cottage cheese). At 220 pounds he aims for 220 grams of protein a day, and for 150 or more by the end of his post-gym meal, so that dinner out can be relaxed: "a good tasting bowl, don't worry about it too much." **Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe. **Frontloading**, his word for putting the demanding parts of the day first so the evening can be lived normally, is the same logic as his morning deep-work block in Chapter 12, applied to food.

The pre-gym ritual is lighter in tone. He has seen reels about "the most natural pre-workout" (honey with Himalayan salt on your hand), but prefers "the more synthetic approach, just like synthetic oil in a car": "There are two C's to any good gym session: carbs and caffeine." His version is Rice Krispie treats ("fast digestible carbs, has salt, makes for a good pump") and a Monster or Ghost energy drink, bought partly out of nostalgia for fitness people he watched online and partly because it is "on brand" (black and white). **Source:** same. It is an anecdote, not a recommendation, and it illustrates the anti-purist attitude of the next subsection.

#### Health resets: Justin Welsh's turning point

The Justin Welsh interview contains a case that shows the body as the foundation of a business, not only of a life. In December 2018, Justin was "really heavy," drinking too much, eating too much and not sleeping enough, and he had a panic attack in which he "literally thought I was dying." His wife called 911 and EMTs came to the house. About two weeks later, in January, he told his co-CEOs that he would step down, and he stayed another eight months, during which he got into shape, reduced his drinking and slept more. He frames the crisis as necessary: "that was the sort of the thing that needed to happen in order for me to look at my life through a different lens." **Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md.

Because of two back surgeries he could not do much lifting or running, so he and his wife walked ten miles a day in Los Angeles. The square around their house was 1.1 miles; they did three loops in the morning, three after lunch and three after dinner. Combined with no alcohol, healthy food and a lot of sleep, he lost about 40 pounds "and I just got clear." He stresses the timing: "It was a perfect time for me to get clear, cause I was going out on my own, and I needed to be as clear as possible." **Source:** same. In Justin's account clarity is not a side benefit of getting healthy; it is a prerequisite for starting a business. That is the same claim Koe makes when he puts the body first.

#### Against health neurosis: the destructive self-improvement game

The other side of Koe's view of health is a warning against overdoing it. In the 2023 conversation, Dickie Bush says he stopped wearing his Whoop (a wearable that scores recovery) six months earlier and has not felt tired once since. Before, his routine was to "wake up, ignore bodily feeling, check Whoop score... red, better take it easy today, I'm tired... a horrible habit." The device was useful for the feedback loop of learning what led to what, but once the fundamentals were in place he dropped it: "I have too much to build to be tired." His warning is that the tool "played its role; now don't tip too far," and he gives the example of blue-light glasses leading to feeling tired, which leads to more caffeine, which spirals. Koe names the pattern: "That is **the destructive self-improvement game**: you listen to too many people's opinions of what you should be doing until... you completely forget that it's all about how you feel." **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. The story is Dickie's; the name is Koe's.

Later in 2023, in a video on "tactical stress" strategies for getting out of a rut, Koe defends what he calls **degeneracy**, in the narrow sense of allowing occasional excess without neurosis. "Strategy number five is another touchy one, but it's degeneracy." He targets "a lot of the Huberman cult and straight-edge people," who think nobody should ever drink alcohol or use any recreational drug: "I don't think that in the slightest," while acknowledging addiction and the excuses people can make. He asks the viewer to set aside "your narrow identity and ideology about alcohol" and to see that some people can have a drink with dinner "or go out and get screwed up occasionally." His own habits: he drank a decent amount in college and a bit after; now "like one, two drinks a week at dinner with friends." He mentions "some people in foreign countries like Italy or whatever" who smoke and drink a lot and "live longer than the biohackers and people that are overly stressed about their health," and reasons that "the stress is more unhealthy than smoking a cigarette all the time." "If you're so neurotic about just having a drop of alcohol... that's probably more unhealthy than the guy that's having a drop of alcohol." **Source:** A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md (2023-10-02). These health comparisons are his impressions and are offered without sources; the structural point is that anxiety about health is itself a health variable. The term "Huberman cult" is Koe's label for absolutism, not a description of Huberman's own views.

In 2025 he extends the critique to the fashion for longevity. Describing a "longevity optimizer" archetype at level three of the body in his developmental framework (Chapter 38), he says, "this one is big on false transformations. Everyone is hopping on the longevity game now. It's the new status game." "Most people hopping on the Brian Johnson longevity optimization aren't optimizing their longevity at all and aren't thinking for themselves." **Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md (2025-10-19). **False transformation**, as Chapter 4 explains, is putting on the mask of a higher level without solving the problems of the current one. Applied to health, it is adopting the visible rituals of optimization as a status signal while the fundamentals (sleep, food, movement, stress) remain unaddressed.

**Complementary context:** Bryan Johnson (spelled "Brian Johnson" in the transcript) is an entrepreneur known for a highly publicized, data-driven anti-aging regimen.

The attitude Koe recommends instead is captured by a metaphor from the Dickie Bush conversation. He has to be careful with the picture he paints: if he keeps talking about drinks he sounds like an alcoholic; if he talks about raves (he goes roughly every three months, "a weekend out of the year"), he sounds like "a druggie raver." But what he wants to convey is that "you don't have to close yourself off to any aspect of reality." Dickie adds that "it's all reminders to yourself too." Koe's point is that a big goal can threaten what you currently enjoy: if he feared that building his software business would end his music festivals, that fear could affect how, or whether, he builds it. "That's the entire fun: to treat all of these little things as **Legos** in your lifestyle and slowly... take pieces out here and there and learn from your mistakes." **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. The Lego image (which Koe uses elsewhere for notes, work blocks and ideas) makes the lifestyle modular: no element is sacred or forbidden, and each can be removed and observed.

#### Alcohol: an unresolved tension

On alcohol specifically, the corpus does not settle on one position, and it would be misleading to present one.

In the 2021 interview with Justin Welsh, Koe says that even light drinking cost him up to a week of brain fog, and presents quitting as a gain in control; Justin used 90 alcohol-free days as part of his reset. In the 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, Dickie argues that total abstinence is easier than moderation (comparing it to an ice bath), and Koe agrees that zero is better while you are building. In October 2023, as shown above, Koe defends occasional "degeneracy" and reports one or two drinks a week.

In January 2025, Sahil Bloom offers a contrarian view of the "zero alcohol" movement. "We've lost the plot a little bit on some of the health optimization stuff, on the impact that it has on social life." He has "questions about whether [the no-alcohol thing] is a net negative for society," for three reasons. First, as content that "gets likes on social media," it "creates this binary picture where either you drink none or you are a degenerate binge drinker," ignoring "the shades of gray": many people have a couple of drinks a week, or one, "and have no massive negative impact"; one beer "will impact your sleep slightly," not catastrophically. Second, going to zero can mean "I don't really feel comfortable going to the bar to meet up with that friend... so I'm not going to see that person," and "knowing what we know about the impact on social connection, on health, in the long run," that may be worse. Third, "humans have been gathering around the idea of alcohol for a long time; it sort of has the Lindy effect," though it "doesn't need to be a beer"; it can be something non-alcoholic. His own arc: "in my old life, when I was going down a darker path, I was drinking seven nights a week... probably 14 drinks a week," which hurt his physical and mental health; then zero; now "one drink a week, maybe one drink every two weeks," only "on special occasions when I'm catching up with someone I care about." He predicts more people will find a similar "happy medium." **Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30). In the same conversation Koe describes his own difficulty finding balance with alcohol, saying it tends to be all or nothing for him.

**Complementary context:** The Lindy effect, popularized by Nassim Nicholas Taleb, is the heuristic that the expected remaining lifespan of a non-perishable thing, such as an idea, technology or custom, grows with its current age.

The tension remains open between Koe and his guests and within Koe himself. The most defensible reading, consistent with his habit of dating advice by stage, is that a radical reset fits a period of abuse or of intense building, and a middle path fits a stabilized life. The reason Koe gives for the variation is simply that it depends on the stage. Sahil's argument also prepares the next section: the cost of a health practice must include its cost to relationships, which, as the Harvard study he cites suggests, are themselves a health variable.

### 40.3 Relationships and the Time Billionaire

Relationships are the domain that Koe's own corpus develops least. He names them as a pillar of the good life, includes them inside "spirit" or "connection," and makes several sharp observations about them; but the most systematic treatment comes from a single 2025 podcast with Sahil Bloom, author of *The 5 Types of Wealth*. This section first gathers Koe's own ideas, which concern how self-development changes relationships, and then presents Sahil's frameworks on time and social wealth, marking Koe's responses along the way.

#### Relationships improve by proxy

Koe's main claim about relationships answers the question of where they fit in his sequence of mind, body and business. In 2025, addressing viewers who asked about relationships in that order of priorities, he says: "By developing yourself you improve your relationships by proxy. You become less of a leech, your cup overflows into theirs, and you no longer need them to pour into yours." **Source:** How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md (2025-03-02).

The image of the cup explains why relationships are not a separate track in his plan. A person who is empty (low energy, no direction, no work that matters) enters relationships needing to be filled, and the relationship becomes extraction: the "leech." A person who is developing in the other domains brings surplus, and the relationship becomes exchange or gift. Relationships improve not because they were worked on directly but because one of the people in them changed. This is the holistic interdependence of section 40.1 applied to the domain of other people.

Two older observations explain why this does not license ignoring people. The first concerns social skill. Being introverted, Koe says in 2023, "isn't about being anti-social": "being social is a skill, not a personality trait, that can be improved." People "hide behind their introverted identity" to avoid the inevitable discomfort they will have to face if they want success in any domain of life. It makes no sense to write off business, relationships or meeting new people because you think of yourself as an introvert, as if the roughly 50% of the population that is introverted "just doesn't do those things"; that is "completely against the point." **Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md (2023-02-12). He speaks from his own experience: he was introverted in college and overcame it through direct messages and Zoom calls. The argument belongs to the theory of identity in Part II: the label "introvert" functions as an identity that protects the person from discomfort, not as a description of what they are able to do.

The second concerns what a relationship is. "When interacting with other people, social situations, relationships, you need to create a **shared reality**." Projecting outside that reality causes problems. If both people talk from their own perspectives and one does not understand the other "because it's not a shared perspective and you haven't created that reality that you can act in with shared goals," then problems arise ("that's what the problems are"), and there will be no system, no reality, to maintain. **Source:** How To Stop Feeling Lost, Anxious, Or Uncertain About The Future.md (2022-10-16). In Koe's vocabulary from Part III, a mind is held together by its goals. The concept of a shared reality extends that to two or more minds: a relationship holds together to the extent that the people in it have goals in common to act toward.

A small practical suggestion follows from both ideas. Koe wishes that, when he was younger, he had had friends who shared this kind of content with him. People share memes, which is no problem, but you can cultivate a much deeper relationship with friends and family by sharing your interests and your desire to change, "so that you can do it together." **Source:** The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md (2024-10-10). Sharing a desire to change is one way of building a shared reality.

#### The time billionaire: a graph of time spent with people

The conversation with Sahil Bloom opens with a graph that Koe puts on screen and describes for listeners. It opens Sahil's book *The 5 Types of Wealth* and was one of his most popular threads, on "the concept of the time billionaire." It shows time spent with specific people across a lifetime. As Koe describes it:

| Who | Shape of the curve |
|---|---|
| Family (parents and siblings) | Sharply decreases after you reach age 20 |
| Your children | Peaks in the early years of their lives, then sharply decreases |
| Friends | Peaks around 18, then normalizes to a baseline |
| Coworkers | Steady during working years, then declines after retirement |
| Yourself | Seems to slowly increase, apart from being "kind of stable" in your 20s |

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30). The exact shape of the curves cannot be reconstructed from the transcript beyond this verbal description.

Sahil explains the family curve. The majority of time with family, "defined in that time use survey data as parents and siblings," is experienced in childhood. He cites a statistic, hedged ("I think there's some statistic"): "like 95% of the time you have with your parents is in the first 18 years of your life, because then you go off and you're living your own life... and you're never going to get that amount of time back with them." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** Charts of this kind are generally built from the American Time Use Survey, which records how people distribute their time across activities and companions. The 95% figure is Sahil's approximate recollection and is presented by him as such.

#### Chronos and kairos: not all time is equal

The graph, Sahil says, is "fundamentally about an understanding that not all time is created equal." He borrows from the ancient Greeks, who "had two different words for time." **Chronos** is "quantitative linear time from point A to point B." **Kairos** is "the idea that there were certain moments in time that had higher importance. They were more textured than other moments." His adaptation: "It's kairos time. It's the idea that there are windows during the course of your life that matter more for these specific relationships, for these specific people in your world." The practical consequence: "understanding when you were in one of those **kairos time windows** is essential for actually taking advantage of it, for actually capitalizing on those specific moments." **Source:** same. The windows in the graph are examples: childhood for parents, your children's early years for them, the late teens for friends.

**Complementary context:** In classical Greek, *chronos* denotes sequential, measurable time, while *kairos* denotes the opportune or decisive moment; the distinction was used in rhetoric and later in theology. Sahil applies it to the timing of relationships.

Why does the chart provoke "such an emotional and visceral reaction"? Because "it smacks you in the face with the fact that the amount of time you have left with the people you care about most in the world is dwindling. It is impermanent, it is finite, and you don't think about that on a daily basis. It takes a chart like this to bring it to life for you, in a real and visceral and visual way." For a young person, "time is effectively irrelevant." Sahil proposes a thought experiment: if you graphed how much a person thinks about time over a lifetime, "it would basically be zero until the very end, when it's the only thing that... matters. But it's too late." There would be "little blips" when someone in your life died, and then the line would return to zero. **Source:** same. The argument connects with the principle of impermanence in Chapter 39: impermanence is always true but rarely felt, and the chart is a device for making it felt while there is still time to act on it.

The term that names the whole idea follows. The graphs are "trying to punch you in the face with this realization that time is your most precious asset, that you are a **time billionaire**. That idea that you literally have billions of seconds in your lives." The gap Sahil points to is behavioral: "you're not relating to yourself that way. You are not engaged in the activities that take advantage of the fact that time is your most precious asset." **Source:** same.

#### Would you trade lives with Warren Buffett?

Sahil's proof that time is worth more than money is a question he asks young people. Warren Buffett "is worth $130 billion. He has all of the things that you say that you want in life... access to anyone in the world... private jets... mansions. He reads and learns for a living." But "you wouldn't trade lives with him, because he's 95 years old. There's no way you would agree to trade the amount of time that he has left for all of the money that he has. And on the flip side, he would give anything... to be in your shoes." The conclusion: your time "has literally incalculable value." Then the contrast with daily behavior: "how much time are you sitting around scrolling on this thing, comparing yourself to other people, worrying about bullshit... stressing over things that have no impact on your life. Wasting away that precious, incalculably valuable time." **Source:** same.

The thought experiment works by revealed preference. You may say that you value money above time, but your refusal of the trade shows the opposite. The point is not that money is worthless but that you already rank time above any sum, and your behavior does not reflect your ranking.

#### Do you need to unwind more than you need to change your life?

Sahil's prescription for someone who feels stuck is to "dedicate 30 minutes or 60 minutes a day to building the life that you actually want to live." To the objection "I don't have time," he answers: "You have to decide what you want more." His case: a 27-year-old working a nine-to-five he hated, who wanted to build something of his own and said he had no time. Sahil asked him to walk through his day. At the end of it was "this hour and a half... block of, like, Netflix and chill." Sahil suggested deploying 30 minutes of it into the new thing. The reply: "I need that time to unwind." Sahil's question: "Do you need to unwind more than you need to change your life?" He insists this is "not... harsh... purely tactically." Change requires "pain and struggle... the hard thing now that you don't want to do... the pain of not having the amount of time that you want to unwind. It's a sacrifice necessary. You have to create that structure in your life where you currently are." **Source:** same.

This case links the time billionaire back to section 40.1. It is the same prescription Koe gives to people past their twenties (one hour a day), with the same diagnosis: the time exists, but it is spent as currency rather than invested. The 90-minute Netflix block is the "modern comforts" trap at the scale of a single evening.

#### Social wealth: the front row, tiny investments and the Harvard study

The second part of the conversation moves from time to what Sahil calls social wealth. He opens that chapter of his book with a question: "Who will be sitting in the front row of your funeral?" He is "a big believer in visualization, and particularly in mental time travel, like the idea that you can zoom out from your present state and envision yourself in the future and use that as a tool to adjust your actions in the present." The empowering part is the question it raises: "What does that future visualization tell me about what I am doing in the present... because the actions you are taking in the present are creating that future, whether it's a good one or a bad one." This specific visualization is "about recognizing who those people are in your life that occupy that really special place, and recognizing whether or not in the present you are taking actions to let them know, to invest in those relationships... to cherish them, and to be that front row person to someone else." **Source:** same. Structurally, the funeral question is a relational form of the anti-vision and vision exercise of Chapter 7: a future image used to correct present behavior.

The call to action that unites his chapters on time and social wealth is small by design: "make the tiny investment today in those relationships... send the text, send the one little text. When you're thinking about the person, that has a ripple effect into the future." The mindset shift is to treat relationships as you treat money. With money "you know that investing $10 today is better than zero, that investing $100 today is better than ten... because it's going to compound." But "you don't think about that when it comes to relationships... that the tiny little investment today is going to stack and compound in the future." His examples: "taking the one second to text your friend that you were thinking about," "ten minutes out of your day to call your mom while you're driving to the office," telling your sibling "some funny story or memory." **Source:** same.

Koe admires that Sahil randomly texts him ("hey dude, how are you doing?") and asks whether it comes naturally or was developed. Sahil: it was developed, "because I didn't do it for so long. And then I started to think about it and have this mindset shift. I now have really tried to build a practice around it." But he rejects the reflex to systematize it: "everyone now is like, okay, what's the implementation? I need a CRM for people in my life... it's going to send auto texts to those people. That's not really the point. I want to actually think about these people and actually have something that I want to share with them." Koe adds that it does "so much for you in more than just relationships": he would be "there for you at any time." **Source:** same. The exchange is a useful corrective inside a book about systems. Staying in touch is a practice, not a CRM, because the value of the text lies in the fact that someone was actually thought about; automating it removes the thing that made it valuable.

Why take any of this seriously? "Relationships are fundamentally the key to healthy aging." Sahil cites the Harvard Study of Adult Development, "done over the course of 85-plus years," following "1,300 original participants, plus another 700 of their descendants." Its finding, as he reports it: "the single greatest predictor of physical health at age 80 was relationship satisfaction" at age 50, not "your blood pressure... cholesterol... smoking, your drinking habits. It was how you felt about your relationships that determined... how well you aged." His conclusion: take relationships "as seriously as anything that you take in your life." **Source:** same. (The transcript's sentence boundary is garbled; the reading that satisfaction at 50 predicts health at 80 is the coherent one.)

**Complementary context:** The Harvard Study of Adult Development began in 1938 and has followed two cohorts of men (Harvard students and young men from inner-city Boston) and, later, their children. Its current director, Robert Waldinger, has popularized the finding that relationship satisfaction in midlife predicts later health better than cholesterol levels. The participant figures are Sahil's.

Two principles that Sahil states as personal beliefs complete the picture. The first concerns giving. "The best things in life come when you are giving with no expectation of return." He says that Koe "genuinely [has] helped me on several occasions when I really didn't have much to offer you in return," saying "don't worry... I'm super happy to help." "The people that are like that by default tend to have the best returns in life, and I don't know how it happens. Call it karma, call it whatever you want." His aspiration is "to be one of the people that friends can call on... when they are down and out... You can't always do that, but you can try to make it a value that you hold close. And when you do, you just end up on the best journeys." **Source:** same. Sahil explicitly does not offer a mechanism, and the principle should be read as an observation and a value, not a law.

The second concerns the people around you. Sahil disagrees with the cliché "focus on the journey, not the destination." "My whole thing is **focus on the people**, because when you surround yourself with inspiring, kind, positive... big-thinking people, you end up finding yourself on the best, most brilliant journeys, and you land at the best destinations. But it's the people. It's not picking the perfect path... It's... surrounding yourself with those people who lift you up." **Source:** same. He supports this with what he calls science: "the **Pygmalion effect** is this scientific phenomenon that you actually rise to the level of expectations that other people have for you. So if you're surrounding yourself with those big thinkers, people that encourage you to do more, that you're capable... you actually rise to the level of those expectations." Hence "finding that tribe is actually good for your life and for your professional outcomes." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** The Pygmalion effect is named after the classroom experiments of Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson (*Pygmalion in the Classroom*, 1968), in which teachers' raised expectations of randomly selected students were associated with gains in those students' performance. The size and robustness of the effect have been debated since; Sahil uses it in its popular form.

The tribe argument is the positive version of the warning Koe gives about the twenties (you cannot save the people who stop believing in themselves; do not let it infect your mind). Both rest on the same premise: the expectations of the people around you are part of your environment, and environment shapes behavior.

#### The eject button: why depth is rare

Koe asks why young people seem lonely and not very social, finding it hard to make good friends. Sahil, holding up a phone, gives two causes.

The first: "Social media and technology have conspired against us... this technology that has made us more connected to the world than ever before has made us less connected to the people sitting right in front of us." We have "slowly but steadily lost our ability to truly interact, to create these **textured relationships**." It is "much easier to just sit at home and interact with your friends on Snapchat." His statistic: "teenagers in the US are spending 70% less time in person with their friends than they were two decades ago." **Source:** same. (The figure is his; he does not name the study.)

The second cause is the **eject button**. "Social media and your phones have made you press the eject button way too fast on relationships. The second relationships get a little tough, the second they come out of the honeymoon phase... you just hit the eject button, because you have 100 options right at your fingertips." This applies "to romantic relationships and to friendships." The underlying reality is that "the best relationships are built through **shared struggle**. They're built through sitting in the mud with people," and that kind of growth, he says, "scientifically releases oxytocin, creates feelings of love and connection." So "if your default is to press eject, you are never by default going to get through the struggle that builds that depth of connection." The result is "this self-fulfilling prophecy of lack of connection, lack of depth. You press the eject button, you go to the next surface relationship." **Source:** same.

The argument has a precise structure. Depth is produced by passing through difficulty together. Abundant options lower the cost of leaving when difficulty begins. So abundant options systematically prevent the passage through difficulty that produces depth, and every relationship stays at the surface. The loneliness is not a lack of contacts but a lack of relationships that have been tested. The parallel with Chapter 39 is direct: there, chosen struggle is the condition of a meaningful life; here, shared struggle is the condition of a deep relationship.

Sahil's metaphor for what is lost: "Surface relationships are fine when times are good, like during the summer when it's warm and sunny and... your life is going well. But if you have surface relationships and things go bad, you are in a lot of trouble, because you're going to call on people and they're not going to be there for you... they're **tourists, not locals**. So as soon as the weather gets bad, poof, they're gone... flooded out of town." **Source:** same. Good times map to good weather; deep friends are the locals who stay year-round.

Koe connects this to the question of how to find "the select group" of right people. "You can obviously use the phone to interact with them, but if you have that tight-knit group of people that are in your life, you're going to find it extremely hard to maintain those connections if you're allowing so many people into it. Like, you can't just ghost or hit the eject button on a real relationship, that relationship that you have in real life." **Source:** same. His question trails off in the transcript, so the implication is partly implicit, but it points to selectivity: fewer real relationships rather than many surface ones, because attention spent on many connections is attention withdrawn from the few that can become deep. This is the theory of attention from Part III applied to people.

#### The Life Dinner: from "me versus you" to "us versus the problem"

At the time of the conversation Koe was a year and a half into a relationship, and he asked Sahil for strategies to keep it strong. Sahil's answer is a method he attributes to the investor Brad Feld: the **Life Dinner**. "Create a fixed monthly date where you and your partner get together and actually have a cadence around connecting on some of the bigger-picture things in your relationship and in your life." The rationale: early in a relationship there is a natural daily cadence of catching up, but "as life starts to get more complicated, as you both have responsibilities, maybe as you have kids, as you get married, it becomes harder and harder to make sure that you are zooming out." **Source:** same.

The format, as Sahil describes it:

1. Once a month, at your favorite place.
2. Each partner comes "with a set of things that you're thinking about... challenges, opportunities, visions for the future."
3. Each has the opportunity to talk about their side.
4. "Then you come together to address whatever problems you need to face together."

The effect: "You take all of these sort of me-versus-you things and turn it into an us-versus-the-problem thing... collaborative and synergistic." Sahil and his wife "have found a ton of benefit in it." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** Brad Feld is an American venture capitalist; the Life Dinner is described in *Startup Life* (2013), which he wrote with his wife, Amy Batchelor.

Read against Koe's own ideas, the Life Dinner is a ritual for maintaining a shared reality. Daily logistics keep two people in contact; they do not keep them pointed at the same goals. A fixed monthly occasion to zoom out, the same verb Koe uses for the holistic goal, restores the shared goals that, in his 2022 formulation, a relationship needs in order to have a reality to maintain. And turning "me versus you" into "us versus the problem" is exactly the move from two perspectives projecting at each other to one shared perspective acting on something outside both.

### 40.4 Spirituality and Meditation

"Spirit" is the domain of the four that Koe defines least consistently and defends most often. This section gathers three things: his definition of spirituality as connection to something greater than yourself, and the surprising conclusion he draws from it about goals; his insistence that spirituality is a lens rather than an identity; and his practice of meditation, which changes over time from detachment from thoughts to a way of perceiving.

#### Spirituality as connection: why goals are spiritual

The clearest definition comes in a 2024 video on intelligence. Koe distinguishes his use of goals from "your typical self-help lens of, oh, you need to set a goal for the sake of productivity." He talks about goals in a **teleological** sense (the transcript renders the word as "teologicos"): "a purpose is a goal," and "everything is a part of a greater whole, and if you lose that connection you lose a lot of meaning in your life." Then the definition: "Spirituality is connection to something greater than yourself." **Source:** How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md (2024-11-03).

From this he builds a short chain. If you do not have a meaningful, self-chosen goal in alignment with reality, and you make mistakes, "reality will not tolerate those mistakes; you will feel the pain. So correcting from your pain is what allows you to live in alignment and with meaning." Therefore "goals in this sense, that people think are very practical, are not anti-spiritual; they are spirituality." **Source:** same.

The steps are worth separating. (1) A goal, understood teleologically, is a purpose: what something is for. (2) Purpose places you as a part within a larger whole. (3) Connection to a larger whole is what Koe means by spirituality. (4) A goal aligned with reality also exposes you to correction: errors produce pain, and pain steers you back toward alignment. (5) So pursuing a real goal is a continuous practice of staying connected to, and corrected by, something larger than you. The practical and the spiritual are not two domains but the same activity seen from two sides. This is the cybernetic mind of Chapter 6 (a goal plus error correction) restated in spiritual vocabulary.

**Complementary context:** Teleology, from the Greek *telos* (end, purpose), is explanation in terms of ends or purposes rather than prior causes. Aristotle's "final cause" is its classical form; the book introduces it in Part III in connection with Koe's theory of goals.

He continues with a distinction between kinds of game. Goals in the self-help sense are **finite games**: get this much money, go to school, get a job. Purpose in the teleological sense is different: "you are a part of a greater whole, and that whole is a part in and of itself that is a part of a greater whole; that's the structure of reality." By connecting to and maintaining this relationship with reality, "you begin to play the **infinite game**, and that is the house of all of the finite games that you are playing." The infinite game "allows you to bring meaning to those things that you thought were superficial and shallow before, like making a lot of money or doing whatever your desires are." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** The distinction between finite games (played to win, with fixed rules and an end) and infinite games (played to keep playing, with rules that change) comes from James P. Carse, *Finite and Infinite Games* (1986). Koe uses the vocabulary without naming a source. The image of wholes nested within wholes is Ken Wilber's holon, presented in Chapter 38.

The phrase "the house of all the finite games" is the key to the argument. Making money is not spiritual or unspiritual in itself. It becomes meaningful when it is a finite game played inside an infinite one, in the way a single match is meaningful inside a season or a career. A person who plays only finite games experiences each one as an end that turns out to be empty when reached; a person who plays them inside an infinite game experiences each as a move.

A 2024 passage adds what the infinite game feels like from inside. Koe believes that "the universe or evolution" has been solving a problem for a long time: people do not like to work when it feels like work. They want work to be a necessary balance to the rest of their life, and that is what we call their life's work or meaningful work, which is up to each person to choose and use as a creative challenge. It continuously evolves. That is how you know something is an infinite game rather than a finite one: you create the rules and can keep creating them, so there is no end; you never win, never lose, you just play. "When work and rest collapse, they turn into play." **Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md (2024-06-02). **Play**, in Koe's sense, is not leisure as opposed to work; it is what activity becomes when the distinction between work and rest no longer applies. The idea connects to the "getting paid to play" theme of Part X and to Justin Welsh's "quasi work and quasi play" in section 40.2. The claim that evolution has been "solving" this problem is an interpretive frame, not an empirical statement.

The definition of spirituality has its own history in the corpus. In 2022 Koe treated the mystical as something that cannot be articulated, and recommended Alan Watts's figure of the bodhisattva, who balances the mystical and the material (section 40.5). In 2023 he warned that spiritual people who dismiss business will fail without realizing it (section 40.1), and described spirituality as "understanding your part in the whole of the universe," felt when you reverse entropy; it is not, he said, going off into the woods. In 2024 he described it as discovering your part in the whole, transcending that whole to the next, and then the next, until you reach your life's purpose of contribution; and, in the passage above, he concluded that goals "are spirituality." The movement is from balancing two planes (the mystical and the practical) to integrating them, with the added idea of successive transcendence and an explicit link between purpose and problem-solving. Both versions remain usable: the earlier one describes contemplative experience; the later one subordinates it to a teleological goal.

#### Spirituality is a lens, not an identity

Koe is as wary of spiritual people as he is of materialists. "One of the big issues here is with the hyper-spiritual people, the ones that have adopted that as an identity when it's not meant to be. It's supposed to be a lens that you can take off and put on to view situations in a certain way, preferably a big-picture point of view, so you can get the whole picture and not narrow yourself into this ideological trap of what you think spirituality is." **Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md (2023-11-12).

The distinction between a lens and an identity is central to his theory of identity (Part II). An identity is defended: challenges to it are felt as threats, and the person narrows to protect it. A lens is used: it is put on when it helps you see and taken off when it does not. Spirituality adopted as an identity becomes one more ideology to defend, which is exactly the narrowing it was supposed to dissolve.

The same logic governs how he relates spiritual and scientific explanations. In a 2022 video, discussing Joe Dispenza's neuroscience-based account of how personality creates personal reality, Koe says that he likes the spiritual aspect because he can apply it to his life easily, while other people are more congruent with the scientific point of view and can apply that better. Both are "just a different lens of viewing it." **Source:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md (2022-06-12). His criterion for choosing a lens is congruence: which framing you can actually apply.

In another 2022 video he gives philosophy and spirituality a status. Many of the books he recommends are philosophy and spirituality, "which is pretty much like **non-scientific psychology** in a sense": they describe things psychology has discovered scientifically or "has yet to discover," based on direct experience. They are a way to get information, try things and experience them directly "rather than relying on science to give you this black and white: yes, you should do this; no, you shouldn't do this. That's not how humans work." And he adds: "I'm not discounting science at all." **Source:** 7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md (2022-04-23).

This is the epistemological stance behind the whole of sections 40.4 and 40.5, and it should be read precisely. Koe does not claim that spiritual teachings are scientifically validated. He claims that they are reports of direct experience, that some of them anticipate findings psychology later confirms, and that the test of a teaching is whether you can try it and observe the result in your own life. That is a claim about method (first-person experimentation), not about evidence in the scientific sense.

He also guards against the opposite error. Explaining in 2022 that the Matrix is made of concepts layered over reality, he anticipates the objection "so you're just saying nothing exists," and replies: "In essence I am, but this is where the **balance of spirituality and practicality** comes into play." There is a polarity, and both poles have to exist. His aim is only to show the side of the picture most people do not see, the one whose absence prevents them from becoming deep or creative thinkers; he does not advocate abandoning practical life. **Source:** The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md (2022-11-20). Polarity, as Chapter 39 explains, is his principle that nothing exists without its opposite; here it is applied to the spiritual and the practical themselves.

Finally, which teachings? Koe declines to prescribe. In 2022 he lists the books that resonate with him: *The Power of Now* (which he had not read in a while), something transcribed as "awareness" (it is unclear whether a separate title is meant), *The Way of the Superior Man*, *Flow* by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, *Rest* by Alex Soojung-Kim Pang, and "everything Alan Watts." They may not resonate with you, and that is a signal: you must find the people, writing and interests that really resonate with you, which makes study effortless and makes you want to talk about them. **Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md (2022-10-31). The criterion of resonance mirrors the criterion of congruence: the right lens is the one you can actually use.

**Complementary context:** *The Power of Now* (1997) is by Eckhart Tolle, whose teaching on presence was, by Koe's account elsewhere, a catalyst in his own life (Chapter 1). Alan Watts (1915–1973) was a British-American writer and lecturer who popularized Zen, Taoism and Vedanta for Western audiences.

#### Meditation: from skeptic to practitioner

Koe used to be skeptical of meditation. He thought it was for hippies channeling divine energy to become like Avatar from *The Last Airbender*; like most things he disliked, he says, he simply did not understand it. His 2024 definitions are three: meditation is the act of freeing your mind; it is decreasing your attachment to thoughts; it is the practice of being at one with reality, not with expectations of the future or happenings of the past. With a consistent habit, life gets better "because you're actually living it." **Source:** The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md (2024-06-16).

The diagnosis that makes meditation necessary is what he calls surface-level living. Most people do not realize how surface-level their lives are: they bounce around in their heads, creating problems that do not exist, and spiral into deep anxiety and overwhelm. Their lives are ruled by stressful past situations that shape how they perceive the future. The boss who yelled yesterday makes you dread work today. Yesterday's bad writing makes you worry it won't get done tomorrow. You keep projecting into a future in which you don't get it done, your side business fails, and you start a plan B, "without realizing you are creating that reality with your thoughts, because your actions will follow." You trap yourself in a "narrow-minded state of suffering," and the only way out is reprogramming the mind with meditation. **Source:** same.

The mechanism is a self-fulfilling projection. A past stressor is projected into the future as an expectation; the expectation produces emotion now; the emotion shapes action; the action produces the feared outcome. Meditation intervenes at the first link, by reducing the grip of the thought, so that the chain does not start. This connects with the gap between expectation and reality described in Chapter 39 and with the distinction between "what is" and "what isn't" in section 40.5.

His method came out of failure. He always failed to build a seated meditation habit, so he tried **walking meditation**. On many walks, mainly first thing in the morning, he focuses on the feeling of the ground under his feet or on the breath entering his nose (especially noticeable when it is cold or hot); he counts in his head and breathes; whenever attention floats to a thought or a task, he simply refocuses on the breath. "That's how you recondition your mind to detach from the thoughts" and give them less attention, living more in the present and paying attention to the signal around you rather than the noise. **Source:** same. The walking meditation is one of the uses of the creativity block in section 40.2, which is why Koe can count a single walk toward body, mind and spirit at once.

#### One meditation: a way of perceiving

By 2025 the definition widens. In a video built around a set of single daily habits ("one project, one book, one meditation, one workout"), Koe introduces **one meditation**. Work can be beautiful and spiritual, he says, but it does not replace a habit that maintains connection with reality. Meditation here is not only sitting in a dark room focusing on the breath, though that is viable too; it is "a way of life, a way of perceiving the world through a lens of wonder and gratitude," noticing the amazing things that are glossed over when you are stressed and narrow-minded. "The purpose of meditation in this sense is to notice depth, to escape the trap of shallow living." **Source:** You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md (2025-06-29).

The practice: for at least ten minutes a day, notice reality in greater detail. His examples:

- Walk focusing only on your feet touching the ground.
- Stare at a tree and notice detail you have never seen.
- Listen deeply to a loved one, noticing tone and expression, not just words.
- Stare at your hand for ten minutes ("yes, I'm serious"), letting your mind wander into its detail.
- Feel the water on your hands while doing the dishes; feel everything you touch.
- Listen to silence, or to distant cars.
- Pick apart the smell of boring food.

"Don't overcomplicate it." **Source:** same.

The change from 2024 to 2025 is a broadening rather than a reversal. The 2024 practice is subtractive: reduce attachment to thoughts and return to the breath. The 2025 practice is additive: increase the depth of perception until ordinary things become remarkable. The second includes the first (attending to the feet on the ground appears in both), and the reason Koe gives for the broader version is that work, however spiritual, does not by itself maintain connection with reality. The phrase "listen deeply to a loved one" also connects the practice to section 40.3: attention is the substance of a textured relationship.

#### Think beyond the apple

An earlier exercise from 2022 points in the same direction and shows how depth of perception changes desire. Instead of taking the shallow pleasure hit of something, dive deep and question it. Koe's example is an apple. What are the impacts of this apple? What made it? Can you gain a deeper appreciation for it? Where did it come from? What was the process of getting it to the store? Who was affected by it, and who created it? Is it causing suffering to produce it? What does it do when I put it into my body? Do I have to learn about my body to answer these things? "Do I have to break out of this unconscious life that I'm going through and actually learn something for once, and expand my awareness and consciousness to the point of deep appreciation for the things that are conducive to my growth?" **Source:** Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md (2022-07-02).

The exercise belongs to a video on reinvention, and its function there is to build a new identity: the depth of questioning makes it possible to enjoy what supports growth (the apple) more than what gives a shallow hit. "Think beyond the apple" is therefore both a contemplative practice and a technique of desire. It is the same operation as one meditation (noticing depth), applied to a choice rather than a perception, and it shows how, for Koe, spirituality and self-improvement keep turning into each other.
