## Chapter 35 — Agency and Self-Reliance

Chapter 34 ended with money as a unit of value and with the argument that leverage, not hard work, is what makes an individual wealthy. That argument left a question open. Leverage, code, media, the internet and now artificial intelligence are available to almost everyone. Information that once cost a university degree costs nothing. If the tools are this widely distributed, why do so few people use them? Koe's answer, developed over four years and sharpened in conversation with the novelist Devon Eriksen, is that the scarce ingredient is not access, intelligence or opportunity but **agency**: the disposition to set one's own direction, act on it without being told, and keep correcting course until it works.

This chapter assembles that answer. It connects back to Part I, where the diagnosis began: the Matrix installs assigned goals, the default path turns people into followers of other people's plans, and the employee is trained to wait for instructions (Chapters 1 and 2). Agency is the positive counterpart of that diagnosis. Where conformity hands your direction to society, agency takes it back. The chapter also prepares Chapter 36, because Koe's case for agency becomes most urgent when he describes a world in which the tasks people were trained to perform for others are being automated.

The chapter proceeds in five steps. Section 35.1 defines agency, first by what it is not (conformity), then through a sequence of formulations: the difference between employee and entrepreneur, Devon Eriksen's claim that agency rather than intelligence is the bottleneck of success, Koe's redefinition of agency as "the ability to iterate without permission," and Eriksen's typology of easy, impossible and hard problems. It closes with how agency is trained and how Koe applies, and later questions, the idea inside his own company. Section 35.2 widens the lens from agency to self-reliance: personal responsibility, "nobody is coming to save you," the seven traits of the "Irreplaceable individual," and the 2026 framework of individual sovereignty. Section 35.3 shows the terrain on which agency is exercised: a world so full of problems and opportunities that the abundance itself paralyzes. Section 35.4 presents the role Koe proposes for the agentic individual, the creator or value creator. Section 35.5 reconstructs the successive "skill stacks" Koe has recommended since 2023, ending with the 2026 post-AI hierarchy that places agency at the top.

Three features of the material shape the presentation. First, much of the core vocabulary comes from a third party, Devon Eriksen, and Koe later reuses some of it without attribution; the chapter keeps track of who said what. Second, Koe's position on agency moves: from "the most important skill" to "the meta-skill" to "not the only one thing," and from "act without permission" to "iterate without permission." Third, the skill stacks change almost every year. These changes are presented as they occurred.

### 35.1 Agency: Acting and Iterating Without Permission

#### Understanding agency by its opposite: conformity

Koe's most developed treatment of agency, a December 2025 video titled "The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now," opens not with a definition of agency but with a quotation about its opposite. He reads a line from Jiddu Krishnamurti: "It is only those who are in constant revolt that discover what is true, not the man who conforms, who follows some tradition." The quotation sets up the method of the section: agency will first be understood by what it is not.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

**Complementary context:** Jiddu Krishnamurti (1895–1986) was an Indian-born speaker and writer who rejected organized religion, gurus and inherited tradition as routes to truth, and who insisted that each person must discover what is true through direct, unmediated observation. Koe quotes him in several places in the corpus (Chapter 1 uses his remark that thought is conditioned and therefore mechanical).

What agency is not, Koe says, is "mechanical conformity." He then gives a definition that he treats as one of the central claims of the video: "Conformity is when your mind is still connected by an umbilical cord to society. Conformity is a stage of development where your mind operates entirely through cultural programming, judging truth based on popularity and acceptance by others rather than your own direct experience or independent investigation." He pauses to tell the viewer to relisten if the point did not land, calling conformity "one of the greatest threats to living a good life."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

The definition has three parts, and each connects to earlier chapters. The **umbilical cord** image says that the conformist mind has not yet been born as a separate organism: it still receives its nourishment, its beliefs, from the body of society. The phrase **a stage of development** places conformity on the developmental maps of Chapter 38 (in Susanne Cook-Greuter's sequence, which Koe uses elsewhere, "conformist" is an early stage of ego development), which means it is not a moral failure but a level that can be outgrown. The criterion, **judging truth by popularity and acceptance rather than direct experience or independent investigation**, is the same criterion that will reappear in Section 35.2 as the basis of self-reliance: true learning comes from directly experiencing something rather than taking advice as law.

Koe had stated the problem more bluntly ten months earlier, in a video about people with multiple interests: "The problem is that we conform, we copy other people, we lack vision for a future and the agency to make it a reality." In that sentence, conformity, copying, the absence of vision and the absence of agency are four aspects of a single condition. The sentence also states the logical relation that this chapter follows: vision supplies the direction (Chapter 7) and agency supplies the capacity to make it real.

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md (2025-02-23)

##### Why people conform: psychological survival

Koe's explanation of why conformity is so common draws on the "law of conceptual survival" from Chapter 3. Humans survive not only physically, as animals reproducing genes, but psychologically, "reproducing beliefs, ideas, and information." Whatever your survival depends on becomes a domain in which your agency is low. His first example is employment. "If you work a job, you have low agency in that domain": if the job went away, your survival would be at stake, so you must conform, first to get the job (you conform to what the boss likes) and then on the job (dress code, approved processes, perhaps a 9-to-5 schedule). His second example is ideology. Hard-set beliefs that bind you to a political party or a religious organization also mean low agency, because "your ideas of good and bad originate from your culture, not your own personal investigation or discovery."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

The argument is structural rather than moralizing. Conformity is not laziness; it is the rational behavior of a mind whose survival, material or conceptual, depends on others. That is why the remedy in Section 35.2 will be framed as reducing dependence (self-sufficiency) rather than as exhortation.

Koe then turns the argument on the very discourse he is joining. Everyone in tech, business and AI is talking about "being high agency," and that, too, "is just another form of conformity" to what is popular in that milieu: "if you're not talking about it, you're not with it." And he turns it on himself. His own video, he admits, contains some conformity: "we're all conformist in some ways," and since conformity is a survival strategy, "it can be used like a tool." He conforms, for example, to stay relevant on YouTube.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

This self-admission marks a change in the corpus. In February 2025 conformity was simply "the problem," and the Krishnamurti quotation that opens the December video praises "constant revolt." In the same December video, a few minutes later, conformity becomes something that can be used deliberately. Koe gives a reason (conformity is a survival strategy, and a strategy can be used as a tool) but does not reconcile the two positions explicitly. They can coexist if one distinguishes **mechanical conformity**, which is unconscious and defines the identity, from **conformity as a tool**, which is chosen consciously and serves a goal one has set oneself. The adjective "mechanical" in "agency is not mechanical conformity" supports this reading: what agency opposes is conformity that runs automatically, not every act of going along with a convention.

The distinction matters for application. A person who refuses every convention on principle is not exercising agency; they are reacting to society, which still keeps them attached to it, only negatively. A person who adopts a platform's formats because they serve a goal they chose is using conformity as an instrument. The test is not whether you follow a convention but whether the decision to follow it originates in your own direction or in your need for acceptance.

#### Agency as "the most important skill" and as the meta-skill

Having said what agency is not, the December 2025 video says why it matters. It opens with a prediction: "Most skills will be irrelevant in 10 to 20 years," unless you completely change how you think about success. For a high-agency individual the prediction does not matter, because "you aren't dependent on a specific skill for your success because you aren't a specialist." You did not narrow your mind, preventing learning outside a focus, in exchange for the status of a high-paying job or degree. Instead you have a vision and understand that today you can learn any skill or acquire any knowledge needed to achieve it.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

The argument reuses the critique of specialization from Chapter 20. The specialist's security rests on one skill continuing to be valuable; the high-agency person's security rests on the capacity to acquire whatever skill the vision requires. The first is a bet on the stability of the market; the second is a bet on oneself.

Koe adds a remark about inheritance that frames the rest of the video. If your parents did not cultivate agency in themselves, they probably did not pass it on to you, and unless you have "deliberately and painfully gone through the process of relearning," you have work to do before you can feel in control of your future. The word **relearning** is precise: agency is presented here as something that was not installed by the default program of Chapter 1 and must therefore be installed afterward, at a cost.

Then comes the thesis: "The most important skill to learn that will be relevant now, in 10 years, and until you die, is agency. Because if you can set your own life direction, do what is required to achieve it, and avoid the infinite number of temptations and distractions in today's world, you will never be at risk of replacement. And if you do get replaced, it doesn't matter because you can quickly adapt."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

This sentence contains a working definition with three components, and each component summarizes a part of this book:

| Component of agency (Dec 2025) | What it requires | Where the book develops it |
|---|---|---|
| Set your own life direction | Anti-vision, vision, goals | Part IV (Chapters 7–10) |
| Do what is required to achieve it | Focused work, learning, building | Parts V–VI (Chapters 12, 14–15) |
| Avoid the infinite temptations and distractions | Dopamine management, environment | Chapter 11 |

Read this way, agency is not one more skill alongside the others but the capacity that makes the whole system of the book operate under a person's own direction. That is exactly how Koe frames it two months later. In a February 2026 video on the future of work, he calls agency "the ability to act without permission or prompting": creating your own story, setting your own trajectory, not waiting to be told what to do or who to be. It is "the meta-skill" because it allows you to develop all the others. And he adds the sentence that links the topic to automation: "Without agency, you're just following someone else's path. And that path is being automated."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md (2026-02-12)

The February 2026 video also gives a method of practice that the December video does not: agency is practiced through what Koe calls the **three generators**, struggle, status and curiosity. **Struggle** means making deliberate choices, rejecting conformity and choosing your problems (the "chosen struggle" of Chapter 39). **Status** here means money as fuel for agency, a reading consistent with Chapter 34's account of money as a resource for building. **Curiosity** means filtering signal from noise and following your own threads instead of copying the last person who jumped on a trend: "how do you think you create trends?" The starting point, which he says also appears in his earlier video on "the most important skill to learn in the next 10 years," is to reject the default path ("school, job, retire at 65, that's over"), try something new, take one step even if you don't think it is right, make a mistake and correct it.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md (2026-02-12)

The phrase "three generators" is used in this source without a full definition of the framework; the corpus presents it in passing as the means of practicing agency, and the three terms are glossed only by the parenthetical explanations above.

##### Why AI raises the stakes: problems are infinite

The connection between agency and AI had appeared a year earlier, in December 2024, in a different form. Koe imagines an objection to his own business: if AI could teach you exactly how to write, why buy his course? His first answer is that the course contains his process, drawn from experience. His second answer goes further: even if AI could teach writing, "is that going to teach you how to master writing or do you still actually have to do the thing? ... is that the end of your education or does it continue going on, because knowledge creation is infinite." He then generalizes: "Why are you looking at AI as this thing that's just going to solve all problems... when problems never cease to exist? Even when AGI is here and the curve goes exponential, problems are infinite... progress, knowledge, problems are all infinite."

**Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md (2024-12-29)

From the infinity of problems he draws the practical consequence: what matters is "agency and being able to give yourself permission to solve problems and not rely on anyone else ever... to give you a problem to solve or a goal to achieve, because you will be replaced. Schools, jobs, etc., everything, reject it."

The logical chain is worth making explicit, because Section 35.3 builds on it. (1) Problems never cease to exist; solving one reveals others, and progress itself generates new ones. (2) Therefore AI, however capable, will not exhaust the supply of problems. (3) What becomes scarce is not problem-solving capacity in the abstract but the disposition to notice problems and to grant oneself permission to work on them. (4) Whoever waits to be assigned a problem depends on the assigner, and the assigner can replace them, with another person or with a machine. (5) Therefore the self-assignment of problems, which is agency, is the durable position. The phrase **give yourself permission** recurs throughout the chapter: it is the internal act that replaces the external permission that schools and employers used to grant.

#### Employee and entrepreneur: two states of mind

The most frequent formulation of agency in the corpus is a contrast between two figures. It appears first in July 2024, in a video whose title, "Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years," already announces the theme. After presenting vision, goals, systems and open-mindedness as earlier steps, Koe introduces a fifth step that he calls "arguably the most important," high agency: "we need to use that open-mindedness in the here and now to identify problems." Then the contrast: "The difference between an employee and an entrepreneur is agency. An employee is given a problem to solve to achieve the goals of the company they work for. An entrepreneur identifies and solves problems without the need to ask permission from someone or else they will never achieve their goals."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md (2024-07-13)

The definition is functional, not legal. What distinguishes the two figures is where the problem comes from (given versus identified) and whose goals it serves (the company's versus one's own). It also contains a reason why the entrepreneur cannot wait for permission: "or else they will never achieve their goals." Nobody else has an interest in assigning the entrepreneur the problems that lead to the entrepreneur's goals.

In February 2025 Koe restated the contrast as a pair of definitions that he would repeat, almost word for word, later that year: "The difference between an employee and an entrepreneur is the difference between low agency and high agency. High agency individuals are those who create their own goals and actively pursue them without permission from another. Low agency individuals are those who are assigned goals and pursue them because they don't have a mind that allows them to see any other option."

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md (2025-02-23)

The definition of low agency is the most revealing part. The low-agency person does not choose assigned goals over self-generated ones; they "don't have a mind that allows them to see any other option." This is the vocabulary of Chapters 1 and 6. A goal acts as a lens, and the mind perceives only what is relevant to the goals it already holds; a mind programmed with assigned goals literally does not perceive the alternatives. Low agency is therefore a perceptual limit before it is a behavioral one, which is why Koe's remedies always begin with awareness and exposure rather than with willpower.

The same passage adds two claims that the later versions drop. The first is a demanding condition: "True agency can only be developed when you blame yourself for every problem even when you're not at fault." The claim is presented as a principle of development, not as a moral judgment about fault. Its logic, which Koe does not spell out here but which follows from the rest of the corpus, is that blaming others transfers the solution to others: if the problem is someone else's fault, then the solution is someone else's responsibility, and the agent waits. Taking the problem as one's own, even when it is not one's fault, keeps the solution within one's own range of action. The same idea appears in Section 35.2 as "personal responsibility" and, negatively, in the "victim mindset."

The second claim corrects a possible misreading of the contrast: "An entrepreneur is often an employee but an employee is rarely an entrepreneur." At top companies, he says, the most sought-after employees are those with high agency: "they just go and solve the problem and deliver some kind of value ... they don't need their hand held. They're an entrepreneur in mind." The term **entrepreneur in mind** names the high-agency employee.

**Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md (2025-02-23)

The September 2025 version, from "12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months," repeats the definitions ("High agency individuals create their own goals and pursue them without permission. Low agency individuals are assigned goals and pursue them because their programming doesn't allow them to see other options") and adds the reframing that resolves the apparent conflict between the figures and their titles: "Stop thinking of employee and entrepreneur as titles. Think of them as states of mind. Employees are passive individuals told what to learn. Entrepreneurs are assertive individuals who set their own vision, learn by curiosity, and create solutions that push humanity forward." He introduces the redefinition by observing that "entrepreneurship" and "business" "have become dirty words," believed to be reserved for talented people with a lot of money and time. And he closes the passage with a claim about human nature: industrial-style schooling and employment "breed complacency and are dangerous for your psyche," because they go against our need for uncertainty, challenge and constant improvement: "growth, evolution. That's what the universe is doing."

**Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md (2025-09-14)

The final sentence links agency to the entropy model of Part III. If the universe tends toward growth and complexity (Koe reads evolution as the creation of order against entropy, Chapter 31), then a life arranged to avoid uncertainty and challenge is a life arranged against one's own nature. The claim is metaphysical and interpretive; Koe presents it as a worldview, not as an empirical finding.

##### "Are you a slave or are you free?"

The question that Koe uses to make the contrast personal appears in a November 2024 video on cheap dopamine. "It's the entrepreneur versus the employee mindset. You don't have to be an entrepreneur to have an entrepreneurial mindset; you can still be an employee that has high agency." The difference between the two "is agency: how often are you doing your own thing and working towards your own goals, whether it's in the company or not." And then: "Are you a slave or are you free is the question that I'm asking and I'm not saying physical slavery I'm saying mental with attention." The alternative is framed as "are you riveted to someone else's dream or are you building your own?"

**Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md (2024-11-24)

The clarification "mental with attention" places the question within the theory of attention of Chapter 6: if attention is the only root control a person has, then whoever directs your attention directs your life. The question is not about legal status but about who owns the attention. Its context matters too: the video is about dopamine, and the implication is that a person who spends their free attention on someone else's feed and their working attention on someone else's goals has given away both halves.

##### The employee mindset

Before these 2024–2025 formulations, a December 2023 video had described the employee mindset as a set of traits. "Most people dream of being the CEO of a billion-dollar company but can't even be the CEO of their own life." The employee mindset is very hard to break, so, the 2023 Koe advises, get out sooner to avoid further conditioning. Its traits are four: you don't pursue anything other than what you know; you don't develop skills beyond assigned work; you wait to be told what to do in work and in life; and "your parents are still your bosses even when you've moved out," because the programming still tugs at your choices. Entrepreneurs, by contrast, have traits "aligned with our ancestors and nature": they push into the unknown and create new paths, build solutions that advance humanity, and never stop acquiring and applying knowledge.

**Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md (2023-12-31)

The fourth trait is the most psychologically pointed. Moving out of the parental home does not end parental authority if the parents' programming continues to decide; the boss has simply been internalized. This is Chapter 1's thesis (the inherited operating system) applied to work.

The December 2025 video turns these traits into a diagnostic, which Koe calls the second "tell" of a high-agency person. Low-agency people are characterized by "the employee mindset": assigned a task, often accompanied by status or a credential that "triggers the part of their brain that craves acceptance by the tribe," their decision-making is immediately compromised; they cannot think outside the confines imposed on them. High-agency people, by contrast, "are scientists of their own lives." They have an idea, set their own goal, create a hypothesis (an educated guess) about how to achieve it, then test, tinker, research and attempt, and they fail a lot, which is part of the process: "they expect to fail because how else are they going to narrow down what doesn't work until they find what does." The counterexample is the person promised a high-paying job or a get-rich-quick business, who does exactly as told and, when they inevitably fail, deems the goal impossible and blames everyone but themselves, without noticing that they could correct the mistake and try again.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

The term **scientists of their own lives** connects agency to the self-experimentation of Chapter 15.4. The point is not that high-agency people fail less but that they interpret failure differently: as data that narrows the search, not as a verdict on possibility. The low-agency person's response to failure ("it's impossible") will reappear below as the collapse of hard problems into impossible ones.

##### How the contrast changed: from career advice to state of mind

These formulations do not all say the same thing, and the difference is not trivial. In December 2023 the employee mindset was a reason to leave employment early, before more conditioning set in. From November 2024 onward, Koe insists that "you can still be an employee that has high agency," and in 2025 he describes high-agency employees as the most sought-after at top companies and tells his audience to stop thinking of employee and entrepreneur as titles. The change is a refinement rather than a reversal: entrepreneurship stops being only a trajectory (leaving a job to start a business) and becomes a disposition that can exist inside employment. Koe does not present it as a correction; he presents it as a redefinition of words that had become "dirty." The two positions can coexist if one distinguishes the condition (the high-agency state of mind, available to anyone) from the path (one's own business, which for Koe remains the route to full control of time and income, as Section 35.5 will show in the "five steps").

#### The bottleneck is not intelligence: Devon Eriksen's argument

The most systematic theory of agency in the corpus does not belong to Koe. It comes from a December 2024 conversation with Devon Eriksen, a former software engineer and author of the science-fiction novel *Theft of Fire*, published as "The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen." Koe's own later formulations borrow heavily from it, so it is presented here in Eriksen's terms first.

**Complementary context:** the transcripts spell the name in several ways ("Devon Eriksen," "Devin Erickson," "Devon Ericson"). The quotations from an article titled "The third ingredient of success" are attributed in the transcript to "Devon Ericson"; the corpus treats this as probably the same person without confirming it. This chapter follows that reading with the same reservation.

Eriksen begins with a claim about rarity: agency is "the one most rare factor that determines highly successful people in life." His argument proceeds by comparison. Elon Musk is clearly very intelligent, with an IQ "probably 150 plus." But there are thousands upon thousands of people with IQs above 150 in the United States alone. Many of them are cloistered in academia, producing "postmodernist papers" or "slightly more useful" physics, and "they don't get this magical effect where everything they touch turns to gold." Therefore "the bottleneck is not intelligence." Intelligence is "only one of the things that is needed for success. It's necessary but it's not sufficient, and intelligence is a very narrow thing." What is special about "the Elon Musks of this world" is "intelligence coupled with a very high degree of agency."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The word **bottleneck** carries the argument. In a production system, a bottleneck is the step that limits total output; improving any other step does not increase output. Eriksen's claim is that among intelligent people, more intelligence does not produce more achievement, because what limits achievement is elsewhere.

Eriksen gives agency two linked formulations. The first: "agency is the tendency to initiate action to achieve your goals." The second: agency is "the belief that you will eventually be successful." What agency does, in his account, is make us willing to take risks and resilient to failure. Later in the conversation he adds that "it's about beliefs and it's about emotional resilience," and he characterizes it as "belief in the existence of hard problems" (developed below) and as the willingness to get moving, make mistakes and learn as you go.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The two formulations are not redundant. The first describes behavior (initiating action). The second describes the belief that sustains the behavior when the first attempts fail. A person can initiate action without believing in eventual success, and they will stop at the first failure; a person can believe in eventual success without initiating anything, and nothing will happen. Agency, in Eriksen's sense, is the combination.

##### Why raw intelligence is not enough: the missing feedback loop

Eriksen then explains why intelligence alone does not produce results. His argument is a chain, and its order matters:

1. We do not look at the world, think very smart thoughts and understand everything. "That's not how it works."
2. When we think very hard, we get a few things right and generally get most of it wrong.
3. So what we actually do is guess, test the guess, see some of it fail (perhaps some succeeds, "but usually it fails"), refine the guess and try again.
4. What "applying raw intelligence to the universe is missing is that feedback loop."
5. Intelligence "is just the ability to analyze. It doesn't give you data. It doesn't tell you what the universe is like."
6. Agency is what makes us willing to take the risks and absorb the failures that the loop requires.
7. Anything worth achieving that has not been done before fails the first time, "no matter how smart you are."
8. You therefore have to keep trying "until you eliminate all of the errors from your model or from your plan," and that requires the notion: "this problem is too hard for me to solve right now, but if I keep at it I will learn how to solve it and I will succeed."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The chain is a theory of knowledge in miniature. Intelligence processes data, but data comes from contact with reality, and contact with reality, for any new undertaking, means failing. Whoever cannot tolerate failing does not get the data, and whoever does not get the data cannot apply their intelligence to anything real. This is the cybernetic model of Chapter 6.4 (intelligence as steering by feedback) seen from the side of emotion: the loop of action, error and correction runs only if the person can bear the error.

Step 8 is the bridge to Koe's later redefinition of agency as iteration. "Keep trying until you eliminate all of the errors from your model" is already a definition of agency as sustained iteration; Koe will make it explicit a year later.

##### Koe's reading: Musk's impossible vision

During the same conversation Koe offers his own reading of the Musk example, and it shifts the emphasis from intelligence to vision. He contrasts people who like the thought of a very high goal but, because it seems impossible, "write it off as if they can't do it," with Musk, who "has this massive vision. He knows that it's not possible right now, and it wasn't before, but he built the steps." Koe uses this to explain why agency matters: the person with agency does not ask whether the goal is possible now but whether the steps toward it can be built.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The reading connects agency to the "big irrational goals" of Chapter 7.5. A goal that is impossible with present means becomes workable when it is decomposed into steps, each of which is merely hard. This is exactly the typology that Eriksen develops later in the conversation (easy, impossible, hard), and Koe's example anticipates it.

##### Necessity versus sufficiency, and the disagreement about intelligence

Eriksen draws two further conclusions, both polemical. The first concerns education. We constantly produce people with high IQs, he says; when you read a magazine interview with someone whose IQ is 180 or 200, "you never heard of this guy." "The only reason we really care about intelligence is because it's a component of getting things done," and there are other components that civilization could make a much better effort to teach high-IQ children, "instead of just shoving them into government schools and then... postgraduate education," and then wondering why academia produces nothing but criticism of old literature (his polemical examples include Michel Foucault). His conclusion: "we've confused the condition of necessity with the condition of sufficiency." For groundbreaking progress, high IQ is necessary but not sufficient, "and we are not grooming our high IQ children for success."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

**Complementary context:** in logic, a **necessary condition** is one without which the outcome cannot occur; a **sufficient condition** is one that guarantees the outcome. Eriksen's claim is that intelligence belongs to the first category but has been treated as if it belonged to the second, so that schools select for it and then expect achievement to follow.

The second conclusion is about what can be changed. "You can't make yourself more intelligent. Intelligence is biological." The "narrow band analytical ability, IQ-type intelligence" is determined very early in biological development and is "a physical and chemical feature of the brain; there's not much you can do about that." By contrast, "agency is a skill you can learn, and more importantly you can teach it to your kids." You can raise them with an attitude that lets them go out, do hard and interesting things, and "fail and fail and fail and fail until eventually they succeed." Agency is "about beliefs and it's about emotional resilience."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

Here Eriksen marks a disagreement explicitly. He acknowledges that Koe disagrees with him in some videos and says that Koe is "defining intelligence more broadly than I am." The disagreement is real and unresolved in the corpus. Eriksen means a narrow analytical capacity measured by IQ tests. Koe, in other videos (Chapter 17 and Chapter 38), uses "intelligence" in broader senses: a pragmatic one (iterating, persisting, getting what you want, a definition he associates with Naval Ravikant) and a holistic one (pattern recognition, the capacity to zoom out until boundaries dissolve), which can be widened through perspective. Both men agree that agency, not intelligence, is the bottleneck. They disagree about whether intelligence itself can grow. The two positions can coexist if the reader keeps the two meanings of "intelligence" apart: Eriksen's narrow band is fixed (on his account); Koe's broad sense is developable (on his).

##### Agency does not abolish fear

One more element of Eriksen's account prevents a romantic reading of agency. Most people, he says, do not want to take risks: "most people don't want to do greenfield development of something that's never been done before because that's scary, unless you have a whole lot of agency, and then it's still scary but you could stand it." **Greenfield development**, a software term for building something new from scratch rather than modifying an existing system, stands here for any undertaking without precedent. The point is that agency does not remove fear; it makes fear tolerable. Elsewhere in the same conversation, speaking to aspiring authors, Eriksen says that "the fear is constant" even as success begins.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

This qualification matters for practice. A reader who waits to stop being afraid before acting has misunderstood agency as a feeling. In Eriksen's account it is a capacity to act while the feeling persists.

#### Agency, opportunity and intelligence: Koe's version

A year after the Eriksen conversation Koe draws his own conclusion from the abundance of access. "You now have access to any knowledge you'd ever need," yet people still do nothing with it, which he calls "a crucial point." "Success is now easier than ever. Yet the people who weren't going to achieve it still aren't going to achieve it. Meaning this was never about access or equal opportunity. It's always been about agency." High-agency people will outpace everyone else by ten times, he predicts, because they act without permission and the barriers to action are now nearly nonexistent. For someone who cannot reach a big goal because of limited money or resources, he suggests a **stepping stone goal**: a smaller goal whose purpose is to acquire the resources the larger one requires.

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

The argument has the structure of a natural experiment, although Koe does not present it as one. If access had been the obstacle, removing it should have changed who succeeds. In his reading, removing it has not changed much, so the obstacle was elsewhere. The argument is an interpretation of observation, not a measured finding, and its premise (that the same people fail regardless of access) is asserted rather than demonstrated.

A week later, in "How to fix your entire life in 1 day," Koe placed agency within a broader **success formula with three ingredients**. One is agency, discussed in his previous video. One is **opportunity**, "which many people like to mistake as privilege because they lack the other ingredients." The last is **intelligence**. The ingredients limit one another. With high agency and low opportunity, it does not matter how likely you are to act; the goal will not bear much fruit. With opportunity and agency but low intelligence, "you'll never fully benefit." Koe admits what he cannot control: your physical location (he could tell you to move, but that is a big ask). But if you do not see the opportunity of the internet in front of you, "I don't know what to tell you."

**Source:** How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md (2025-12-28)

**Complementary context:** the Eriksen article Koe quotes in the same month is titled "The third ingredient of success." The title suggests that the three-ingredient framing may come from Eriksen's article, but the transcript does not make the connection explicit, and Koe presents the formula as his own.

Two tensions are visible when the formula is placed beside the earlier material. The first concerns intelligence. In December 2024 both Koe and Eriksen agreed that intelligence was not the bottleneck; in December 2025 Koe restores intelligence as one of three ingredients, without whose presence "you'll never fully benefit." This is not a reversal (Eriksen had also called intelligence necessary), but the emphasis has moved, and Koe does not reconcile the two statements. The second tension is internal to the same month: on December 21 success "was never about access or equal opportunity, it's always been about agency"; on December 28 opportunity is one of three ingredients. The two claims can be read together if "access" in the first means access to knowledge, which is now nearly universal, and "opportunity" in the second means the concrete situation in which one acts, which still varies by place. Koe's own example (moving location) supports that reading, but he does not state it.

#### From acting to iterating without permission

Between July 2024 and October 2025, Koe's definitions of agency all turn on the act of beginning. In July 2024 the entrepreneur "identifies and solves problems without the need to ask permission." In December 2024 agency is doing "what you want without permission from someone else." In October 2025, "Agency is the ability to act without permission." The December 2025 video changes the verb.

Its first idea, Koe says, is that "agency is the ability to iterate without permission." He presents this explicitly as a "slight redefinition" of the popular online definition, "the ability to act without permission," and hedges before making it: "maybe that's the definition... but I want to redefine it in a way that is actually useful." He starts from the dictionary: agency means "the condition of being in action or operation," and for a person, "the tendency to initiate action towards a goal without outside prompting, instruction, or permission." But, he argues, what makes people successful is not just acting. Anyone can start a business, and most fail, because they miss "the one critical piece": if something does not work, reflect, adjust and try again, over and over, until you reach the destination. Agency is therefore "not only action, but an undying commitment to iteration, learning and doing in unison, making mistakes and correcting mistakes without being seduced back into a comforting conformity because it's not working." And, addressing his audience directly: "Yes, I'm talking to you people who start and quit after 2 weeks."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

Two coined terms appear in the passage. **Iterate without permission** is the redefined agency: action plus sustained correction. **Comforting conformity** names what failure tempts you back to. The phrase reveals the mechanism of quitting. When an attempt fails, the person is not simply discouraged; they are drawn back toward the conformist state described at the beginning of this section, where decisions are made by the group and nobody can blame you for following the default. Quitting is a return to the umbilical cord.

The redefinition takes up what Eriksen had said a year earlier in step 8 of his chain ("keep trying until you eliminate all of the errors from your model"). Koe does not cite Eriksen for this point, but the convergence is clear: Eriksen's "belief that you will eventually be successful" is precisely what sustains iteration after the first failure. The change from "act" to "iterate" moves the focus from the initial decision to the sustained process. The later definition includes the earlier one (every iteration begins with an action), so this is a refinement rather than a contradiction. The reason Koe gives is empirical in tone: most of those who act without permission start and then quit.

##### The subject of the sentence

In the same video, Koe uses a quotation from the article "The third ingredient of success," attributed in the transcript to Devon Ericson, as the first "tell" of high agency: "To have agency is to be the subject of a sentence rather than its direct object. It is the tendency to act rather than wait to be acted upon." He then extends it with his redefinition: high-agency people "iterate without permission."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

The grammatical metaphor is compact and precise. In "the company assigned me a project," the speaker is the object; in "I chose a project," the speaker is the subject. The metaphor also clarifies the relation to conformity: the conformist's life is narrated in the passive voice, with society as the implicit agent.

##### Nobody will give you permission

Several formulations from different years converge on the same psychological point: permission is not coming. In May 2024, in a video on self-discipline, Koe calls it "the first realization that you have to make: nobody is going to give you permission to do what you want." The reason he gives is informational. Others were not exposed to your information; "they don't have the story that you were telling yourself, unless they are developed enough to open their mind to that story." Waiting for others' approval of a direction they cannot see is waiting for something structurally impossible. Hence the image: "You have to jump out of the nest at some point and trust that you can learn to fly."

**Source:** It's actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md (2024-05-12)

The nest image will return in negative form in 2025, when Koe describes the low-agency individual as one who "will stay in its nest waiting for mama bird to bring it a worm" (below). The two images bracket the same choice: leave the nest and learn to fly by trying, or remain and be fed by others.

Eriksen gives the point an economic edge in the December 2024 conversation, speaking about writers: "That's that agency again. You don't wait for someone to say you can create... you say I can create and I will do so whether you wish it or not." He then describes what the absence of agency costs: "There are so many new authors who are so much in doubt about whether their work has value that they will offer up 80% of their earnings to... Tor Books or something, in exchange for what basically amounts to a pat on the head."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

**Complementary context:** Tor Books is a major American science-fiction and fantasy publisher. Eriksen's figure of 80% is his rhetorical estimate of the share of earnings a traditionally published author gives up relative to self-publishing; it is not a contractual figure stated in the source.

The example converts the abstract idea of permission into a price. The author who needs external validation pays for it, and the payment is most of the value they create. This is the same logic as Chapter 25's argument about distribution: whoever does not own the channel to the audience pays whoever does.

##### Doing what you want without permission, and when it becomes a mistake

In December 2024 Koe gives a version of the principle that sounds, at first, like a license: "Do what you want without permission from someone else, that's agency." Go to the party, get drunk, start the business, scroll all night: "denying those desires is only going to bind you to them." The catch follows immediately. You must realize when those things are mistakes. Getting drunk every night is not a mistake if you have no meaningful responsibilities, because it does not hurt any goal. Managing parties, alcohol and vices becomes much easier when they affect something more important. If you do not care about going out and showing up hungover to school or a job, that indicates that the school or job is not more important to you, which means you are not happy in it, however you justify it ("I like the structure"). Koe then draws a psychological conclusion: you are unfulfilled because you have not poured your heart and soul into something so that it becomes your purpose and passion; if you had, you would not want to harm your performance on it, because it would be the source of your fulfillment.

**Source:** How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md (2024-12-15)

The heuristic is more subtle than its opening line. It does not say that every desire should be indulged. It says that the strength of a vice is a measurement of the absence of a goal: where a goal you care about exists, the vice becomes a visible mistake and is easier to drop; where no goal exists, suppressing the vice by will only binds you to it. This is the thesis of Chapter 6.6 ("without a goal there is no problem") applied to habits, and it agrees with Chapter 11's treatment of dopamine. Agency, in this version, includes both the freedom to act on desire and the judgment to recognize when a desire has become an obstacle to something larger.

#### Belief in hard problems: easy, impossible and hard

Eriksen's most influential contribution to the corpus is a framework. "I like to characterize agency as belief in the existence of hard problems." He divides tasks into three categories:

| Category | Eriksen's definition (Dec 2024) | Eriksen's examples |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | Things we can do the moment we turn our hand to them, with the intelligence, skills, knowledge and resources we already have | Going to the store to buy a carton of milk |
| Impossible | Things you cannot do with your capabilities and resources and where you do not know how to overcome the distance between you and them, so "there's no meaningful attempt"; no motivational speech changes this | Eating a bicycle ("your teeth don't work that way"); tunneling through the center of the earth to Java |
| Hard | "You can't do this right now but you can learn if you keep trying" | (The category the framework exists to make visible) |

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

"The trick," Eriksen says, "is to raise children who believe in a third category of tasks, those are hard." The implication, which he does not state in a single sentence but which follows from the framework, is that low agency consists in collapsing everything that is not easy into the impossible. For a low-agency mind there are only two categories: what I can already do and what cannot be done. The hard category, where all learning and all new achievement occur, is invisible. Eriksen places the teaching of agency "on an individual parental level," because "if children have agency, then even if there's something you miss, they can address it themselves as an adult because they believe they can."

The framework explains much of what came before. The Musk example is a vision placed in the hard category by someone who "built the steps." The person promised a quick business who fails and "deems it impossible" has reclassified a hard problem as impossible after one attempt. And "belief in the existence of hard problems" is a more precise statement of Eriksen's second definition, "the belief that you will eventually be successful": the belief is not general optimism but the conviction that a specific class of problems exists that yields to persistent effort.

##### A long-term hard problem: Eriksen's anarcho-capitalism

Later in the conversation Eriksen applies the category to his own political goal, and the example shows the scale at which the framework can be used. Calling himself an anarcho-capitalist, he says, "is not about the methods I want to use, it's about my goals": he wants eventually to make government obsolete and to get rid of it. He knows that government currently does certain things that we do not know how to do otherwise, "so I need to build a new tool or I need to help my species build a new tool." "It's a long-term goal. It's a hard problem that we will face and fail and fail and fail at until eventually we learn how to succeed." His illustration: "We as a species recently figured out how to do money without a government, but we haven't yet figured out how to do criminal justice without a government, so we need another tool... I don't know exactly, but let's work at it."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

Koe connects the example to "we can't do it now but we can build the tool," and adds a point of his own: a goal, "once you achieve it, may change when you realize, okay, this was a mistake." Eriksen agrees ("absolutely"). The exchange is useful precisely because the political content is contentious. The framework does not depend on agreeing with the goal; it describes a stance toward any goal whose means do not yet exist. Koe's addition, that achieved goals may turn out to be mistakes, links the framework to the iterative view of goals in Chapter 8: goals are hypotheses, and their achievement is itself feedback.

##### Koe's version: three buckets of goals

Ten months later, in an October 2025 video titled "You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape," the framework reappears as part of Koe's own argument. He presents a third step, "arguably the most important": teach yourself and your children to be high agency. "Agency is the ability to act without permission. Therefore agency is the belief that difficult tasks can become easy, because goals fall into three buckets": easy goals (doable with current knowledge, skill and resources), impossible goals (things you cannot do, or that are "outside the laws of physics"), and difficult goals (things you cannot do right away but eventually can with the right skills and resources).

**Source:** You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md (2025-10-05)

He adds a thought experiment. Imagine a gun to your head and the instruction to bench-press 315 pounds within a year. You either interpret the task as impossible and let yourself die, or you do everything in your power and probably achieve it, learning and doing everything required. And later in the same passage he adds a sentence that changes the framework's scope: "Agency isn't just taking action, it's deciding what actions are worth taking."

Three differences separate this version from Eriksen's, and they are worth stating because the source does not.

1. **Attribution.** Eriksen is not named. The typology that was "belief in the existence of hard problems" in December 2024 is presented in October 2025 as Koe's own "three buckets."
2. **The definition of "impossible."** For Eriksen, impossible means that you do not know how to overcome the distance between yourself and the goal, a psychological and epistemic condition. For Koe in 2025, impossible means what you cannot do or what lies "outside the laws of physics." The narrower definition enlarges the category of the difficult: almost everything that does not violate physics becomes, in principle, learnable.
3. **What is being classified.** Eriksen classifies tasks in order to describe a belief; Koe classifies goals in order to choose among them. The added sentence ("deciding what actions are worth taking") moves agency from the willingness to act toward judgment about where to act, a move that anticipates the place of "taste" just below agency in the 2026 hierarchy (Section 35.5).

The two versions can coexist: Eriksen's describes a psychological belief, Koe's a practical classification. But the reader should know that the framework originated with Eriksen.

The gun thought experiment also needs a caution. It is a rhetorical device designed to show that what feels impossible is usually only hard when the motivation is extreme; it is not evidence that any particular goal is achievable in a year. Its function is to expose how quickly the low-agency mind files a difficult goal under "impossible."

##### Two problems in average thinking

The same October 2025 passage continues with a diagnosis of what Koe calls average thinking. It has two problems.

The first is an **external locus of control**: "believing that all great accomplishments are a matter of luck rather than skill." When such people need help, they look to friends, the government, anyone but themselves; their first reaction is to blame rather than to think or take responsibility.

**Complementary context:** "locus of control" is a concept from personality psychology, associated with Julian Rotter (1954, 1966), describing the degree to which people attribute outcomes to their own actions (internal locus) or to external forces such as luck, fate or other people (external locus). Koe uses the term in this standard sense.

The second problem is missing what Koe calls **the intersection of importance and urgency**. Such people have not educated themselves or experimented enough to generate passion around a specific life task, and they are not aware of where their life is heading without that aim. "They are not pushed or pulled in any direction by their own desire, but they are thrown around like a puppet by someone else's." The coined phrase is not defined further in the source; the context suggests it names a task that is both important to the person (it connects to what they care about) and urgent (it creates pressure to act now), and that the coincidence of the two is what generates passion and direction. This reading is consistent with Chapter 8.7, where passion is created by investing energy in a goal, but the source does not elaborate.

From the two problems Koe draws a conclusion about education and a final image: "Education is not about math or spelling or historical facts. It's about instilling agency." "A high agency individual will learn everything they need to in order to do what they want to do. A low agency individual will stay in its nest waiting for mama bird to bring it a worm."

**Source:** You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md (2025-10-05)

The first sentence is a redefinition of education that connects to Chapter 2's distinction between training and true education and to Chapter 14's "meta skill" of learning. The second makes the generalist of Chapter 20 a consequence of agency: the high-agency person learns "everything they need to," whatever domain it belongs to, because the goal, not the curriculum, decides what is learned.

#### How agency is trained

If agency is a skill rather than a fixed trait, it must be trainable, and the corpus contains three methods for training it: Eriksen's method for adults, Koe's five practices of 2025 and Koe's process of December 2025.

##### Eriksen: the belief muscle

Asked what people can do to practice agency, especially adults who went through conventional schooling, Eriksen gives a short sequence. First, start thinking about your own emotional makeup. Second, try to notice the ways in which you are risk-averse: where you are too afraid to do something although there is something you want on the other side. Third, realize that watching YouTube success stories and motivational videos is not the answer: "belief in your ability to succeed is like a muscle; it has to be trained." Fourth, being aware of this, make a plan with goals that "you can't do right now but they're achievable in steps." The mechanism is cumulative: each successful step trains the belief "that if you work at something you can't do now, you will eventually succeed."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The method follows directly from the framework of hard problems. If low agency is the inability to see the hard category, then the training consists in accumulating personal evidence that the category exists: goals that were not easy, that were not impossible, and that yielded to steps.

Eriksen's concrete analogy is weightlifting, which he calls "actually excellent for this." You do not go to the gym and bench 300 pounds on the first day. As a beginner, you put weight on the bar and lift until you cannot anymore; next time you add "like three or five more pounds" and think, "wow, I got better." This "is not only making you stronger, but it trains you in the belief that if you work at something you can't do now, you will eventually succeed."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md (2024-12-08)

The analogy works on two levels. Literally, progressive overload in the gym produces measurable progress in a short time, so it is a fast source of evidence for the belief. Figuratively, it describes the structure that every hard goal should be given: small, measurable increments, each just beyond the previous capacity. The same image of progressive overload appears in Chapter 15.2 as a principle of skill acquisition and in the gradual model of identity change in Chapter 4; the corpus also contains the opposite register, the advice to "be extreme" and change everything at once. Koe alternates between the two without resolving them; the agency material sits on the gradual side, with the important exception of the gun thought experiment, which uses extreme pressure as a heuristic.

##### Koe's five practices (October 2025)

In the October 2025 video, right after the three buckets, Koe lists five practical steps to practice agency:

1. "Do not allow others to help you for as long as possible, even if you struggle."
2. When you want something, practice taking small steps to learn about it and act on it.
3. "Drop out of school or request to be homeschooled." He acknowledges that this advice carries baggage, but only, he says, for those who think lacking credentials will ruin your life; he declines to expand.
4. "Consciously expose yourself to difficult problems at least every 3 to 6 months": build new projects, study new material.
5. "Question what you are supposed to do and make a different decision."

Most often, he adds, this leads to entrepreneurship and self-direction, because schools and jobs have values you must conform to, which narrow your mind and limit your ability to learn and act.

**Source:** You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md (2025-10-05)

Each practice maps onto an element of the theory. Refusing help for as long as possible is a way of entering the hard category voluntarily, since help converts hard problems into easy ones before the person has learned anything. Small steps are Eriksen's stepwise goals. Leaving school applies Section 35.1's argument that dependence on an institution lowers agency in its domain; it is also the most contestable practice on the list, and Koe himself flags its "baggage" without arguing for it in detail. The periodic exposure to difficult problems keeps the belief muscle under load, much as Chapter 10.5 asks the reader to stay at the edge between boredom and anxiety. Questioning what one is "supposed to do" attacks conformity directly.

The first practice deserves a qualification the source does not provide. Taken literally and without limit, refusing all help would contradict other parts of the corpus, which recommend mentors, courses and studying "processes others have found success with" (see the next subsection). The phrase "for as long as possible" suggests that the point is the order of operations: struggle first, so that help, when it comes, lands on a mind that has already engaged with the problem.

##### Koe's process for practicing agency (December 2025)

The December 2025 video closes with a process that reorganizes several earlier ideas. First, you need something to pursue, "and it can be anything, because nobody actually knows what they want. Instead, they deeply understand what they don't want and allow that to create an aim for their future." That gives a direction; then set a goal to make it practical and achievable. Then five steps follow:

1. Research the processes others have found success with: YouTube, social media, courses from reputable creators or mentors.
2. Experiment with various techniques, implementing them and attempting results ("most of these won't work for you, and that's okay").
3. Identify patterns, principles and levers: the most important aspects, which tend to be what produces results.
4. Create your own process, tailored to your lifestyle and situation.
5. Pass it down to others, "because the teacher learns more than the student, and you don't truly understand it if you can't explain it in a way that is beneficial to someone else."

**Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md (2025-12-21)

The process repeats, with two additions, the five-step self-experimentation process Koe had described in 2024 (Chapter 15.4). The first addition is the preceding step of direction derived from what one does not want, the anti-vision of Chapter 7. The second is the word **levers** in step three. The process is therefore a compression of the book's own path: anti-vision and vision (Part IV), learning through building (Chapter 14), self-experimentation (Chapter 15), creating one's own philosophy (Chapter 17.2) and teaching (Chapter 14.5). That Koe presents it as the way to practice agency is consistent with the thesis that agency is the meta-skill: practicing it means running the whole system under one's own direction.

Placed side by side, the three methods share a structure: a direction chosen by the person, steps calibrated just beyond present capacity, failure treated as information, and a gradual accumulation of the belief that hard problems yield. They differ in emphasis. Eriksen's method is psychological (notice risk aversion, train belief). The five practices are environmental (refuse help, leave school, expose yourself periodically). The December process is epistemic (research, experiment, extract principles, build your own process).

#### Agency inside a team: ownership and the freedom to make mistakes

Agency is usually discussed as an individual trait, but the corpus also shows Koe thinking about it inside the company he co-founded, the writing software that the 2024 transcript spells "Cortex" and that later appears as "Kortex" (its history is told in Chapter 37).

In July 2024, right after defining agency as the difference between employee and entrepreneur, Koe illustrates it with his own team: "I'm incredibly grateful that our team is high agency." He does not see them as employees, "one, because they aren't, they're contractors," but as "entrepreneurs collaborating with entrepreneurs, or one-person businesses collaborating with one-person businesses." "They hold the same vision for our product. When they spot a problem, they solve it and share the solution with us, even if we have to make small changes to that solution. But even then, we can't improve on a solution that doesn't exist." His example is a developer named Ian, who spotted a problem, worked on it in the background, and "came to us with a complete app redesign." "Yes, it set us back a bit, but the app is in so much better a spot now, and I don't know where we would be without that singular person exercising his agency." In such an environment, Koe adds, Ian will be compensated "in more ways than money, but money too."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md (2024-07-13)

The sentence "we can't improve on a solution that doesn't exist" states a principle of collaboration under agency. A team that waits for permission produces no drafts; a team in which people act produces imperfect drafts that others can refine. The cost (the redesign "set us back a bit") is accepted because the alternative is nothing to improve. The same principle appears at the individual level in Chapter 29.4 as "permission to suck."

In January 2025, in a conversation with the founders of Stan, the co-founder Vitali offered what amounts to the leader's side of the same principle. One of the hardest lessons for him was "learning how to trust people to make mistakes." Anyone good, he says, has "incredibly high standards for the quality of their work"; as you lead dozens of people of different skill levels, some work is "just not good enough," and you are tempted to "go in... with my hands, just do it better, because I can." "But then you're taking away an opportunity from a person to learn, and then learning is what creates, down the line, leverage for you that enables you to do even less work of that kind, so you can do even more work of the kind that is truly fulfilling." His recommendation is to "build a skill of tolerance and feedback loops."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28)

Vitali's lesson is the organizational version of Eriksen's feedback loop. People build agency by acting, failing and correcting; a leader who corrects their work for them removes the failure and therefore the learning. Vitali's argument is also self-interested in the sense Section 35.2 will defend: the leader's tolerance is an investment in their own future leverage.

By November 2025 Koe's tone had changed. In "Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake," he asks how to apply his worldview to a team, not to impose it but to make sense of the team so that he can best guide or lead: "I can lead myself just fine... sure, I'm an idiot sometimes." The question frustrates him. He feels he needs to guide people, "but it's also a value of mine to let people do their own thing," and he does not know where to draw the boundary. He wants everyone to feel that they partly own the company, with "the ownership mindset to the point where they can make decisions, solve problems on their own" ("whether they want it or not is another problem"). He calls this "a very interesting and rewarding challenge," "new sets of problems... better sets of problems."

**Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md (2025-11-24)

The movement between the two Koe statements belongs to a larger correction documented in Chapter 37. In 2024 the autonomy of high-agency contractors appears as a strength, illustrated by Ian's unrequested redesign. In the same November 2025 video, Koe diagnoses the team's original structure, compartmentalized by technology "like big tech," as a cause of slowness, admits that he did not know how to manage a team, and notes elsewhere the value of in-person work. The two positions can be partly reconciled: individual agency and team structure are different dimensions, and a loose federation of contractors may suit an early phase but not a growing organization. What the 2025 statement adds to the theory of agency is a genuine tension that the earlier material ignored. Valuing other people's agency ("let people do their own thing") and leading them toward a shared goal pull in different directions, and the **ownership mindset** cannot be imposed without contradicting itself. Koe leaves the tension open and reframes it, characteristically, as a "better set of problems," which is the attitude Section 35.4 will describe as falling in love with the challenge that problems present.

#### The 2026 correction: "high agency" as a buzzword

The claims of December 2025 and February 2026 were strong: agency was "the most important skill... until you die" and "the meta-skill." In June 2026, in "How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You," Koe qualified them. Everyone is obsessed with being high agency, he observes; it is a buzzword that tech bros popularized, copying each other because telling people to be high agency goes viral, and "them just doing that alone signals that they're not high agency, they're low agency." Agency does matter, he grants: initiating action toward a goal is what distinguishes many entrepreneurs. But "it's not the only one thing." And theory will not produce it: "Theory, and tweets about becoming high agency will not make you any more high agency." Agency, taste, persuasion, persistence and iteration "aren't your typical high value skills that you can go and watch a YouTube video about"; "the only way to practice them is to start doing your own thing."

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md (2026-06-28)

The correction has two parts. The first applies the December 2025 argument about conformity to the agency discourse itself: repeating a popular slogan about agency is conformity, so the loudest advocates of "high agency" demonstrate its absence. Koe had made a version of this point in December 2025 ("just another form of conformity"); in June 2026 he makes it sharper. The second part is a change of emphasis. Agency moves from being *the* skill to being one ingredient among five (agency, taste, persuasion, persistence, iteration, a list that Section 35.5 will relate to the 2026 skill hierarchy). Koe does not explain the change, and the corpus elsewhere contains a general caution, voiced by Koe himself, that his many "most important skill" claims (writing, persuasive communication, agency) should be read as rhetorical emphasis rather than as literal rankings. Even within the December 2025 video, agency is called both "the most important skill" and, at another point, "not a skill or a trait, but an art form," so the status of agency was never entirely stable.

What the correction does not change is the method. In every version, from Eriksen's weight plates to the June 2026 statement, agency is acquired only by acting on one's own projects. The irony Koe points to, that people consume content about agency instead of exercising it, is the "tutorial hell" of Chapter 14.3 applied to this very topic. A reader of this section is in the same position.

### 35.2 Self-Reliance and Individual Sovereignty

Agency, as Section 35.1 defined it, is a disposition to act and iterate on one's own direction. Self-reliance is the broader condition that agency produces and requires: a life in which judgment, learning, energy and income do not depend on someone else's decision. The vocabulary of this section is older than the vocabulary of agency. Koe was speaking about self-reliance and personal responsibility in 2022, two years before the conversation with Eriksen, and the section ends with his most recent synthesis, the 2026 triad of individual sovereignty.

#### Self-reliance as a value

In October 2022, in a video arguing that high-value people should start one-person businesses, Koe says that self-reliance has brought him the most value of anything, "as values should." His example is from his own business. When people were "screaming" at him to go do short-form video or to hop on the next business model, he already had something good going and chose to go in the opposite direction, writing long-form newsletters instead. Figuring things out for himself, he says, taught him more than any course or college. From this he generalizes: "true learning is by directly experiencing something, by not taking people's advice as law, testing it for yourself." He extends the principle to health and to "any ideology or belief system."

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md (2022-10-02)

Later in the same video he connects self-reliance to teaching. If you pay someone and follow their advice "to a T," you have not learned anything for yourself, so you cannot teach it from your own unique point of view. His example is the fitness coach whose program you follow exactly: the result may be good, but the understanding belongs to the coach. And in the one-person business, he adds, self-reliance also means being self-aware.

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md (2022-10-02)

This is an early form of a thesis that runs through the book: the value a person can sell is the understanding they have built through their own experiments (Chapter 33.3, "productize yourself"). Self-reliance is therefore not only a personal virtue but an economic precondition. Whoever borrows all their methods has nothing original to distill.

##### Self-reliance cannot be taught

In August 2023, in "I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money)," Koe gives self-reliance a paradoxical definition: "Nobody can teach you self-reliance, otherwise it's not self-reliance." The definition is followed by a practical claim. To become self-reliant in any domain (money, relationships, health), you need the skills that bridge the gap between where you are and what you want. Most people do not know what they want, "and even more don't see where they are as a problem." Koe stresses the word "need": these skills are not optional but fundamental, and you should at least have a big-picture understanding of them and learn them first.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

The two halves fit together. The disposition of self-reliance cannot be transmitted, because receiving it from someone else would contradict it; but the skills that make self-reliance possible can be learned. The video then lists those skills, and the list (the "meta skills" of Section 35.5) is the practical side of the concept. The phrase "don't see where they are as a problem" is also the bridge to Section 35.3: self-reliance begins with perceiving one's present situation as a problem to be solved rather than as a fixed condition.

The same video opens with an example that illustrates the definition by its absence. Rich kids, Koe argues, are not self-reliant, because they did not teach themselves to be. Success is so normal to them that they have no new potentials to discover and no journey: no goal like making $1,000 a month doing their own thing, with (1) the emotional labor that comes with it, from which one learns and increases one's emotional tolerance, and (2) all the failures that lead to nuanced understanding and experience. When you achieve the $1,000 goal, the next one reveals itself, because "the aim of your life, it's what gives you meaning, is to consistently make progress." Not all rich kids are depressed and socially inept, he concedes, "but many are," because they had no reason to cultivate a skill set to reach a **self-generated goal**.

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

The example is an opinion based on observation, not a study, and Koe qualifies it ("not all"). Its function is to isolate the variable. Rich children have resources, access and opportunity, the ingredients that Section 35.1 discussed, and yet, on Koe's account, many of them lack direction and meaning. What they lack is the self-generated goal and the struggle toward it. The example therefore supports the claim that access was never the issue, and it connects self-reliance to the definition of meaning as progress that Chapter 39 develops.

##### Why only self-reflection can tell you whether an action was good

In a March 2024 video on deep work and monk mode, Koe gives an epistemological argument for self-reliance. "It is impossible to have 100% certainty in the actions you take now and how they're going to impact your future," because with cause and effect "you don't immediately have the effect of your actions." You can approximate by taking advice or by comparing yourself to others' results, "but the only way you're truly going to know for certain whether they were good or bad actions is through self-reflection, and then pivoting from there." Even good advice comes from someone "operating from a completely different worldview in a completely different environment." His example: taking advice from Socrates, from centuries ago, for a world "where money rules" may not be as practical as you think. "You still need to filter through direct experience."

**Source:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md (2024-03-21)

The argument has two premises: effects are delayed, and advice is context-bound. Together they mean that no external source can evaluate your actions for you; it lacks either the information (it does not know your results) or the context (it does not live in your world). The conclusion is not that advice is useless but that it is input to a loop that only you can close. This is the same feedback loop as in Eriksen's account of agency, now applied to judgment rather than to action.

#### Personal responsibility and its opposite

Two months before the 2022 statement on self-reliance, in "How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet," Koe had described the response to a world in which external structures no longer give order to one's life: cultivate **personal responsibility**. Create your own vision for the future, your own set of values, your own routines, "a code for your life" conducive to optimal outcomes. Such a code helps you navigate reality, dive into the unknown and increase your skill set, which in turn breeds creativity and a good life. "In short, it's about not accepting handouts or putting your fate or your future in another person's hands."

**Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md (2022-06-25)

The coined phrase **code for your life** names the internal structure that replaces the external one. It is the same structure that Chapter 13 describes as routine designed as the rules of one's own game, and the same that Chapter 39.5 distinguishes as autonomy from mere freedom. The definition places responsibility not in obedience to rules but in the authorship of rules.

In March 2023, in "Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things," Koe named the opposite. It is obvious, he says, that you must educate yourself to achieve a specific and meaningful goal that someone else did not set for you. But most people "adopt a victim mindset and hide behind their static identity." They know they can educate themselves, they know there is opportunity, they know it will take time, but they do not want to face reality, so they eliminate all responsibility for themselves and blame others.

**Source:** Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md (2023-03-30)

The pairing **victim mindset** and **static identity** links responsibility to the theory of identity in Part II. A static identity ("this is who I am") is the defense against the discomfort of change that Chapter 3 describes; the victim mindset is the narrative that makes the defense acceptable, by locating the cause of the situation outside the self. Koe's claim is that the knowledge is not missing ("they know") but is refused. This is the same distinction as in the "true agency" principle of Section 35.1 (blame yourself for every problem even when it is not your fault): the victim mindset is that principle inverted.

In an October 2022 video on the one-person business model, Koe states how these values relate to his whole audience. If you do not value self-reliance, personal responsibility or taking control of your life, this content "isn't for you yet." The qualifier "yet" carries the argument: eventually, he says, you will realize that "if you want true happiness, those are going to have to become values of yours."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md (2022-10-23)

The claim is strong, and it is an opinion rather than a demonstrated result: self-reliance and personal responsibility are presented as prerequisites of happiness, not as one path among others. Chapter 39 develops the account of happiness (resistance overcome plus connection) that makes this claim intelligible within Koe's system: if happiness requires overcoming chosen resistance, then someone who never takes responsibility for any resistance cannot be happy in that sense.

#### Nobody is coming to save you

The most direct formulation of self-reliance in the corpus opens a May 2024 video addressed to people who feel lost. "Nobody is coming to save you: not your friends, not your family, not the government. They can offer advice and tools, but at the end of the day it's up to you to change your mind, view your situation from a new perspective, and act regardless of how you feel." He offers "a framework, or at least some clarity, to help you dig yourself out," and includes himself among those who will not do it for you: "I'm not going to save you either."

**Source:** My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md (2024-05-30)

The sentence names three things that only the person can do, and they follow the order of the book. **Change your mind** (Part II: identity and the beliefs that compose it). **View your situation from a new perspective** (Part III: the goal as a lens; a different goal reveals a different situation). **Act regardless of how you feel** (Part V: action not conditioned by mood). Advice and tools, which others can provide, belong to none of these three.

Koe then states the mindset to adopt "from the beginning of this video": "If you don't take responsibility for digging yourself out of that hole, you are not going to get out of that hole, and by complaining and whining like most people do, you're only going to dig yourself further in." The image of the hole makes complaining an action with consequences, not a neutral expression: each complaint reinforces the external locus of control and postpones the action that would help.

**Source:** My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md (2024-05-30)

He also specifies when the principle becomes relevant. It comes into play "when you're actually trying to do something with your life": taking back control, starting a business, learning a new skill, becoming financially free, finding a relationship, getting fit, or clearing your mind "so that you can have better ideas, so that you can act in a better direction, because **ideas beget behavior** and you can't act on something that you don't know is possible."

**Source:** My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md (2024-05-30)

The coined phrase **ideas beget behavior** explains why a principle about self-reliance begins with changing one's mind. Behavior is constrained by the set of options a person can conceive. If an option is not represented in the mind, it cannot be chosen. This is the perceptual account of low agency from Section 35.1 ("they don't have a mind that allows them to see any other option") stated as a general rule, and it is why Koe's content, by his own description, works mainly by expanding what the audience believes is possible.

Two years earlier, in "7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures)," Koe had compressed the same principle into a lesson he called **figure it out**: stop relying on the external to give you what you want. "If you don't have energy, then generate your own energy with purpose and passion" and by pursuing your curiosities. If you do not have an income, self-educate online and figure out how to make one.

**Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md (2022-06-18)

The claim that energy can be generated, not just received, is characteristic of the corpus. Energy here is not caloric but motivational, and its sources (purpose, passion, curiosity) are the same internal goals that Chapter 8.7 says create passion. A person who waits for energy to arrive before acting is waiting for permission from their own mood.

##### The catastrophe cycle

The clearest illustration of what happens without self-reliance comes from an October 2023 video, "If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind." Broke people, Koe argues, are okay being broke until something catastrophic happens, and catastrophes are more likely for them: an old, unmaintained car blows up; there is a break-in in a bad neighborhood; parents fall sick and cannot be helped on a low income. Then they are motivated, but they think it is too late, do nothing, and wait for the next catastrophe. They lacked foresight and could not break out of the narrow mind produced by their narrow routine, so they are trapped in "an infinite cycle of I should have started sooner." Things equalize: stress comes down, the situation becomes normal, survival is no longer at stake, they are comfortable again, and they do nothing "to reverse entropy." Successful people, by contrast, use such events to change who they are, and who they are determines their standards and values, which determine their tiny daily choices.

**Source:** If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md (2023-10-22)

The argument assembles several models from earlier in the book into a single causal loop:

1. A narrow routine produces a narrow mind (the environment programs the identity, Chapter 4.4).
2. The narrow mind lacks foresight, so it does not prepare for predictable shocks.
3. A shock (catastrophe) produces urgency, the trigger of change in Chapter 4.2.
4. Without a goal, the urgency is interpreted as too late, and no action follows.
5. Homeostasis returns ("things equalize"); comfort replaces urgency.
6. Without action, entropy is not reversed (Chapter 5): the situation decays toward the next catastrophe.

What breaks the loop, in Koe's account, is not the catastrophe but the decision to use it to change identity; standards and values follow identity, and small choices follow standards (Chapter 3.6). The passage is a generalization from observation, and its framing of poverty is deliberately harsh. Koe does not deny that catastrophes are more likely for the poor (he states it); his claim is about what people do with them. Readers should note that the argument says nothing about structural causes of poverty, which lie outside its scope, and that it is offered as a description of a psychological pattern, not as a full account of why people are poor.

The loop also explains why Section 35.3 will treat urgency as an opportunity. A moment of pressure is when change is most possible; the question is whether a goal exists to channel it before equilibrium returns.

#### The Irreplaceable individual

In March 2024 Koe published two videos that together form a framework for self-reliance under technological change. The first, "The Future-Proof Skill Stack," dealt with external skills (Section 35.5). The second, "Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead)," dealt with the internal traits that make those skills possible. It lists "the traits that comprise the Irreplaceable individual," which allow you "to adapt to any environment":

| Trait | Koe's definition (March 2024) |
|---|---|
| Self-experimentation | "How to solve complex problems through trial and error and come to your own conclusions" |
| Self-development | "Cultivating a valuable mindset and skill set that can help others" |
| Self-reliance | "How to get what you want by taking responsibility for the outcome of your life" |
| Self-awareness | "How to understand the motives of your mind so you can understand the motives of others" |
| Self-education | "The ability to gather, make sense of and utilize information on an unknown subject" |
| Self-sufficiency | "The ability to sustain one's ideal lifestyle and acquire the resources necessary to do so" |
| Self-mastery | "An unwavering dedication to the process of navigating reality" |

He closes the list with a sentence: "when you master yourself, you master the world."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

All seven traits begin with "self-," and the repetition is the argument. Irreplaceability is located inside the person, not in a credential or a position. A credential can be revoked or devalued; an employer can restructure; a trait that lets one solve problems by trial and error, learn an unknown subject and sustain one's lifestyle travels with the person into any environment. Several traits correspond to chapters of this book (self-experimentation to Chapter 15.4, self-education to Chapter 14, self-awareness to Chapter 2.7), so the list functions as a summary of what the system develops. Two definitions deserve notice. Self-awareness is defined relationally: understanding your own mind's motives is the route to understanding others', which is the foundation of the persuasion of Chapter 32. Self-sufficiency is defined economically, as sustaining an ideal lifestyle and acquiring the resources for it; in 2026 Koe will redefine it epistemically (below).

Immediately after the list, Koe introduces a distinction that prevents the traits from being enough on their own: "If you want to become irreplaceable, you are required to become valuable, and if you want to become well paid, you need to convince people to see your value." Being valuable and being perceived as valuable are two separate requirements, and the second requires persuasion. Later in the video he adds that once you understand the seven traits, the next problem is that "your skill set is invaluable if nobody knows or cares about it."

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

The distinction connects to Chapter 31.2 (value is perception) and explains why every skill stack in Section 35.5 includes persuasion, marketing or sales. Irreplaceability is a property of the person; payment is a property of the relationship between the person and others' perception.

The same video ends its sequence of five steps with a quotation: "learn to sell, learn to build; if you can do both, you will be unstoppable." Koe calls it "another quote from" a name that the transcript renders as "poppy Nal," so the attribution is unclear in the source. (The same phrase appears elsewhere in the corpus with an ambiguous attribution to Naval Ravikant, and "poppy Nal" may be a garbled rendering of that name, but this cannot be confirmed.) He follows it with a sentence that may be part of the quotation or his own addition, the source does not make clear which: "every day, set aside 1 hour to build a project that actualizes your ideal future; that is the only way to escape building projects for someone else." In Koe's use, selling and building are the two complementary capacities that complete the irreplaceable individual.

**Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md (2024-03-31)

The "one hour a day" connects the principle to Chapter 12.2, where Koe's daily architecture begins with a single protected hour, even for someone with a job. The phrase "building projects for someone else" is the agency contrast once more: the employee builds the employer's project; the entrepreneur builds their own.

##### Becoming unemployable

Koe has a word for where self-reliance eventually leads: **unemployable**. In a July 2024 video on the future of work he argues that "entrepreneurship may not be a skill, but it is a meta skill, because it teaches you how to be high agency and how to solve problems, how to create a solution to those problems and sell the solution, and along the way you cultivate the traits that make you unemployable or irreplaceable." He defines the term: "you just could never go back to being employed because it doesn't make sense, because you can make more on your own, you can control your lifestyle on your own." And he insists that "it's all a skill, it's not luck": entrepreneurs do not simply know what to do; "they practice it and they fail and they learn something that you don't really get at a job, outside of a narrow boundary of the context of the work."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md (2024-07-28)

"Unemployable" is used here with an inverted meaning. In ordinary speech it describes someone no employer would hire; in Koe's use it describes someone for whom employment no longer makes sense. The inversion is deliberate and shows the reframing of the whole chapter: the condition that the default path treats as failure becomes, from the standpoint of self-reliance, the goal. The argument that entrepreneurship is a meta skill (a skill that develops other skills) parallels the February 2026 claim that agency is the meta-skill. In 2024 entrepreneurship teaches agency; in 2026 agency is the root. The two can be read as two directions of the same loop: entrepreneurship is the practice in which agency is trained, and agency is the disposition that sustains entrepreneurship.

A year earlier, in August 2023, Koe had described the same transition from his own experience, as the fourth step of the high-income-skills video: the shift "from employable to unemployable." Through experience and skill acquisition you reach a point where you can never be employed again, because you have too much network to fall back on. If his business died today, he says, he would message five people and "guarantee" covering $10,000–20,000 a month based on his skill set, at least in the short term. Skills compound to the point where you know how to make money in any environment, regardless of the market. He specifies what kind of skill he means. Real marketing and sales are not "the basic Udemy course" (an opt-in page, an email list, a Facebook page with a good bio) but a deep understanding studied for one to three years with practice, attempts and multiple failures. That understanding lets him "spin up an offer, figure out a distribution channel and launch it within two weeks, and make however much money I want depending on the methods."

**Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08-16)

The figures are Koe's own estimate of his position in 2023 and should be read as an illustration of compounding skills, not as a promise transferable to others. What the example shows is the content of self-sufficiency in the economic sense of the 2024 table: the capacity to "acquire the resources necessary" for one's lifestyle in any environment. Two assets make it possible, and both are built over years: a network (relationships that convert into income quickly) and compounding skills (marketing and sales understood at the level of principles, so that they apply to any offer). The "one to three years" of practice and failure is the hard-problem category at the scale of a career.

#### Individual sovereignty: self-education, self-interest, self-sufficiency

The most recent and most structured version of these ideas appears in a January 2026 video, "If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years." Its argument starts from the critique of specialization developed in Chapter 20 and asks the next question: "If specialization makes people stupid, then what makes someone free? What makes someone sovereign? And there's three ingredients: self-education, self-interest, and self-sufficiency." Each ingredient is given a role: self-education is "the engine," self-interest is "the compass," and self-sufficiency is "the foundation that prevents your life direction from being hijacked."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md (2026-01-20)

The three metaphors form a vehicle: an engine to move, a compass to steer and a foundation (or chassis) that keeps the vehicle from being taken over by someone else. Koe develops each in turn.

##### Self-education: the engine

"Self-education is pretty clear. If you want a different result from the one that traditional education promises, then you must direct your own learning." The definition is conditional and comparative. Traditional education promises a particular result (a credential, a job, a career path); if that is the result you want, follow it. If you want a different one, the institution that produces the standard result cannot produce yours, so the direction of learning must pass to you. Chapter 14 develops what self-directed learning looks like in practice (build to learn, learning in public).

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md (2026-01-20)

##### Self-interest: the compass

Koe acknowledges that the second ingredient "raises some flags because it sounds selfish and short-sighted"; you have been told that everyone should be selfless. "But self-interest simply means concerned with one's own interest. And if that isn't a priority for you, then whose interest are you going to serve? Especially when your survival is dependent on it." The answer to the rhetorical question is that you will serve the interests of schools, jobs and government, "and those interests are not very beneficial to you." "You need to follow your own interest, because your interest is often beneficial to others." He qualifies the claim: whether self-interest benefits others "depends on what level of cognitive development you're at," but he assumes that most people do not want to hurt most people.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md (2026-01-20)

The argument is the same as the one about assigned goals in Chapter 1: there is no neutral position. If you do not pursue your own interest, you pursue someone else's, because attention and effort are always directed somewhere. The qualification about cognitive development links the claim to the maps of Chapter 38: at early stages, self-interest may be predatory; at later stages, one's interest includes others. Koe's statement that "your interest is often beneficial to others" is therefore a conditional claim about developed self-interest, not a general license.

To define healthy self-interest, Koe borrows from Ayn Rand: "The truly selfish person, in Ayn Rand's view, is a self-respecting, self-supporting human being who neither sacrifices others to himself nor sacrifices himself to others. This rejects both the predator and the doormat."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md (2026-01-20)

**Complementary context:** Ayn Rand (1905–1982) was a Russian-born American novelist and philosopher whose doctrine, Objectivism, defends rational self-interest as a moral principle; her essay collection *The Virtue of Selfishness* (1964) argues that the selfish person, properly understood, neither sacrifices others nor sacrifices himself. Koe uses only this definition; he does not adopt Rand's broader political or ethical system in the source.

The predator and the doormat are the two failures of self-interest. The predator pursues their interest at others' expense; the doormat sacrifices their interest for others. Koe's compass points between them: one's own interest, pursued without sacrificing others, which (at a sufficient level of development) tends to serve them. The same structure appears in Chapter 32.2's ethics of persuasion: persuasion is as ethical as the person who uses it.

##### Self-sufficiency: the foundation

"Self-sufficiency, the last ingredient, is the refusal to outsource your judgment, your learning, and your agency. So, if self-education is the engine and self-interest is the compass, then self-sufficiency is the foundation that prevents your life direction from being hijacked."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md (2026-01-20)

This definition differs from the 2024 one. In the table of the Irreplaceable individual, self-sufficiency was "the ability to sustain one's ideal lifestyle and acquire the resources necessary to do so," an economic capacity. In 2026 it is "the refusal to outsource your judgment, your learning, and your agency," an epistemic and volitional stance. The two definitions are compatible (the refusal to outsource judgment is what makes it possible to acquire resources independently), but the emphasis has moved inward, from what one can acquire to what one will not delegate. The word **hijacked** recalls the dopamine and attention chapters (Chapters 6 and 11), where the risk is precisely that one's direction is taken over by an algorithm, a feed or another person's goal.

##### The triad as a cycle, and the emergence of the generalist

Koe then argues that the three ingredients form a cycle, and that "the generalist naturally must emerge" in someone who develops it. The cycle has three links:

1. **Self-interest motivates self-education.** You learn things because they improve your life, not because the system assigns them.
2. **Self-education enables self-sufficiency.** "You can only be as sovereign as the domains you understand," and a specialist understands one domain. Koe adds a pointed observation about organizations: "There's a reason you can't run the entire operation and why you're kept dumb to that and you're kept siloed." If you knew how to run the entire company, why wouldn't you do it yourself?
3. **Self-sufficiency clarifies self-interest.** "When you're not dependent on others' interpretations, you can actually perceive what serves you."

"It's a triad, but it's also a cycle."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md (2026-01-20)

The third link closes the loop in a way that connects back to the beginning of this chapter. The conformist judges truth by others' acceptance; a person who depends on others' interpretations cannot see their own interest clearly, because the interpretations come with the interests of the interpreters. Self-sufficiency removes that filter, which makes the compass more accurate, which directs learning better, which extends sovereignty to more domains. The cycle is self-reinforcing in both directions: a person who outsources judgment perceives their interest less clearly, learns what others assign, and becomes more dependent.

The second link is the bridge to Chapter 20. The generalist, in Koe's definition, is the person who learns whatever their goal requires; here the generalist appears as the necessary result of pursuing sovereignty, because sovereignty is bounded by understanding and understanding is bounded by the number of domains one has learned. The remark about being "kept siloed" is a claim about organizational design that Koe offers as interpretation: compartmentalization of knowledge keeps employees dependent. It is not supported in the source by evidence, but it follows from the triad's own logic.

##### How the definition of freedom developed

The 2026 triad is the latest stage of a definition of freedom that Koe built over several years.

In May 2024, in "The Age Of The Generalist," he wrote: "Only slaves are expected to perform one task for their entire life," and that, he said, is what the current education and employment system reflects; "wage slavery is a very real thing." By contrast, "a free man is defined as someone who acts on their interests and does many things throughout their life." The source of this definition of the free man is not attributed in the transcript. He closes the passage with a sentence that restates Chapter 1's thesis: "If you don't create a goal, you will be assigned one, and society is great at handing out goals to those who can't think for themselves."

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md (2024-05-19)

In August 2025, in "You Have About 36 Months To Make It," he extended the definition: "The definition of a free individual is those who pursue their interest and do many things throughout their lives. It's those that don't need permission to identify and solve a problem to create value in the world." He placed it in the context of AI: as reality grows more complex with AI and technology, new problems emerge, more problems and opportunities than ever. Becoming a "director" (a figure Chapter 36 develops) is the best way to live regardless of AI; AI is just the catalyst, so complaining makes no sense.

**Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md (2025-08-17)

The development is cumulative. The 2024 "free man" has two marks: acting on one's interests and doing many things (the generalist). The 2025 "free individual" adds a third: not needing permission to identify and solve problems that create value (agency, in the July 2024 sense of Section 35.1). The 2026 triad reorganizes all three into engine, compass and foundation: self-interest is the 2024 "acts on their interests," self-education produces the "many things," and self-sufficiency contains the refusal to outsource agency, the 2025 "doesn't need permission."

| Date | Term | Defining marks |
|---|---|---|
| May 2024 | Free man | Acts on own interests; does many things in life (contrast: the slave performs one task) |
| Aug 2025 | Free individual | Pursues own interests; does many things; does not need permission to identify and solve a problem that creates value |
| Jan 2026 | Individual sovereignty | Self-education (engine), self-interest (compass), self-sufficiency (foundation), forming a cycle from which the generalist emerges |

The corpus contains an earlier and more provocative line that sums up the political implication of all three. In a May 2023 video on NPCs, inserted into an argument about conditioning and inputs, Koe says: "the most dangerous threat to society is someone who isn't reliant on it." The context is the contrast between the NPC, who relies on society's default path, and the main character, who creates their own (Chapter 1.4).

**Source:** Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md (2023-05-08)

The sentence is an opinion stated without development, and its meaning depends on what "society" refers to. In the context of the NPC argument, the most plausible reading is that "society" means the system of default paths and institutions described in Chapter 2, which depends on people relying on it, and that a person who does not rely on it is "dangerous" to that system because it cannot direct them. It is not presented as a call to harm anyone. The sovereignty triad, with its rejection of both the predator and the doormat, makes clear that independence from the system's direction is compatible with, and in Koe's view tends toward, benefiting others.

Elsewhere in the corpus (Chapter 39.5), Koe adds a further qualification that bears on how to read this section: absolute freedom is "synonymous with absolute chaos," and what people want is autonomy, the freedom to create their own rules and structure. Sovereignty in the sense of this section is that autonomy: not the absence of structure but the authorship of it.

### 35.3 Problems Are Everywhere

Agency, as Section 35.1 established, is the disposition to identify problems and solve them without permission. Self-reliance, as Section 35.2 showed, requires perceiving one's situation as a problem. This section describes the terrain on which both are exercised. Koe's claim is that the present environment is not short of problems or opportunities; it has too many. The abundance creates its own pathology, and agency is the response to it.

#### From overwhelm to opportunity paralysis

In February 2023, in a video on networking, Koe describes a historical inversion. In the past, people "had to work their ass off" to get the few opportunities available. Now there is "an overabundance of opportunities and options that make people overwhelmed, uncertain," so they flock to what he calls **secure career systems**, the career paths developed over time to which schools are tightly connected. Meanwhile, he says, opportunities are everywhere: the person in the comments needs a website, the tweet you scrolled past is from someone who needs a video editor, the person on a subreddit wants to learn how to be happy. The internet gives "indirect access to the 4.9 billion people that are on it."

**Source:** The Non-Needy Networking Process (How To Make High Value Connections).md (2023-02-12)

The argument explains why abundance can reduce action. When options are scarce, the decision is made for you by what is available; when they are abundant, choosing requires criteria, and a person without their own goal has no criteria. The secure career system offers a ready-made choice, so it relieves the overwhelm at the cost of agency. This is the same mechanism as the assigned goal of Chapter 1, now seen as a refuge from too much freedom. The three examples (website, video editor, happiness) are deliberately mundane: problems that others express publicly every day and that a person with the relevant skill could solve.

**Complementary context:** the figure of 4.9 billion internet users is Koe's figure for 2023; it roughly matches the estimates published by the International Telecommunication Union for 2021–2022.

By July 2026 Koe had given the same phenomenon a sharper name. "Nobody knows what skill to learn right now": AI? Coding? Marketing? Is school a waste "while everyone else is vibe coding apps they'll never get traction on"? YouTube? Substack? A job or your own thing? "Since there is so much opportunity, it just paralyzes more people than ever into not doing anything, because you feel like if you start learning one thing it'll just become irrelevant in one to two years."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md (2026-07-05)

The coined term **opportunity paralysis** names this condition. It differs from the 2023 overwhelm in its cause. In 2023 the problem was too many options; in 2026 it is too many options combined with the fear of rapid obsolescence. The person does not choose because every choice seems likely to be wasted. The aside about "vibe coding apps they'll never get traction on" refers to the use of AI to build software; Koe elsewhere defends the tool and criticizes its use without distribution, taste or persistence, so the remark targets building without the other ingredients, not the tool itself (Chapter 36.5).

The diagnosis connects to the beginning of this chapter. Section 35.1 opened with Koe's December 2025 prediction that "most skills will be irrelevant in 10 to 20 years" and with his answer: the high-agency person is not dependent on a specific skill, because they can learn whatever their vision requires. Opportunity paralysis is the state of someone who has accepted the prediction without the answer. If security lies in choosing the right skill, and no skill is safe, then no choice is possible. If security lies in the capacity to learn whatever is needed, then the choice of a first skill matters much less, because it will not be the last.

#### "This time it's real": the uncertainty premise

Koe frames part of his argument with a premise he takes from someone else. In a July 2024 video, "The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills)," he inserts a clip of a speaker who is not named in the transcript. The speaker says that this generation is undergoing a more rapid change of its technological environment than any before; "every generation thinks like that, but this time it's real"; "it's the first time in human history that nobody has any idea how the world would look like in 20 years. Today nobody has any idea what to teach young people that will still be relevant in 20 years." Koe then announces "these next three things" that will benefit your future.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md (2024-07-28)

**Complementary context:** the content of the clip matches a position the historian Yuval Noah Harari has stated in books and talks (for example in *21 Lessons for the 21st Century*, 2018): that nobody knows what the job market will look like in a few decades and therefore what to teach children. The transcript does not name the speaker, so the attribution remains uncertain. Elsewhere in the corpus, Devon Eriksen explicitly rebuts the Harari position, arguing that education should teach people to teach themselves.

The clip functions as a premise that Koe accepts and then answers. If nobody knows which specific skills will be relevant in 20 years, the answer cannot be a specific skill. Koe's answers across the corpus are the ones this chapter collects: agency (Section 35.1), the self-traits of the irreplaceable individual (Section 35.2), and skills defined at the level of persistent principles rather than tools (Section 35.5). The premise is therefore not a reason for paralysis but a reason to change what one learns.

#### Hidden opportunities and the irrational reaction to loss

Two smaller arguments complete Koe's picture of the terrain.

The first concerns opportunities hidden by language. In July 2025, in a video on strategic thinking, he observes: "You see young kids all the time in the digital world, on the internet, making a ton of money because, one, they speak English, or they just saw the opportunity, and this opportunity wasn't available to older generations." If you are unaware of this, you will not have those opportunities. Koe speaks English and "a little bit of Spanish," and he is "sure that within each language there are these opportunities that are just hidden there." "If you don't know what to do with your life, or you just feel like there aren't a lot of opportunities, learning English may be a good place to start in just getting immersed in the digital world to notice more opportunities." He acknowledges that this is "a large task."

**Source:** This mental model will make you think like a strategic genius.md (2025-07-20)

The example illustrates "ideas beget behavior" from Section 35.2: an opportunity that is not perceived cannot be acted upon, and language is one of the filters that determine what is perceived. Learning English is offered not as a guarantee but as a way of enlarging the field of perception. The advice is tentative ("may be a good place to start"), and Koe's own acknowledgement of other languages' hidden opportunities suggests that the principle is about immersion in a larger information environment rather than about English as such.

The second argument concerns the reaction to the end of an old way of life. In August 2025, in "You Have About 36 Months To Make It," Koe tells a story about his little brother. His brother never played a certain video game, but the moment Koe picked it up, he went insane: "Get out of my room. Give me my game back." That, Koe says, is what is happening as **industrial living** goes away: we have hated it for decades, and yet when it is taken away we react like the brother, "except you're just being irrational," lacking the clarity or agency to find a new path. His summary: "Zoom in and it's scary. Zoom out and it's wonderful."

**Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md (2025-08-17)

The anecdote is an analogy for loss aversion: what one did not value becomes valuable at the moment of loss. The "zoom" vocabulary comes from Chapter 6.3. At the zoomed-in level, the disappearance of entry-level jobs and familiar career paths is threatening; at the zoomed-out level, the end of work that people disliked for decades is an opening. The anecdote does not deny the fear; it asks the reader to notice that the fear is directed at the loss of something they did not want.

#### Urgency as the moment to change

In the same August 2025 video Koe states what to do with the pressure. There are two options: sit back and do nothing, which is obviously not good, or "use this moment of urgency as a time to turn your life around, because that's when you turn your life around the most, when you're under pressure." Changing your life, he adds, does not mean packing up, moving across the country and so on. It requires thinking about your work, thinking in general, pursuing something you care about, taking risks, creating a story worth telling and sharing it with those who can benefit. And he closes with a sentence that sums up the historical argument of this section: "Everything that created the good life in the past is now becoming a requirement."

**Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md (2025-08-17)

The final sentence is a compressed thesis about the economy of the individual. In the industrial era, a good life could be built on someone else's structure; thinking for oneself, pursuing one's interests, taking risks and telling one's story were optional enrichments, available to those who wanted more. Koe's claim is that these are now the minimum for remaining relevant. The sentence also explains why agency appears in this chapter, at the end of the practical part of the book: the habits that the earlier chapters presented as routes to a better life are, on this reading, becoming conditions for any life of one's own.

The statement that relocation is not required contrasts with other passages of the corpus, where Koe recommends extreme measures: a "war mode" in which one signs an unaffordable lease or flies across the country, burning the boats to force change (Chapter 10.6 discusses tactical stress). Koe does not resolve the two registers. He alternates between gradual and extreme approaches to change throughout the corpus, and he gives reasons for each at different times: his preference for extremes is personal; pressure tactics carry more risk at lower levels of awareness; and in August 2025, the urgency of the historical moment already provides the pressure, so it does not need to be manufactured. The last reason is the one that applies here. The catastrophe cycle of Section 35.2 described people who waste the urgency that a crisis provides; this passage asks the reader to use the urgency that the present moment provides.

#### Exercising agency: identify, then solve

The corpus also contains a method for exercising agency on problems once the terrain is seen. It comes from a January 2025 conversation with Sahil Bloom, author of *The 5 Types of Wealth* and *The Time Billionaire*.

Koe introduces it with a story. His co-founder Matt had noted that the early hires at their company (mostly referrals, "ten engineers he wanted to get on") were "extremely high agency," which sent Koe down "the rabbit hole of what agency is, why it's so important, especially going into the future of AI." His conclusion was that agency is about "solving problems... in any area of your life, not even entrepreneurship, like time, social, physical, financial, mental," the five types of wealth in Bloom's framework. "They're just scattered with problems... whenever you notice boredom, anxiety, overwhelm, stress, all of these different things, it's an opportunity to create a solution for yourself, which could then be... passed on to other people or your family or your friends. And that feeling of exchange, whether it's for money or not, is such a rewarding thing."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

Two moves in this passage extend the concept of agency. The first removes it from business: agency applies to every domain of life, not only to entrepreneurship. The second turns negative emotional states into signals. Boredom, anxiety, overwhelm and stress are not only discomforts to be managed; they indicate a problem, and each problem is an occasion for a solution. This is the reading of boredom and anxiety as feedback in Chapter 10.5 (they signal that challenge and skill are out of balance) generalized to all areas of life. The final sentence connects to Chapter 28.2: a solution created for oneself can be "passed on," and the "feeling of exchange" is rewarding whether or not money is involved. Solving your own problems and selling (or giving) the solution is, in this account, the natural extension of agency.

Bloom then gives the method, and it is his, not Koe's. To identify problems, use "blank sheet exercises and walking through your day with an eye towards things that created friction in your life or in others' lives." The key rule is to identify first and not to solve yet. The common error, Bloom says, is that "you get into this tendency of, like, okay, I found one, let me try to come up with a solution, when really, like, let me just open the aperture, see all the problems I can identify, and then I can pick the problem that I feel like is most solvable. If you go down the rabbit hole on the first problem you find, you close your eyes to seeing the problem that you actually might be able to solve more efficiently or effectively."

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

The photographic metaphor **open the aperture** describes widening the field of view before focusing. It is a deliberate separation of two phases that the mind tends to merge: divergent identification and convergent solution. The rule protects against a specific error: the first problem found is not necessarily the most solvable, and committing to it early closes off the comparison. It parallels the zoom-out/zoom-in sequence of Chapter 6.3, applied to problem selection.

Once a problem is chosen, Bloom describes solving it as "figuring out... what is the limiting factor in that situation. Like when you identify a problem, usually it's that, okay, there's some sort of bottleneck or some sort of issue or some sort of kink in the hose that's not allowing this thing to work in the way that it should. And is there a way that I can actually undo that kink? Like, is that hose bent and I can actually just straighten it out?" He calls this "a deconstructed way" of thinking about problems.

**Source:** The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md (2025-01-30)

The **kink in the hose** is a metaphor for the limiting factor: the water pressure is there, the hose is there, the destination is there, and a single bend stops the flow. Straightening the bend restores everything without adding anything. The metaphor implies a method: look for the one constraint whose removal releases the most, rather than adding effort everywhere. It is the same concept as Eriksen's "bottleneck" in Section 35.1, applied at the level of an individual problem rather than of success in general. In Eriksen's analysis, the kink in the hose of a highly intelligent person's career is usually agency.

The complete method, combining Bloom's two steps with Koe's framing, can be summarized as follows:

| Step | Action | Error it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Notice | Treat boredom, anxiety, overwhelm and stress, in any area of life, as signals of a problem | Treating negative states as mere discomfort |
| Identify (open the aperture) | Blank sheet; walk through the day noting friction in your life and in others' lives; do not solve yet | Committing to the first problem found |
| Select | Choose the problem that seems most solvable | Choosing by salience rather than tractability |
| Solve (find the kink) | Locate the limiting factor and remove it | Adding effort everywhere instead of at the constraint |
| Pass on | Share the solution with others, for money or not | Keeping a solution that could create value for others |

Koe had stated the stakes of this method six months earlier, in July 2024, right after describing his high-agency team: "In your own life, if you want to stay relevant in the coming years, you can't depend on anybody for your security. Problems will always exist. If you can't spot them, you lose. If you can't give yourself permission to solve them, you lose."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md (2024-07-13)

The sentence identifies two distinct failures. The first is perceptual: not spotting problems, which is the low-agency mind that cannot see beyond its assigned goals. The second is volitional: spotting problems but not granting oneself permission to solve them, which is the person who waits to be asked. Bloom's method addresses the first failure; the rest of this chapter addresses the second. And the premise, "problems will always exist," is the same as the December 2024 argument in Section 35.1 ("problems are infinite"). Security, in this account, does not come from having a position that protects you from problems; it comes from the permanent capacity to find and solve them.
