## Chapter 36 — The Economy of the Individual in the Age of AI

Chapter 34 treated money as a unit of value and argued that wealth comes from leverage, not from hours. Chapter 35 argued that agency, not intelligence, is the bottleneck, and that the individual who iterates without permission becomes hard to replace. Both chapters assumed a particular world: one in which a single person, with an internet connection and a few skills, can reach an audience, sell a product and live from the value they create. This chapter examines that world directly. It is the place where Koe's system meets its historical moment. His claim is that the economy is reorganizing around the individual, that artificial intelligence accelerates the reorganization, and that the one-person business, the personal brand and the education product are responses to a structural shift rather than fashions.

The chapter has six steps. Section 36.1 presents the thesis of a transfer of power from institutions to individuals, together with the signals, figures and predictions Koe uses to frame its urgency, and the framework of three layers (internet, social media, AI) and three "superpowers" that he built in 2025. Section 36.2 explains what he means by the "creator economy," why he regards creators as a decentralized education system that replaces a school system unable to keep up, and why he says that every business is now a media business. Section 36.3 shows what kind of work AI replaces first: replicable, formulaic, entry-level work, the work of "technicians" rather than "creators." Section 36.4 turns to what AI cannot do on its own: it lacks direction, executive function and vision, so the decisive distinction becomes the one between "doers" and "directors." Section 36.5 gathers Koe's practical teaching on using AI well: why its output depends on your skill, why most people use it like a slot machine, how to become "AI-first," how to automate yourself out of work, and what he thinks of agents and "vibe coding." Section 36.6 closes with what he calls the "meaning economy": the thesis that machines will take over utility, that humans will be paid for meaning, and that AI threatens meaning as much as it threatens jobs.

Three features of the material shape how it is presented here. First, most of it comes from videos published between 2023 and 2026, a period in which Koe's mood about AI changed visibly: from "I only see good" in automation (2023), to being "pretty worried" (March 2024), to declaring himself "indifferent" to the forecasts (August 2025) and admitting that the change would "probably not" be "as drastic as many people made it out to seem, myself included" (September 2025). These shifts are marked where they matter. Second, Koe supports his urgency with many figures that he does not source, and some of them do not agree with each other; the chapter reports them as he gives them and says so. Third, a considerable part of the material comes from third parties (Sam Altman, David Patterson, the CEOs of Fiverr and Shopify, Balaji Srinivasan, Naval Ravikant, Dickie Bush, Devon Eriksen, an anonymous writer called Signal, David Shapiro), and the chapter keeps their contributions separate from Koe's own adaptations.

### 36.1 The Transfer of Power to the Individual

#### The context of disruption: signals, figures and predictions

Koe usually opens his videos on the future of work with a burst of signals. They are not the argument itself; they are the atmosphere in which he wants the argument to be heard. Reading them together shows both the kind of evidence he relies on and the conclusion he draws from it, which is almost always the same.

In "The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills)" (July 2024), he begins with layoffs. In 2022 Elon Musk acquired Twitter for $44 billion and soon fired 80% of its staff, "upward of 6,000 people," and headlines are "flooded" with layoffs that leave people worried they are "next on The Chopping Block." He adds that freelance work "has increased from 36% of the workforce to 46.6% since 2020," that the creator economy "is projected to double from 250 billion to 480 billion by 2028," and that, according to his own research, some prominent figures predict artificial general intelligence "by the end of this year," while others say it may take "up to 2 years because costs are increasing and development is slowing down." He names no sources for these statistics. What worries people, he says, is concrete: their skills are becoming outdated, and their means of survival, their family's lifestyle and their ability to find new work are on the line. His conclusion does not depend on which forecast is right: whether we live to 500 or have robots doing chores in four months or ten years, "the only solution is to take matters into your own hands that's what you should have been doing in the first place." **Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md.

In "The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First)" (May 2025), the signals come from third parties. A post on X by David Patterson claimed that the top AI model is already smarter than 85% of humans and will be smarter than 99.9% by the end of 2026, "and you think you will still have a job?"; Elon Musk replied "roughly correct." The CEO of Fiverr (transcribed as "Micah Kaufman") sent his team an email with three points: the "unpleasant truth" that AI is coming for your job, his job and every job; the prediction that "easy tasks will no longer exist. Hard tasks will become the new easy and impossible tasks will become hard"; and the instruction to scream into a pillow, pick yourself up and become future-proof. The CEO of Shopify emailed his staff that everyone should become AI-first, and an article reported that Duolingo was going AI-first and replacing contractors with AI. Koe acknowledges that such signals frighten people who do not know which skills, careers or jobs will remain. He nevertheless says that "this is one of the most incredible times to be alive": the task is to find the opportunity and take advantage of it. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

A year earlier his tone was different. In "The Future-Proof Skill Stack" (March 2024) he admits to being "pretty worried," deep in "the AI and AGI rabbit hole," and lists the questions on his mind: what can humans do that machines can't; what will the future of work look like; which jobs, if any, will persist over the next twenty years; and what can we do to secure an above-average future. He singles out one worry that he thinks most people share: "we don't know what to teach children because we don't know what will be relevant in 20 years." **Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md.

The same worry appears in an interview clip that Koe inserted into his July 2024 video. An interviewer recalls that "we told our kids to learn Russian in the '80s Japanese in the '90s Chinese probably in the 2000s and then engineering," and asks someone described as being "on the board of OpenAI" (Mustafa Suleyman, who "runs AI for Microsoft," is also mentioned) what he would tell a grandchild to study. The answer: "I would be telling my grandchild not to go study coding... it was only 15 years ago that everybody was supposed to learn how to code." The speaker compares coding with skills the world stopped requiring of individuals: his father told him he had to learn to fix a car when he got his driver's license, but he never told his own children that, because "the world kind of figured out how to do it"; just as nobody needs a slide rule or to calculate a square root on paper, "coding is going to be like that." The transcript does not clearly identify the speakers, so the attribution is uncertain. Koe uses the clip as evidence that specific skills become obsolete; it matters later in the chapter (Section 36.2) because his own position on learning to code oscillates. **Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md.

Alongside the warnings, Koe offers predictions in favor of the creator. In December 2023 he cites the projection that the creator economy will grow from $250 billion to $480 billion "over the next 5 years," admits that he is "somewhat biased" because the creator economy is part of his identity, and says he "only see[s] that increasing exponentially." He imagines a world in which everyone is a creator, employers hire creators, creators work under other creators to gain experience, and creators are self-sufficient through products that solve real problems they have experienced. **Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md. In 2022 he offered, as a "fun fact," that 60% of future jobs have not yet been created, citing "marijuana therapists" and "twitter ghost writers" as examples of jobs that did not exist before and noting that creators are creating their own; the creator economy as the future, he said, is "a big theory and philosophy" of his. **Source:** Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md. In 2023 he went further: being a creator, doing your life's work, "is going to be for I believe 80 percent of the population over the coming 50 years." **Source:** The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md.

The most revealing of these predictions concerns freelancing, because Koe gives a mechanism for it. In April 2023 he said that 36% of the workforce are freelancers and that, given AI and automation, he "genuinely" believes the figure will "skyrocket to 90 percent plus." He labels it himself: "I like making delusional predictions." The mechanism is economic: companies will realize that they can pay one freelancer $1,500 as one client for the same work for which they pay a full-time in-house salary, and freelancers can earn more by taking on several clients. "Everyone will be a freelancer because the state of work will demand it," until they decide to evolve into a creator: "everyone will be a freelancer until they decide they want to evolve into a Creator." **Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md.

Two further data points complete the picture. In a 2026 video Koe states that intangible digital assets, which do not appear on a company's balance sheet, represent 90% of the value of the S&P 500; he again gives no source. He uses the claim to argue for building digital assets, and immediately qualifies it: Kim Kardashian, Taylor Swift, Ryan Reynolds, MrBeast and Elon Musk have massive audiences and brands, but you probably do not want that kind of fame, "and that's perfectly fine because you don't need millions of followers to make this work." **Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md. And in a 2025 video he plays a short clip of someone whose name is garbled in the transcript, saying: "I would focus on the content creator side of things because I believe that's where the puck is going. That's a much more important shift in how we all consume information and content and are entertained." The speaker cannot be identified from the transcript. **Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md.

The figures are easier to evaluate when placed side by side.

| Claim | Date stated | Source given | Koe's own framing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 36% of the workforce are freelancers; will rise to 90%+ | April 2023 | None | "I like making delusional predictions" |
| Freelance work rose from 36% to 46.6% "since 2020" | July 2024 | None | Context of layoffs |
| Creator economy: $250 billion to $480 billion (in five years / by 2028) | December 2023; July 2024 | None | "I'm somewhat biased" |
| 60% of future jobs not yet created | May 2022 | None | "Fun fact" |
| 80% of the population doing creative life's work within 50 years | June 2023 | None | "I believe" |
| AI smarter than 85% of humans, 99.9% by end of 2026 | May 2025 | David Patterson's post, Musk's reply | Third-party signal |
| Intangible assets are 90% of S&P 500 value | July 2026 | None | Argument for digital assets |

The table makes one inconsistency visible. The same 36% figure appears in 2023 as the current share of freelancers and in 2024 as the 2020 baseline from which the share grew to 46.6%. Koe does not reconcile the two. Elsewhere in the corpus he also cites "a projected 62% of jobs being threatened by Automation," again without a source. None of these numbers should be read as established fact; they are rhetorical supports for a conclusion he reaches on other grounds. That conclusion, repeated across years, is that the individual must take responsibility for their own future, because nobody else will secure it for them.

#### "About 36 months": from worry to indifference

The video that gives this chapter its frame is "You Have About 36 Months To Make It" (August 2025). Its title repeats a claim that was circulating online, and Koe is careful to say that he neither endorses nor rejects it. The circulating claims were that AI will keep replacing jobs; that money as we know it will change or cease to exist, because millions of artificial superintelligences will execute tasks beyond human comprehension; and that crypto is seeing its last great gains because it is being regulated and adopted and will become more like stocks. Koe says these are not his opinions, that he is "indifferent" to them and expects most of them to be wrong. He reframes the claim instead: "you have about 36 months until the definition of making it changes drastically." Whether the horizon is 24 months or ten years, the change is "still probably happening." His stated intention is to provide a perspective from which to notice opportunities in a future that is arriving fast. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

"Making it" is an ordinary phrase used here with a specific sense: not success in the abstract, but the standard by which success is measured at a given time. Koe's point is that the standard itself is moving. What counted as making it under the old arrangement (a stable job, a degree, a career ladder) may not count under the new one.

The reframing also marks a change in Koe's attitude. In 2023 he said he saw only good in automation; in March 2024 he was "pretty worried"; in August 2025 he was "indifferent" to the forecasts; a month later he said that the future of AI was "probably not going to be as drastic as many people made it out to seem, myself included." This is an explicit self-correction of his own dramatism, though he does not explain what changed his mind beyond the general uncertainty. What does not change is the practical conclusion. The urgency to prepare survives the downward revision of the expected magnitude, because the preparation he recommends (taking responsibility for your own learning, audience and income) is the one he was recommending before AI entered the picture.

#### Sam Altman's premise and the three layers of power transfer

In the same 2025 video Koe sets the scene with a quote he calls famous, from Sam Altman: "The amount of power that an individual has is vastly more now than it has been at any time in the past." The quote is Altman's; the explanation of how that power was transferred is Koe's own. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

He describes three successive layers, each of which moved power away from a particular kind of institution.

| Layer | What it gave the individual | From whom power was transferred |
|---|---|---|
| The internet | Access to any and all knowledge | Schools and institutions |
| Social media | Leverage to attract your own audience | Employers, publishers, centralized media, record labels |
| AI | The ability to create, automate and outsource almost anything | Traditional gatekeepers and intermediaries |

The first layer removed the institutions' monopoly on knowledge. The second removed their monopoly on distribution: people can now "learn, build and earn without dependence on someone else's authority." The third removes much of their monopoly on execution: "people will soon be able to operate at the level of large teams with a fraction of the manpower." Koe qualifies the claim himself: it is "not true for all situations, but... largely true." He adds a generational observation. Gen Z, and the "zillennials" born roughly between 1995 and 2000 (his own cohort), are digital-first; Gen Z's lives are both substantially better and substantially worse than those of earlier generations, because they have the tools but have not learned to negate the worst of them. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

The three-layer framework is the formalization of an argument Koe had been making for years without the third layer. In 2022, in "Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years," he was amazed that people did not see the power of modern tools: building a membership platform for a course or community used to take a team of web developers, and now there is a website where you can build one in "less than maybe 10 to 60 minutes." He deliberately refused to name the tool ("go research, you have Google") and asked: "how does this not fuel you with like the most insane energy," since modern internet tools let you do whatever you want. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md. In March 2024 he condensed the idea into a formula: "To build you need technical know-how: you must either learn to code or use the technology available to you." "What used to take 10 people now takes one; what used to take 10 hours now takes one; what used to take $10 now takes one." You live in "a beautiful time where humans can leverage technology to build what they want": website builders, email marketing software, AI, social media, course builders. "It is your job to create and distribute value by learning and building on the internet." **Source:** Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md.

The same month, he stated the political side of the argument as a list of gatekeepers that are no longer necessary: "You no longer need startup Capital to start a business you no longer need a job board to find a career you no longer need a publisher to launch a book you no longer need a record label to make music you no longer need a formal education to become an expert you no longer need religious institutions to feed you meaning." His conclusion: "The centralized authority over learning meaning and earning is only a thing for those who are still asleep." All you need is "an internet connection social media account and the courage to teach fail improve and pursue." **Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md.

The word "asleep" ties this passage to the diagnosis of Part I: the person who still depends on centralized authority for learning, meaning and earning is the person who has not yet become aware of the default path (Chapters 1 and 2). The list also anticipates the whole structure of this chapter. Learning without a formal education is the subject of Section 36.2; earning without a job or capital is the subject of Sections 36.3 to 36.5; and finding meaning without religious institutions is the subject of Section 36.6. Between 2022 and 2024 the argument rested on the internet plus no-code tools; in 2025 AI was added as a third layer that extends the same movement.

#### The three superpowers

Having described how power moved, Koe asks what an individual needs in order to use it. Whether or not you have taken advantage of the three layers, he says, you have three "superpowers" with which to take control of your future:

1. **Learning**: the ability to adapt and to figure out which actions to take in order to get specific results.
2. **Persuasion**: the ability to build trust and attract people to a mutually beneficial vision or narrative. He insists on the qualification: "We're talking about persuasion and mutual benefit, not manipulation."
3. **Execution**: the ability to turn ideas into reality through automation, creation and delegation.

**Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

Each superpower corresponds to one of the layers. The internet made learning possible without institutions; social media made persuasion possible without intermediaries; AI made execution possible without a team. The list is also a compressed version of the skill stacks that Koe had proposed since 2023 (writing, marketing and sales, entrepreneurship, technical know-how), and of the post-AI hierarchy he would propose in 2026 (agency, taste, perspective, persuasion, technical know-how). Section 35.5 treats those stacks in detail. The direction of the change is from market skills toward human traits and judgment: the superpowers describe capacities, not job descriptions.

#### The condition: self-education and self-reliance

The transfer of power does not benefit everyone equally, and Koe names its condition. In "Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich)" (February 2023), the first aspect of what he calls "the new rich" is technology: "the internet has democratized wealth generation for the self-educated and self-reliant." The internet lets you learn skills that schools will not teach, often precisely the skills that make you irreplaceable. The logic is comparative. If schools mass-educate people, those people are replaceable, because the next generation passes through the same curriculum and competes for the same positions. The alternative is to learn "in a non-linear way via curiosity and through the internet in alignment with your personal goals," not goals assigned by school. That is how you forge your own path: by acquiring skills in alignment with your own hierarchy of goals (the structure developed in Chapter 8). Koe's practical advice follows: invest time, and money if you have it, in education that will not lead to a static salary; use that education to get results; and then use the results as leverage to make more money and free up time. **Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md.

This condition is what turns the thesis of power transfer into a demand. The power is available, but only to those who take responsibility for their own education. The same technology that frees the self-educated individual also exposes the person who waits to be taught, which is why Koe's conclusion to every list of disruptive signals is the same: take matters into your own hands. The next section examines where, in his view, that education now comes from.

### 36.2 The Creator Economy and the New School

#### What Koe means by "creator economy"

"Creator economy" is a common term in the technology press, where it usually refers to the market of platforms, sponsorships and tools that serve people who publish content. Koe uses it in a broader and more normative sense. He gives several definitions, each from a different angle.

The first is social. In "Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You)" (April 2023) he opens with: "the Creator economy is a decentralized society where people can learn teach and create a better life for themselves," a realization he says he made over the past decade. Later in the same video he adds the community angle: "The Creator economy is where you find people with shared goals, cur[ate] your own Community, provide value to that community and get paid for your contributions." **Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md. The second is economic. In June 2023 he describes it as composed of individuals distributing value in the form of content and products: they educate their audience on the skills and interests that help them actualize their goals, and their products or services are meant to generate results and move people faster toward those goals. **Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md.

Read together, the definitions describe a cycle. Content educates people toward goals they already have; products accelerate the results; the community forms around shared goals; and payment is the return for a contribution. This is the value-for-value exchange of Chapter 34 organized as a society rather than as a single transaction.

The word "creator" in the phrase also has a specific meaning. In 2025 Koe calls creators "decentralized teachers," "the new school system on the internet," and then clarifies: "Creators, not content creators. Just people who create their life are those who forge new paths and pass down the esoteric information that allow you to take advantage of new opportunities." **Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md. A creator, in his usage, is defined by the life they make, not by the format they publish in. Publishing is how a created life is passed on.

He also distinguishes the creator economy from what he calls the "influencer economy." In his 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, he explains that he labels it the creator economy "because being a Creator implies growth." An influencer "could be static," and "the influencer economy can definitely get saturated"; the creator economy "assumes that you're consistently trying to make progress in life" individually, and the community you bring up with you evolves too. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. The distinction connects to Koe's claim, discussed in Section 27.6, that the creator economy cannot be saturated because each creator is an extension of a unique self, whereas an economy of static personalities competing for attention can be.

In a 2023 video built around the metaphor of life as a video game (Chapter 10), Koe maps the creator economy onto the game:

| Element of the game | Element of the creator economy |
|---|---|
| Virtual reality | The creator economy |
| Your character | Your personal brand |
| The game and its progression | Online business |
| A powerful tribe | Networking |
| How you make a living | Purposeful products |
| Marketing firepower that attracts those like you | The intrinsic philosophy you cultivate throughout your journey |

**Source:** Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md. The mapping ends with a question rather than a description. You are already part of the creator economy, he says; the only question is which side you are on: whether you engage in a mutual exchange of value (product for money), or whether you drain attention, overload your mind and waste your creative ability by producing nothing in return for the value you take. Consumption without contribution is still participation, but on the losing side.

Koe's most idealized image is that of a "self-sufficient Utopia." In the April 2023 video he says that education is "only one aspect of the Creator economy but it is a very important one" and that he believes everyone should start with an education business, but that he sees the creator economy as something wider. He buys from individual creators: shoes, kitchenware, blue light glasses, apparel, coffee and supplements, fresh herbs, milk and meat. "All of these hold philosophies behind them." The kitchenware seller does it out of passion and because he does not want you buying plastics that damage your health; the blue light glasses are stylish but also prevent eye strain and help you sleep. **Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md. The image is of an economy in which almost every purchase is a vote for someone's philosophy. At the end of the same video he generalizes it into a principle: "The future belongs to people that pass down their valuable experiences to improve others lives. That's what all the best businesses do." He proposes a test anyone can run: "take a look at the next social media post you see from the lens of a Creator not a consumer and you can verify this for yourself." **Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md.

Why do creators sell knowledge rather than objects? In 2024 Koe offered an explanation based on commoditization. You follow creators for education and entertainment because what they say is new to you, and "ideas shape society." Creators earn an independent income by pursuing their interests: they solve problems in their own lives and sell the solution. Most of them sell knowledge because commoditized products are increasingly the norm: "we can order anything on Amazon." So "the only thing left to share is Unique Systems and processes that come from the life we experience," passed down "by documenting our mind on the internet." Productivity systems, psychology practices, meditations and business advice are examples. On business advice in particular, he notes that college professors probably do not own a business (unless they teach out of passion after getting rich), so creators are among the few people qualified to teach it. **Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md. This is the same logic that Section 33.5 develops as "systems are the new product."

Koe also answers the suspicion that everyone selling to creators is jumping on a bandwagon. "Creators are small businesses. It's a B2B business to business or C to C, creator to creator." Everyone sells to creators "because there's so frickin many," and the business-to-business and creator-to-creator markets are both huge: "You think everyone's just hopping on the bandwagon. No, it's because that's a massive market." His practical conclusion in that 2025 video is to build an education product first; otherwise you will waste a great deal of time "unless you have a lot of money and experience already." **Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md.

The strength of his conviction is clearest in a March 2024 statement: "I can tell you with confidence through my own experience that the Creator economy is the most true model that you can follow while doing this especially considering human nature post- labor economics and the future of work." "This" refers to building, teaching, selling and evolving a project around your obsession. **Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md. Two later developments complicate this confidence. In January 2026 Koe asked himself, "am I just like promoting this dogma?", and answered that "the more I break it down, the more it seems like the one true way"; he kept the thesis but acknowledged the objection that not everyone can do this, reformulating it as "anyone ambitious can." In the same period he moved from saying that the creator economy grows "exponentially" and cannot be saturated to admitting that models and tactics within it can "become saturated quite quickly" and that a "lower class" of the creator economy exists (Section 36.3). The two positions can coexist if the distinction is kept: the market of identities and interests may be unsaturable, while specific formats and business models within it saturate fast.

#### The Golden Age: where Koe's conviction comes from

Koe's belief in creators as teachers has a biographical origin, which he told in "The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work)" (October 2024). As a teenager he was deeply into fitness and watched small creators on YouTube when the platform was first gaining popularity. He calls that period "the Golden Age of the creator economy," "a peak in authenticity where people were just picking up cameras to share their passions" and teach what they were learning. He names Matt Ogus and Chris Levado (spelled as transcribed) and others in the fitness world as people who strongly influenced his life.

The contrast he draws with his own situation at the time is specific. He was a kid who came home from school, played video games all day, cooked frozen burritos, drank five Dr Peppers and woke up early to maintain his Halo 3 rank so that a friend would not pass him. He had "massive ambitions" but no knowledge or awareness of how to make them real. The only plan he had was to become an anesthesiologist "because it paid a lot," since he had been told his whole life to go to college; that plan framed his perception and everything he absorbed. For a week his search history was universities, majors and top-paying degrees, because he did not want to spend eight to twelve years becoming an anesthesiologist. He says he "can't thank those creators enough" and hopes to be that person for others, which he names as a driving force behind his videos. **Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md.

The story is a small-scale version of the whole thesis of this section. The school offered him one assigned goal (a high-paying degree); a handful of independent creators, teaching what they were passionate about, offered him a different model of how to live. It is an instance of the decentralized education system before he had a name for it.

#### Two modalities of education

Koe's central claim about education is that two systems now coexist. He stated it in December 2022 and repeated it, in nearly the same words, in a February 2024 compilation. The formal education system trains you into the employment system it is directly tied to. The creator economy consists of people who put out free information after forging a new path and teach you how to do it yourself. If you want a career of independence, you may not find it in formal education, nor, he adds, entirely for free online. But if you think critically and find a person worth investing in, someone whose course teaches what they do, what they discovered as a better way, or a new career path created by the internet, that is a good route. "Those are the modes of education that are going to teach you how to do your own thing rather than how to do someone else's thing." He concedes that some courses get a bad reputation, while many course buyers know incredible ones that changed their lives. **Source:** You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md.

In March 2023 he presented the contrast as a diagram (his "visual of the week"). The visual itself was on screen; the transcript allows the two paths to be reconstructed as lists, but not their exact layout.

| The school system path | The creator economy path |
|---|---|
| Prefers conformity | The student pursues their curiosities |
| Memorization | Finds a community |
| Standardized tests | Learns relevant skills you can actually make money with |
| Convergent thinking | Starts a real-world project without needing credentials: "there's no Gatekeepers on the internet" |
| Becoming a cog in the machine | Acquires the specific knowledge necessary along the way |
| Trained into the societal system, the social fabric, as an employee | Becomes an entrepreneur |

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md.

A few months later he described what the creator path allows the individual to do. In the "creator society," passionate creators research topics they love and distribute lessons through their own lens to people who resonate with it. This lets individuals pursue their curiosity; find the teachers or creators they learn best from; treat learning as part of life rather than an obligation; learn modern skills to adapt quickly to a changing environment; find specific knowledge that contributes to self-generated rather than assigned goals; and prioritize entrepreneurship as "the only logical end goal for those that want more out of life." "The new school system is internet content and courses"; none of it is labeled as school, it is simply part of virtual life. **Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md. The distinction between self-generated and assigned goals is the one Chapter 2 and Chapter 8 develop: the school assigns goals, and the creator economy is where a person learns to choose their own.

In 2022 Koe had already framed this as a job description for anyone who publishes. He believes that the one-person business, personal branding and the creator economy are new "for a reason": society has advanced to the point where "everyone and their mother" can have their own business, and as people "we are creators." He promotes being a "value Creator," whose "job is to educate and entertain better than the schools can," because schools cannot keep up and cannot cover every aspect of reality the way everyone researching their own unique interests can. Synthesizing what you learn into articulate educational content "is just the best way to learn," a claim he says he can make only because he has done it. **Source:** Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md.

#### Why school cannot teach this

Koe's critique of school is treated at length in Chapter 2. What matters here is the specific argument for why formal institutions cannot teach the skills of the individual economy. He makes it in several layers.

The first layer is structural. In "The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself)" (October 2022, re-edited in the February 2024 compilation), he argues that the internet makes a new business path possible. Because social media and the internet are accessible and cheap to start with, you do not have to pay to post something online and ask for money in exchange for a valuable service (freelancing, consulting or a beginner-level coaching call) that you then build up as you gain experience. It is "a new path," a new business model created by technological advancement. People consider it taboo because they "aren't in the game" and because it has not been around long, but "it's here to stay." He then generalizes: "every single person on YouTube that is a value Creator rather than an entertainment Creator is an educator," whether or not they label themselves that way; and he counts himself as one. This is a new method of education that college and formal institutions cannot keep up with, because their curriculum cannot be updated to match something that does not lead to a job. The school system, corporations and government are tied together, "training into one another." The internet threatens that arrangement by giving the individual the power to make a creative income on their own, and the education around that income, delivered by individuals on social media and course platforms, is something the school system cannot follow. He mentions "some statistic about the education industry increasing by XYZ" without giving a figure. His conclusion: it is completely in your hands. **Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md.

The second layer is temporal. In April 2023 he observed that freelancing and online business offer endless options (niches, skills to stack, outreach strategies, content tactics), and that every step that feels like progress is met by another roadblock, producing "overwhelming uncertainty" just as you leave the previous phase, "because it's all so new." The online business world has been prevalent "for maybe a decade," which he calls "insane on the grand Scale of the Universe." That is why "College isn't going to teach you any of this right they can't catch up with a decade's worth of curriculum like that fast." **Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md.

The third layer concerns incentives and the qualifications of teachers. In January 2024 he argued that making money online is not taught well in school because it takes away from the "social machine," from being educated into a job that runs the old economy on which society depends: "you doing your own thing is the biggest threat to society in many cases." Moreover, if professors made money online, or more than they make as teachers, they would not be professors; they lack the experience and results. So he recommends taking courses from creators and not closing your mind with "all courses are a scam," because such courses are the only way to learn the new opportunities that creators themselves created. His formula: "Society creates jobs creators create jobs." Where you get your education, he adds, determines much of your life. **Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md. In April 2023 he put the point about teachers more sharply. People would rather get "a sub six-figure job that isn't a promise" by spending four to eight years at university learning the same curriculum as everyone else, than learn from "a decentralized individual teaching what they know because they've gotten results from it." Professors may sometimes have results, but "they're hired by the school system to teach the school system because they went through the school system and they were a good student." Learning from an individual for $50 (a course) or perhaps $5,000 (coaching) the exact information you need, aligned with your interests, "in order to make that a part of your life," is, in his word, "invaluable." **Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md.

The fourth layer adds a concession. In "The Future-Proof Skill Stack" (March 2024), a video whose answers, Koe says, came partly from a long-form X post by Devon Eriksen (spelled "Devin" or "Devon Ericson" in the transcript) and from podcasts Eriksen appeared on, he states that a decentralized education system is being built "right in front of our eyes": creators forging their own paths and passing down lessons, experiences and new career paths as education on social media. Eriksen was then "not a huge name yet" and had just released a novel, *Theft of Fire*, which Koe had started reading ("I haven't read a fiction book in so long"); throughout the video Koe mixes Eriksen's points with his own, and the transcript does not always allow them to be separated. **Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md. The argument runs as follows. If you do not have a habit of learning online, "you probably aren't going to make it," for two reasons: university curricula cannot update fast enough, and you must be self-reliant because no one will teach it to you. It is "silly to bank your future of education on those who are employed to be teachers." The concession is that he means not general education, which is useful for "getting people on the same page," but further education at "the pivotal moment where you must choose what interest you want to pursue." "It is wise to avoid education by those teaching how to be free but aren't free themselves." STEM fields make sense in school because they are hard skills; but schools do not teach entrepreneurship, how to live better or how to make more money, which is why the internet is flooded with teachers of those subjects. People call this a scam or a grift, "when that's the only place it can be taught": "You can't get credentials from school that make you a business owner; you just have to be a business owner." The recommendation is to learn from people who are doing it, through posts, podcasts, videos, courses and coaching. **Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md.

This concession is part of a broader pattern. Across the corpus, Koe's critique of school becomes harsher and more causally precise over time, but he consistently grants that general, basic education has a function. The target is specialized and professional education presented as the only legitimate route to a career.

#### Creators as the decentralized education system

From these arguments Koe builds his positive thesis. In July 2024 he stated it as a chain of identities: "the school system is failing and education is the foundation of humanity social media is the New Town Square creators are the New Renaissance men creators are the decentralized education system creators are the new economy." His evidence in that passage is commercial: a company (its name is garbled in the transcript as "the wallet company") brought on Marques Brownlee, "a creator with massive distribution," as Chief Creative Partner. "We will only see this continue along with companies bringing up in-house creators," and companies want their leaders to start personal brands because "they want to survive." **Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md.

In February 2024 he made the argument as an accusation of inconsistency. You complain that some subject should be taught in schools; now that creators teach it, you call it a scam, even though it is given away nearly free or for a fraction of the cost of a degree. Schools cannot teach it, because professors, by the nature of their job, lack results in the domain that leads to a better-than-average life. The creator economy is filled with "Sovereign individuals" who have gotten results and pass down the solution: "is that not the best education system possible, is that not reality correcting itself." Instead, people distract themselves with memes and entertainment. His verdict: "the only scam is you not taking responsibility for your future with the plethora of information available to you outside of schools." **Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md.

In 2026 he restated the thesis with more moderation and named the business model behind it. "I see the good parts of the creator economy as somewhat of a decentralized education system. Passionate individuals dive deep into their interests, pull out the compelling parts, make them useful, and teach them." Most people now get most of their education related to their own goals ("not their parents' goals, not their universities' goals") from YouTube and from social media creators they resonate with; you will not learn this kind of content in college or high school, so you must supplement your education yourself. "Their entire business model, my entire business model, is improve yourself and then improve others." It follows that anyone with interests to talk about has a way of building an audience, with the traffic and leverage that come with it. "The difficulty lies in talking about your interests in a way that captures attention and makes people want to follow you." **Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md. The qualification "the good parts" is new: in 2026 Koe no longer presents the creator economy as uniformly good, only as containing a decentralized education system at its best.

He also observes that creators are becoming primary sources of information. Anyone who posts on social media ("let's just call them a creator") is becoming a primary source of news or education, because "people trust creators more than they trust centralized institutions." This, he says, is a massive opportunity for those with genuine expertise or a unique way of viewing the world and the ability to articulate it, "all skills that can be learned." **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

Koe gives this decentralized system a historical precedent. In ancient Greek societies, the upper class (he names Marcus Aurelius) had the best teachers one-on-one in mathematics, strategy, philosophy and literature. That disappeared for a long time, especially with schools in which we sit before "a government trained expert" and are encouraged to memorize, conform and get good grades. The internet changed it, though most people do not realize it: "you can go and look up the greatest thinkers of our time and get technically one-on-one education from them." He disclaims being Marcus Aurelius but believes his own videos have value. **Source:** The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md.

In January 2026 he quoted a passage from his own book *Purpose and Profit* (free on his Substack): "It's no longer about sitting in front of a government-trained expert and learning the same thing as everyone else to end up with a soon-to-be irrelevant skill stack. It's about finding someone you relate with, someone with a shared vision for the future." He listed the beliefs he has held for a long time: the creator economy is an interest-based education system far more effective than traditional schooling at teaching relevant skills; people learn more, and faster, from someone they relate to; "anyone can follow four to five people on a topic and become very good at that thing very fast"; and there is knowledge and experience that only you can acquire, which it is noble to acquire and pass on. The vessel for passing it on, however, must evolve, as it always has: from speaking by the fire, to sending letters, to building libraries, to the internet. **Source:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md. "Vessel" is one of Koe's recurring words (Chapter 9): the container through which something is carried. Here the claim is that the content of education (lived knowledge transmitted from person to person) is old; only its container is new.

Two further metaphors describe what this education is like. In 2023: the creator economy condensed information into actionable courses on business, health, wealth and relationships; "content courses and books are like zip files for your mind." In the modern world it no longer takes four to twelve years, forty thousand dollars and a diploma to earn a replaceable income. But this implies personal responsibility: nobody holds your hand. **Source:** The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md. A zip file is compressed: it contains the result of someone else's years of work in a form that has to be unpacked by the person who receives it. The metaphor captures both the advantage (speed, cost) and the condition (you have to do the unpacking).

From the importance of education Koe derives an imperative. "Education is the pillar of progress and evolution, education changes human behavior, education creates useful people that contribute to society earn more and create jobs." The education industry "will only continue to grow and those that don't accept it may be left behind." Hence: "start an education business I don't care who you are start one with a personal brand and contribute to the Future that's where all of this is heading." **Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md. In "The Value Creator" (July 2023) he explained why he prefers the education model over other business models: it taps into your individual experience, it helps people live better lives through your products, and it lets you "actively create an education system online that new people can join," choosing the creators they learn best from, "rather than all going through the same education system and turning into cogs in the machine." **Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md. The imperative was later qualified. In 2026 Koe described static information products as having reached "the final stage of market sophistication" and recommended evolving them into software and implementation tools (Section 33.5); he did not, however, abandon the claim that education as a function is too foundational to disappear.

#### The public school and the full life cycle of knowledge

Koe describes the future of schooling with a precise image. In March 2023, as step three of his model for one-person businesses, he told his audience to turn their philosophy into a "public school." Social media is the public school. He is "convinced that the future of schooling will be done online with creators as teachers"; each student joins the school that aligns most with their interests, values and preferred method of learning. Instead of one school system dominating twelve or more years of their life, a student evolves beyond one creator-based school after a few years, moves to the next, and eventually starts their own. He calls this "the cyclical nature of life": we are always passing on what we learn, as in everyday communication, where you pass on what you know and believe in accordance with a conscious, subconscious or unconscious goal. **Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md. In the summary of his book *The Art of Focus* (January 2024), he repeats the prediction: with formal education "nearing its end as all things do," students "would evolve from Creator to Creator according to the Deep knowledge they provide in their courses, mentorships and content as decentralized schools." **Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md.

The image of the student who eventually founds a school connects to a principle Koe states in "How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More" (May 2024): "the full life cycle of knowledge." Passing knowledge on "in the form of education or writing or content or media in general" is "how you get feedback and you refine it and you identify gaps in your knowledge so that you can learn more." "If you aren't actively conversing educating or teaching other people your knowledge has gaps in it and you aren't as smart as you think you are." Not teaching means not "taking the full cycle," and, he adds, "you are in essence being selfish by not using knowledge for what it is meant to be used for, which is to give to other people and to receive from other people so that you can survive as a species." **Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md. The argument has two parts that should be kept apart. The epistemic part (teaching reveals gaps and produces feedback) is related to the principle of learning by teaching that Part VI develops (the protégé effect). The moral part (not teaching is selfish) is Koe's own value judgment.

Koe places the entire shift inside a larger pattern, which he presents as an observation about reality. In February 2024 he said: "You don't need statistics to observe reality." The main pattern of reality is "division and reunion centralization and decentralization." In *The Art of Focus* he illustrates it with what he calls "The Uncommon Sense of the rain cycle": something we all know but treat as a superficial process rather than as an indicator of life itself. The ocean evaporates into divided droplets, the droplets reunite in clouds, the clouds divide into rain, and the rain keeps dividing and reuniting in plants, in our homes and elsewhere, on all dimensions and scales of reality. His prediction, which he says is "backed by 4.5 billion years of evolution," is that the centralized school and economy are decentralizing thanks to new technology (the internet, code and now AI), producing the creator economy: one-person businesses educating, entertaining and inspiring the masses while earning thousands to millions a year. **Source:** Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md. This is a metaphor that functions as an argument by analogy. Koe offers it in place of statistics, and it should be read as an interpretive lens on history rather than as evidence. Its value is that it makes the decentralization of education look like one phase of a recurring cycle rather than a one-time disruption; its limit is that, by the same logic, the cycle would also predict a later re-centralization, which Koe does not discuss.

#### The scam objection and Koe's answers

The most common objection to creator education is that it is a scam: people with little to teach selling courses on how to sell courses. Koe answers it in several ways.

The first answer is about where knowledge of a new economy can come from. In November 2023 he asked: "Do you not see that the only way to make money in the new economy is from people who created the jobs and opportunities to make money in that economy?" People say creators are "grifting and scamming" because "they're teaching what they know, they're teaching what they've done, they're passing down their lessons," and they call it "a pyramid scheme because he's doing this so he's teaching the other person how to do this." His reply: "that's the only thing you actually can sell ethically is your experience." "Just because you don't understand it doesn't mean it's [not] useful" (the transcript reads "useful," but the sense requires "not useful"). **Source:** People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md. The argument turns the accusation around. Teaching what you have done is not a defect of creator education; it is the only legitimate thing it could sell.

The second answer is about who complains. In June 2024 he argued that people know they can learn, build anything and earn online because tools and resources are readily available and efficient. Real estate is physical and scarce, neither efficient nor cost-friendly; digital tools are affordable and accessible: software does a great deal, and courses cost a fraction of college. Very few people, whom he calls "idiots, frankly," worry about scams, and they are the ones who get scammed and are loud about it: a "vocal minority." The space has evolved, many courses are decent, refunds exist, and often people "get scammed" because they do not do the work or because "you buy it thinking that oh if I pay $35 I'm going to make a million." **Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md.

The third answer is moral. At the end of his June 2023 video on the one-person business model, Koe asks his audience to take a course, invest in their self-education and contribute to the creator economy. If you will ask for money from it but do not put money into it, "that's kind of selfish"; you should not join the new society that way. Put money in because you believe in it, because you are investing, and because doing so puts "pressure on the line" for yourself to become part of that economy. **Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md. The phrase links to the "invest energy" mechanism of Section 29.2: putting something at stake commits you to the path.

These answers sit beside a tension that the corpus does not fully resolve. In 2024 Koe also said that "there is absolutely no set path that someone can give you... or else that's an assignment," while at the same time recommending paid courses from creators and teaching his own methods step by step. His partial resolution elsewhere is that advice and courses are maps, not territory: scaffolding for beginners that must be made one's own through trial and error. Read that way, buying a creator's course is not accepting an assigned path; it is acquiring someone else's compressed experience (the "zip file") as a starting point for your own.

#### Every business is a media business

The last element of Koe's picture of the new economy concerns what kind of business the individual builds. His answer is that every business is now, in part, a media business.

The argument starts from a framework he presented in 2022. The internet has two layers: "the back end of the internet is code the internet runs on code the front end what you see is media." To tap into "the infinite leverage and possibilities of the internet" and connect with anyone, you need to learn one or the other; these are two very broad pathways. Code, however, has reached the point of no-code tools: anyone can build a website, and anyone can get on social media and reach anyone in the world "if they are smart enough and know how to network." So, although he had been a web developer, Koe announced that he would not talk about code: you can use code or no-code tools to distribute your media as fast and as widely as possible, and media is what captures attention. You need attention, and people, to get eyes on your products or services; and if you want to make money outside a job, you need a product or service: a course, a physical product (a bag of coffee for a coffee brand), or a service business such as freelancing with writing or other media and marketing skills. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md. In 2023 he compressed this: to make money outside your job you need distribution and a product, and to do it as one person without a physical location you need code and content. "Code is how you host content content is how you capture attention." You do not need to learn to code: what took ten employees to build (a membership site) can now be done by one person buying software. You can post, have one big account share it, and get hundreds of thousands of views without ever having posted before; you can build a readership that lets you do anything, without being bound to one static niche. **Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md.

**Complementary context:** the pairing of code and media as the two forms of "permissionless leverage" comes from Naval Ravikant, whom Koe cites extensively elsewhere (Section 25.3). Koe's specific contribution is the front-end/back-end image and the decision to prioritize media for beginners.

His position on code did not stay fixed. In December 2023, reading *The Anthology of Balaji* (Balaji Srinivasan), he took from it, by his own account paraphrasing, the sequence "first physical, then digital, then native digital," and concluded that "if it can be done on the internet right now it will be done on the internet in the future." He also took a second idea, which he says is not a direct quote: money is a tool for building what you want but does not yet exist. He then used the first idea as a reason why learning both code and content "is not optional." **Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md. The positions can be laid out in order:

| Date | Position on code |
|---|---|
| 2022 | He will not talk about code; media is the pathway to choose |
| August 2023 | "You don't need to learn to code" |
| December 2023 | After Balaji, learning code and content "is not optional" |
| March 2024 | "Learn to build" becomes a step of his skill stack (technical know-how, "learn to code or use the technology available to you") |
| July 2024 | Plays the clip "I would be telling my grandchild not to go study coding" |
| 2025–2026 | AI democratizes code; "media matters more than code," while building software with AI is encouraged |

The oscillation is real, and Koe does not resolve it explicitly. A reading that is consistent with all the statements is that he never asks the reader to become a professional programmer, but does ask them to understand enough to build with the available tools, first no-code platforms and later AI. For someone who has mastered content, code is a multiplier; distribution remains the bottleneck.

From the two-layer framework follows the principle that every business is a media business. In November 2023, amid what he called the "digital transformation" (everyone going digital, huge companies with no idea how to run a social media account), he said: "This is all businesses are, they're all media companies nowadays. If a company isn't a media company then you need to turn them into one because that's what your skill is going to help them with." **Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md. In January 2026 he restated the chain of reasoning: "Every business is a media business right now. And remember, you need attention. How do you generate attention? Media. Where is media? Where is attention? Social media." He added a qualification: at least right now. Platforms may not last forever, something will take their place, and you will have to adapt. For people with multiple interests, he said, without wanting to give "a blanket prescription" to everyone, the best move is probably to become a content creator. **Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md.

In January 2024 Koe pushed the idea in a more philosophical direction. Creators are "decentralized media companies." Media runs the world: it shapes culture, keeps people in chains and exposes people to their potential. Everything on your screen is media, and so are billboards, roadside shops, the car brands next to you in traffic and the song on the radio. "We are in a spiritual war of ideas." Koe prefers the word "content" to "media" because it is more encompassing: "everything you hold within Consciousness is content the content of your attention." Mindful content helps people grow, evolve and expand their sense of self; mindless content encourages decay, stagnation and the defense of a low-development ego. "That's the game we are playing." **Source:** The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md.

This last move connects the economics of the chapter to the psychology of the earlier parts of the book. If content is whatever occupies consciousness, then a creator who publishes is not only running a business but competing to shape what other people's attention holds, which Chapter 5 described as the order or entropy of consciousness. The creator economy, on this reading, is the market in which the contents of attention are produced, and choosing what to publish is choosing which side of the "spiritual war of ideas" you are on. The phrase is Koe's interpretive framing, not a description of a measurable conflict, but it explains why he treats the decision to create as a moral one as well as an economic one.

### 36.3 Replicable Work Gets Replaced

The transfer of power to the individual has a reverse side. The same technologies that let one person do the work of a team also let a company do without the people who performed that work. This section reconstructs Koe's account of which work is replaced first and why. His answer, stated in different ways between 2023 and 2026, is that what gets replaced is not a profession but a type of work: replicable, predictable, assigned work.

#### The entry level is going extinct

Koe's first point in "The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills)" (July 2024) is that "it's becoming harder and harder to get an entry-level job it doesn't matter if it's programming or marketing AI can or is very close to being able to perform basic specialized work in most domains." He immediately adds a nuance that changes the meaning of the claim: "this doesn't necessarily mean that the entry level is leaving it just means that the bar is being raised what used to be an entry-level position is now or will be a junior level or senior level position." "The existing Workforce will be composed of highly paid and highly skilled individuals who have a select few traits." He leaves the video to answer the questions that follow: what traits those people have, what beginners should do to earn an income, and how developers, writers, marketers, salespeople and designers will survive. **Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md.

The phrase "the entry level is going extinct" is Koe's, and it means something narrower than it sounds. Entry-level work still exists; what has disappeared is the low threshold. The tasks a beginner used to be paid to do while learning are the tasks AI now does adequately, so the first paid position now demands the competence of someone who has already learned. In September 2025, in "12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months," he condensed this into a slogan under his seventh rule, "entrepreneurship is spiritual": "the future of work will consist mostly of entrepreneurs and if not entrepreneurs, elite employees who have entrepreneurial traits in increasingly rare positions. The entry level will go extinct. AI isn't replacing the bar, it's raising the bar." Two options remain, he says: "rely on government support with a marginal chance at a good life or take full responsibility and become an entrepreneur." **Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md.

This position is the outcome of an evolution worth tracing, because Koe's view of employment changed several times. In 2023 he predicted that freelancers would reach 90% or more of the workforce (Section 36.1) and spoke of being "on the horizon of complete removal" of labor work; he also said that creators employ other creators and that employers would hire creators. In 2024 he said, "I don't see employment going away," only decentralizing. By 2025 the claim had become that "the jobs of the future will be reserved for the elite, the top 1%, the NBA of jobs," with entrepreneurship as the only route for ambitious people with multiple interests, and the formula of "entrepreneurs or elite employees" quoted above. The change is from the disappearance of employment to its transformation and elitization. Koe's stated reason is that AI and automation turn the entry level into a senior level; he does not explain the earlier reversals.

#### Why specialists go first: predictable inputs, predictable outputs

Why does AI take over entry-level and specialized work in particular? In February 2025 Koe gave a mechanism: "AI is replacing specialist roles because predictable outputs come from predictable inputs. Since many jobs are repetitive series of tasks that lead to non-creative outputs, AI can do this good enough. But when you add another layer of abstraction or multiple, the number of outputs that can occur are infinite. AI only gives you one unless you refine it or tell the AI to refine itself." That one output is probably not the best, "and it's absolutely not the one that will garner the love and attention of the supporters who consume the output, because most AI Generations right now are slop." **Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md.

The argument rests on a distinction between two kinds of task. In the first, the input determines the output: a known request yields a known deliverable, and "good enough" is easy to judge. In the second, each added layer of abstraction (a purpose behind the task, a brand behind the purpose, a vision behind the brand) multiplies the number of acceptable outputs, and choosing among them requires judgment. AI, in Koe's account, collapses the space of possibilities to a single default answer unless someone steers it. "Slop," a common internet word he uses with a specific sense, names that default answer: output that is adequate but generic, and therefore unable to attract anyone's love or attention. The concept returns in Sections 36.4 and 36.5, where the distinction between slop and art turns out to depend on taste.

The same month, answering the objection that AI will first replace social media managers, copywriters and content marketers, Koe replied: "Correct," but only the formulaic ones. His examples are the social media manager who posts a fancy Canva image that gets zero results; the content marketer who puts a pretty girl on screen with a Gen Z caption that people are already tired of (something he says can already be produced with a tool whose name is garbled in the transcript); and the copywriter who writes blog posts only so that a company has an online presence and is fired because AI was faster. "If you're writing simple blog posts to just allow the company to have an online presence then yeah you're pretty replaceable." **Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md.

These examples share one feature: the output is measured by its existence, not its effect. A post that exists, a caption that fills a slot, a blog article that gives a website "presence": none of them depends on anyone's particular judgment, and so none of them requires a particular person.

#### Trading time for replicable tasks

Koe's broader argument against replicable work predates his focus on AI and is phrased in terms of leverage (Chapter 34). In "How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More" (May 2024), the third of his realizations is that "you are trading time for money when technology will eventually make those tasks more efficient." "What you do for work will be less valuable in the future and you'll either take a massive pay cut or get fired." The general principle: "Technology lowers the cost of replicable tasks so we can focus on solving creative and human problems." "The repetitive work that you're doing right now isn't going to be worth what you're being paid for right now; that's just how evolution goes." If your job is "a repetitive string of tasks" that a robot can eventually do, "it's just a matter of time," and when that time comes "it's not that you're going to be paid less for those tasks it's that you're going to be paid nothing," because the robot does it more affordably and employers see "employees and overhead" as "a problem that they need to solve." "Society has been solving the problem of labor work since the dawn of time." **Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md.

The step from "paid less" to "paid nothing" is the core of the argument. Koe is not predicting a gradual decline in wages for repetitive work; he is saying that once a machine can do a task, the market price of a human doing it falls to zero, because no employer will pay for what a cheaper substitute provides.

To separate the work that will be replaced from the work that will not, Koe borrows a criterion. He introduces it with a quote he attributes to someone transcribed as "Nal rant" (later in the same video as "nval"), almost certainly Naval Ravikant, "who has begun to shape my worldview yet again as I've dove back into his content and his insights revolving around leverage and just work in general." The quote: "if the work doesn't require creativity delegate it automate it or leave it." **Source:** How Smart Creatives Work Less & Earn More LEVERAGE.md. The transcript does not spell the name, so the attribution is plausible rather than certain. Koe uses the criterion to define "leveraged work" as work that requires creativity; everything else is a candidate for delegation, automation or abandonment.

Replicable work also has a cost that is not economic. In "The Age Of The Generalist" (May 2024), Koe says that when your work is a repetitive string of tasks, "you're not a creative," "not pulling from multiple sources to solve a problem that you choose to solve." Two consequences follow: "not only are you a prime candidate for replacement but life loses its zest," because there is no challenge or novel goal to bring meaning. Your time collapses into working all day, being tired all night, paying the bills and "hoping your family doesn't leave you." **Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md. The pairing is significant for the rest of the chapter. The same feature of work, the absence of a self-chosen challenge, makes it both replaceable and meaningless. Section 36.6 builds on this when Koe argues that AI threatens meaning as well as jobs.

In August 2024 Koe gave the distinction its sharpest form: "technicians" versus "creators." The world no longer benefits the individual who works 40 to 80 hours a week as a permanent lifestyle; it is shifting "from technicians to creators," with the entry level turning into a senior level thanks to AI and automation (a reference to his July video on the future of work). Technicians will either be replaced or forced to become creators. The definition: "technicians work toward the goals they are assigned creators cultivate a lifestyle that naturally results in the goals they choose." **Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md.

| | Technician | Creator |
|---|---|---|
| Who chooses the goal | Someone else (the goal is assigned) | The person (the goal is chosen) |
| How the goal is reached | By grinding directly toward it | As the natural result of a lifestyle |
| Exposure to AI and automation | Replaced, or forced to become a creator | Uses the tools for chosen goals |

The two differences in the definition are independent. One concerns who chooses (assigned versus chosen goals, the axis of Chapter 2 and Chapter 8); the other concerns how the goal is reached (direct grinding versus a lifestyle whose by-product is the goal, the logic of Chapter 12's 4-Hour Workday). A person could choose their goals and still grind toward them; Koe's "creator" combines both.

In August 2025 he extended the argument to identity. Most people identify with the "traits of a machine": schooling, the job, mundane repetitive tasks. Identifying with those traits means being left behind. "That's exactly why general fulfillment is abysmal because AI and machines are actually coming in to do the work that should have been done by machines in the first place." He acknowledges that there will be turbulence and uncertainty and that many people will not benefit; he says he can only see it logically, position himself and others, and share his perspective. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md. The claim reverses the usual framing of automation as loss. On Koe's reading, the dissatisfaction that people feel in modern work is evidence that they were doing machine work all along; automation removes something that was never suited to humans. This is an interpretive claim, not a measured one, and Koe himself concedes that the transition will hurt many people.

#### The raised baseline in the one-person business

By 2026 Koe applied the same logic to his own field. The one-person business, which he had presented as the escape from replicable work, is subject to the same raising of the bar.

In "How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026)" (January 2026), he said that, as in every career sector and especially because of AI, "the baseline that gets results has been raised." A person at a low level of development can take the quick route: tell AI to generate an ebook, write viral content, generate images and call it art, and this can make some money. But such people will never compete with those who understand what is going on. "It's never been easier to start a mediocre oneperson business." If you do not want to be in the lower class of the creator economy ("I hate saying that... the lower class absolutely exists"), you must understand why information products are dying. **Source:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md. Section 33.5 treats the evolution of information products; what matters here is the phrase "lower class of the creator economy." In 2023 Koe described the creator economy as a self-sufficient utopia (Section 36.2). In 2026 he admits that it has a stratified structure, with a bottom layer composed of those who use AI as a shortcut.

Two months later, in "How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI" (March 2026), he described the double effect of AI on the model. "Before AI, social media was the technology that allowed such a one-person business to exist, but you could create a comfortable lifestyle business; you couldn't build some crazy, insane, super high-revenue business; you would eventually have to hire a team." Now "the bar has been raised as to how much you can make because you can do more as one person with AI, but the barrier of entry to starting a one-person business is now lower than ever. Anyone can do it." That means more competition, but "most people don't try beyond a certain point: 95 plus percent of people hit one failure and then quit altogether" (a figure he gives without a source). **Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md.

AI therefore moves the business in two directions at once. The ceiling rises: what one person can earn without a team grows. The floor drops: anyone can start. The middle becomes more crowded, but persistence filters it, because most entrants quit after their first failure. The ceiling Koe names has itself risen over the years. In 2023 he said that "nobody's really pushed the boundary past five, ten million as one person"; in 2024 he spoke of "one person businesses to make $10 million a year"; and in 2026 he reinterpreted the pre-AI model retrospectively as a "lifestyle business." Each technological stage, in his telling, raises what one person can do.

#### Technology as an accelerant of polarity

If AI raises both the ceiling and the floor, who benefits? The question was raised explicitly in Koe's March 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, and the exchange is worth reconstructing because the two men did not agree.

Koe had argued optimistically that as more people solve their basic problems, and as automation, AI and technology take over "the productive mechanical robotic-ish style work that is against human nature," people will move to solving creative problems and surviving "on that mental plane" (Section 36.6 develops this argument). Bush pushed back. If you are not putting yourself in a position to leverage AI, technology and automation, you will not solve those problems; he pictured the humans of *WALL-E*, at 500 pounds with every dopamine receptor hooked up. "Technology is an accelerant of polarity": "you can arm yourself with it and it's all going to turn into a power law where the people who use it effectively are going to continue to capture more and more... value." His evidence was that the highest-leveraged individuals, Jeff Bezos and Elon Musk, used technology, automation and labor to ascend, and that the gap between trillion-dollar companies and the smallest ones "is only going to get wider." "If you don't see that happening in five years... and you're still not figuring out a way to use these Technologies... it's game over." Koe's reply did not contest the power law; he added a point about awareness. "You're eating crickets," he joked, and then said that awareness is the first step: a listener who decides to start "is 100 times better place than someone who... didn't even know this podcast existed... otherwise you're just kind of an NPC." **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

The phrase "accelerant of polarity" is Bush's, not Koe's, and the distinction matters. In 2023 the two positions stood side by side: Koe's expectation that technology frees humans for creative work, and Bush's expectation that it widens the gap between those who use it and those who do not. By 2025 Koe had incorporated Bush's side into his own view. He acknowledged that "many won't benefit" from the transition, and he predicted that 99% would choose mediocrity when given the option (Section 36.6). The two views can be reconciled: liberation is possible, but only for a deliberate minority, and technology increases the distance between that minority and everyone else. This is why Koe's practical advice never depends on the technology alone. The same tool that lets a director do the work of a team lets a passive consumer sink further into dopamine. Which of the two a person becomes is the subject of the next section.

### 36.4 AI Needs Direction

If AI replaces replicable work, what does it leave? Koe's answer is that current AI is a tool without a purpose of its own. It can execute, but it cannot decide what is worth executing. The human contribution therefore moves up a level: from doing the work to directing it. This section reconstructs the argument, Koe's account of what current AI is not, and the distinction between "doers" and "directors" that organizes his practical advice.

#### A tool that needs a master

Koe opened his December 2025 video "The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now" with a formula: "The future belongs to those who understand that AI needs direction, tools need masters, data needs insight, content needs context, success needs vision. The mind is a story engine, and those who learn to control it will thrive." **Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md. Each pair names a raw capability and the human element on which it depends:

| Raw capability | What it depends on |
|---|---|
| AI | Direction |
| Tools | Masters |
| Data | Insight |
| Content | Context |
| Success | Vision |

The last sentence of the formula, "the mind is a story engine," returns in Section 36.6, where it explains why humans keep paying attention to other humans.

The most precise statement of the principle came a year earlier, in "A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life" (December 2024): "AI, maybe not AGI but AI what we have now, lacks executive function. It is a tool that needs a master. It is a system that achieves a goal that it is assigned. It is a specialist that needs a generalist." He follows it with a diagnosis of why the fear of replacement is so widespread: "When so many people have been turned into tools by their parents, schooling and repetitive task-oriented jobs, it's no wonder why people are so afraid of being replaced. It's because they are going to be replaced unless they start acting like a human: agency, conscious experience, risk, mistakes, discovery, perception, struggle." **Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md.

"Executive function" is a term from psychology for the capacities that set goals, plan and regulate behavior. The term also appears in Koe's conversation with Devon Eriksen from the same month, where it is Eriksen who uses it to describe what a human "is": the capacity to decide what one wants, how to get it with the tools at hand, and which new tool to create. Koe's own use applies the concept to AI: what current AI lacks is precisely the ability to set its own goals. The argument then connects to the core of the book. A system that "achieves a goal that it is assigned" is the definition of a person living on assigned goals (Chapters 1 and 2). People who have been trained to execute assigned goals are therefore functionally equivalent to the tool, and the tool is cheaper. The list that ends the passage (agency, conscious experience, risk, mistakes, discovery, perception, struggle) describes what acting like a human means in contrast.

In July 2024 Koe applied the same reasoning to writers and programmers. Will writers be replaced if AI can write a landing page or a term paper; will coders be replaced if AI can build an app? "The answer is yes some of them if they aren't in control of the vision." "Computers are hyper Specialists humans are deep generalists a great combo but not too effective on their own." "Computers will only replace those who deserve to be replaced." AI helps you write the paper, create the design or edit the video faster, "which gives more power to the individual using the AI and that's what's created the ability for one person businesses to make $10 million a year." "There still has to be an individual orchestrating the path toward the vision," whether that is a creator using AI in every part of their business or a company using AI to replace workers in pursuit of the company's vision. **Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md.

The phrase "computers will only replace those who deserve to be replaced" belongs to a tone that Koe later softened. In March 2024 he had said that "people that are worried about AI taking their creative jobs are the ones that weren't going to make it anyways." By February 2026 he framed the issue as a crisis of meaning that affects everyone, and acknowledged that livelihoods are at stake; by June 2026 he prefaced his argument by saying that he spoke to a specific individual, "not people in circumstances with less control." He never explained the change. The underlying claim (that what protects you is control of a vision) remains constant; the moral judgment of those who are replaced fades.

Why do people end up without a vision? In "Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years" (July 2024), Koe offers a mechanism. There is too much on everyone's mind: social media, notifications, "AI is taking over," twenty-year-olds who do not know what to learn, children who do not know what to study. "All of these things start to add up in your mind and they just lead to paralysis by analysis you end up quote unquote doing nothing with your life but in reality you do what other people want for you and that becomes more efficient with time and as technology advances so by doing nothing you're just making yourself more of a replacement for AI." The people who are replaced are "the people that don't have Direction the people that don't have Vision the people who can't make a decision for themselves create their own goals and achieve them." "That alone is this entire video it's learning how to think for yourself learning how to make decisions for yourself and understanding that problems will always exist." He adds a mechanism for why replacement keeps happening: people are replaced "because the problems that are being solved by that replacement are easy to solve with the thing but then that's going to birth more problems." The central question, in his words, is "how do we choose and pursue the things that are going to secure our future when those things continuously change as technology does." **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md.

The chain is worth making explicit. Information overload produces paralysis. Paralysis looks like doing nothing, but in practice it means following other people's plans by default. Following other people's plans is executing assigned goals. Executing assigned goals is exactly what AI does more efficiently. So the person overwhelmed by the AI debate becomes, through that overwhelm, more replaceable by AI. The remedy is the capacity to choose one's own goals, which is the subject of Part IV of this book.

Later in the same video Koe explains how vision and goals protect a person: "Because the people being replaced by AI are achieving the goals that the AI helps them achieve rather than getting replaced you're becoming the Visionary who can use AI as a tool to further your own goals you're becoming a free thinker you're becoming a master simply by having a vision and a goal for your future." He immediately adds a caveat: vision and goals "are fickle those things are guides those things are so far out so we need to bring it closer to us so we can act every day with confidence," which is why he goes on to discuss systems. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. The positions are symmetrical: either AI helps you reach your goals, or you are one of the means by which someone else's goals are reached, and the second position is the one AI fills.

Near the end of that video he gives a concrete illustration of what AI cannot provide without a vision. You can create images with AI, potentially video and animation, and write with AI, "but it doesn't [ __ ] matter if you don't have a vision like you can tell the AI to write an article but how does that article play a role in 5 years of newsletters connecting together how does that lead to a book that illustrates your life philosophy that you came to from experience it can't." Unless he were the one directing it with his vision, "that's going to suck," because without that you "don't understand persuasion Marketing sales the future proof skill stack." "The AI doesn't have my vision the AI isn't attracting people that are similar to me and if I wasn't the one using the AI with the audience that I have or the products that I have it would be completely and utterly useless." His summary is transcribed as "AI in the hands of the vision list is nothing to worry about"; the key word is ambiguous and could be "visionless" or "visionary," which would give the sentence opposite meanings. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md.

The example of the article and the five years of newsletters is the clearest way to see what "direction" means. An individual output (one article) can be generated. What cannot be generated is its place in a larger structure: how it connects with what came before, how it leads to a book, how that book expresses a life philosophy reached through experience, and how all of it attracts a specific kind of reader. The value lies in the structure, and the structure exists only in the mind of the person who holds the vision.

In February 2025, in the provocatively titled video "The creator economy is dying thanks to AI," Koe added a point about intelligence itself. Suppose this exact video were made word for word, scene by scene, with AI, and gave you the value you wanted: would it matter? He guesses that you would not care. The objection "if I knew it was AI something would feel off" comes from misunderstanding AI. Benchmark hype and talk of "all jobs replaced" cloud people's perception, because "intelligence is only one resource and it's proving to be a pretty poor one in isolation it falls flat if there is no vision taste or agency." "If you aren't already good at the thing AI won't be good at the thing." If he made the video with AI, he would still orchestrate the images, the scenes, everything; it would still be him creating, just faster and not on camera. "AI hasn't replaced content it's just made bad content easier to create." People without skill who use AI for quick results, like buyers of get-rich-quick courses trying to avoid long-term commitment, will fail, because "the principles haven't and won't change ever." **Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md.

The title of that video promises a death that the argument denies. Koe's actual thesis is that AI lowered the cost of bad content, not that it replaced good content. "Intelligence is only one resource" connects to his broader claim (Chapter 35) that agency, not intelligence, is the bottleneck; here AI makes the claim concrete, because AI is intelligence made abundant, and its abundance shows how little intelligence alone achieves.

#### What current AI is not: AGI, consciousness and the five capabilities

Koe's argument depends on a claim about the limits of current AI, and he made that claim in three different ways over two years.

The first is a distinction between AI and AGI. In December 2024, after OpenAI released a benchmark for its new model "o3," "some people are screaming AGI is here, some... that jobs are going to be replaced within 5 years." Koe's response: "AGI is not AI; AI is what we have right now." AI makes things easier ("you can go and write a book"), but "that doesn't mean that people know how to at all still, and that doesn't mean they'll go do it... they'll publish the book." "The gurus and entrepreneurs of the world have been screaming at you to just start for the past 8 billion years and you still haven't." "There's not much to worry about if you're just a person who acts on their own interest; if you're just floating along through life then yes there is a lot to worry about, but that hasn't changed." He defines "actual AGI" as human-level capability "of being able to choose its own goals, discover new goals and act within any domain of life or medium, physical or mental"; until then, current AI cannot replace writers or designers. **Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md. The definition is consistent with his executive-function argument: the line between AI and AGI is drawn exactly where goal-setting begins. The observation that AI makes starting easier without making people start is an application of his theory of agency: tools lower the cost of action, but they do not supply the decision to act.

The second is an argument from consciousness. In July 2024 Koe cited what he described as "a research paper titled ChatGPT is [bullshit] which is a sort of sequel to the declaration that ChatGPT is a [bullshit] generator from computer scientists Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor": "because these programs cannot themselves be concerned with truth and because they are designed to produce text that looks truth-apt without any actual concern for truth it seems appropriate to call their outputs [bullshit]." From this he draws his own interpretation: "the major downfall of machines is they lack conscious experience if you can harness the singular yet infinitely complex trait of self-reflective Consciousness you have a chance to survive that is to say if you learn to earn or survive with the creative ability of your mind not time labor looks status Prestige handouts or any other fickle and replaceable materialistic resource you unlock a level of power reserved for those of true wealth." **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md.

**Complementary context:** the paper "ChatGPT is bullshit" (2024) was written by the philosophers Michael Townsen Hicks, James Humphries and Joe Slater, who used Harry Frankfurt's definition of "bullshit" as speech indifferent to truth. The earlier essay "ChatGPT is a bullshit generator" (2022) was by Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor. Koe's sentence can be read as attributing the earlier piece to Narayanan and Kapoor, which is correct, but the transcript is not clear about who wrote which. More important is the step Koe takes: the paper argues that the models are indifferent to truth, and Koe reads that indifference as a symptom of a deeper lack, conscious experience. That reading is his interpretation, not a conclusion of the paper.

The third, and most speculative, comes from December 2025. In "The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now," Koe asks whether AGI will make human intelligence irrelevant. Agency is valuable, he says, but we are bound by physics, and AGI hype cycles raise the worry that human intelligence will become irrelevant. So: are human capabilities limited or infinite? As high-agency generalists, can't we learn and do anything our genes do not prevent? We thrive in many niches by adapting with knowledge and tools. The fundamental question is whether there are limits on what and how we can think. If the main limit is the brain's processing speed and memory, can't that be augmented, and with AGI even more so? "Will we not be AGI? Are we not already AGI?" He calls this "fun speculation" and says he focuses on the near future. **Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md.

He answers with a framework of "five fundamental human capabilities": computation (mental), transformation (physical), and three that concern navigating "idea space," that is, how knowledge is created: variation, selection and attention. His conclusion: "AGI does not seem like it can surpass us in any way unless it bends what is possible," in which case we would face a very different problem. **Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md.

He develops the first two capabilities in detail. On computation: is there any limit to what we can compute? No: "once you have a universal computer that we can hold in our hands, it's just a matter of time and memory to compute anything. We have that." An AGI, or aliens, with the same capacity would have the same repertoire and "no advantage over us." AGI may compute faster, but faster computation does not speed up the physical transformation that builds things: you can have an idea for a particle collider and still need the resources to build it. **Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md.

On transformation: "Transformation is creation. We turn raw materials into rockets given the right knowledge." Human hands and bodies are especially good at creating anything, given a sequence of operations; we have built spaceships and telescopes: "we can build the thing that builds the thing." "We are generalists that build tools to thrive in any environment; we are not animals bound to one niche." Is there a limit to what basic operations can achieve when strung together? No: "if humans could teleoperate a gorilla, there is a sequence of steps it can take to build a rocket given time" ("imagine if Elon Musk were operating the gorilla"). But transformations take time, and "a singularity isn't going to change that," just as neither the Big Bang nor the Enlightenment created rocket ships, only their prerequisites. "Time is a compression algorithm that prevents everything from happening at once." AGI may compute faster but will not necessarily create faster; it would still need to acquire resources. **Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md.

| Capability | Domain | Koe's argument |
|---|---|---|
| Computation | Mental | With a universal computer, anything is computable given time and memory; an AGI has the same repertoire |
| Transformation | Physical | Humans "build the thing that builds the thing"; transformation takes time and resources that faster computation does not remove |
| Variation | Idea space | Part of how knowledge is created |
| Selection | Idea space | Part of how knowledge is created |
| Attention | Idea space | Part of how knowledge is created |

The units of this chapter reconstruct the first two capabilities in detail; the last three are only named here as the ones that concern navigating idea space, and the corpus develops them elsewhere in connection with how knowledge is created. Koe presents the framework as his own, but the material suggests an uncredited source. **Complementary context:** the vocabulary of "universal computer," of humans as generalists who can build anything physics allows, and of knowledge growing through variation and selection closely resembles the ideas of the physicist David Deutsch (*The Beginning of Infinity*), who builds on Karl Popper's account of knowledge as conjecture and criticism. The corpus does not name Deutsch, so the attribution cannot be confirmed.

The three arguments about AI's limits moved over time. In December 2024 AGI was something future and different; in August 2025 Koe mentioned, without endorsing them, the circulating claims about "millions of ASIs"; in December 2025 he asked whether we are not already AGI; and in January 2026 he admitted that AGI "may provide a basic foundation for survival." What stays constant is the practical conclusion: whatever AI becomes, the person who sets their own goals and builds toward them has less to fear than the person who floats.

#### Doers and directors

The practical form of the argument is a distinction Koe coined in August 2025. "Are you a doer or are you a director?" The question matters, he says, whether or not AI takes over. The distinction lines up with others he has used: doers versus directors, employees versus entrepreneurs, low agency versus high agency, "those who assign work rather than being assigned work." Directors assign work either to themselves (self-direction, directing their own career) or to an AI prompt or automation tool, which lets them do more and pursue personal goals that bring meaning and fulfillment. "You need to become a director... an orchestrator... high agency." **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

The definition covers two cases that might seem different. Directing yourself (choosing your goals and assigning yourself the work) and directing an AI (assigning work to prompts and automations) are the same act at different scales. Both consist of deciding what should be done and delegating its execution, whether to your own future behavior or to a machine. This is why Koe can say that the skill that protects you from AI and the skill that lets you use AI are one skill.

In the same video he explains the first decision of an "AI orchestrator": "The first and foundational decision to make when adopting AI lies in when to leverage it and when to do it by hand. Usually, that decision lies in what you enjoy doing." For him that is writing: books, newsletters, social posts. He still has AI prompts complementing his process, but they are not a writing process; they are "a process that refines ideas to the point of being able to put out something great." **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md. Later in the video he gives the lesson its full form: "specialize in your craft and orchestrate a fleet of tasteful prompts that align with your values and vision." For those who have not started, "the best way to learn AI is to try automating yourself out of a job," and not only your job but the things you do in general. Yes, this increases the risk that your current job is replaced, but "if you can replace the job, then someone else can"; at least you upskill, "abstract out a layer," and have more opportunities (a new job or something different) because you learned a new skill. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

The criterion "what you enjoy" is not arbitrary. It follows from Koe's view that meaning comes from engaged struggle with something you care about (Section 36.6): the work you enjoy is the work in which you find meaning, so handing it over would remove the point of doing it. The work you do not enjoy is the work that can be delegated without loss.

To explain the relationship between the director and the AI, Koe uses the image of the ghostwriter. In February 2025: think of AI as a ghostwriter, ghost designer, ghost coder or ghost filmmaker. "A ghost writer doesn't create the entire story they just write it." James Patterson uses ghostwriters for his novels ("a shock to most people"), but he still orchestrates the plot, characters, lore, marketing, sales and business; the ghostwriter strings it together in writing. AI will do exactly that for all of those functions, letting a Patterson do it all by himself if he wants. **Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md. In December 2024 he had told the same story with more detail about what the master keeps. Patterson, "a pretty famous author," "still likes to create the plot, he's the storyteller, he likes to craft the narrative, he still creates the tone and the style and makes sure the final output is up to his mind's quality." He also has "a big picture awareness of the sales, the distribution, the community" that made the book a success; it is not a matter of putting a book on Amazon and letting it sit. "A ghostwriter or AI in this context removes the amount of time and labor it requires to produce said writing and the auxiliary tasks." "The master still has to be a generalist they still have to be an entrepreneur they still need the vision story and value." **Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md.

The Patterson example clarifies what is delegated and what is kept. What is delegated is the stringing together: turning decisions into finished sentences. What is kept is everything that makes the result worth reading and buying: plot, narrative, tone, quality control, and awareness of distribution and community. The word "master" here echoes "a tool that needs a master": the master is a generalist and an entrepreneur because directing requires understanding the whole.

Does delegating to AI make a creator inauthentic? Koe's answer, in May 2025, is a coined phrase. Having AI handle marketing, sales, support and design "is not inauthentic. That's authenticity at scale. It's the same thing as hiring employees to do it for you"; either way you are not doing that work yourself, and it is necessary to achieve your mission. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md. "Authenticity at scale" depends on what is being delegated. The authentic part (the craft, the ideas, the voice) stays with the person; the scaled part is the machinery that carries it to more people.

In January 2026 he drew the boundary more precisely: "AI is great for utility based tasks but not so much for meaning based tasks," unless you find a way to work it into your workflow meaningfully (he promised a future video on the future of writing, applicable to almost any skill). His example: help-center articles produced with AI were utility writing, unlike his newsletters, from which he derives a great deal of meaning and where he would have to be far more critical of AI's output. **Source:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md. The distinction between utility and meaning at the level of tasks anticipates the distinction between utility and meaning at the level of the economy in Section 36.6.

#### Tools get replaced, vision and agency don't

The fear that AI will drown human creators in machine-made content is, for Koe, a failure of clear thinking. In December 2025 he took the fear head-on: "AI will create so much content that human creators don't stand a chance" exists because people "can't think clearly." AI is a tool, and tools need someone to use them for a purpose. Yes, anyone can have AI generate a thousand viral posts from a podcast and rank or simulate them, "but what good is that?" Have you thought about it, or did you just hear it and stop thinking? You may get likes and followers, but what about monetization, loyalty and everything that makes a brand work in the long term? You can ask AI to help you understand those things, "but now you're doing something completely different. You're not asking AI to do it for you. You're learning now. You're orchestrating the realization of a larger vision," which is not that different from doing it yourself, except that you get information from a faster source. "You are still the decision maker." **Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md.

He follows the argument with three cases. Building a business: you need a brand mission that AI helps execute, and you must iterate constantly. Writing a book: you must control all the minor details and still get people to read it (audience, marketing and sales, "which the book is not going to do itself"). Creating art: you still need an idea to bring into reality. "Nothing has changed. People just hate what's new. And that new is shining a light on what mattered in the first place. If you can't create art with AI, then you were never an artist to begin with. You were simply good at using a tool like Photoshop, and tools get replaced. Vision and agency do not." **Source:** The Most Important Skill To Learn Right Now.md.

Koe had used a version of the same sentence four months earlier, with a different emphasis. In August 2025 he said that photographers, video editors and others in creative jobs are often "just executing project instructions that you didn't set": the doer, not the director. "That's not art... This is still industrial work disguised as creative work. Just because you have the job title of an artist doesn't mean that you are one." What they fail to see is that "a word is the new brush stroke, the new lasso filter, and the new select and mask" (the transcript reads "lowass filter"); every minor decision that imbues the work with a personal touch is still available, and they could create beyond their dreams, faster. "The single distinction that will separate art from slop is taste." Nothing changed, he says; there is just a new tool, as there was with CGI. "If you can't create art with AI, then you weren't an artist to begin with. You were simply good at using a tool and tools get replaced." **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

The two versions are layers, not duplicates. The August 2025 version is about the employed artist and about taste: the work of executing someone else's instructions was never art, and what separates art from slop is the judgment applied to each choice. The December 2025 version is about vision and agency: the idea to be realized and the will to realize it are what persist when tools change. Together they say that a creative person has three things a tool lacks (an idea worth realizing, the judgment to recognize when it has been realized well, and the agency to see it through) and that the tool itself, whether Photoshop or a prompt, is interchangeable.

"Industrial work disguised as creative work" is a sharp phrase because it applies the distinction between technicians and creators (Section 36.3) to occupations that are commonly considered creative. A job title does not determine whether the work is creative; what determines it is who sets the goal. This also explains why Koe can be optimistic about AI and pessimistic about many creative jobs at the same time. AI threatens the execution of instructions, not the giving of them.

### 36.5 Using AI Well

Section 36.4 established the principle: AI is a tool that needs a master. This section gathers what Koe teaches about being that master in practice. Most of the material comes from four videos: "The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First)" (May 2025), "You Have About 36 Months To Make It" (August 2025), "How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People" (November 2025) and "How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI" (March 2026). His practical writing workflows with AI belong to Section 24.6; here the focus is on the general principles and on his positions in the public debate about AI.

#### The output depends on your skill

The foundational claim is stated in May 2025: "The output of AI is up to your skill and imagination. It's not that AI isn't as good as you. it's that you're not good enough at AI to make AI better than you." It is not just a matter of typing questions into ChatGPT. Given extremely specific instructions, long or short, AI performs quite well. Asking "can you help me write a persuasive landing page?" without guidance gives poor results. Good results require four things: knowing how to find the best instructions (there are many ways to write a landing page, and you do not know which one the AI will pick); a "voice analysis" of how you write, so that it writes like you; your offer information; and an understanding of how to create an offer, so that you can put all the pieces together and the output is what you would have created yourself. "So it's still you creating the thing," with much less work. His example is a post on his Substack about creating a landing page for a digital product and writing its copy, including instructions for building a copy prompt. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

The sentence "you're not good enough at AI to make AI better than you" inverts the usual complaint. The complaint assumes that the AI's quality is fixed and the user merely receives it. Koe assumes that the quality is a function of what the user supplies: instructions, voice, information and understanding of the domain. Three of the four requirements are knowledge of the craft, not knowledge of AI.

In August 2026 he illustrated the same point with a planned meme: a bell curve of AI usage. Many people, he observes, "swapped their obsession for collecting Notion templates to an obsession of collecting Claude skills." At the low end of the curve, the simpleton says "you just tell it what to do." In the middle are "all the crazy skills and stuff." At the top, the master says, again, "just tell it what to do." "But the thing there is that in order to be this guy, you need to know what to do, which most people don't." **Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md. The meme format (the idiot and the sage agree, the midwit overcomplicates) makes the point that the master's simplicity is earned. The two ends of the curve say the same words but mean different things: the simpleton does not know what to tell it, and the master does.

The deeper reason was given in December 2024. "Can't you just ask ChatGPT for the ideas, the vision, the story, the value? You can, but unless you want to be at the whim of how AI writes by default and the baked-in bias, you will still be doing what most other people are doing, you will still lack agency, you will still get lumped in with the homogeneous output of the biased model, and as markets go you will be a commodity." You cannot sustain meaningful results "because you don't know the inputs of the AI that leads to the outputs." "Everyone's saying prompt engineering is the greatest next thing to learn, but in order to be a prompt engineer you're still learning the exact same thing that allows you to write the prompts": to write good prompts for writing, "you need to understand writing," not necessarily do it yourself, but understand the big picture of the skill. Asking AI for that understanding puts you "at the whim again of the bias of the AI; it's not unique." **Source:** A Daily Routine To Work Less, Earn More & Enjoy Life.md.

"Homogeneous output" is Koe's term for what a model produces by default: the average of its training, biased in the same direction for everyone who uses it the same way. A person who relies on it ends up with the same output as everyone else and becomes, in market terms, a commodity. The argument about prompt engineering closes an apparent escape route. If prompting were a separate skill, one could learn it without learning anything else; Koe's point is that good prompting is just the craft expressed as instructions.

His own experience provides a counterpoint and a confirmation. In March 2026, explaining how to train AI on the frameworks of great marketers, he admitted: "This section of the video actually makes me kind of mad because I spent like 5 years learning this stuff, doing it all manually, and now that AI is a thing, oh it's so much easier... you get to shortcut all of the process. But I don't take that for granted because I still have all of the knowledge and pattern recognition that would allow me to do it better than another person. So you're still building that along the way." "In today's world you can have AI do most of this for you if you know how to train the AI to use frameworks and knowledge from great marketers, salesmen and copywriters." The method he describes is to take an expert's knowledge or a successful post, have AI break it down, "turn it into a guide," and then turn the guide "into a prompt." "Since I understand all of the moving pieces, these prompts are very comprehensive." **Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md. AI shortens the path Koe took, but it does not remove the need to walk some version of it: the person who uses the shortcut still builds pattern recognition along the way, and the quality of their prompts depends on how much of the territory they understand.

#### The slot machine and the digital employee

Koe's November 2025 video begins with a metaphor: "most people treat AI as a slot machine rather than something you can program to do exactly what you want it to." AI is "a cool new way to ask questions and get answers. It's the new Google search, so to say. But most people stop there. They don't see the power under the hood." Despite the hype, if you asked the average person whether AI had changed their life, he argues, most would say no. The slot machine is the pattern of typing a short request, hoping for a good output and re-rolling. By "AI" he means only large language models in a chat box (ChatGPT or Claude), text only; nothing special is required. **Source:** How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md.

A slot machine rewards intermittently and unpredictably, and it keeps people pulling the lever. Koe argues that this is partly by design. "You can't rely on how the AI is programmed by default because... ChatGPT, Claude, it's packaged up. It's tuned. It's given a personality for the average individual. I hope that you're not an average individual... because that's what consumer products do. They dumb it down so that it can be useful and sycophantic and make you feel good for using it and give you your cheap little dopamine hits so you keep coming back to the slot machine." **Source:** How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md. The connection to Chapter 11 is direct: the default AI is a consumer product with the same incentive structure as the feeds that capture attention. In April 2026 Koe used the phrase again in a broader diagnosis: people stuck at their computers hoping that "pulling the AI slot machine" or doing assigned repetitive tasks will lead to something good (Section 36.6).

The alternative image is the "digital employee." "This is how you go from AI slop to imposing your own sense of taste on the AI. You need to think of AI as this sort of digital employee that will do exactly what you tell it to do. Meaning, if you don't know how to do the thing... well, or you don't know how to guide the AI to finding how to do it well, then it probably won't do well," you will be disappointed and "resort back to the slot machine style guessing game." "If you don't tell it exactly what you want, the LLM has to guess what you want. And in order to do that, it pulls from this onslaught of mediocre methods that are all over the internet," producing something slightly good "but not good enough to get outsized results because anyone can do that." **Source:** How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md.

| | Slot machine | Digital employee |
|---|---|---|
| Typical input | A short request | Detailed instructions, often 500 to 2,000 words |
| What the AI does | Guesses, drawing on mediocre methods from the internet | Acts on your instructions and learns as you correct it |
| Who supplies the method | The model's defaults | You |
| Typical result | Slop: slightly good, available to anyone | Output that carries your taste |
| Your relationship to it | Re-rolling and hoping | Teaching and refining |

His example shows what guessing looks like. Type "generate a viral YouTube script on productivity" into ChatGPT and you get something "okay," but nowhere near what a channel with a million subscribers would publish. He critiques the output line by line. "Length 4 to 6 minutes": following it blindly does not produce views. "Tone, fast, energetic, highly sharable. What if that's not your personality?" "Cold open... What if you don't want to add custom B-roll? ... just talk to the camera? ... use your phone? What if this isn't even a topic that you're an expert in?" Ali Abdaal, Alex Hormozi and other successful creators "don't have the exact same videos. Over time, they have cultivated and created their own frameworks and methods," along with their ideas, speaking style, personality, quirks, brand and presentation. "Is there any one best way to coming up with a YouTube script? No." Asking for a viral script "is not a long-term strategy. And you're not learning anything. You're just reciting what this box told you to do." Even if Hormozi and Abdaal used ChatGPT, they would tell it exactly how to match their style, and they would still redo it "over and over again until it gets close enough to being useful." **Source:** How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md.

The prescription follows: "To get AI to do something well in a high-quality way, you need to teach the AI exactly how you would create the YouTube video. At that point, it's not randomly generated slop. It's an employee that's acting on your instruction and learning as you refine the process by correcting mistakes." "You're going to be writing 500 to 2,000 word prompts. Not one sentence, not one paragraph like you see all over the internet" (he grants that some of the "steal this prompt" offerings "can be helpful"). "The shorter the prompt, the more guessing the AI has to do. The more of your agency you outsource to the agent, and the more the output increases on the slop spectrum." The remaining problem is the person who does not yet know how to do the task, or has not made hundreds of videos and so does not have a method; Koe addresses that in the same video with four options for creating instructions, which belong to the material on writing and learning with AI (Sections 16.4 and 24.6). **Source:** How To Use AI Better Than 99% Of People (This Changed My Life).md.

The "slop spectrum" turns slop from a category into a scale. Every output sits somewhere between generic and distinctive, and its position is determined by how much of the decision-making the user has kept. The sentence "the more of your agency you outsource to the agent" connects the quality of AI output to the central concept of Chapter 35. Prompt length is a proxy for the amount of agency retained.

This prescription seems to contradict something Koe said six months earlier. In May 2025, describing AI-native companies, he celebrated that with AI "you do it with a single sentence rather than hours of work" (see "Becoming AI-first" below). In November 2025 he said that good results require prompts of 500 to 2,000 words, "not one sentence." The contexts differ. The single sentence referred to operational tasks in an AI-native company (digesting comments, turning a support thread into documentation); the long prompts refer to high-quality creative output that must not be generic. Koe does not reconcile the two statements himself, but his own distinction between utility-based and meaning-based tasks (Section 36.4) does: utility tasks can be delegated in a sentence; tasks that must carry your taste cannot.

#### The dead internet iceberg

What happens to all the slop? Koe answered with a metaphor in February 2025, after reporting an experiment of his own. "AI does not generate good content on its own." He had tried to get AI to replicate his writing with simple prompts and with prompts "the size of a mini course." Out of 100 posts, maybe one could be published; the rest were useful ideas that he completely rewrote, some needing only minor edits. The crucial point: "AI content isn't good unless you are good at content." Most AI content sinks to the bottom and is never seen by human eyes. "The dead internet is like an iceberg": the majority of dead content is so deep that nobody will ever see it. **Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md.

"Dead internet" is a phrase from online culture for the theory that much of the internet is now bots and machine-generated content. Koe accepts the premise and changes its implication with the iceberg image. Most of the iceberg is underwater; what people actually see is the small part above the surface, selected by algorithms and human attention. Machine-generated content mostly accumulates below the waterline.

In the same video he went further and argued that AI-generated content could even be a good thing, a claim he notes nobody else was making. It does not matter if the internet is flooded with garbage: it has been for years. If AI content hurt social media companies (if users disliked it and revenue fell), the companies could solve the problem with cryptography or biometric passes that prove you are human; they have not, so it is not enough of a problem. Complaining about the dead internet distracts from what you can do. The worry that bots have removed your ability to find information is false; most people just want things fed to them. And you can only view so much content and follow so many people at any time. **Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md. The argument is partly economic (platforms will fix what hurts revenue) and partly about attention (the amount anyone can consume is limited, so the total quantity of content matters less than what reaches the surface). Its practical conclusion is the same as in Section 36.4: the abundance of bad content raises the relative value of good content.

#### Against the anti-AI identity

Koe's most combative material concerns what he calls the "anti-AI crowd." He addressed it at length in August 2025, and the argument has several steps.

The first is a prediction. He thinks the anti-AI side is the losing side and advises not to identify as anti-AI. As technology develops, there will be an uproar of emotional people attached to past tools who discredit those who integrate AI, especially in creative work, because of pent-up feelings about what creativity and art are; those feelings block them from seeing AI's role. Most people think AI generations are random (tell AI to make a post and pass it off as art): "you're only seeing the surface." "People hate what they don't understand." His advice is to ignore them and not become one, because a pessimistic mindset over the coming years creates a destructive confirmation bias. Reasonable critics would respond calmly and look for a solution, "but that's not how YouTube comments go." "You become what you focus on. And if you're trying to stop an avalanche from the bottom of a mountain, you're going to get crushed." **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

The second step places AI in the history of creative tools. Highly emotional reactions happen in every technological shift. Photography and video are very recent; CGI and Photoshop are now considered art forms in some way, and none of them could have been created before. A hyperrealistic CGI scene in a few clicks was once impossible. These tools require skill, but they are easier than earlier software and keep getting easier. "Just because a few clicks on your screen are being replaced with natural language doesn't mean it's any easier to create something we see as art," especially since what counts as art changes with rapidly shifting culture. "We don't consider people artists for what specific tool they use. We consider people artists for why and how they use the tool": they are judged on the final product, the story, the emotion, the vision and the soul, not on hours of labor. Spending ten years or ten seconds writing a book does not make it good, or art. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

The third step describes how the anti-AI position thinks. Its holders lump everyone who uses AI into one category and even cut people off ("my friend in high school uses ChatGPT. He's no longer my friend"). Koe argues that they have let "a little chat box" control their minds, boxing themselves into the ideology "AI is bad": "that's how you become dumb... limit your ability to think and see nuance." They see AI only as a chat box (write my newsletter, make this post, make this image); some outputs look appealing but are not necessarily art, useful or able to gain traction. And if people flood the internet with such outputs, "you have to understand what market sophistication is. Eventually, those things get boring," stop working, and the pendulum swings back. The doomsday never arrives. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md. "Market sophistication" is Eugene Schwartz's concept (Section 32.3): as a market is exposed to the same claims, the claims stop working. Koe applies it to AI content: generic AI output is a claim the market will quickly tire of, so its flood corrects itself.

The fourth step is a diagnosis of identity. "Most people have put too much emphasis on the labor that goes into creating something visually appealing so that it has become their identity," and they fight to defend it. "I personally do not believe that art should be tied to a series of buttons that people press, but instead why they press that series of buttons." He adds a caveat that limits his own position: it is not smart to outsource all control, decision-making and work to AI, which is "a surefire way to become stupid," but that is what stupid people do and what stupid people focus on. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md. The diagnosis applies the theory of identity from Part II: a person who identifies with a way of working defends it as they would defend their body, and the threat to the method is felt as a threat to the self.

In June 2026 Koe gave the anti-AI identity a sharper description. If you listen to loud people on social media (or, he admits, to YouTube titles like his own), it feels as if all jobs will be gone in five seconds. You may adopt "the anti-AI ideology as your new identity," screaming that you hate AI so that you feel you are making a difference "without actually changing your behavior or broadening your skill set or adapting." "AI isn't the threat that you think it is. The real threat is what it's always been. You are dependent on everyone but yourself for your survival and well-being." Employers and governments have their own survival to protect; when threatened, "they drop to a lower level of thinking" and usually fire you, which is human nature, and blindly trusting them to care for you leads to disappointment. AI is too big to control by complaining about it; posting will not stop job losses (he explicitly does not argue about whether they will happen) or stop the skills required for success from changing. "You can't just have the same skill for your entire life"; you must evolve and adapt, otherwise "you don't have any room to cry about it." He prefaces all of this by saying that he is speaking to a specific individual, not to people in circumstances with less control. **Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md.

This passage relocates the threat. The danger is not the technology but the dependence that the technology exposes: on employers, on governments, on a single skill. It is the same diagnosis as the "nobody is coming to save you" of Section 35.2, now applied to AI. The anti-AI identity is a way of feeling active while remaining dependent, since it changes what you say without changing what you do.

Koe's own position is stated in two short passages. In March 2024, resolving the worry he had expressed at the start of "The Future-Proof Skill Stack": "Technology only continues to advance; being scared of it is a great way to ruin your future. The wise decision is to use it, dissect it and understand it; allow it to help you acquire skills faster so that you can take control of your future." **Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md. In February 2025, clarifying the stance of his videos "the death of the content creator" and "the death of the personal brand," and noting that "we're putting it inside of Cortex" (his software): "I'm not anti-AI. I'm anti- Quick Fix, I'm anti- Outsourcing your thinking and just becoming a drooling blob of Flesh. I'm anti- being used by machines. I'm Pro using machines." **Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md.

The distinction between using machines and being used by them is the doer/director distinction in another form. It also bears on a question on which Koe's position changed: does AI make people stupid? In May 2025 he said he held the opinion that AI does not make people stupider or take away their creativity. In August 2025 he said that handing AI all control and decisions is "a surefire way to become stupid." Later he warned against the language of "becoming dumber with AI" while urging people to use it "so that you don't become as dumb as a rock," and by April 2026 he said that AI can exhaust creativity. The movement is from rejecting the thesis to accepting it conditionally: the tool does not make anyone stupid by itself, but passive use of it does. He does not explain the change, but it fits the "anti-being used by machines" formula he had used in early 2025.

#### Becoming AI-first

Koe's positive program begins with a typology. In May 2025 he said that the first solution to the disruption is to become "AI-first," because there are three types of people who use AI: those who tried ChatGPT once a few years ago and thought it was not special; those who use AI tools for searches, summaries and simple tasks that would take perhaps ten more seconds without AI; and those who have used it enough "to see the light" and use it everywhere they can. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

An obvious objection follows: many people say AI does not give good responses even with detailed instructions. Koe grants it: "it's not there yet. But it's going to be." Why become AI-first if it is not there? "That's the exact point": if you understand AI, by the time it gets there "you will run laps around anyone else who didn't learn it." His biggest piece of advice for learning it is to try to automate yourself out of work. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md. The argument is about timing. Learning a tool before it is fully capable means that its future capabilities arrive in the hands of someone who already knows how to use it.

He then reads an unattributed quote that defines what being AI-native means for a company: it is not about building features for users; "it's an operating model for how to run your company. It's how you work, how you think, how you and your company breathe," a rearchitecture and a rewiring, "a philosophical shift from how do we scale humans to how do we scale decisions, creativity, and action with machines." **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

To make the shift concrete, Koe uses a comparison written by "Signal," an anonymous internet writer, in a paid post arguing that most companies are already behind because they are not AI-first. Signal covered product, marketing and support (engineering and design are mentioned but not developed), and gave example prompts. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

| Function | Not AI-native | AI-native | Example prompts (Signal) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product | Slow feedback loops, manual research, roadmap debates based on opinion | Summarize all user interviews in minutes, generate roadmap options from clusters of feature requests, simulate user behavior before launch | Summarize the last 50 user interviews and cluster pain points by frequency and intensity; suggest three product bets with the highest signal-to-noise; write a product spec for a new onboarding flow, with edge cases and counterarguments |
| Marketing | Manual content, guessing what resonates, slow campaigns | Generate and test content at massive scale, tune messages to psychographics and not just demographics, live feedback loops with creative optimization | Ten headline variations tuned to five founder archetypes, ranked by projected engagement; a launch email in brand tone for early adopters who bounced from the pricing page |
| Support | Human triage; representatives answering the same questions repeatedly | Auto-solve tier-one issues with LLMs available 24/7, summarize complex threads for human escalation, generate help-center content dynamically from ticket volume | (Described by function rather than by prompt) |

Koe adapts the comparison to his own two situations. As a creator, he can ask AI to "digest these 50 comments from my latest post" and extract pain points, interests and questions that steer his content, products and business. For his software company, Cortex, a support thread can be turned into a documentation article that a 24/7 LLM can then feed to other users. "You do it with a single sentence rather than hours of work." **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md. The comparison is Signal's; the adaptations, and the decision to read it to an audience of individual creators, are Koe's. The adaptation matters because it shows that the AI-native operating model is not only for companies: a one-person business can treat its comment section as user research and its support conversations as documentation.

The principle beneath AI-first is a claim about how humans adapt to technology. "We abstract up a layer when skills inevitably become less valuable through automation." During technological transitions, humans adapt by developing higher-level skills and knowledge. With AI it is happening again: we must abstract beyond most of the skills we have learned and "transition from labor to mind." Since AI can now think and execute about as well as we can, "we must think about how we think in relation to systems that could do the thinking for us." The guiding question to keep in mind: "When should I leverage AI and when should I do it myself?" **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

"Abstract up a layer" is Koe's phrase. It describes a movement that Section 36.3 showed from the other side: if replicable work at one level is automated, the human moves to the level above it, where the work is to decide, design and judge. The guiding question is the same as the "foundational decision" of the AI orchestrator in Section 36.4, which shows that Koe's 2025 videos were developing a single framework.

#### Automating yourself out of work

The practice Koe recommends most consistently is to automate yourself out of work, and in May 2025 he gave it as a procedure for any task:

1. Write down the entire process in detail, as if teaching someone your job, including your thought processes and creative processes.
2. Assume that any task, or any piece of the process, can be done with a prompt.
3. Try to turn it into a prompt.
4. Test it and note where it does not do well.
5. Refine it until it is at least 90% of the way there.
6. Store the prompt somewhere safe.

If you do not know how to do the task yourself, you can ask AI for detailed instructions, but this depends on the task. For content or YouTube videos you will often get general advice, because "every creator has their own way"; content is "a highly relative domain" where AI will not thrive unless people give out their specific processes. Over time, the procedure builds "this library of prompts that does most of your workflow for you." **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

The first step is the decisive one, and it is not about AI at all. Writing down a process as if teaching it forces you to make your tacit knowledge explicit, which is the same move as the "full life cycle of knowledge" in Section 36.2: teaching reveals what you know and what you do not. The prompt is a byproduct of that articulation. The limit Koe names (highly relative domains) is consistent with the rest of his argument. Where there is no single correct method, the AI's default is an average of many methods, and only your own documented process can make it specific.

A warning accompanies the practice. In March 2026, walking his audience through a series of prompts, Koe said: "One thing I would recommend during this section is just make sure you actually open the outputs of these... edit it and look through it just to make sure everything is good. Make sure it didn't hallucinate, because you're still having to baby the AI here." **Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md. The delegation is never complete. Even a refined prompt produces output that someone with judgment has to check.

Automation also has limits that Koe sets deliberately, not technically. In May 2025 he gave two rules for a one-person business: first, use AI to do the things you do not want to do; second, do not give AI complete control over the things you deeply care about, which means not letting it do that work for you. His own craft is writing, not in the sense of grammar or poetic style, but of "putting ideas on paper in the way that I want them to... be articulated." When AI takes over the whole process, "it doesn't feel mine": he has not created or given something he cares about, and "that's where the meaning comes into play." For everything else a business needs (marketing, sales, support, design), AI can do the work so that his writing is seen by more people and sustains his life. **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md. This is the context of the "authenticity at scale" claim in Section 36.4: the craft stays human; its amplification can be automated.

Koe's position on AI in writing evolved further after this, from refusing to let AI write for him to accepting it as a drafting tool "as long as they're in control of the ideas"; that evolution is treated in Section 24.6. The two rules remain the stable core: what you care about stays in your hands, and what you would rather not do can go to the machine.

#### No shortcut: agents and the instant-money illusion

By 2026 the public conversation had moved from chatbots to agents, and Koe applied his principles to the new tools. In March 2026 he described people "that don't want to learn" who download "something like OpenClaw and buy a Mac Mini and start running all of these agents," and who, "two, three months from then, they have nothing to show for it." "They just like looking at an agent doing things, and they don't understand what quality is. They don't understand [that] when something doesn't work, you change it. They don't understand that spamming every single platform" does not mean growth or sales. He mentions that some of his business friends, "some very high up in the business world," tried this too, and he does not say that agents are bad; his own software, Eden, was adding a similar agent available through Telegram. But "most people just get a dopamine hit from it": they follow a curve that starts with "this is so awesome, I'm going to have it do everything," waste "200, 300, 1,000 dollars on AI credits, and then it collapses, and then they have nothing because they don't know what good looks like. They don't have the skill or the knowledge to do the thing in the first place. So how are they going to be the manager or the CEO of the agents?" **Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md.

The question at the end is the doer/director distinction at the level of agents. An agent is a more autonomous tool, but it still needs a master, and a master who "doesn't know what good looks like" cannot manage it. The pattern Koe describes (excitement, spending, collapse) is the slot machine stretched over months.

Later in the same video he generalized: "AI, frankly, still, and probably for the foreseeable future, isn't that good yet. It just can't do these things for you. It's missing something, and we don't know what that something is. It's definitely not more intelligence." And even if people could open OpenClaw "or another agent thing and make hundreds of thousands of dollars the next day," which is "probably going to be impossible... because that's not how the market works," it would be "just like a commodity. If anyone can do it immediately, then it's not going to work. If it works for a small period of time, then that's called an exploit, and it's going to be squashed really fast." "You will still have to learn. You will still have to practice. And most importantly, you will still have to iterate when something doesn't work until it does work." "The learning in this video actually happens by doing the prompts and implementing them." **Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md.

The argument is a market argument, and it applies beyond AI. A method that produces money for anyone, immediately, without skill, cannot last, because its availability to everyone destroys the advantage it confers. Either it is a commodity (and pays nothing) or it is an exploit (and closes quickly). Durable income requires something that is not available to everyone at once, which, in Koe's account, is skill built through practice and iteration.

These 2026 statements mark a shift of emphasis. In 2025 Koe stressed AI's power: learn it before it is "there," and soon people "will be able to operate at the level of large teams." In 2026 he stressed that without skill, taste and iteration, that power does not differentiate anyone; in May 2026 he put it as "when everyone has superpowers, nobody has superpowers." The two emphases are compatible: AI multiplies the person who knows what good looks like and does nothing for the person who does not.

#### Vibe coding

"Vibe coding" is a common term for building software by describing it to an AI rather than writing the code by hand. Koe uses it in its common sense but objects to its connotation. In June 2026 he said that he dislikes the term because it makes people think they are doing something cheap. "In reality, the people that are vibe coding that also have the knowledge of marketing and sales are doing a lot better than the programmers who don't have the marketing and sales knowledge because [they] are able to create a much better product." "Is it perfectly written code? No. Do I think that's a requirement to a good product? Probably not, especially in the future." Others, he notes, think building a tool with AI is too easy or cheap, "a tier lower than info products because info products actually require you to have knowledge." **Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md.

In the same video he gave a beginner's method. "Oh, well, how do I start? Just ask Claude. How do I start building an app? Literally just say that. 'Hey, I've heard about this vibe coding thing. I've heard that you can help with this. How do I do it? Give me a plan.'" It breaks the project down; you ask how to start step one, do it, then step two, and so on. "It's not like you're just having Claude do everything because you have to guide it. You still have to understand what's going on." You follow the steps until you have a version one. "Frankly, the version one is going to suck." From there it is up to you: you can say "this is a vibe coded app so I could only get this far," or you can keep iterating, asking the next question or solving the next problem when it comes up. **Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md.

The method has the shape of Koe's general theory of learning (build to learn, Chapter 14): start with a plan you do not fully understand, execute it step by step, accept a bad first version, and let the problems that arise tell you what to learn next. AI changes the speed of each step, not the structure.

Koe also deflates the opposite fear: that if anyone can vibe-code anything, software businesses are finished. In January 2026 he argued that "opinionated creative work" still matters. Would you trust a vibe-coded alternative to Dropbox that is not maintained by a team able to handle billions of users? He says he did not understand the software space until he began building a Dropbox alternative with his own team. People talk as if users care a great deal about the money they spend on useful solutions and want to build them themselves: "you don't even spend 20 minutes cooking your own food every day for health and nutrition. You pay 20 to 30 bucks for Uber Eats." "Humans just want convenience on so many different levels," and people are "blowing this way out of proportion." "Not everyone in the world just wants to be inside of Claude code building whatever suits their needs that day." **Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md.

The final piece is a piece of data that Koe presented in June 2026 as a graph, whose source he does not state and which the transcript does not allow to be reproduced. With agentic AI there have been more app releases than ever, but app reviews and apps with significant usage are going down. Koe grants that you can build an app now: you ask Claude "I have this idea, how do I start?"; the first iteration will not one-shot Notion or Salesforce; you improve, iterate, fail, get users, things break, you do not quit, you ask it to fix things, and eventually you have something very good even if it was built only with AI, "and if it's valuable, it's valuable," vibe-coded or not. But apps go up while usage stays flat because people lack the ingredients: no taste (they do not know what to build), no persistence, no iteration on feedback (low usage is obvious feedback), and no persuasion or distribution (they cannot get the app into people's hands or make them care enough to use it and pay). "Agency is only so much." **Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md.

The phrase "Agency is only so much" is a notable qualification from an author who usually treats agency as the decisive trait (Chapter 35). Agency gets you to build the app; taste tells you what to build; persistence and iteration make it good; persuasion and distribution get it used. Elsewhere in the corpus Koe lists these as "five ingredients" (agency, taste, persuasion, persistence, iteration; Section 18.5). The graph shows what happens when only the first ingredient is present: production soars and nothing changes.

Koe's statements on vibe coding can look inconsistent. He defends the term and the people who use it; he dismisses the panic that anyone can build anything; he shows that most vibe-coded apps fail; and in July 2026 he mocked "everyone else vibe coding apps they'll never get traction on." The tension is only apparent. He defends the tool and criticizes its use without the other ingredients. What fails in the failing apps is not the code but the taste, persistence, iteration and distribution around it, which is the same conclusion every part of this section has reached about AI.

### 36.6 The Meaning Economy

The previous sections answered the question of which work AI takes and which it leaves. This section asks a different question: what will people pay for when machines can do most useful things well? Koe's answer, which he developed and renamed several times between 2024 and 2026, is meaning. The argument has three parts. First, AI threatens meaning as well as jobs, because work has been a source of purpose, identity and status. Second, the division of labor between machines and humans runs along the line between utility and meaning. Third, an economy is emerging in which people are paid for providing meaning, and Koe offers a framework for what meaning is made of.

#### "Why are you watching this?"

Koe opens his February 2026 video "The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack)" with a question to the viewer: why are you watching this particular video when you have ChatGPT, Claude and other AI tools that are "supposed to get rid of information, make creators obsolete"? You could paste the YouTube link into an AI and get a decent summary, yet you are still watching. Before going "doomer" about AI replacing everyone, he asks, reflect on that. The question "sets the foundation" and "shines a light on what the future of creative work could look like." **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md. The answer is implied rather than stated at that point, and he develops it over the rest of the video: people follow a specific human's perspective, curation and story, which a summary does not provide.

The question is a demonstration more than an argument. It uses the viewer's own behavior as evidence. If information were what people wanted, a summary would satisfy them; the fact that they keep watching a person shows that they want something else.

Koe had made the underlying claim more than two years earlier. In December 2023 he said that with automation and AI it only makes sense that people will lean toward actual people for their media and learning, out of "the innate drive to learn," not entertainment or quick dopamine. Personal brands, creators and synthesizers educate, entertain and inspire according to their interests. "People are attracted to people not robots." "Unless we rewrite the collective psyche which hasn't changed much in a few hundred years then this is inevitable." Robots, he added, have their place: decreasing labor and increasing human creativity. **Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md. The conditional clause is the important qualification. The prediction holds as long as human psychology stays as it is; Koe treats that as close to certain, but he states it as a condition.

#### AI is coming for meaning

The central thesis of the February 2026 video follows. People say AI is coming for everyone's jobs, and it looks as if that will become true. The bigger problem, Koe says, is that "AI isn't only coming for the jobs, it's coming for the meaning." There is already "this mass scarcity of meaning and purpose." If work, especially creative work, is a source of purpose, what happens when it is gone? What happens when work no longer equals purpose, identity, the way you mattered, or the way you acquired status? He includes status deliberately: work is "a generator of meaning," and "whether we like to virtue signal about not playing status games or not you play one every day." **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

Most people see a crisis in this; Koe says he sees one of the greatest opportunities. When AI can do everything well, everything becomes a commodity, not worth paying for. Hence his second foundational question: "how do you become a scarce resource that people want to pay for especially as a creative?" He answers it, in that video, with taste, perspective, story and a set of skills (the skill hierarchy treated in Section 35.5). **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

The thesis connects two lines of argument that ran separately in the corpus. One is economic: AI commoditizes what it can do, so value moves to what is scarce. The other is psychological: replicable work drains life of "zest" (Section 36.3), and the modern person suffers from a lack of meaning (Part IV). In 2026 Koe joined them. The loss of jobs is also a loss of one of the main ways in which people have found purpose, identity and status, so the scarcity created by AI is a scarcity of meaning, and meaning becomes what is worth paying for.

The thesis also changed the tone of his discussion of replacement. In 2024 he said that those who are replaced "deserve to be replaced" (Section 36.4). Framing the issue as a crisis of meaning that affects everyone who works is a broader and more sympathetic account, though Koe does not comment on the change.

#### Utility versus meaning

Which work goes to machines and which stays with humans? Koe gave a framework in August 2025. It seems, he says, that machines will solve utility so that humans can transcend to meaning. We hate long lines at the DMV, a server getting our order wrong, meetings that could have been five bullet-point emails; AI and automation solve the necessary work that humans hate. Conversely, "humans crave the potential for failure": we love the final batter in the ninth inning, we travel across the world to dine at a five-star restaurant, we cry at a wedding during the vows. If the vows had been written with ChatGPT, you would probably be able to tell, you would not cry, and the moment would not be meaningful, because AI would have been applied to a domain it does not belong in. "Machines are for speed, repetition, and necessity. Humans are for story, novelty, myth, and meaning." **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

In July 2026 he restated the framework and tied it to the formula "the mind is a story engine" from Section 36.4. "The mind is a story engine. That's what humans can't help but pay attention to, and that's what separates us from robots, because robots are useful for utility." Nobody (apart from DMV workers) would care if the DMV were automated: who looks forward to hours in line for a license photo? The same goes for fast food (the cashier gets your order wrong) and for taxis or Uber (a dirty back seat, small talk for a tip). "So let the robot solve utility. That way humans can abstract out to meaning, purpose, and narrative." "Humans love stakes": your chest tightens when your team is about to lose in the final inning. "Humans love novelty": we travel across the world to eat at a five-star restaurant. "Mastering human nature is about mastering story, storytelling, narrative," not in the sense of writing a novel, but as "the structure of reality in a sense": the principles of value creation that get people to pay attention, position what you offer as valuable, and get them to act (pay you, change their behavior, or give you what you want). **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md.

| | Machines (silicon) | Humans (carbon) |
|---|---|---|
| What they are for | Speed, repetition, necessity | Story, novelty, myth, meaning |
| Typical domain | Utility: the DMV, fast-food orders, rides, meetings that could be emails | Stakes and novelty: the ninth inning, five-star dining, wedding vows |
| What people feel about the work | They hate doing it and will not miss it | They crave it, including its potential for failure |
| What happens if the other side does it | A human doing utility work is doing machine work (Section 36.3) | AI in a domain of meaning drains it: AI-written vows produce no tears |

The phrase "the potential for failure" carries the argument. A machine's output is valued because it reliably succeeds; a human performance is valued partly because it could fail. The batter might strike out; the chef might be having a bad night; the person reading the vows might break down. Stakes require a being who can lose something. That is why, in Koe's account, AI is not merely worse at meaningful work but categorically out of place in it.

The framework is the 2025 formalization of an intuition Koe had expressed in his March 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush (Section 36.3). Many people, he said then, are stuck in low-level problems such as income and their body, but the self-improvement space and the creator economy are reaching a point where more people have solved their basic, biological problems. Eventually "automation AI Tech" (he notes that Naval has said something similar) "are going to take over a lot of the groundwork... the productive mechanical robotic-ish style work that is against human nature." Once you solve your own problems, "you open up that mental space to start solving creative problems," you survive "on that mental plane," and life becomes more rewarding and fulfilling because your mind expands, so that you do not get trapped in "petty thought bubbles or pockets of the internet." **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. "Mental plane" is Koe's term for the level at which a person whose physical survival is secure goes on surviving: as a self that must be developed and expressed (Chapter 3 discusses the law of conceptual survival). Dickie Bush's reply in the same conversation, that technology accelerates polarity, is the counterweight discussed in Section 36.3: the liberation to the mental plane is available, but not automatic.

The sequence is consistent across three years: an intuition in 2023 (people will move to the mental plane when machines take mechanical work), a framework in 2025 (silicon for utility, carbon for meaning), and a grounding in human nature in 2026 (the mind is a story engine, and stakes and novelty are what it attends to).

#### From the actualization economy to the meaning economy

Koe gave the economy that emerges from this division several names, and following the names shows how the idea developed.

The first version, in March 2024, did not yet use the word "economy." In "The Value Equation," Koe said: "There is one career that will never go out of style and that is earning in accordance with nature." Earning in accordance with nature means reversing entropy by solving an endless series of problems, turning the solutions into potential profit and distributing value, no matter the state of the economy or how many jobs AGI replaces. Even your worry about the future is a problem that others share, which you can solve for all parties and profit from "with education and meaning content and products." Meaning (philosophy, spirituality, metaphysics, purpose, entrepreneurship: what value creators do online) is a job that will persist, and education goes with it. The ideal would be one-to-one educators for everyone, which will probably never happen; so "20% of the population plus" who become educators on social media, teaching their interests and attracting those who value them, "are going to be set in the future." **Source:** The Value Equation How To Become A High-Status Individual.md. "Earning in accordance with nature" applies Koe's definition of value as the reversal of entropy (Section 31.1) to the question of a career. If nature continually produces disorder, the solving of problems will never run out, and neither will the work of people who solve them.

In July 2024 the idea acquired the name "actualization economy." "AI can help with the dissemination of tools and knowledge but it can't actually change the behavior of an individual it can't actually solve a human problem an actualization problem that's what we're going into an actualization or a purpose or a meaning economy and the majority of that is built on education." Why are problems such as productivity, fitness and mental health so prevalent and so valuable? "Because people can't enjoy life with those problems and that's at the base of anything else they can't do anything well if they don't enjoy life so what better and more profitable problem is there to solve than [one] you experience every day." Later in the same video he linked the idea to what follows once AI solves basic needs: "the growth needs Mind Body Spirit business improving actualizing that's where the money is going to go very soon and that's where it's been going," while other lines of work "are going to be replaced or automated except for the highest skilled people in those domains." He pointed viewers to look up "the purpose economy or the meaning economy or the human economy." **Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md.

**Complementary context:** "basic needs" and "growth needs" come from Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs, in which the lower needs (physiological, safety) must be met before higher ones (esteem, self-actualization) become pressing. "Purpose economy" is also the title of a 2014 book by Aaron Hurst; Koe mentions the phrase as something to look up, without citing any author.

The key claim in this version is a distinction between knowledge and behavior. AI can supply knowledge, but knowledge alone does not change behavior. Actualization problems (being productive, fit, mentally well) are problems of behavior, and so they remain human problems, solved through education in the broad sense: the kind that changes how a person lives.

The same month, in a different video, Koe sharpened the point by locating it in choice. "Can AI solve the need for challenge Health connection meaning spirituality and every other aspect of self-actualization that can't be reduced to an external solution my answer to that is potentially it can help them it can't solve them because that's dependent on your choice so the lesson here is decision-making and choice that is what you need to optimize for and work on because your livelihood depends on it." This, he said, "shines a light on the emerging meaning economy": "solve your own problems and sell the unique solution," "dive into the unknown set your mind on the vision for the future collect the knowledge and skill that allows you to make it reality and distribute your findings to others to unify Consciousness to create or be created." **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. The final phrase, "create or be created," is the title of this book. Here it names the alternative that the meaning economy makes urgent: either you choose and build, or the default choices of others shape you.

In May 2025 the idea became the "mastery and meaning economy." You have a few years, Koe said, before you are let go, or kept and eventually let go, or must upskill endlessly to take new roles, or must fend for yourself. "AI isn't just coming for companies and programmers. It's coming for everyone that has a brain." In the far future there may be no money worries, but for now there will be "an acclimation period" of job losses without safety nets such as universal basic income ("whether you agree with that or not"), which will be painful for many. What an AI-first world leaves is "a mastery and meaning economy": "discovering and pursuing your life's work... the thing you could have and probably should have been doing all along." That may eventually happen in virtual reality, on Mars or in space, but for now it happens on the internet: choose something you deeply care about; study, research and master it ruthlessly with an AI-first mindset; and shamelessly share what you know, what you do and why, in public. "The only real safety net in today's world is a body of work that's impossible to ignore." **Source:** The Future Of Work (How To Become AI-First).md.

"Body of work" is a common phrase that Koe uses with a specific weight: the accumulated, coherent output of a creator, the thing people follow and the thing that keeps paying off. Calling it "the only real safety net" replaces the institutional safety nets (employment, welfare, a credential) with a personal one, consistent with the self-reliance argument of Chapter 35.

In September 2025, in "The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation)," the contrast became explicit: "join this new and emerging meaning economy. Not the attention economy, the meaning economy." A subset of people are already doing this; find them, join them and perhaps make friends. He quoted Naval: "go do something great and your network will instantly emerge." His closing line: "the shift from attention economy to meaning economy is already taking place." **Source:** The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md. The contrast is with the economy that Chapters 11 and 25 describe: one that competes for and monetizes attention, often by draining it. The meaning economy is defined by what people receive in exchange for their attention, not by how much of it is captured.

In February 2026 Koe identified the creator economy with the meaning economy and described a change in how money flows. The old way: you are paid for labor and output. The new model: "you get paid by the people who believe in what you're doing and who want to see more of that in the world." That requires doing something you deeply care about and attracting others to care about it, which are two distinct skills: art (doing what you care about with love) and business (persuading others of its value, so that you do not become a starving artist). When you follow someone meaningful (as opposed to searching for information or a quick tip, which you can ask ChatGPT for), you are buying and supporting their perspective, opinion and curation, whether or not they use AI, because they pull the right ideas together and make sense of them. **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md. This answers the opening question of the section. You watch the video instead of reading the summary because you are paying for someone's curation and sense-making, not for the information it contains.

In April 2026, in "I'm begging you to start writing essays," he made his strongest claim: meaning is "the scarcest commodity in civilization right now." The meaning economy has been emerging for a few years, AI accelerated it, and he and many value creators form it or its beginnings. He tells a short history: "Before industrialization, we praised gods in the sky. During industrialization, we made productivity our god. Today, more is our god": more money, more information, more content. "We have more stuff and less purpose than ever." Meaning will be sold at a premium because most people feel lonely, empty and purposeless, not working toward anything greater, stuck at their computers hoping that "pulling the AI slot machine" or doing assigned repetitive tasks will lead to something good. Those best placed to provide meaning are those who shape the "epistemic commons," the shared environment of knowledge and ideas: value creators who fight the poisoning of the water supply. **Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md. The "epistemic commons" and its poisoning belong to the diagnosis of the information environment in Section 1.5; here they define the role of the meaning provider as the person who improves that environment rather than polluting it.

The successive names can be summarized:

| Date | Name | Core claim |
|---|---|---|
| March 2024 | "Earning in accordance with nature" | Solving endless problems never goes out of style; education and meaning will persist |
| July 2024 | Actualization, purpose or meaning economy | AI spreads knowledge but cannot change behavior; money goes to growth needs |
| July 2024 | Emerging meaning economy | AI can help with meaning but cannot solve it, because it depends on choice |
| May 2025 | Mastery and meaning economy | After AI, what remains is discovering and pursuing your life's work; a body of work is the only safety net |
| September 2025 | Meaning economy versus attention economy | Join those already doing meaningful work |
| February 2026 | Creator economy equals meaning economy | You are paid by people who believe in what you do; they buy your curation |
| April 2026 | Meaning as the scarcest commodity | "More" is the current god; meaning will be sold at a premium |

The changes are renamings and extensions rather than reversals. Two elements are present from the start (AI cannot do what humans need most, and education is the vehicle), and two are added later: the explicit opposition to the attention economy and the claim that the flow of money itself changes. A third addition is the scarcity claim of April 2026, which makes meaning the latest step in a sequence Koe had been tracing for years: from information as "the new currency" (2022), to ideas and attention (2023), to taste as "the new intelligence" (2025), to meaning (2026), each becoming central as the previous one is made abundant.

#### Jobs where the human is the product

Not all jobs disappear in Koe's account, and in February 2026 he listed those that require humans in the loop: high-liability roles, where you want a human who can be held accountable; statutory positions with legal requirements; the experience economy (bartenders, boutique shops, art galleries, live performances), which he expects to "see a huge boom"; meaning makers, the people who help others navigate the human experience; and relationship and trust jobs (sales, diplomacy, negotiation). The pattern he draws from the list: "the jobs that survive are the ones where the human is the product. It's not what they produce, it's who they are." **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

The list refines the claim that the entry level is going extinct (Section 36.3). The work that survives is not defined by skill level alone, but by whether the value depends on a particular person being there: someone to blame, someone legally required, someone to watch, someone to trust. That is the utility/meaning division applied to the labor market. It also explains why Koe treats the personal brand as an asset in this context: in the creator economy, as in the surviving jobs, the human is the product.

#### Post-labor economics and universal basic income

If AI takes over a large share of paid work, how will people live? In the same February 2026 video Koe addressed the question through someone else's framework. The traditional economic loop runs as follows: you work a job, earn a wage, spend it, the company profits, and the profit creates more jobs or the ability to pay. AI breaks the loop: if AI does the jobs, wages collapse, spending collapses, and the economic system falls apart. He introduces the post-labor economics framework of David Shapiro (who has a book, *Labor Zero*, forthcoming; Koe recommends Shapiro's YouTube playlist on the topic). In brief, household income has three sources. The first is wages, which AI threatens. The second is transfers, such as government payments and universal basic income; their problem, if they dominate, is that they are politically unstable and distort markets. The third is capital income, from owning income-generating assets; the future probably requires "broadening capital participation," regular people owning assets, not just billionaires. **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

Koe adds his own position to Shapiro's framework. He would not personally find meaning in "sitting on the couch all day collecting money"; he needs to grow, with "maybe 60 more years on this planet." He wants to create, and people will need the meaning derived from creation: "that's where you and I come in." He presents the framework as a theory that may not come to pass. **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md. The division of authorship is clear: the economic analysis (the broken loop, the three income sources, the instability of transfers, broadening capital participation) is Shapiro's; the personal stance (transfers do not supply meaning, so the creator's role remains) is Koe's.

Six months earlier, Koe had addressed universal basic income from the side of individual choice. Some argue that a monthly deposit would give everyone time to self-actualize and transcend; others that everyone would become mediocre and lazy. "The truth here is that it's just a deliberate personal choice which one you become." Ninety-nine percent of people, he says, have always proven that they will become lazy and mediocre, "the bottom of the barrel," whether through UBI or through easy, mediocre content. But "if there's no friction there, it's going to get old quick," and the people who deliberately pursue excellence rise to the top. **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

The figure of 99% is rhetorical, not measured, and the claim that "frictionless mediocrity" gets old quickly is an application of his theory of meaning (below): without struggle, there is no meaning, so a life without friction eventually feels empty even to those who chose it. The view of UBI shows the general shape of Koe's thought about technology. A change in external conditions does not decide the outcome; it raises the stakes of the individual's choice.

His position on UBI developed over several years. In 2022 he admitted that at first he did not understand Naval's idea that there could be "7 billion companies" ("not everyone can have a business"), and came to believe it a year or two later; in 2024 he called the idea "just speculation" and said that even with UBI people would want to work. In May 2025 he spoke of an "acclimation period" without safety nets such as UBI; in August 2025 he framed UBI as a choice between excellence and mediocrity; and in February 2026, with Shapiro, he analyzed transfers as politically unstable and pointed to broadening capital participation. Through all these changes, the conviction that humans need to work or create in order to have meaning stays fixed.

#### The anatomy of meaning

If meaning is what people will pay for, what is it made of? In the February 2026 video Koe offered a framework that he says can be used to "engineer meaning," and he placed it inside a history.

The history comes first, in four acts. Act one: meaning came "from up there." For most of history it was given by gods, kings, elders and scriptures; you did not find it, it was assigned (he places this "in very early foraging societies"). Act two, with the industrial revolution: meaning came "from out there." Science replaced religion as the dominant framework, meaning shifted to productivity and progress, and it was earned through work and "contribution to the machine." Act three, now: meaning has been deconstructed into nothing and is "found nowhere." This is postmodernism: no perspective is privileged, nothing is objectively true; meaning is neither given nor earned, it is simply gone. Act four: meaning "in here," generated from within, by you, for you. The consequence: "it becomes our responsibility to create meaning," and "Creatives are the meaning architects of society." **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

| Act | Where meaning comes from | How it is obtained |
|---|---|---|
| One | "Up there": gods, kings, elders, scriptures | Assigned |
| Two | "Out there": science, productivity, progress | Earned through work and contribution to the machine |
| Three | "Nowhere": postmodernism | Neither given nor earned; gone |
| Four | "In here": the individual | Generated by you, for you |

**Complementary context:** postmodernism is the late twentieth-century current of thought associated with Jean-François Lyotard's "incredulity toward metanarratives" and with the critique of objective truth and privileged perspectives. Koe uses the label as shorthand for act three and does not discuss any specific author. His placement of act one "in very early foraging societies" is his own; it is a broad-brush sketch rather than a historical claim he defends.

The four acts closely parallel the sequence Koe gave two months later (gods in the sky, then productivity, then "more"), and both sequences have the same function: they explain why a scarcity of meaning exists now. External sources of meaning have been used up or dismantled, and the remaining source is internal. That is why creatives become "meaning architects": in a culture where meaning has to be generated, those who generate it and show others how become valuable.

The framework itself has three parts. To understand meaning, Koe says, first understand meaninglessness. There are two "killers" of meaning. Stagnation is the absence of forward movement or progress, the feeling of being stuck. Isolation is the absence of connection to something greater, the feeling that you do not matter. This is why modern life feels empty: on your phone you are neither progressing nor contributing; you think you are connected, but you are not, really, and companies profit from your addiction. Against the two killers stand two "pillars." Progress is forward movement: humans need to feel that they are going somewhere, not to arrive and stop (his example is a person who built a $100 million company, stopped, and found that life lacked meaning). Progress comes through creative problem solving, through "struggle, not through suffering," which requires removing the negative connotations from the word struggle. Contribution is connection to something greater: progress must matter to someone besides yourself, which is why solo projects feel hollow after a while. You need an audience, a community or a cause; not a million followers, since tiny followings or real-life communities can sustain an income. Finally, there are three "generators" of meaning: struggle, which is the engine of progress; curiosity, which gives progress its direction; and status, which is the proof of contribution. **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

| Component | Elements | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Killers | Stagnation (no progress); isolation (no connection to something greater) | What destroys meaning; what the phone delivers |
| Pillars | Progress (forward movement through struggle); contribution (progress that matters to others) | What meaning rests on |
| Generators | Struggle (engine of progress); curiosity (direction of progress); status (proof of contribution) | What produces meaning in practice |

The structure is symmetrical: each killer is the absence of a pillar, and the generators are the means by which the pillars are built. Stagnation is the absence of progress, and struggle and curiosity produce progress; isolation is the absence of contribution, and status is the evidence that a contribution has been recognized. The units of this chapter give the framework and the function of each generator; Koe develops each generator in more detail in units treated in Chapter 39, where struggle, curiosity and status (renamed "recognition") also appear as the basis of a story, and where the formula of happiness as resistance overcome plus connection to something greater corresponds to the two pillars.

The inclusion of status deserves a note. In earlier years Koe said that status "is not bad if it is conscious"; in 2026 it becomes an explicit engine of meaning, the visible sign that what you did mattered to others. This fits his remark that "you play [a status game] every day" whether or not you admit it, and it explains why the loss of work threatens meaning: work has been one of the main ways in which people obtained proof of contribution.

The framework appears in a shorter form a month earlier, in January 2026. The good news, Koe said, is that most people do not do anything. You are probably in an internet echo chamber of ambitious people, but the average person treats AI as somewhere to ask questions, or hates it, and may never change, because they do not use their free time to learn and explore. They think they can do one thing, see success, do it forever and never do anything again; they have been sold an "illusory retirement" in which their efforts pay off and they relax forever. That is not meaningful. "The mechanical or linear way of life is not something to bet your future on because meaning is generated from struggle, status, and curiosity. The day you stop following one of those drives is the day that your life continues to get considerably worse." **Source:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md.

"Illusory retirement" is Koe's name for the linear life plan in which effort ends in permanent rest. The $100 million founder of the February framework is its embodiment: someone who achieved the goal of the linear plan and found it empty. The idea also links back to the opening of this chapter. If the definition of "making it" is changing (Section 36.1), one of the things that changes is the endpoint. In the old definition, making it meant reaching a state where you could stop. In Koe's definition, there is no such state, because meaning is generated by continuing to struggle, explore and contribute.

#### The artisanesque lifestyle

Koe compressed the practical conclusion of all this into a single sentence in August 2025, offered as a way to figure out what you want: "Choose something you deeply care about, dissect it ruthlessly, and shamelessly share your story, values, vision, and accompanying life's work in public to form an artisanesque lifestyle with a small tribe of people who support what you do. Pair that with AI, and you free up room for leisure, the ultimate compliment to creative work." **Source:** You Have About 36 Months To Make It.md.

Each part of the sentence corresponds to something this chapter has covered. "Something you deeply care about" is the domain of meaning-based work that should not be handed to AI (Section 36.5). "Dissect it ruthlessly" is the mastery half of the "mastery and meaning economy." "Shamelessly share... in public" is the creator's role as a decentralized teacher and as a media company (Section 36.2), and the act that turns progress into contribution. "Your story, values, vision" are the things the meaning economy pays for, the human element that makes the human the product. "A small tribe" restates the point Koe makes throughout the corpus: you do not need millions of followers. "Pair that with AI" is the director's use of the tool for utility tasks. "Leisure" is the time freed by that delegation; Koe calls it "the ultimate compliment to creative work" (the transcript's spelling; the sense is closer to "complement," something that completes creative work rather than flatters it).

"Artisanesque" is Koe's coinage. An artisan is someone whose work bears their hand: made with care, in small numbers, for people who value that particular maker. The word is the opposite of the industrial work "disguised as creative work" of Section 36.4, and of the slop that AI produces when no one directs it. It describes the kind of life the whole chapter has pointed toward: one in which a person is not competing with machines at what machines do well, but doing what only that person can do, for people who want it from them, with machines handling the rest.

The chapter began with the claim that power is shifting to the individual. It ends with what that power is for. In Koe's account, the transfer of power, the new school, the replacement of replicable work, the need for direction and the skillful use of AI all lead to the same place: a life organized around meaningful work that you choose, master and share. Chapter 37 turns to Koe's own trajectory as the case that integrates these ideas, and to the limits of the one-person model when it grows into a team. Chapters 39 and 40 return to the question of meaning at the level of a whole life, beyond the economy.

### Exercises

1. **Audit the urgency.** Section 36.1 lists the figures Koe uses to frame the future of work, most of them without a source and some inconsistent with each other. Choose two of them and explain what role each plays in his argument. Then decide whether his practical conclusion ("take matters into your own hands") would still follow if every one of those figures turned out to be wrong. What, exactly, does the conclusion rest on?

2. **Technician or creator?** Using the definition "technicians work toward the goals they are assigned; creators cultivate a lifestyle that naturally results in the goals they choose," analyze your current work or studies. Separate the tasks that have predictable inputs and predictable outputs from those that require a layer of judgment. For the first group, apply Naval's criterion as Koe uses it (delegate, automate or leave). For the second, describe what decision you are making that a tool could not make for you.

3. **Automate yourself out of one task.** Pick one recurring task you do well and apply Koe's six-step procedure: write the process as if teaching it, assume it is promptable, convert it into a prompt, test it, refine it toward 90%, and store it. Then reflect: which part of the written process was hardest to articulate, and what does that difficulty reveal about the knowledge you hold tacitly? Did the task turn out to belong to a "highly relative domain"?

4. **Where do you draw the line?** Koe's two rules are to use AI for what you do not want to do and not to give it complete control over what you deeply care about. Name the craft you would keep in your own hands and the tasks you would hand over. Then test the boundary against his other claims: is delegating the remaining tasks "authenticity at scale," or would some of them, in your case, carry meaning you would lose? Defend your decision.

5. **Evaluate the anti-AI argument.** Koe claims that the anti-AI identity lets people "feel like you're making a difference without actually changing your behavior," and that "the real threat is... you are dependent on everyone but yourself." Construct the strongest objection a thoughtful critic of AI could make that is not covered by Koe's description of the "anti-AI crowd." Then assess whether his argument still holds against that objection, and under what conditions it would not.

6. **Map your sources of meaning.** Apply the anatomy of meaning (two killers, two pillars, three generators) to your own life right now. Where do you see stagnation or isolation? Which generator (struggle, curiosity, status) is most active and which is absent? Then imagine that the work you currently do were fully automated tomorrow: which of the pillars would collapse, and what would you need to build to replace it?

7. **Question the utility/meaning division.** Koe argues that machines are for "speed, repetition, and necessity" and humans for "story, novelty, myth, and meaning," and that AI-written wedding vows would fail to move anyone. Find a case where the line is not clear: a task that is both useful and meaningful, or a context in which people knowingly accept machine-made meaning. Does your case refine the framework, or does it break it? Rewrite the framework so that it accounts for your example.

8. **Design an artisanesque path.** Using Koe's sentence ("choose something you deeply care about, dissect it ruthlessly, and shamelessly share your story, values, vision, and accompanying life's work in public..."), sketch a one-year plan: the subject you would choose, how you would study it with an AI-first mindset, what you would publish and where, who the "small tribe" would be, and which tasks you would hand to AI to free time. Identify the point at which the plan would most likely fail, given the "five ingredients" Koe says most vibe coders lack (agency, taste, persistence, iteration, persuasion and distribution).
