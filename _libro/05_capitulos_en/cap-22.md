## Chapter 22 — What to Write: Ideas, Raw Material and Perspective

Chapter 21 established why writing is the base skill: it is the foundation of online media, the most accessible form of leverage, the apparatus with which a mind thinks, and the daily practice that turns a life into a public body of work. That argument leaves the most common question a beginner asks Koe unanswered. In his own recurring phrasing, the question is "Dan, what do I write about?" This chapter collects his answers to it across five years. They are numerous, they overlap and they do not always agree, but underneath them runs a consistent logic: the material of writing is not a topic chosen from a market list but a perspective, and a perspective is made of a goal, the problems one has lived through on the way to it, the beliefs formed in the process and the ideas one has hunted down and made one's own.

The chapter proceeds in seven steps. Section 22.1 fixes the criterion of value that governs everything else: "value is ideas written from the lens of a specific goal or problem," and the niche is not a market but "a perspective or worldview." Section 22.2 turns inward to the writer's raw material: the topic one cannot stop talking about, the excavation questions, the "contrarian spine," and the thesis that one's story is one's brand. Section 22.3 presents the tools Koe has used to organize interests into something that can be written about for months: the topic tree, the Domain of Mastery, content pillars and the paper map from point A to point B, together with his later doubts about all of them. Section 22.4 describes the hunt for ideas: golden nuggets, "modern hunting," the idea "you wish you wrote," the study of high-performing and outlier content, the balance of performance and excitement, and a guest's system, Justin Welsh's content matrix. Section 22.5 defends repetition against the demand for novelty: "people need to be reminded more than they need something new," the evergreen "small-scale book," saying one thing a thousand ways, and the value of perspective over prescription. Section 22.6 addresses the stance from which one writes: confidence and conviction, picking a side, authentic polarization and congruence. Section 22.7 closes with the claim that makes the whole chapter coherent: interests are not innate but persuaded, so the writer's task is to make an interest interesting, which is also how customers are created through education.

The chapter depends directly on Chapter 19 (you are the niche), whose thesis it converts into a writing practice, and on Chapter 20 (the generalist), whose multiple interests it organizes. It also depends on the model of the mind built in Parts II and III: the goal as a lens (Chapter 6), identity as a set of beliefs that can be reprogrammed (Chapters 3 and 4), and the idea of the "program" or conditioning (Chapter 1), which here returns in a positive form, as the writer's ability to program interest in others. It prepares Chapter 23, which deals with how the material chosen here is structured to hold attention, and Chapter 24, which deals with the weekly process and the content ecosystem.

### 22.1 Value Is Ideas Through the Lens of a Goal

#### Content is limited to value, not to topic

The foundational answer to "what do I write about?" is a definition, and it arrives before any technique. In a March 2023 video, Koe tells his audience that they can pivot whenever they like, because their content "shouldn't be limited to a topic or niche; it's limited to value." He then defines the word: "value is ideas written from the lens of a specific goal or problem," regardless of topic.

The definition does two things at once. First, it demotes the topic. Topics, in this view, are useful only for idea generation: if one writes about self-improvement, business and spirituality, those labels tell one where to look for material, but they are not what the reader is buying. "In essence nobody cares about the topic," Koe says; people care about how anything and everything will benefit their life, because "we are selfish creatures." Second, the definition promotes the lens. An idea becomes valuable when it is written through a goal (something the reader wants to achieve) or a problem (something the reader wants to escape). The same idea about, say, the psychology of habits can be worthless as abstract information and valuable when it is shown as a way to reach a goal the reader already holds.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

This definition is one of several Koe gives of "value" across the corpus, and it is worth situating it among them. Elsewhere he defines value as "the relationship between what you do, why you do it, how you do it and who benefits" (developed in Chapter 34), as "positive behavior change," and as "structured communication or sense making to the mind" (both developed in Chapter 31, which also sets all these definitions side by side in Section 31.1). The 2023 formulation used here is the one specific to content: it tells the writer what to do with an idea before publishing it. It also connects back to Chapter 6, where the goal was presented as the lens through which the mind filters reality. Here the same lens is turned outward: the writer filters ideas through a goal so that the reader can see them as relevant to their own goals.

#### Point A and point B: content as programming a video game

In a June 2026 video aimed at people with multiple interests, Koe gives the definition a spatial form through a metaphor he uses as an explanatory device:

> "You treat content like you're the programmer of a video game. You have point A, where your audience is now, level one, and then you have point B, where you can help them get to, which can be level 100, or 50, or 10, or even two, just one level ahead of them."

The criterion that follows is permissive by design: "Any idea or content is free game if it lands between your point A and point B." A writer with ten interests does not need to decide which of them is "the niche." The question is only whether a given idea helps move a reader from where they are toward where the writer is leading them.

The metaphor carries a second point about the writer's own position. Koe adds that "even if you aren't level 100, you're allowed to grow. You should grow. Actually, you're the leader. That's why you attract followers... because entrepreneurship is self-improvement in disguise." The coined phrase **"self-improvement in disguise"** condenses an argument developed in Part X: building a one-person business forces the builder to develop, so the writer is not a finished expert dispensing knowledge from level 100 but someone a few levels ahead who keeps climbing in public. This anticipates the advice in Section 22.2 to write "from your current position."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

#### The map and the world

In the same video, immediately before the video-game metaphor, Koe adds a second image. "If your brand is the transformation, your content is the map that is built over time. It's the world." He compares it to the Marvel Cinematic Universe: Iron Man, the Avengers, Spider-Man, "built up over time in this like bingeable fashion," with spin-offs, films and action figures, "an entire lore behind it. That's kind of what you're building over time."

The two images fit together. Point A and point B define the transformation (the brand); the content is everything placed between them, and over time it accumulates into a navigable territory that a newcomer can explore from many entry points. The emphasis on the "bingeable" quality recurs elsewhere in the corpus: in 2025 Koe speaks of building "your own small-scale Netflix that people can binge watch or binge read" (Section 22.4). The practical consequence is that a single piece of content is never judged alone; it is judged as a location on a map that is still being drawn.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

#### Frame the idea through the mission

The definition of value becomes a working method when the "goal" is specified as the writer's mission. Koe describes his own: his brand "is to help people become future-proof... That's my niche, so to speak." His content goal is to find great ideas he wants to talk about "and just frame them through the lens of becoming future-proof." He explains that the very video in which he says this was born from such a question: faced with the topic of multiple interests, he asked "how does that help you become future-proof? And this video was born."

He then gives a worked example. Studying the psychology of flow, he finds an idea he loves: "how psychic entropy causes the mind to slowly become disordered with time unless effort is put into maintaining a meaningful hierarchy of goals." He asks: "How does this apply to becoming future-proof?" And he draws the conclusion:

> "Through that question alone, I now have a unique piece of content. I'm not copying the idea one-to-one, I'm applying it to a new domain from my own voice and perspective. And that is content creation in a nutshell for people who don't want to dance in front of a camera or be on TikTok."

The coined instruction is **"you frame the idea through the mission."** The method has three parts: hunt for ideas in one's interests (Section 22.4 develops this), have a mission, and tie the two together, checking against the map how the idea helps people get from A to B.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

**Complementary context:** "Psychic entropy" is a concept from Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's work on flow, where it names disorder in consciousness caused by information that conflicts with one's goals. Koe introduces it here without naming the author, as "flow psychology"; Chapter 5 of this book treats the concept and its sources in detail. The example is useful precisely because it shows a borrowed idea becoming original by being transposed into a new domain, which is the same mechanism Chapter 18 described as synthesis.

#### Filter it through your big goal: a post in four steps

An earlier version of the same method, from a May 2024 video on the generalist, is more procedural. Koe gives four steps for writing a post:

1. Make a list of content ideas based on your interests.
2. Focus on the how and the why.
3. Write the post.
4. **Filter it through your big goal.**

The fourth step carries the nuance. One does not need to make the goal explicit ("hey, we're achieving this goal together"); people infer it. The writer should "just write good content people want to read," and a small addition of the big goal is what makes the post impactful and desirable enough to follow.

His example illustrates the mechanics. Take a psychology topic: how do I manage my emotions? The "how" yields a list of steps; the "why" yields compelling reasons drawn from experience; the post is then framed through financial freedom. The resulting post reads roughly: become aware of negative reactions, pause before acting, stay quiet or make a better decision; repeating this reprograms your mind to be less negative; and "life, money and relationships will flow with ease." The last clause is the filter: a post about emotional regulation becomes, by one added line, a post about the goal the brand pursues.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The 2024 "filter it through your big goal" and the 2026 "frame the idea through the mission" are the same operation described two years apart; the later version names the goal more precisely (a mission rather than a "big goal") and attaches it to the explicit map of point A and point B. The step "focus on the how and the why" also anticipates Section 22.7: the "why" is, for Koe, what makes all content good.

#### The niche as a perspective or worldview

If value is ideas through the lens of a goal, then the niche cannot be what conventional business advice says it is (a market segment defined by demographics or a single skill). Koe draws this conclusion explicitly. In an August 2024 essay-video, "Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche," he writes: "A niche is a perspective or worldview." He follows it with a short manifesto:

> "Become a nobody. Become everything. Become a designer, writer, marketer, socializer, runner, bodybuilder, philosopher, scientist, psychologist, and polymath who knows how to sustain your obsessive curiosity. The universe rewards those who don't impose their own limits."

Later in the same video he gives the fuller definition that this chapter treats as the anchor of what to write:

> "Your niche is the frame of big goals and burning problems that compose your worldview. Your job is to program the minds of your audience to adopt this frame or worldview, pursue those goals, and solve those problems. This is a massive yet fulfilling undertaking to live a life of purpose."

And he reduces the business to three roles: "you're attracting people to the goals. You're helping them solve the problems with your content. And your product is the implementation. It's how they actually go about solving the problem by changing their behavior."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

Three features of this definition matter for the rest of the chapter. First, "niche" is a common word used with a meaning of Koe's own: not a segment one targets but a frame one holds. Second, the writer's job is described with the verb "program," which reverses the vocabulary of Part I. There, conditioning was the problem: the Matrix programs minds toward goals they did not choose. Here, the writer deliberately offers a program, a set of goals and problems, which the reader may adopt. Section 22.7 returns to this reversal when Koe argues that interest itself is programmed. Third, the triad of goals, problems and implementation is the same structure as Chapter 19's "four pillars" (goals, problems, systems, benefits), viewed from the side of content.

The earliest version of this idea dates from November 2023. In a video on productizing one's mind, Koe argues that perspective matters because "your perspective shapes the entirety of your brand, that is your niche," and he announces a future video called "The Anti-Niche," glossed as "the most profitable niche is you." He then decomposes a perspective into three parts (a goal, a problem, and the clarity, path, skill or knowledge needed to achieve the goal) and says: "that's all a brand is." The writer leads people toward a goal, helps them with skill set and mindset through content, and gives them a system or clarity through a product. "That's turning yourself into the business, turning your mind or perspective into the business, that's earning with your mind." He closes with a characteristic generalization: "Once you peel back to just what the foundation of life is, life and business becomes pretty easy."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The term **anti-niche** is one in a series of labels for the same thesis that Chapter 19 traces ("niche of one," "the most profitable niche is you," "you are the infinite niche," "nicheless," "mission as niche"). What this section adds is the consequence for writing: if the niche is a perspective, then what to write is anything that expresses, applies or extends that perspective, and the remaining sections of the chapter describe where the perspective comes from (22.2), how to organize it (22.3), how to feed it (22.4), how often to repeat it (22.5), with what stance to express it (22.6) and how to make others adopt it (22.7).

### 22.2 Excavating Your Own Raw Material

#### The raw material is already inside you

If the niche is a perspective, the next question is where a perspective comes from. Koe's most developed answer comes late, in a June 2026 video titled "How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You," but it gathers threads that run through the whole corpus. He begins with a diagnosis. Most people love the idea of a personal brand, of a creator's or YouTuber's life, but get stuck fast. They look for "the quick dopamine hit," searching for the best niche to make six figures, "rather than digging into the value they already have from years of accumulated experience and story that they think is worthless because it's normal to them."

The thesis follows:

> "The raw material for your life's work is already inside you, buried under years of being told to specialize, to be practical, to stop asking so many questions."

The phrase **raw material (for your life's work)** names the accumulated experience a person discounts because it is familiar to them. Koe explains why it is buried: most of what makes a person interesting has been "trained out" of them. Curiosity was treated as distraction; varied interests were labeled lack of focus; "the system wanted you an obedient worker." This is the diagnosis of Part I (the school as an institution of the default path) and of Chapter 20 (the critique of specialization) applied to the question of content. The process he is about to describe "isn't meant to give a novel idea but to show what you already have," and content, he says, only works if it comes from material that is actually yours.

The passage also contains a product reference: Koe mentions that the onboarding of Eden, his software, builds "your identity" so that AI can speak like the user, "not just voice replication... your mind." The pitch is incidental, but it shows how he frames the stakes in 2026: in a period when AI can produce generic content without limit, the excavated material is what the machine does not have.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

#### A topic you can't shut up about

Before the 2026 excavation exercise, Koe's first step for beginners was simpler and remarkably stable. In August 2022, May 2023 and again in a February 2024 compilation, the first step of his writing process is the same: **choose a topic you cannot shut up about**.

The 2022 version describes where to find it: your favorite books, the ones you enjoy and "get sucked into"; your YouTube watch history; what you are genuinely interested in, love learning, and love teaching to friends; and your favorite social media accounts that are "not meme accounts but value creators giving good advice." It then explains the mechanism with a scene. You tell a friend, "I'm learning how to code... have you ever heard about HTML, CSS?" and the friend does not care. "Your friends don't really give a [damn] about that, but there's an audience online that does and they want to hear you talk about it no matter your experience, because that's all perspective based."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md

The May 2023 version, the first of nine steps in the video "I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day," compresses the procedure into a list to write down: favorite books, YouTube watch history, favorite social media accounts, and what you would write about "if money wasn't an issue." The last criterion is new and functions as a test: it separates a genuine interest from one chosen because it seems to pay.

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

The 2024 compilation repeats the 2022 passage almost literally and adds the element that resolves the beginner's typical objection ("but I have no results to show"). If you cannot claim "$40–60K a month writing," talk from a different perspective, for example "seven things that I learned about impactful writing," which builds authority without faking it. The principle is the coined phrase **"brand is what you do"**: whatever you are doing right now is how you write. If you are learning, write as someone learning, and teach what you learn. "You just be the guy that is learning; brand is what you do."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

A July 2024 formulation gives the same answer in more lyrical form, in response to the broader question "what do I write about, what do I market and sell, what do I email, design or leverage technical skills with?" The answer: "the interest you can't help but tell others about, the books you can't pull yourself away from, the ideas that flood your search history, the projects you dream of building but can't seem to make the time to build." The question is wider than writing (it covers marketing, selling and technical skills), which shows that for Koe the topic one cannot shut up about is not just an editorial choice but the core around which the whole one-person business is built. Section 22.7 shows that the same passage, in a March 2024 version, leads directly into the thesis that one does not find a niche but creates one through persuasion.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The earliest statement of the underlying rationale is from October 2022: "Post the things that are interesting to you because they're going to be interesting to someone else." If a line in a book catches your attention, other people will think it too; distill it for your audience, synthesize it with your interests or what you are trying to achieve, and it will interest them "because they are similar to you." The logic is the same as Chapter 19's "you attract who you are": the writer's own attention is treated as a sample of the attention of a population of similar people.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

This last heuristic is the starting point of a change Koe made later. In 2022 and 2023 the writer's own interest is presented as a sufficient criterion ("post the things that are interesting to you"). By 2026, as Section 22.4 shows, he says that "the best creators don't just post what's on their mind," and asks for an intersection between what interests the writer and what has been shown to interest others. The two positions are not strictly contradictory (the 2022 advice already asks the writer to "distill" and "synthesize"), but the emphasis moves from self-expression toward market research.

#### Step one: three questions to excavate the raw material

The 2026 excavation is presented as an exercise with precise instructions. Set aside 15 minutes, close all tabs, open a blank document and answer in writing. If no answer comes to a question, move on and let it sit in the subconscious. Then answer three questions:

1. **"What do you know too much about for it to be an accident?"** That is: what topic have you researched across dozens of sources, for years, without being paid?
2. **"What problem did you solve for yourself that you assumed everyone else had figured out?"** And, as a variant: what comes naturally to you that seems to break everyone else? Koe adds: "Hint hint, that's your product." One solves a problem for oneself and then for someone else.
3. **"What did you get in trouble for as a kid that was actually just early taste?"** That is: what did you obsess over before anyone told you it was impractical?

Then take one answer, the one that felt best, and start there. Koe adds explicitly: ignore niche and content pillars.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

Each question targets a different layer of the buried material. The first targets accumulated knowledge, using unpaid research as evidence of genuine interest (the same test as "what you would write about if money wasn't an issue"). The second targets solved problems, which connect directly to Chapter 19's "build, write and sell to yourself": the product is the solution one already built for one's past self. The third, with the coined term **early taste**, targets what the school and family environment labeled as misbehavior. The question presupposes the diagnosis of Part I: what the default path suppressed may be precisely the interest that defines a person's taste (a concept treated in Chapter 18). The instruction to "ignore niche and content pillars" is significant, because it is Koe in 2026 telling his audience to set aside tools he himself taught earlier, a shift Section 22.3 examines.

#### Step two: the contrarian spine

The second step of the same exercise begins with a sentence that summarizes Koe's late position on originality: **"Nobody needs another person repackaging common sense."** Content needs a perspective that only the writer sees, and that perspective comes from "the one thing you believe that the mainstream gets wrong." He calls this the **contrarian spine**. Three more questions are meant to locate it:

1. What piece of mainstream advice actively made your life worse, and what did you have to unlearn to become functional?
2. What do you believe about your domain that experts would call stupid or naive, but you can't stop thinking about?
3. What is everyone in your industry pretending not to see, or not talking about?

The six answers from steps one and two, he says, "gave you the six fundamental types of posts." The coined phrase **six fundamental types of posts** refers to these six answers as the starting point of "proven posts" that generate authority and trust rather than simply chasing virality. The instruction is to pick one of them as the first post.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

The metaphor of the "spine" suggests structural function: the contrarian belief is what holds a body of content upright and gives it a recognizable posture. It connects to two later sections of this chapter. In Section 22.5, Koe's earlier and repeated advice that "people need to be reminded more than they need something new" sits in visible tension with "nobody needs another person repackaging common sense." In Section 22.6, the contrarian spine is the content of the conviction with which one is asked to write: one cannot "pick a side" without a side to pick.

#### Your story is your brand

The second layer of raw material is the writer's story. The phrase **your story is your brand** appears across the corpus from 2022 to 2026, and its strongest justification is a metaphor from April 2023:

> "From a multiverse perspective": there are a million different actions you could take in this very moment, and all of those split off into infinite different realities. You take one specific action, then there are a million more, you take one, and so on, and everyone is doing that every second of the day. "That's what creates your story and your story is your brand and that's what makes you unique and that is why every single person on this Earth can have a business that does not have any competition."

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

The multiverse image is an explanatory metaphor, not a claim about physics: it dramatizes the combinatorial uniqueness of any life history. Its practical point is that competition is a property of offers made from generic positions; an offer made from a specific history has no exact competitor.

A January 2025 passage, from a conversation about growing from zero followers, answers the objection that follows naturally: "other people are talking about this too, how do I stand out if I'm giving the same information?" Koe's answer uses a homely metaphor: "There's maybe a few ways to weave a basket, but let's say there's one way to weave a basket. What makes me different from the next person talking about this?" And: "It would be your story and who you relate with." Even when one talks about a single thing, what makes it unique "is how it applied to your life and how it helped you." He calls the story "the glue between these different perspectives," and gives a combined formula for the niche: "interests combined with the pain points in your life that those solved, and led to the story that you have now."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

The basket-weaving metaphor is useful because it concedes the strongest version of the objection. Koe does not claim that the information is different; he grants that there may be only one correct way to weave a basket and locates the difference elsewhere, in the application and in the reader's ability to relate to the person explaining it. The formula "interests + pain points they solved + the resulting story" also shows that the story is not an extra ingredient but the connective tissue between the interests (Section 22.3) and the problems (this section).

To recover the story, a January 2026 passage gives a reflective exercise. Spend a day, or go on a walk, and think: Where did you come from? What is the lowest point you have been? What other lows? What have you overcome? What is your actual story? What skills have you learned? What traits have you developed? What are some of your weird and extreme beliefs? What do you think that other people don't? "What are your contrarian takes? What makes you different?" The final questions overlap with the 2026 contrarian spine, which suggests that, for Koe, story and contrarian belief are two faces of one material: the beliefs are what the story taught.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

He anticipates resistance in the same video: "The difficult part for a lot of people, and this was myself included, is that you don't really think your story is worth telling because you think it's boring or you haven't reflected on your growth. But it is, and you'll come to realize that." The warning matches the 2026 diagnosis of raw material that seems "worthless because it's normal to them." The obstacle is not the absence of a story but the absence of reflection on it.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

Because this section turns the writer's own story into material for content, it invites a misreading that Koe himself later corrected, and the correction belongs next to the slogan. "Your story is your brand" is one face of his thesis "you are the niche" (Chapter 19), and that thesis was refined in two dated steps. In April 2026, in a video urging viewers to write essays, he added a condition: a person's point of view is their most valuable resource because AI has no access to it, but a point of view worth writing about requires living novel experiences; one cannot sit at a desk "on Claude 24/7" living "the optimized, efficient productivity life of just staring at your laptop all day." The instruction is to "go out, experience life, build a good life, and then bring that online because nobody else is doing that." In August 2026 he answered critics who took "you are the niche" to mean "posting about you, you, you": "No, you're documenting, you're placing your mind in public for people to explore. Your mind is not just your personality traits and... your story and the things about you. It's the ideas that create this web of interconnections that make how you say the ideas unique." Read with these two refinements, the story in this section is not the subject of the content but the source of the lens: it explains why one holds the ideas one publishes, and it must keep being fed by new experience. Between 2022 and 2023 the slogan rested on biography; by 2026 the asset is the mind and the point of view the biography produced. Chapter 19 (Section 19.2) develops both refinements and the AI context in which Koe made them.

**Source:** I'm begging you to start writing essays (even if you hate writing).md; How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

#### Story and core beliefs as topics and as reframes

A June 2025 video on personal branding turns story and beliefs into a method with two uses each. Koe's stated aim is to "replicate authenticity" by illustrating one's story and **core beliefs** (beliefs held with conviction) often. Each can serve as a topic or as a reframe:

| | As a topic | As a reframe of a validated topic |
|---|---|---|
| **Story** | "How I failed at seven different business models" | Take a high-potential topic such as "how to build a personal brand" and make it unique by opening with one's own story, as Koe did at the start of that very video |
| **Core beliefs** | "Digital products are the best way for beginners to start," with one's reasons, while others disagree | Learn how Steve Jobs builds a product and frame it under a new lens: for digital products or creators rather than for a Fortune 500 company |

His comment on his own example is the key: "I started with a story and that's what makes me talking about personal branding unique." The distinction between topic and reframe matters because it reconciles two pieces of advice that otherwise pull apart. Section 22.4 recommends taking topics that have already proven to work; this section insists on personal material. The reframe joins them: the topic is borrowed and validated, the lens is personal.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

To do this well, Koe gives an exercise: write out "where you were, what sparked change, and where you are now," and for each topic or theme list the **core principles** you hold with conviction. He warns that this is hard in front of a blank screen, because beliefs "aren't just all sitting in your conscious mind. You have to dig them up from your unconscious." He suggests thinking about it for about a week, listening to content and letting it bring up ideas to write down. As an alternative, he offers an AI prompt that guides the user's thinking and extracts their story and beliefs into a single document.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

The week-long excavation and the claim that beliefs live partly in the "unconscious" echo Part II's model of identity as a structure of mostly unexamined beliefs. The difference is that, in Chapters 3 and 4, those beliefs were examined in order to change them; here they are excavated in order to publish them.

In the same video, Koe adds a third lens to story and belief: reframing a topic "by reframing through a novel idea." If one follows what he calls the **growth pillar** (the part of a brand devoted to reach, which brings the influx of traffic without which "you won't get paid"; Chapters 26 and 27 develop it), one is constantly researching ideas, and some of them can serve as a new frame. His example is what he presents in this passage as Naval Ravikant's ideas: that "ideas are the new oil" and that "this generation is getting rich in idea space, not physical space." These let Koe introduce personal branding more compellingly: traditional business models needed a physical location and so on, "but with an online business, with a personal brand, you just need ideas." The term **new lens** names this operation. The attribution needs care. "Idea space" is Naval's phrase, adapted. "Ideas are the new oil," however, is a formula Koe had already used as his own a year earlier, as the title and thesis of a June 2024 video (Chapter 21); here, in June 2025, he credits the underlying idea to Naval. The most faithful reading is that the phrase is Koe's reformulation of a Naval idea, which in this passage he attributes back to its source. The example also shows the attribution chain: Naval provides the frame, Koe applies it to a topic Naval did not address.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

**Complementary context:** Naval Ravikant is an investor and essayist whose aphorisms on wealth, leverage and "specific knowledge" circulated widely on Twitter; Koe has said that Naval's philosophy "became mine without realizing it." The "idea space" image belongs to that body of aphorisms. Chapter 18 treats the related metaphor of idea space as a map of light and dark.

#### Three sources of topics and five lived problems

For beginners, Koe has also offered a plainer inventory. In a December 2024 video, he tells viewers to break the topic of their product or service into three sources:

1. **Your personal story**: where you were before, in the middle and after, in relation to the topic; your transformation.
2. **Pain points people face**: this is "how you start 80% of the content that you write," in hooks and introductions. He points out that the introduction of that very video is a pain point.
3. **Content topics that already did well**: search YouTube, note the best-performing videos, watch them for ideas and note ideas as you go.

The practical instruction is to create a document with five to ten points for each source, and to worry about how to write later: "act like you're texting a friend and they're asking you questions about your topic of interest; write your answers in public."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The figure "80%" is a rule of thumb about his own practice, not a measured statistic. The list places personal material (story) beside external material (topics that already did well), with pain points as the bridge between them, since a pain point can be both lived and widely shared. The "texting a friend" instruction addresses the paralysis of form; Chapter 23 deals with form properly.

The most compressed version of the same idea is a one-line exercise from July 2026: "Name five problems you've experienced in your life. Seriously, do that... and boom, now you have five content ideas that you can go and post, and they're going to be a lot better than if you just thought of them from scratch." The claim that lived problems produce better ideas than ideas "thought of from scratch" is the whole thesis of this section in a sentence. In the same passage Koe mentions a prompt he built that acts as a persuasion coach, teacher and writer: run it, it asks what you want, and you practice with it to learn human nature and persuasion faster, whether writing posts, newsletters, scripts, talks or a book.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md

Taken together, the material of this section answers "where does a perspective come from?" with four sources: interests one cannot stop talking about, solved problems, a story of transformation, and beliefs that contradict the mainstream. None of these has to be invented; all of them have to be dug up. What remains is to organize them so that they can sustain months of writing, which is the purpose of the next section.

### 22.3 The Topic Tree: Organizing Interests With Yourself at the Top

The raw material of Section 22.2 is personal and unordered. A writer who has excavated five interests, three solved problems and a contrarian belief still has to decide what to write on Monday, and then on every Monday for the next year. Between 2022 and 2026 Koe offered several tools for this: the Domain of Mastery, the topic tree (earlier called the "content pyramid"), content pillars and the content map, and a sheet of paper with point A and point B. They share a structure (a small number of broad interests, broken down into narrower topics that become individual pieces of content) and differ in emphasis. This section presents them in roughly chronological order, because the order shows a real change in how much weight Koe places on planning, and ends with his own later relativization of all of them.

#### The Domain of Mastery (2022 and 2024)

The earliest of these tools comes from Koe's course Digital Economics, which he cites in an October 2022 video as his answer to "Dan, what do I write about?" He calls it the **Domain of Mastery**. The instruction is to pick three interests:

1. One that makes money. Almost any interest can, and this is the **priority interest**.
2. One that excites you.
3. One that is development-based: psychology, self-improvement, self-development, metaphysics, philosophy, spirituality. Its function is to help one understand "the progression pattern of every single thing anyone could ever do," so that one can have a unique perspective by applying spirituality, philosophy or psychology to the other two.

Each interest is then broken down into components. In the 2022 version, these are four: **principles** (the fundamentals of getting results with that interest, in relation to one's goal), **topics**, **mentors** (people one follows and takes advice from, as inspiration) and **connections** (between all of them and real-world problems). These, Koe says, are "the starting places for effective, educational, valuable content."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md

The same passage reappears almost word for word in a February 2024 compilation.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In July 2024 Koe teaches the framework again, with three refinements. First, he insists that the interests overlap: "all of these overlap, these aren't like hard interests." Second, he separates "real world problems that they can solve" into its own component, so the breakdown now has five parts: principles ("the things that really matter within that interest"), "topics you can write about outside of that," "mentors you can study and emulate," "connections that can be made between them," and real-world problems. Third, he states the purpose of the whole exercise in terms of this chapter's definition of value: "Principles, topics, mentors, connections: those are all ideas that can be filtered through a goal of what you want to achieve in life and then be written to help people achieve the goal."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The framework can be reconstructed as follows:

| Layer | 2022 version | 2024 version |
|---|---|---|
| Interest 1 | Makes money (priority interest) | Makes money |
| Interest 2 | Excites you | Excites you |
| Interest 3 | Development-based (psychology, self-improvement, metaphysics, philosophy) | Development-based (philosophy, spirituality, self-improvement, self-actualization) |
| Relation among interests | Not stated | "These overlap; these aren't hard interests" |
| Breakdown of each interest | Principles, topics, mentors, connections (to each other and to real-world problems) | Principles, topics, mentors, connections, real-world problems |
| Purpose | Starting places for educational content | Ideas filtered through a life goal, written to help others achieve it |

In all three appearances Koe refers to a graphic shown on screen that the transcripts do not reproduce. The components and their order can be recovered from the narration, but the exact visual layout (whether the interests are drawn as overlapping circles, for example, or how the components hang from each interest) cannot be reconstructed with certainty. The 2024 change is a refinement rather than a contradiction: the earlier version already mentioned real-world problems inside "connections"; the later one makes them a component and makes the goal filter explicit.

The Domain of Mastery is the most deliberate of Koe's tools in one respect: it builds an economic criterion into the selection of interests (one must make money) and a developmental one (one must explain the "progression pattern"). The development-based interest is what gives the writer a vantage point on the other two. This is, in miniature, Koe's own brand: business as the money interest, writing or fitness as the exciting one, and philosophy, psychology and spirituality as the development lens through which he discusses the others. The corpus also uses "Domain of Mastery" for a different map (entrepreneurship as vessel, marketing as message, writing as medium, and so on), which Chapter 21 (Section 21.3, "Where content sits in the business") presents in a table; the version treated here is the one about choosing interests to write about.

#### Filter interests through your ideal future and zoom out (2023)

A February 2023 video, "How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101)," gives the most complete early process. It opens with a precondition. If one does not know what to write about, or whether to write about a specific interest, one needs a goal for one's ideal future: what are you working toward, and which interests help you get there? Interests are then filtered through that goal.

Koe's example comes from a viewer's dilemma: wanting to talk about spirituality while having a marketing-based product. His reasoning connects the two through an intermediate field. Marketing connects to psychology; spirituality is "like a philosophical psychology in a sense"; and if the end goal is to live autonomously and be sovereign, spirituality plays a huge role, so it makes sense to incorporate it. The first step, then, is to choose two to three interests to write about.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The example shows that the goal filter is permissive rather than restrictive. It does not exclude spirituality from a marketing brand; it finds the path by which spirituality serves the brand's goal. This is the same operation as "frame the idea through the mission" (Section 22.1), applied at the level of whole interests rather than single ideas.

The same video then warns against the opposite error. Most people, asked to write down their interests, write hyper-specific ones, such as "biomechanical movement for holistic health people." When few people turn out to be interested, they get stressed, and the stress damages their ability to write good content, because they think that if they do not talk about that one specific interest nobody will buy. "That's not how it works, my friend. Zoom out." The phrase belongs to a family Koe uses elsewhere ("when in doubt, zoom out," where the solution to struggle is perspective); here it means broadening the interest until there is room to write in it.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

#### Broaden and break down

The second step of the same 2023 process gives zooming out a procedure, under the coined name **broaden and break down**. Koe uses three example interests: web design, spirituality and weightlifting.

First, broaden each into its overarching market. Web design becomes online business, or business. Weightlifting becomes bodybuilding, or health. Spirituality becomes self-actualization or self-improvement. Koe adds that all three could be gathered under the single umbrella of self-improvement or self-actualization, even web design, "since skill acquisition plays a major role in self-actualization."

Second, break the three broad markets down into sub-markets or subtopics to write about over a long period. "This isn't like one week of content and your brand just takes off and explodes; this is like six months of content," enough to introduce people to everything one talks about and knows.

He contrasts this with a different logic of selling: personal branding is a long-term game, not a quick direct-response Facebook ad trying to make someone buy immediately. "Facebook ads and personal branding are a lot different."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The movement has a clear rationale. Broadening solves the problem of the hyper-specific interest (too few people care); breaking down solves the opposite problem (a broad market is too vague to write a post about). The result is a structure that is wide at the top for audience and narrow at the bottom for individual pieces. Chapter 19's "Eternal markets" (health, wealth, relationships, happiness) are the same broadening carried to its limit.

#### The topic tree

The result of broadening and breaking down is what Koe, in the same video, names the **topic tree**. He presents it as a graphic, which can be reconstructed from his narration:

- At the top: **you**, the "niche of one."
- Below: three interests (his example: health, improvement, business).
- Below each interest: topics (for health: nutrition, bodybuilding, lifestyle).
- Below the topics: subtopics (best exercises, recipe ideas, training programs, intermittent fasting, daily routine, sleep tips, which he says are "all just made up" for the illustration).

The writer's job is to cover everything written down in the tree over the six to twelve months of writing content. He comments on the name: "I call this the topic tree. I've called it many things in the past, the content pyramid," but he prefers "topic tree." The transcript does not specify exactly which subtopics hang under which topic; the example subtopics are mostly health-related, but the narration does not assign them one by one.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

Placing "you" at the top of the tree is the visual form of Chapter 19's thesis. In a conventional content plan the top node would be a market or a topic; here it is a person, and the interests hang from that person rather than the person being defined by one interest. The tree is therefore a tool for the generalist of Chapter 20: it gives multiple interests a single root.

A September 2023 variant adds a level above the three interests. "You want to create a topic tree with three broad interests," Koe says, but he argues that most people should first "umbrella under the one interest of self-improvement," self-development or personal development, "because we're trying to improve other people's lives." Then they break that umbrella into three (or more) broad interests, for example productivity, creativity and business. These serve as starting points for research: buy books, look at content, generate ideas from what others have posted on YouTube, Medium or elsewhere, "so you can create that content too."

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

The difference from the February version is what holds the interests together. In February the three interests are unified by the person at the top; in September they are unified first by a shared market (self-improvement) and only then by the person. The later variant also gives the tree a new function: not only a list of topics to write about but a list of fields to research, which links it to the idea hunting of Section 22.4.

A third mention, from September 2024, is brief. To decide what to teach and sell (what one's content and offer are composed of), Koe says he has taught the topic tree: three big, broad topics that "allow you to maneuver within them" and create many subtopics and content topics underneath, which one can fill out in one's own time. In that video he sets it aside in favor of a simpler plan he calls "straight-line to $1,000 in 60 days," which belongs to Chapter 29. The graphic, again, is not reproduced in the transcript.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

#### The authentic content process in six moves

At the end of the February 2023 video, Koe recaps the whole process in six moves, which place the topic tree inside a larger sequence:

1. Incorporate your interests to make your brand authentic.
2. Create a topic tree you can master in six to twelve months through writing.
3. Write multiple list-style pieces to establish authority in your main interest.
4. Broaden your writing if you don't think it will be shared.
5. Study the best content structures and use them "as training wheels."
6. Branch off what you have already written (repurpose).

Paired with his earlier video "The Most Profitable Niche Is You," he says, this is a start in building leverage. The next steps are networking and growth, then marketing, sales, advertising, promotions and product creation, toward a holistic brand and "potentially your life's work."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The recap is useful as a map of where this book places each element. The first two moves belong here. The fourth ("broaden your writing if you don't think it will be shared") anticipates the "broad and beginner" principle of Section 22.7. The fifth, structures as **training wheels**, is the subject of Chapter 23. The sixth, repurposing, belongs to Chapter 24. The study of structures through Twitter search, which the same video describes, is treated in Section 22.4.

#### Two to three intersecting interests: the generalist's niche (2024)

In 2024 Koe restates the selection of interests in the vocabulary of the generalist. In a June 2024 video, step four of a larger plan is to choose two to three **intersecting interests** as one's niche and not to over-specialize, "yet or at all ever." He refers back to his earlier videos "The Most Profitable Niche Is You" and "The Age of the Generalist." The argument has two parts. First, the writer needs to attract people toward the vision of the future they are leading them to, with content and product, and help them achieve goals aligned with that vision. Second, people niche down because they are specialists going toward a specific goal with one skill, which sets them up to be replaced, overtaken or out-competed; a generalist has a unique combination of skills.

His example makes the uniqueness concrete. If two people both seek financial freedom, one may go the budgeting route and the other the online business route; each pivots, learns different skills, and passing that knowledge on contributes value to humanity. The unique intersection of interests is what makes each of them unique in relation to the shared life goal.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

In the same video the next step turns the intersection into content. Break the two to three broad interests into subtopics, which lets one research more effectively for idea generation, and then break those further into post, newsletter, podcast and other media ideas. Start by writing to attract an audience: "start writing to attract an audience, not video, not something else where you need more skills." Repurpose the writing later, once ideas are validated. The purpose is to create media content around the interests and monetize it via a digital product.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

A week later, in "Ideas Are The New Oil," Koe compresses the same logic into four steps for finding one's niche as a generalist: (1) think about what you are trying to achieve in your life; (2) write down the interests and skills you have learned to get there; (3) attract others trying to achieve the same goal; (4) use your combination of interests and skills to make yourself unique. He supplies the definition behind it: "This is the definition of a generalist: a person who pursues a goal and learns multiple interests or skills in order to achieve it." Put differently: what are you trying to do with your life, what are you learning along the way, and how can you pass it down? "That's literally your niche." He adds that most big creators, and many small ones making good money, work this way.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

**Generalist** is here a common word with Koe's own meaning: not someone who knows a little of everything, but someone who learns whatever a goal requires. The four steps make visible that the 2023 goal filter and the 2024 generalist definition are the same structure: a goal first, then the interests that serve it, then an audience defined by the shared goal. Chapter 20 develops the generalist in full.

#### The paper map: pillars between point A and point B (2026)

In June 2026, in the same video where he introduced point A, point B and the map (Section 22.1), Koe returns to a planning tool. "Quite literally pull out a piece of paper, draw point A on one side, point B on the other, and then you can start filling in the middle with topics and ideas." The filling has three levels:

- Two to three broad topics, such as health, psychology or business: "these are your content pillars, or your main interests broadened up a bit."
- For each topic, two to three subtopics.
- For each subtopic, two to three pain points, principles, how-tos "or other types of content styles that you've seen."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

The **content pillars** are the topic tree's broad interests under another name, and the descending structure (topics, subtopics, individual pieces of content) is the same. Two things are new. First, the map is drawn between A and B rather than under "you": the organizing principle is the transformation, not the person. This matches the corpus's late shift, traced in Chapter 19, from "you are the niche" toward "your mission is your niche." Second, the lowest level is not "subtopics" but types of content (pain points, principles, how-tos), which brings the planning tool closer to actual posts.

#### From method to option: "just share the ideas that are important to you"

Five months before that video, in January 2026, Koe had relativized the whole family of tools. Others, he says, teach focusing on two to three content pillars, maybe one, broken down into topics and subtopics, and he has taught the same in the past (for example, his "content map," which creates a web of content ideas). "That's very useful, but if you want to avoid all of that, just focus on the ideas that are important to you and share those. That's it. That's your entire content strategy."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

This is a change in Koe's position, and the corpus does not resolve it into a single line. The sequence, as far as the dated passages show, is as follows:

| Date | Position | Source video |
|---|---|---|
| Oct 2022 (repeated Feb 2024, refined Jul 2024) | Domain of Mastery: three interests broken into principles, topics, mentors, connections, problems | One-Person Business Model; The Future Of Work |
| Feb 2023 | Topic tree (formerly content pyramid): you → three interests → topics → subtopics, mastered in 6–12 months; filter interests through the ideal future; avoid hyper-specific interests | How To Write Authentic Content |
| Sep 2023 | A single umbrella (self-improvement) above three interests | How To Reprogram Your Mind |
| Jun 2024 | Two to three intersecting interests; break into subtopics and media ideas | Change Your Life In 365 Hours; Ideas Are The New Oil |
| Sep 2024 | Topic tree mentioned, then simplified into a 60-day plan | The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business |
| Jan 2026 | Pillars and content map are "useful," but optional: "just focus on the ideas that are important to you and share those" | If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years |
| Jun 2026 (June 13) | The paper map with 2–3 pillars, subtopics and pain points between A and B | If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business |
| Jun 2026 (June 28) | "Ignore niche and content pillars" and start from the excavated raw material | How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You |

Two readings are available, and the corpus supports both. On the first, pillars move from method to option: they remain useful scaffolding, but the criterion for selection loosens from a deliberate intersection of interests to the writer's own judgment of importance. In a July 2026 passage, Koe goes so far as to say that if one finds an idea important, "that is all the niching down you need." On the second reading, the two kinds of advice address different writers. The map serves the person who needs structure in order to generate ideas at all; "share what's important to you" serves the person who already has a collection of ideas, a goal, a mission and a formed style. Koe does not state this distinction himself, but the fact that he returns to the paper map in the same month in which he tells people to ignore pillars suggests that he does not regard the two as exclusive. Koe gives two reasons for the simplification. The first is to avoid overcomplicating the start. The second, given in the same January 2026 video, is anthropological and is the stronger of the two: humans are not niche animals. "If a lion were put in Alaska, it would not build shelter and clothing. It would die. A lion belongs in its own niche. We don't belong in one niche. Everyone's telling you to niche down when everyone lives in a separate freaking niche across the world." A lion is adapted to one environment and dies outside it; a human adapts any environment to itself by building tools, so the advice to confine one's content to a narrow niche treats a generalist animal as if it were a specialist one. Applied to what to post, the metaphor removes the need to justify each idea by its fit with a predefined pillar. Chapter 20 (Section 20.2, "Applying the lion to content") develops the metaphor in its several versions and draws out this consequence for writing.

What remains constant across every version is the structure the previous sections established: a goal or mission that gives the lens, a small number of broad interests that give room, and a descent from broad to specific that turns interests into individual pieces of content. The tools differ in how much of that structure they ask the writer to make explicit on paper before writing.

### 22.4 Hunting for Ideas

The previous two sections located the writer's material inside the writer. This one looks outward. A perspective does not produce posts by itself; it needs ideas to work on, and Koe's consistent position is that ideas are not waited for but hunted. The section first describes the hunt as a state of attention (golden nuggets, "modern hunting," the idea one wishes one had written), then as a research practice (studying high-performing and outlier content, swipe files, the archives of other creators), then as a selection criterion (performance and excitement), and finally through a guest's system, Justin Welsh's content matrix, which solves the same problem from a different direction.

#### Golden nugget ideas: capturing what excites you

The earliest version is from October 2022, in a video on "the value creator." Step three of a process for researching one's obsessions is to note down **golden nugget ideas**. If one is researching topics one is genuinely curious about, it should be "near impossible to not write down your discoveries." Koe recommends keeping a running note on one's phone and capturing ideas, anecdotes, stories, even statistics, anything that made one think "wow, that changed my mind" or "aha, that's it." He admits it is hard to put into words, but "when you get that excitement, that's what you write down."

The function of these notes is structural. When writing content, a video or a podcast outline, the notes become **building blocks**: one copies them over and structures an argument around what one wants to talk about. They serve as the foundational ideas of the piece. And the justification is the same as in Section 22.2: what stuck with you will interest someone else.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The passage reappears almost literally in the February 2024 compilation, with a plug for his free creative challenge as the place to keep the running note.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The criterion for capture deserves attention because it is affective, not logical. Koe does not tell the reader to note ideas that are true, useful or important by some external standard; he tells them to note what produced excitement. The feeling is treated as a signal. Chapter 18 described novelty as what catches attention and the "good dopamine hit" of a new idea; here that signal becomes the trigger of a writing practice.

#### Modern hunting

In September 2023 Koe gives the practice a name. When you feel the excitement, "or dopamine," that comes from discovering a novel idea, write it down, refine it and post it to your profile. "This is just one huge content hack in general: sharing what excites you." That is what one is hunting for when one reads: "This is modern hunting," finding something new, being excited, and thinking "I need to share this with someone else," because that is how one attracts people interested in the same things. He states the principle that links capture to value: "The first step in value creation is capturing an idea so that you can make it your own and give it to someone."

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

**Modern hunting** is a metaphor with a specific implication. The hunter does not produce the game; the hunter finds it, takes it and brings it back to share. The writer, likewise, does not need to generate ideas from nothing; the work lies in attention, capture and transformation ("make it your own") before giving. The mention of dopamine should be read as Koe's shorthand for the felt excitement of novelty rather than as a neurochemical claim he supports in this passage; Chapter 11 discusses how he uses dopamine more broadly.

A December 2025 formulation, from a video on articulation, describes the hunt as a deliberate activity for when one has no idea:

> "You need to hunt for them. You need to read old books, go down rabbit holes on a topic, listen to a new podcast, or just sit with your thoughts and follow them until you reach a compelling insight. When you hunt for an idea, you aren't just letting the information go in one ear and out the other. You are listening intently for an idea that you wish you wrote. Then you jot it down so you don't lose it. Then you articulate it in your own words using these frameworks so it takes a new shape."

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The sequence (hunt, recognize, capture, re-articulate) is the complete practice, and its last step points to Chapter 23, where the "frameworks" are discussed.

#### The idea you wish you wrote

The recognition step has its own name in the corpus: **an idea you wish you wrote**. It first appears in September 2023. Koe argues that the feelings produced by meaningful events (excitement, fulfillment, gratitude) "should be seen as an opportunity to document the important parts of your life that you can share with others." When a good book sparks excitement, write it down, "because if you plan on giving any form of value, it's going to be the stuff that you find important in your life." He then describes his own practice: "My best tweets are the things where I read something and I'm like, that's really good, I wish I wrote that, and then I rewrite it." Now he has something to write; he shares it, and it resonates with people similar to him.

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

A February 2024 passage adds the mechanism that makes the practice self-reinforcing. When Koe knows he will teach what he learns, reading changes. He sees an idea in a book and thinks "damn, that's one that I wish I wrote, and I take it": "I need to write content about this because I'm ready to teach it." The general observation is: "My mind takes more care in life when I know that I'm going to... do something with any of the information that goes into my head."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md

**Complementary context:** The observation that people learn better when they expect to teach is known in educational psychology as the "protégé effect." Chapter 14 discusses how Koe uses it in his "build to learn" approach. Here it explains why the intention to publish turns reading into hunting: the expectation of teaching raises the quality of attention.

The phrase "I take it" and "then I rewrite it" raise the question of where hunting ends and copying begins. Koe's answer, spread across this section and the next, is that what is taken is the idea and sometimes the structure, and what must be added is one's own articulation and frame. The December 2025 passage says the idea is articulated "in your own words... so it takes a new shape"; the 2026 example of psychic entropy in Section 22.1 says "I'm not copying the idea one-to-one, I'm applying it to a new domain."

#### "Don't consume, research"

A July 2023 passage generalizes the hunting attitude into a rule for all consumption: **"Don't consume, research."** The best creators, Koe says, consume "from the lens of wanting to learn and apply," not for entertainment, and "this perception shift alone will turn you into an idea generation machine." One reads with the lens of turning the material into one's own, "looking for creative firepower." The coined phrases **idea generation machine** and **creative firepower** name what the consumer becomes once the lens changes.

He adds an emotional dimension: "Fall back in love with learning outside of the traditional education system that made you hate it": books, podcasts, blog posts and newsletters aligned with one's goals. The practical instruction is to use a notebook app or an idea development system (he mentions his free course "Seven Days to Genius Ideas"), because "no, you won't remember them; this step is absolutely critical." One notes the ideas that raise dopamine, rivet attention, change perspective or bring clarity, and then contemplates their angles and how they apply to one's vision, goals and problems. The step closes with a sentence that restates Section 22.1: "You're not writing about topics, you're helping people overcome problems in their life towards their goals." If a spiritual idea helped you build a business, it will help someone else.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The insistence on capture ("you won't remember them") has one declared exception in the corpus. In a March 2026 video on recovering "creative genius," Koe proposes a seven-day protocol in which one does not write ideas down ("if it's important it will come back") and does not share what one creates for 24 hours, and he acknowledges that this is "antithetical to everything that I preach." He limits it explicitly to that protocol, so it does not replace the everyday practice described here; it is noted so that the reader does not take capture as an absolute rule in Koe's own view.

#### Naval: hunt for ideas, share ideas

An August 2024 passage attributes a version of the practice to Naval Ravikant. "Naval treats Twitter as his note-taking platform": he reads a book, finds an idea, and shares it from his own perspective; he finds it impactful, shares it, and those who also find it impactful follow him. Koe draws a definition from the example: "That's what an idea is: an important idea that you find important helps you achieve a goal." And he gives a radical simplification for anyone who does not yet know what goal to help people with: "Just focus on ideas... stop thinking about your niche... get ideas, share them, that's it, that's how you build a business. Simple: hunt for ideas, share ideas, a beautiful flow of information that benefits both yourself and others."

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

The attribution needs care, and the corpus does not settle it. What is clear is that the observation about how Naval uses Twitter is Koe's description of Naval's behavior. The slogan "hunt for ideas, share ideas" sits on the boundary: it comes in the same breath as the description of Naval's practice and summarizes it, so it can be read either as Naval's habit put into words or as Koe's own formula drawn from it. The safest statement is that the phrase is a third-party idea used by Koe, adapted from Naval, and that the application to building a business (and to setting the niche aside) is the part that is plainly Koe's. The passage is also one of the clearest anticipations of the January 2026 simplification discussed at the end of Section 22.3 ("just focus on the ideas that are important to you and share those"): already in 2024, for someone without a defined goal, Koe tells people to stop thinking about the niche and simply circulate ideas they find important.

#### Hunting high-performing ideas

Alongside the hunt for ideas that excite the writer runs a second practice, aimed at ideas that have already proven to interest others. Koe's language for it is deliberately blunt: steal or swipe **high-performing ideas**.

The earliest version is from December 2022, in "The One Person Business Roadmap." Creators are encouraged to steal or swipe high-performing ideas; this is "not bad, just overused," and Koe says he does it all the time. His sources:

1. Go to a channel you aspire to be like and filter its videos by "most popular."
2. Go to Medium and skim the homepage; its algorithm serves content around your interests, with "incredible ideas."
3. Look at top tweets using Twemex or Tweet Hunter, filtering any account's tweets to see its highest-performing topics.

"People sleep on Twitter," he says: a good tweet that went viral is "the perfect newsletter, blog, YouTube headline," or the start of a newsletter, with more value in the newsletter itself. The problem, he adds, is that the practice is overused, and his fix comes in what the Roadmap calls stage three: synthesis.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md

The passage reappears in the February 2024 compilation, with the tool name transcribed differently.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The mention of synthesis matters. In the same Roadmap, Koe warns elsewhere that those who copy without synthesis become commodities and that copying others' ideas without novel perspectives will not last. Stealing is presented as a beginner's stage, to be superseded by the synthesis treated in Chapter 18, not as the destination.

The same Roadmap places the study of high-performing content at the very start of the creator's development. Everyone starts at stage one (assuming no prior business experience), and the first thing to understand is that social media growth is a skill that can be practiced, not luck. One cannot post whatever one feels like, or something because it is clever or artsy; one has to understand what leads to growth. Koe adds a sentence that assigns responsibility: "If you aren't growing, it's not the reader's fault... it's because you lack the ability to make your message understandable to them." The method: filter your favorite channel's videos by most popular and ask why they did best; study top tweets; follow accounts you aspire to be like and see which posts outperform; use Tweet Hunter or Twemex; note common patterns, understand why, and emulate.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md

The principle that failure to grow is the writer's responsibility, not the reader's, returns in Section 22.7 in a stronger form: if nobody is interested in your interests, "it's your fault."

An April 2023 passage lists four sources and names a favorite:

1. Filter your favorite YouTubers' videos by most popular and study the titles.
2. Sign up for Medium, select your interests and study the homepage headlines daily.
3. Read books until a novel idea catches your attention, write it down and use it in writing, videos or speaking.
4. Study top tweets, Koe's favorite, "because tweets are just ideas, right? They're digestible ideas and they're time tested." He uses Tweet Hunter (also his tool for scheduling) to type in accounts he likes and see their top tweets for idea generation, "to make sure that your ideas are validated before you go and write them in your own words."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The phrase "validated before you... write them in your own words" introduces a concept that runs through Koe's later work, **validated content** (or validated ideas): ideas that have already proven to generate engagement, rewritten from one's own perspective. Chapter 26 treats validation as a growth strategy; here it functions as a filter on what to write.

Two more versions add specific techniques. In February 2023, in the "authentic content" video, Koe shows how to study structures without any paid tool: type into Twitter's search "from:[username] min_faves:1000" (his own example is "from:thedankoe min_faves:1000"), which shows only that account's tweets with at least 1,000 likes. Look at "your tribe of mentors," the people you aspire to write like, study their top tweets and how they are structured, then plug similar topics into those structures and rewrite them.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

In September 2024 he lists four ways to turn chosen topics into posts: (1) break them into common pain points and write about how to solve them; (2) research other accounts' top tweets with a tool such as the Tweet Hunter X sidebar (a Chrome extension) or SuperX, and rewrite them from your own perspective, since in one view you see an account's most popular tweets, "pretty much proven to do extremely well if you can get the traffic kickstarted"; (3) read books and use social media as a place to take notes, "but write those notes with a hook, body and conclusion," in your own words (he was then reading *The Systems View of Life* and turning passages into posts); (4) filter favorite YouTube channels by most popular and use their titles as starting points for hooks and posts.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Complementary context:** *The Systems View of Life* (2014) is a book by the physicist Fritjof Capra and the biochemist Pier Luigi Luisi that presents living systems, cognition and society from a systems-theory perspective. Koe cites it as an example of a dense book whose passages can be turned into short posts.

#### The author's own process: from a proven title to an argument

Koe illustrates the method with his own work at two moments four years apart. In October 2022 he describes how he chooses YouTube topics: he watches good YouTube videos, knows what performs well in the algorithm, and takes the title. His example in progress is "The Power of Being Alone," a hot topic he was also writing about in his newsletter. He was listening to videos on being alone to study the topic so he could "come with a credible argument, because I have experience" to back it, but he still needed a way to structure the argument.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The same passage reappears in the February 2024 compilation.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The example shows the division of labor that the rest of this section formalizes: the title (the topic and its packaging) comes from what already performs; the credibility comes from the writer's experience; and the remaining problem, structure, is the subject of Chapter 23.

In July 2026 he describes the origin of a specific video. Its inspiration was a video with about 700,000 views called "The notebook system that saved my brain." He asked: "What about the writing system that saved my brain? The writing habit that saved my brain?" That gave him a good starting point for an idea to write about; the title and thumbnail could be crafted later if a better idea came, "because none of this is guaranteed." The resulting video is the one in which he tells this story.

#### Swipe files and outlier research

From 2025 onward, the practice acquires a vocabulary borrowed from marketing and a set of tools Koe himself builds. A **swipe file** (a common marketing term he adopts) is a collection of high-performing posts, titles and ideas to study and reformulate. In February 2025 he presents building one as the first way to practice structure. "Keep your finger on the pulse of the market": "scroll the timeline as a researcher, not a consumer," "scroll to hunt for ideas." When you see a good post ("damn, I want that under my brand"), copy the link and paste or embed it in a capture tool; at the time, his own product, Kortex, whose mobile version he says aims to be "faster than Apple Notes."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Scroll as a researcher, not a consumer** is the 2025 form of the 2023 "don't consume, research." The same shift of lens is applied to the feed itself, the place where most people consume most passively.

In July 2026 Koe walks through the practice in more detail. Capture is free and can be done anywhere (in notes or any capture place); he shows it in Eden, his newer product. Paste links into a library; on the phone, quickly capture an idea or a social link while scrolling; save videos that are doing well, or ideas worth saving, where you can find them later; "if it disappears, that's fine." Next, use an **outlier tool** (for example 1of10, for what is trending on YouTube, or Eden's discover feed) to research the market. His favorite move: go to a specific creator's account and "filter their content by top liked," especially for YouTube titles and angles people already pay attention to (top videos, posts, notes, articles, tweets, reels, carousels). Then "reverse engineer" it: "I want to recreate this with my own idea."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

A May 2026 passage, inside a pitch for the relaunched Eden, describes his full research workflow:

1. **Boards** where he pastes links to social posts or websites, uploads PDFs, and creates notes or documents to write in, for example a "YouTube swipe file" and a "post swipe file."
2. A **discover feed**: set your content pillars, scroll, filter by platform (only tweets, or only YouTube), and add what catches your eye to a board. "If you don't know what to create next, just scroll through here, add it to a board, and then when it's time to actually create, go to your board, create a note in there, and then start writing."
3. **Search a specific creator** across platforms and filter by top-performing content, including his own: "I can just see... my own good ideas, so I can iterate off of those, and I do that all the time. That's really how I continue growing at this point."
4. **AI over a board**: reference a board in chat and ask for variations, or add a creator's top YouTube videos and ask: "Break all of these down. What is this person's worldview? How does he structure his videos? How can I apply this to the next video?"

He mentions in passing that the old Eden pivoted after they "went broke thanks to AI credits."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Three features of the workflow are worth drawing out. First, the content pillars of Section 22.3 reappear as search filters: the planning tool becomes a research tool. Second, Koe's most valued source by 2026 is his own past outliers: the writer studies their own archive for what has already worked and iterates on it, which connects to "say one thing a thousand different ways" in Section 22.5. Third, the AI questions ask for a creator's worldview and structure, not their sentences, which is consistent with what Koe says may be taken (topics, angles, structures) and what must be one's own (articulation, perspective).

The tools change across the corpus: Tweet Hunter, Twemex and Medium in 2022–2023; SuperX and the Tweet Hunter sidebar in 2024; Kortex (also spelled Cortex) in 2024–2025; Eden and 1of10 in 2026. Several of these are Koe's own products, and their mention often accompanies a promotion. The mechanics, however, do not change: filter a creator's output by what performed best, save it, ask why it worked, and recreate it with one's own idea.

A February 2025 passage gives the research a second target. Once one knows one's topic, research five to ten accounts that talk about it, and notice what content they repeat weekly or monthly and what they all talk about. Make those topics a consistent part of your brand: create multiple free guides around them, create content on one of them at least once a day, and write short, medium and long-form content (posts, threads, carousels, which he glosses as Instagram's swipeable sets of about ten images, and newsletters). The goal is to "build a world that people can sink their teeth into," "your own small-scale Netflix that people can binge watch or binge read."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The **small-scale Netflix** echoes the 2026 image of content as a world and a lore (Section 22.1), and the topics that "they all talk about" are what Section 22.5 calls evergreen ideas.

#### Study what creators did to grow, not what they do now

The study of other creators has a trap, which Koe describes in February 2025. He calls the beginner's predicament "beginner hell," and says it can last three months or thirty years: "that's a starving artist," someone who does their art, never thinks about the business side and cannot sustain their life's work. Creators who never progress try to do what advanced creators are doing now "without realizing that that's not what the advanced creators did." Beginners post philosophical quotes or try to be "the next Marcus Aurelius when nobody knows who they are." His correction is blunt: "You aren't a Roman Emperor; you aren't respected because nobody knows who you are." If social media is one's path to one's life's work, one must build authority, building up to being respected by a group of people.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Starving artist** is a common phrase Koe uses with a specific sense: the creator who refuses the business side of creative work and therefore cannot sustain it. It recurs below in the discussion of performance and excitement.

The remedy, in the same video, is to study creators' archives. You are probably copying "feel-good content" from accounts you follow, but they already have an audience, baseline engagement and recognition. Study what they created in order to grow. On YouTube, filter their profile by "oldest"; look through all of it and find "which one created that spike," because "social media growth comes in waves, it's not just linear." Scroll to the bottom even if it takes an hour, copy the content that started to do well ("that was an anomaly") into a capture tool, and use it as a framework.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The **anomaly** (or outlier) is a piece that performs far above a creator's normal level and causes a growth spike. Studying anomalies in the oldest content corrects the beginner's error: it shows what worked when the creator had no audience, which is the beginner's actual situation.

A June 2024 version frames the same study as becoming an **amalgamation**. To change from consumer to creator, study the ten accounts you follow, enjoy and want to become like, and "become an amalgamation of all of them," taking little pieces from each. Ask why a piece of content did well, why a product sells, why a profile picture works, what their best ideas are and how to write your own version; if they went viral, your spin may too. Crucially, study what they did at the beginning by going back into their archives: were they writing the single sentences that get engagement now, or giving actual value in a different way? People copy what big creators do now without seeing what got them there. "Social media is a skill, not random luck," and people blame luck without spending even an hour studying the skill.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

The earliest and least technical version, from October 2023, says one does not even need a course. Write what you want, talk to whom you want, "get your name in front of people by not being a ghost," and get eyes on your writing so it spreads. For an extra push, look at people you have followed for a long time: observe what they write about, how they write it, who they interact with and what they sell to make a living; piece the dots together and begin emulating them. If you lack an answer, look it up, and accept that you will not get all the answers instantly. "If you know you are meant for more, start acting like it." Koe also calls writing "the fundamental mode of communication that has survived throughout human existence," and a great starting point to discover what one truly wants.

**Source:** If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md

Read together, these passages describe imitation as a learning method and not as a betrayal of authenticity. The corpus records that Koe's position on this moved over time: he first spoke of a "consistent authenticity" that takes thousands of hours, then of "intelligent imitation," and in 2026 he argued against "authenticity bros" that "you imitate because you learn." The change, in the corpus's own summary, is that authenticity stops being the opposite of imitation and becomes imitation in the service of one's core. Section 22.6 returns to authenticity from the side of the reader's experience.

#### Performance and excitement: a balance of art and business

The practices above could suggest that Koe recommends writing only what has already worked. His 2026 formulation corrects that impression with a selection criterion that combines the two kinds of hunting. In January 2026:

> "The goal of curating ideas to include under your brand should fall at the intersection of one, performance, which is ideas that have the potential to do well, and this is a measure of how much other people will care, and two, excitement, which is the ideas that give you a sense of excitement to write about them. This is a measure of how much you care."

"It's a balance of art and business." On the side of performance the questions are: will others care, has this done well before, do I have a good hook? On the side of excitement: what do I care about? "It's not blatantly copying what works. It's doing something unique, but also taking into account the principles that are actually going to capture attention."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

In August 2026 he repeats the criterion as step one of writing a long piece (a Substack article, a YouTube script or an X article; long form is growing, he says, "because they're tired of brainless short-form"). Choose a topic at "the intersection of performance and excitement." Performance means "topics that have already proven to do well": save viral posts or use an outlier research tool to study creators' top posts, "this is just what you do." He supports this with an analogy: do musicians become famous "by just making little sounds, or did they have a structure... 'How will the market respond?'" And he names the failure mode: "A lot of artists skip the business part, and that's why they become starving artists... that is their downfall." Using proven principles "isn't sacrificing your voice... because it still runs through your taste filter." The **taste filter** is the coined term for the writer's judgment through which any borrowed principle passes, which preserves the voice.

His example is the very video: its title includes "how to remember everything you read" because hundreds of videos with that phrase went viral, yet the video argues the opposite. Excitement, in turn, means "topics and ideas that you want to explore more out of curiosity," and the tactic for joining them is: "pair the topic you want to write about that probably wouldn't do well on social media with a topic that has already proven to do well."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

The framework can be summarized:

| | Performance | Excitement |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | How much other people will care | How much you care |
| Questions | Will others care? Has it done well before? Do I have a good hook? | What am I curious about? What do I want to explore? |
| Sources | Viral posts, outlier tools, creators' top content | Interests, curiosity, the idea you wish you wrote |
| Failure if alone | Blatant copying; a voice lost | The starving artist |
| How they join | Pair an unproven personal topic with a proven one; run proven principles through the taste filter | |

This criterion marks a shift in Koe's emphasis. In 2022–2023 the writer's own interest was enough ("post the things that are interesting to you"; elsewhere in 2023, "write about whatever you want in a way that's interesting"). By 2024–2026 he says that if one's goal is income, content should be driven by the offer, and in May 2026 that "the best creators don't just post what's on their mind... that's not a strategy." The intersection of performance and excitement is the synthesis: interest is necessary but not sufficient, and market research is necessary but not sufficient. The reason he gives is that treating social media as one's life's work implies strategy, and that artists who ignore business end up starving. He also acknowledges that he himself sometimes simply posts what he is thinking, so the two attitudes can coexist depending on the goal (expression or income).

#### A guest's system: Justin Welsh's content matrix

A December 2021 interview with Justin Welsh, a former SaaS sales executive who built a one-person business on LinkedIn and Twitter, contains a system that solves the problem of what to write by combination rather than by hunting. The system is Welsh's, not Koe's, and is presented here as a guest's contribution that Koe later cites; Koe adapted several of Welsh's other ideas elsewhere in the corpus (Chapter 19 traces the "niche of one" to him).

Welsh describes the problem plainly: he used to stare at a blank white screen thinking "what do I want to write today?", and "that's never very fun." Needing "ways to ideate rapidly," he built what he calls "the content matrix, for lack of a cooler or better name." Its components:

1. **Rows**: all the topics he normally likes to write about, listed down the left side.
2. **Columns**: his favorite structures across the top: "observations, step-by-step guides, how-tos, analytical, X versus Y, past versus present... listicles."
3. **The session**: once a week, Saturday morning over coffee, "when I'm feeling really energized, I got tons of free time."
4. **Pairing**: "Topic, structure. Pair, pair, pair... quickly, quickly. The idea is to just ideate quickly."
5. **Headlines**: as soon as a pair produces an idea, he writes the headline.
6. **Output**: "10 headlines right away. Now I've got at least 10 starting points."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

A schematic version of the matrix (the specific topics are illustrative, since Welsh does not list his) looks like this:

| Topic \ Structure | Observation | Step-by-step | How-to | Analytical | X vs. Y | Past vs. present | Listicle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Topic 1 | headline? | headline? | … | … | … | … | … |
| Topic 2 | … | … | … | … | … | … | … |
| Topic 3 | … | … | … | … | … | … | … |

The matrix turns ideation into combination: the blank page is replaced by a grid of cells, each of which is a prompt. Welsh notes that this is his system at the "10k and beyond" stage, "more robust and... more systematized" than at the start.

The headlines then enter a production pipeline. Welsh plugs them into tools ("I think it's called Typeform, and there's another, there's Hypefury"), using their "inspiration and templates" to make sure the writing is "formatted correctly." The name "Typeform" is uncertain in his own words, and it is not clear which tool he means beyond Hypefury. He runs the text through the Hemingway app, which tells him "if it's grade one, grade two, grade three," that is, whether sentence structure is clear and easy to read. He loads it back into Hypefury. The target is to "create 10 pieces of content sitting down for 2 hours." And "because I've created 10 pieces of content, I might as well put them everywhere that's relevant": LinkedIn, Twitter (through Hypefury), Instagram posts and his website.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

**Complementary context:** Hemingway is a text editor that estimates the US school grade level needed to read a passage and flags long or complex sentences. Hypefury is a tool for scheduling and templating social media posts. Grade one to three is an unusually low target; it reflects Welsh's priority on readability in a scrolling feed, a theme Chapter 23 develops under legibility.

Two further pieces of Welsh's system concern what happens after publishing. Once he started to stand out, he "started to just do things that I had done in my sales leadership job. Analyze the data. What's working? What isn't working? Double down on what does, cut what doesn't." Through this he learned "about formatting, right? Being compelling, being polarizing." The operator's data loop from SaaS sales transferred to content.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

And he is candid about the motive that sustains the loop: "The interesting thing about getting attention online is that it's addicting. And anyone who says that it isn't is full of [it]." Once you get attention, you want more; to get more, "you got to study more." So his cycle has been "writing, analyzing, studying, writing again, repeat." Koe later echoes the idea when discussing paid ads: after "getting kind of addicted to the growth" on organic platforms, he did not feel he needed ads.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

The content matrix and the data loop complement Koe's own approach rather than repeating it. Koe's hunting starts from the idea and asks how to frame it; Welsh's matrix starts from known topics and known structures and generates ideas by crossing them. Koe's study of outliers looks at other people's data; Welsh's loop looks at one's own. Welsh's frank remark about addiction is also a useful counterweight to the rest of this chapter: the same attention economy that rewards repetition and outliers can capture the writer, a risk Chapter 11 discusses in its own terms.

### 22.5 Reminding More Than Innovating

The hunt for ideas could create an expectation that every piece must be new. Koe argues the opposite, and he does so repeatedly, which is itself an illustration of the argument. The position has four parts: content does not need to reinvent the wheel, because people need reminders more than novelty; every topic has a small set of evergreen ideas that a brand must cover; the best ideas should be said a thousand different ways, because repetition is what changes behavior and refines the idea; and the value of a piece often lies less in its prescription than in the perspective it gives. Each part has a tension in the corpus, and the section presents them with the tensions included.

#### People need to be reminded more than they need something new

The principle appears first in October 2022: in content, one is not trying to reinvent the wheel. Koe asks the writer to be self-aware about the content they themselves like to consume: is it too basic, unique to them, or the same thing week after week? "People need to be reminded more than they need something new." All content is joining in and giving one's own take on the same thing people are saying, and that is what results in growth.

**Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md

The February 2024 compilation repeats the passage and adds a diagnosis. If you are not growing, it is usually because your ego is in the way: overcomplicating, trying to be too fancy or clever, getting angry because you think people are not as intelligent as you. The remedy is to listen to feedback and change how you talk so that it is interesting: "If people aren't interested in your interest, it's because you don't know how to make your interest interesting to that person."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The two halves belong together. The demand for novelty is presented as an ego problem: the writer wants to appear original and clever, while the reader wants to be helped. And the last sentence, **make your interest interesting**, is the thesis of Section 22.7, which shows that the corpus treats repetition and persuasion as parts of one argument.

The saying "people need to be reminded more than they need something new" is not Koe's coinage. In May 2023 he says he used to repeat it often, quoting it without attribution (a later subsection examines what he does with it).

**Complementary context:** The saying is commonly attributed to Samuel Johnson, the eighteenth-century English writer, in the form "men more frequently require to be reminded than informed." Koe does not cite Johnson; the attribution is given here only to situate the phrase.

#### Evergreen ideas and the small-scale book

A February 2025 passage turns the principle into a method for building authority. To become an authority, do not write random ideas at first. Every topic has "a select few evergreen ideas that almost every single brand that talks about a specific topic talks about." Find them and talk about them under your brand, so that followers have a library to dig into and see you as an authority. "Think of it like writing a small-scale book on a specific topic," spread across all your content: the pillars, the fundamentals. In business, these evergreen ideas include how to choose a niche, the best business model for beginners, the best skills to learn, and how to make X money in Y time. If one avoids them to be "unique" and "authentic," without fundamentals, "you're going to fail": art and business must blend.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Evergreen ideas** are the few fundamental topics every brand in a field covers; covering them in depth is writing a **small-scale book**. The image is apt: a book on a subject cannot skip its basic chapters merely because other books have them. The final warning, that avoiding fundamentals in the name of authenticity leads to failure, repeats the "art and business" balance of Section 22.4 from the opposite side: there the danger was copying without voice; here it is voice without fundamentals.

An August 2024 passage gives the same idea a different emphasis. In the second section of his "become the niche" argument, Koe describes education as the way to build authority: "Your job is to educate people on the skills or interests that help them achieve your version of the good life or your philosophy. Literally teach them the skills or interests and how you learned it." And: "You aren't creating anything new. You are simply creating a library of information under your brand. That way people can learn from you."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

The **library of information** and the small-scale book are the same structure seen from two sides: the library is what the audience can browse, the book is what the writer is composing. Both connect to the map and the "small-scale Netflix" of Sections 22.1 and 22.4: content accumulates into a body that can be explored. And both are relieved of the burden of novelty: "you aren't creating anything new."

#### Say one thing a thousand different ways

The strongest form of the argument for repetition is a heuristic Koe says came out of a conversation. In April 2023 he recounts that, over coffee with the YouTuber Clark Kegley, he realized he should lean more into the one-person business and lifestyle design, the topics his audience associated with him. The reasoning used an analogy with music: when a punk artist goes pop, "you're like, I hate this, I want to hear these songs." Hence: "You just say one thing a thousand different ways in the creator economy, and when people change, that's when they die." He adds that repeating the same thing "conditions your mind to see success in that domain."

**Source:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md

The phrase **say one thing a thousand different ways** is Koe's, emerging from that conversation; the corpus does not attribute the formulation to Kegley.

In July 2023 he develops the musical analogy into a warning. A common mistake is that creators change their sound. He likes heavy dubstep (Excision and lesser-known artists); if Excision switched exclusively to drum and bass, or to country, he would lose about 90% of his audience. "You have a creative boundary to operate within based on who you are." Creators are afraid of repeating themselves "when repetition and conditioning is what actually changes people's lives." He explains that he makes many videos on the one-person business because the concept is powerful and one video did not articulate everything; stopping after one would be, in his crude phrase, "blue balling people." He returns to the vocabulary of programming: "you can be programmed into a life that you hate or programmed into a life that you want." And he states the creator's job: "Your job as a creator is to find an angle that resonates with people and milk it for eternity with your creative ability." In his own case, he weaves philosophy, spirituality, metaphysics and self-improvement into all his videos and finds a way to include business.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The **creative boundary** is the coined term for the range defined by who one is, within which one operates. It is not a restriction on interests (Koe weaves many into every video) but on the recognizable "sound" that holds them together. This matters for reconciling repetition with the generalism of Chapter 20: the generalist can talk about many things, but always in one voice and toward one angle.

In December 2025 he returns to the analogy in a polished written form:

> "Why do you listen to your favorite musician? Because they have a specific sound or style that you enjoy. Most of their music sounds the same with slight variations… You can listen to a few seconds of their song and know exactly which artist plays it. If an EDM artist immediately decided to switch to country music, their first track would be horrible, as most first iterations are, and most of their audience would not like it."

The same applies to creators, speakers and writers: "You need to write or speak thousands of times until your best ideas are obvious. By nature, you must repeat yourself because the most important ideas deserve to be repeated. And how else are you going to refine them?"

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

This version adds a reason the earlier ones lacked. In 2023, repetition was justified by the audience (they want the same sound) and by its effect (conditioning changes lives). In 2025, it is also justified by the writer's development: repetition is how ideas are refined, and the first iteration in a new genre "would be horrible." **The most important ideas deserve to be repeated** joins the audience's need, the behavioral effect and the craft into one principle.

A February 2025 passage gives two further reasons with an anecdote. Fundamentals work because "nobody gets sick of the fundamentals," and as the audience grows, the new audience has not seen the old content, so it needs to be "on repeat." Koe recounts that he re-recorded a video on how he made $6.9 million writing two hours a day; a commenter said it was repetitive and unsubscribed. His reaction: "That's fine, that's what you're supposed to do." One is not supposed to keep consuming but to do something with it; repetition signals importance. Others like repetition "because you need it": "that's how you condition your mind to do something different, that's how you change your behavior." He closes with the original saying: "People need to be reminded more than they need something new."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The response to the unsubscribing commenter shows how Koe understands his content: not as entertainment to be consumed indefinitely, but as a conditioning program whose success is measured by the reader leaving to act. A reader who is bored by repetition, on this view, should be acting instead of watching.

#### Five to ten ideas, each turned into a thousand

In January 2026 Koe states the arithmetic of repetition. Watching multiple podcasts of the best podcasters or public speakers, "you see that they're kind of just repeating the same five to 10 ideas, and they're just exposing their best ideas to new audiences." "They've refined their ideas enough to know that these are the ones that are the most impactful." The instruction follows: "You have to experiment and try until you have those five to 10 ideas, and then you have to turn those five to 10 ideas into each a thousand ideas."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The heuristic implies a two-phase process. In the first phase one experiments, writing about many things, until the outliers reveal which ideas are one's most impactful. (This is where the outlier research of Section 22.4, applied to one's own archive, does its work.) In the second phase one exploits those ideas by varying them. The two phases correspond to the excitement and performance axes: excitement drives the experimentation; performance selects the ideas to repeat.

#### Ideas are cheap, articulation is expensive

Later in the same video, Koe makes the variation itself the skill. Step three of his process is: "Learn how to write one idea 1,000 different ways, because that's all this game is." One has five to ten, maybe twenty, best ideas, and one rewrites them "from all different angles." He argues this is more effective in the age of AI: AI can generate all information, and people follow a person precisely because that person does not have all information. One is "niche in spirit compared to AI," even if one talks about many things. And he formulates the principle: "Becoming a good writer or speaker isn't only about the idea, but how you articulate the idea. The idea does a lot of the heavy lifting, but the structure of the idea, how it's articulated, does even more." In short: "Ideas are cheap, but articulation of the ideas is expensive."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

Two coined terms appear here. **Niche in spirit** names what distinguishes a human writer from AI: not the possession of information but a particular, limited articulation of it. **Articulation** is a common word Koe uses with a specific weight: the structuring and phrasing of an idea, which in this formulation outweighs the idea itself.

He immediately gives an example of one idea in two structures:

- Structure one, a post he wrote: "One pattern I've noticed in happy people. They're obsessed about maintaining their mental clarity." It has two parts: a hook in the form of an observation, and the delivery of the observation.
- Structure two, the same idea: "Happy people are clear-minded people. They take time for rest. They focus on one singular goal. They ruthlessly eliminate distractions. In other words, happy people are obsessive about maintaining their mental clarity."

"Same idea, a different structure, and a different impact." He expects the first to do a bit better; the second is not bad, "it just shows that this is a skill." His recommendation while practicing: write every idea you come across in a few different ways and post them.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

The example shows what a "thousand different ways" means in practice: not a thousand ideas but a thousand structures, angles and stories around a few ideas. It also shows why repetition does not bore the audience in the way the commenter feared: a reader who saw the first post may not recognize the second as the same idea, and if they do, they receive it from a new angle. The question of which structures exist and how they work belongs to Chapter 23.

#### "Reminded with something new," and back again

The corpus records a change in how Koe formulates the reminder principle, and then a return. In May 2023 he says he used to say often, quoting without attribution, that "people need to be reminded more than they need [to be taught] something new." He has changed it: "people need to be reminded with something new." That is, reminded of "the fundamentals, the base lessons that are going to actually bring progress to their life, but from a new perspective." The best way to do this is personal experience: "tell a story about the things that you're learning, and then you can never run out of angles": one's own story, perspective and thoughts, other people's stories, stories one curates online.

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

**Reminded with something new** is Koe's adaptation of the saying, and it resolves the apparent conflict between repetition and originality. The fundamentals are repeated; the angle is new; and the inexhaustible source of angles is story, which connects back to Section 22.2 ("your story is your brand"). This is also the formulation that best fits the "one idea a thousand ways" of 2026.

Yet in February 2025, defending repetition after the unsubscribing commenter, Koe cites the original version again: "people need to be reminded more than they need something new." The corpus gives no explanation for the return. The most economical reading is that the two formulations emphasize different things rather than contradict each other: the original defends repetition against the demand for novelty, which is the point Koe is making in 2025; the modified version specifies how to repeat well, which is the point he is making in 2023. Both coexist with his 2026 statement that "nobody needs another person repackaging common sense" (Section 22.2), which sets the limit: the fundamentals may be repeated, but not without a perspective that is genuinely the writer's own.

The broader evolution, as the corpus summarizes it, runs as follows. In 2022 stealing validated ideas was tolerated with a risk of commoditization, to be overcome by synthesis. In 2024 the two sides already sit together in his teaching. On one hand: "take the ideas that already work and post them under your brand." On the other, in a September 2024 method for making one's process unique, the borrowing stops at the topic: choose a pain point or a high-performing topic (a YouTube video doing really well, for example), and then "don't even watch the video." Instead of repeating the simple solution everyone has already given, one thinks through which steps, one to five, actually help people overcome the pain point in a unique way, and then names the process (as Koe did with "the one-person business" or "the 4-hour workday") so that the authority attaches to the writer. By 2025–2026, taking what already works and adding one's twist ("sell what's already selling with your own twist," "validated content is the secret") becomes central, while the demand for an original perspective grows at the same time, including the limit that uniquely coined concepts and categories belong to their authors and copy-pasting them makes one look foolish. In December 2025 Koe states the requirement for the solution part of a piece in so many words. In his "advanced" framework of cross-domain synthesis, which is how he structures most of his newsletters (a problem amplified, then a concept from another of his interests, such as entropy from physics used to explain how distractions work, then a unique process), the final steps "should come from your own contemplation rather than someone else's prescription," not from "I watched this YouTube video... I'm going to take that and put it in mine." The resolution the corpus supports is that the writer may take evergreen and validated topics and structures, but not someone else's wording or coined concepts, and must bring their own angle, process and perspective.

**Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md; How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

#### Perspective over prescription: in defense of "fluff"

The last part of the argument concerns what kind of content does the reminding. Many creators pride themselves on "zero fluff," pure actionable steps. Koe, by his own description, is "very philosophical," and people follow him for that. In March 2023 he criticizes posts such as "I only like to read books that don't have fluff" and declares that he likes fluff. It helps him frame things and provides the nuance and big ideas that the same old actionable advice will not. There are only so many principles; one could learn how to start a billion-dollar company "in like 10 steps," and "most people don't do that because they don't have the fluff that resonated with them; they don't have the why" that made them act. People are stuck in what he calls a **surface level trap**: wanting to know exactly how to do this, this and this, without the vision, the why or the clarity.

**Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md

**Fluff** is a common word that Koe reclaims with a positive sense: the why, the nuance and the big ideas that move a reader to act, as opposed to the step lists that the reader will not follow without a reason.

In August 2024 he explains the length of his own work in these terms. He creates "incredibly long newsletters or incredibly long videos" because he is not trying to narrow the viewer's mind onto one task in a fifteen to twenty minute video; he is giving the mind "ideas to attach to." Ninety percent of viewers will think the little ideas are useless and tell him to "cut the fluff"; the ten percent take the different little ideas and open up room for opportunity. "You come here to learn about reading, but you are completely changing the ideas that your mind operates on." Many of his viewers are writers and creators looking for ideas to build on, which he gives as another reason for Cortex, his tool for writing with connected ideas. He shows a boot camp testimonial as evidence that **multi-dimensional writing**, or interconnected writing (open-mindedness and the making of connections, used to write), is a trainable skill, and he jokes that Alan Watts would be a Cortex customer.

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

The 90/10 split is Koe's estimate of his audience's reactions, not a measured figure. The claim it supports is that long, digressive content works differently from prescriptive content: it changes "the ideas that your mind operates on," the deeper layer from which actions follow. This connects to the model of Part II, where identity was a network of beliefs and goals; content that alters the network can alter many future actions at once.

In June 2023 he gives the same idea a horticultural metaphor. Even if a video made little sense to a viewer's current awareness, being aware puts one "further ahead than 99% of the population." He says he has planted ideas as seeds that grow when the viewer starts pattern-recognizing or connecting them to everyday life: "come back to this in a year." His videos are not hyper-practical prescriptions giving a quick dopamine fix that leads to no progress; they sit in the head, slowly influence action, and compound. "Once you are able to create your own prescription... that's when you see results."

**Source:** Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md

**Ideas as seeds** describes a delayed effect: the idea does not act on reception but on later recognition, when experience supplies the context that makes it meaningful. The aim is not that the reader follow Koe's prescription but that they become able to write their own. The same principle applies to the writer: the December 2025 instruction that a piece's solution "should come from your own contemplation rather than someone else's prescription" (see the previous subsection) is the writer's side of the reader's "create your own prescription."

A January 2023 remark states the same ideal about short content. Reflecting on why he enjoys short videos, he says good content "encourages you; it doesn't give you all of the steps; it encourages you to create them for yourself and think for yourself." He describes his own role modestly: "I am but a gentle reminder along the path."

**Source:** How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md

The phrase "a gentle reminder" closes the loop with the principle that opened this section: if people need reminding more than novelty, the creator's role is not the oracle's but the reminder's.

The value of perspective content also appears in a March 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, at its opening. Koe explains why he values very long-form content: when he is not looking for a specific strategy, he consumes it "to gain perspective, so that I can approach a strategy or, like, generate ideas or set a new goal or just gain clarity." The value of a three-hour conversation is that somewhere inside it there may be "one thing that one person picks up that changes the entire course of their life, even if that thing is insignificant to us right now." Both hosts frame the episode as the kind of content they would have wanted when starting out. Koe's examples are personal. The "golden era" of fitness YouTube, around 2014–2015, with creators such as Matt Ogus, Chris "Yucky" Lovato (of Legends of Aesthetics, around the time Gymshark was starting) and Joe Delaney: his favorite videos were when they got together for Q&As or unfiltered back-and-forth. And Abdul (whose name the transcript renders uncertainly), an endurance cyclist whose philosophical lessons from forty-hour rides, while homeless and scavenging for food, gave Koe insights even though Koe is not a cyclist.

**Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md

The mechanism is the one this whole subsection describes: perspective content does not hand over a tactic; it changes the lens through which tactics and goals are later chosen. The cyclist example makes the point vivid, because the content's topic (endurance cycling) was irrelevant to the listener's goals and yet its perspective was not.

This defense of fluff sits in a documented tension with other passages in the corpus. In 2022 Koe called the how-to the heart of a perspective; in May 2023 he said that "actionable advice is how you build an audience at the start" and warned against being "too clever when nobody knows who you are"; and from 2025 onward he criticizes abstract posts that "sound like a philosophical quote" and do not lead to sales, mocks "fortune cookie tweets" from beginners who "act like you're Marcus Aurelius" (compare "you aren't a Roman Emperor" in Section 22.4), and asks to "make tangibility tangible." The corpus treats this as an unresolved contradiction with two crossing movements: from actionable advice toward novel perspective as the driver of attention (2023–2024), and then from philosophy as a signature toward the demand to tie it to concrete behavior and a problem (2025–2026). The reasons Koe gives differ by period: the tactical material was already documented (2023); the market was saturated with generic advice (2024); abstraction does not sell (2025). A reasonable reconciliation, consistent with what he says, is that the two kinds of content suit different situations: actionable and tangible content for the beginner without reputation and for the short-form feed; fluff and big-picture perspective for long-form, for committed readers and for a creator who has already earned attention. Koe himself does not state that rule, so it should be read as an interpretation of the pattern rather than as his position.

### 22.6 Conviction and Authenticity

The previous sections dealt with the material of writing: perspective, raw material, organized interests, hunted ideas and repeated fundamentals. This section deals with the stance from which that material is expressed. Koe's position has two sides that pull in different directions and that he holds together. One side is conviction: write as if you are right, pick a side, sound confident. The other is authenticity: be a human rather than a search engine, polarize honestly, and make sure what you say matches what you do. The bridge between them is a phrase he uses for both: "firm beliefs held loosely."

#### "You are right about everything at this very point in time"

In a November 2023 video on what he calls the most important high-income skill, Koe presents a set of rules for engaging writing that he calls the Ten Commandments of Engagement (treated as a whole in Chapter 23). He singles out the eighth, **confidence and conviction**, as "by far the most important": one can create highly impactful posts with confidence alone, and confidence lets the other elements of attention fall into place. He states it in an absolute form:

> "You are right about everything at this very point in time in relation to your goals, visions, beliefs, values and everything else that forms your perspective of reality."

The qualification is essential and easy to miss: one is right "at this very point in time" and "in relation to" one's own goals and perspective. Writing, he explains, is painting a picture of one's current perspective. If one is "wishy-washy" in one's beliefs, goals and values, one is probably not progressing, does not trust oneself and is not acting in alignment with one's goals. The writer's job is confidence and conviction in their beliefs, plus a credible or clear argument to back them up if needed.

There is a prerequisite: an **educated perspective**, one's own philosophy or worldview, built through self-education outside school, following leaders and filtering their ideas to see what is true to oneself. He recommends this "just for general quality of life, or to not be a clone of someone else or of society."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The commandment links back to Part I and Part II. To "not be a clone of someone else or of society" is the exit from the Matrix described in Chapter 1, and the "perspective of reality" formed by goals, visions, beliefs and values is the identity model of Chapter 3. Conviction in writing is, on this account, the outward expression of a perspective one has built rather than inherited.

#### Everyone is on social media to be told what to do

The same video argues why conviction serves the reader. "Everyone is on social media to be told what to do in a confident way." Nobody is secure in their actions, choices and beliefs; they look for someone to confirm them confidently and give them the clarity to act. Koe frames the realization as a "life hack": you are uncertain, and so is everyone else, so it is acceptable to be confident. If feedback challenges your worldview and you agree with it, change your view ("okay, I'll do better next time").

He then turns the argument to the writer's own development. Restricting yourself from saying what you believe restricts your progress: you remain uncertain in your actions and will never **fail forward**, never confident enough to make the mistake and learn from it. If you are not confident now, it will be hard to become more confident and learn more later.

The most debatable step comes next. Even if confident content may contain false information, it gives someone the certainty to act; wishy-washy content "isn't doing anything." Direct, exaggerated content gives people the confidence to make mistakes and learn. Someone who blames him for their mistakes, he says, has "a bad outlook on life"; seeing that you made a mistake is "one of the best things you can do."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

"Fail forward" is a common phrase that Koe uses here in a specific sense: to make mistakes confidently in order to learn from them, which is impossible if one never dares to say what one believes. The argument contains a real tension that should be stated plainly. Koe accepts the risk of spreading false information in exchange for giving readers certainty, and places the responsibility for mistakes on the reader. His safeguard, which follows immediately, is a limit on the techniques rather than on the claims.

#### Three tools to sound confident

The techniques are three: eliminate words that imply uncertainty; speak in absolutes when possible; exaggerate your point to add energy. The safeguard: "Do not abuse these for the sake of engagement." His example rewrite: instead of "it may be wise if someone developed their skill set," write "it is crucial that everyone on planet Earth develops their skill set." His comment: "Just by being confident, my words flow clearer and the writing is more impactful."

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

He gives a more extreme example that, he says, "worked time and time again" because it was relatable:

> "How to get ahead of 99% of people: go quiet for 3 months, laser in on one big goal, self-educate like mad, apply everything you learn, fail as much as possible, every month take a break and have some fun. Bursts of intensity are how you blow past everyone else."

Used occasionally, the post brought him followers on all platforms; it became the basis of his most popular YouTube video, and afterward "everyone just swiped the title" to get millions of views ("it's kind of the name of the game").

**Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md

The example illustrates all three tools: the absolute ("99% of people"), the absence of hedging, and the exaggeration ("self-educate like mad," "blow past everyone else"). It also closes a loop with Section 22.4: Koe's own outlier became other creators' swipe-file material, which he treats as normal. In Chapter 23 this kind of claim reappears as a hook; here the point is the stance behind it.

Elsewhere, Koe explains that superlative statements of this kind (for example, his various claims about "the greatest skill of the 21st century") should be read metaphorically, as a way of "getting the point" across, not literally. That remark is a useful key to reading his own confident absolutes throughout the corpus.

#### Pick a side: impactful ideas

A December 2023 video makes the same point in terms of impact. The second element of content, in his list, is **impactful ideas**: "Your posts, sentences, paragraphs and words will not hit if you don't pick a side." In the middle, people may kind of like you, but you will not impact them. He defines impact vividly:

> "Impact equals writing words so powerful that they live in their head rent free; it occupies their attention even when their attention is off of your content."

People tell friends and coworkers about such ideas, Koe says, "because they want to strengthen the shared extreme beliefs that they have to feel validated." "You write with impact when you wholeheartedly pick a side and express your beliefs." The best beliefs to express are "the ones that the average person would think are extreme or crazy," since "we don't want people that are dedicated to being average following us." One's beliefs form the foundation and the perception of one's brand; one should talk about them often and use them to provide perspective, experience and examples. His sample questions for taking a side: Is freelancing the best way for a beginner to make money? Are vegans dumb, and should people eat more animal-based foods? Should people wake up at 3 a.m. and go straight to work?

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

The account of why ideas spread (people repeat them to strengthen shared extreme beliefs and feel validated) is Koe's interpretation of social behavior, offered without evidence beyond his experience; it is a working model of virality, not a finding. It connects to the contrarian spine of Section 22.2: the beliefs the average person finds extreme are the material from which one picks a side.

#### The burden of nuance

In October 2025, introducing his framework Human 3.0, Koe states the cost of conviction openly and accepts it. He will try to make the framework "as non-dogmatic and scientific as possible," but he wants to get the point across with clarity and conviction. "If I just water things down all day with, like, 'oh, here's the science, here's the nuance,' then it starts to lose its impact. So I'm placing the burden of nuance on you, the viewer."

**Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md

**The burden of nuance** names a deliberate division of labor: the writer supplies clarity and conviction; the reader supplies the qualifications. This book, by contrast, tries to carry part of that burden for the reader, which is why it distinguishes throughout between Koe's metaphors, interpretations and claims.

The corpus shows a change of emphasis on this point. In 2023–2025 conviction is a tool of attention: "you are right about everything at this very point in time," and nuance is delegated to the viewer. In an April 2026 video on the essay as a form of thinking (treated in Chapter 21), Koe describes the hardest step of writing an essay as asking "Do I actually believe this?" and tells the writer to "resist acting like you're absolutely right." He does not present this as a correction, and the two positions can apply to different moments: conviction belongs to the published piece; doubt belongs to the process of writing in order to discover what one thinks. Both versions share the willingness to change one's mind when feedback is convincing ("okay, I'll do better next time").

#### Not a search engine with a face on it

The other side of the stance is authenticity, and its starting point is a February 2023 observation: "People want to follow humans; they don't want to follow a search engine with a face on it." With the rise of ChatGPT and future iterations of language AI, Koe argues, authenticity is more important than ever. Everyone is desperate for human connection, and social media "is not delivering on that."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

**A search engine with a face on it** is the coined image for a profile that delivers generic advice. The same video explains the diagnosis. From consulting with many creators, influencers and coaches who use social media as their main traffic source, Koe observes that most are not authentic: their profiles are either all promotions or all actionable advice, "which is good and people tell you to post," but it is "the same [stuff] you could find online... you can type into Google and get the same answer." That will not separate you from the crowd or make you more authoritative than the next person.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The critique of purely actionable profiles fits the defense of fluff in Section 22.5: what cannot be found on Google is the perspective, the story and the beliefs. It also explains why the February 2023 video that contains this diagnosis is the same one that introduces the topic tree with "you" at the top (Section 22.3): authenticity is built into the planning by placing the person, not the topic, at the root.

In February 2025 Koe reads and endorses a tweet by an account he identifies only as "Kevin," titled "thoughts on building a personal brand," which he connects to his own video "The Death of the Personal Brand" and to his one-person business videos. The tweet's points, as Koe relays them, are:

- Over-optimized profiles are "try hard," which is low status.
- Chasing engagement trades off authenticity.
- Writing about others is lame and has no moat. (Koe adds that he sees people write about others instead of their own ideas, interests or skills.)
- Being an expert is overrated; just be yourself in public.
- Being a hypocrite for your team (agreeing with someone you like on something you actually disagree with) makes you a loser.
- Reputation beats reach, every time.
- "Posting should be downstream of doing."
- Most important: if you even try to build a personal brand, you are by definition less authentic than someone who does not try and yet has one.

Koe uses the tweet to frame the difference between creators and AI content: personal brands and influencers shape the trends that AI content follows, but AI "can't replicate that beautiful authenticity."

**Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md

These are a third party's ideas that Koe adopts, and the attribution is limited to a first name. "Posting should be downstream of doing" is the same idea as Koe's own "brand is what you do" (Section 22.2): content follows from activity rather than replacing it. The paradox in the last point (trying to build a brand makes one less authentic) is one Koe accepts without fully resolving; his answer elsewhere is to treat the brand as a byproduct of a mission, a life's work, rather than as an end.

#### Authentic polarization and alignment of values

Authenticity, for Koe, includes polarization. In December 2023, immediately after the principle of picking a side, he gives a ratio: "Your goal is to be 90% liked and 10% disliked." If one does not maintain that polarity with the audience, "like you would in a thriving relationship with a significant other, you give people no reason to actually care about you." He generalizes the image: "You need the push-pull patterns of the universe." The sun rises and sets, waves go up and down, songs have highs and lows; "it's just the nature of reality," and a mind interacting with other minds that share a perspective is no different. "This isn't writing advice, this is spiritual advice." On the craft side, he asks for writing that is raw, punchy and digestible, removing and replacing words that make one sound less confident. And he balances it with openness: write with impact, but be open to changing your mind when someone presents a better perspective in the replies. "Have firm beliefs held loosely."

**Source:** How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md

The **90% liked, 10% disliked** ratio and the **push-pull patterns** are Koe's coinages; the ratio is a rule of thumb, not a measurement. The cosmological image (sunrises, waves) is a metaphor that presents polarity as a natural law; it should be read as a way of making the principle intuitive, not as an argument that it holds. **Firm beliefs held loosely** is a phrase Koe uses in several senses across the corpus (identifying with one's goals but lightly, in 2023; acting with conviction but changing direction given better information, in 2025, adapting Sextus Empiricus); here it means writing with conviction while remaining open to correction. It is the bridge between this section's two halves.

In June 2025 he adds two elements to the picture. **Alignment of values**: when people know who you are, where you came from and what you stand for, they form a deeper relationship with your ideas as a whole. **Authentic polarization**: "If you're liked by everybody, you're liked by nobody. You need to give people reasons to heavily disagree, and thus heavily agree with you." That is "the difficult part of putting yourself out there." He supports it with an observation from his own audience: people who hate him love someone else who says the exact same idea through a different story, beliefs and lens. "It's typically not that you hate or love the specific person... it's that you resonate with them more."

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

The observation about haters is a strong confirmation of Section 22.2's thesis from the opposite direction. If the same idea attracts one reader and repels another depending on the story and lens through which it arrives, then the story and lens are not decoration; they are the variable that decides who resonates. Polarization is therefore not a tactic added to content; it is the inevitable result of a specific perspective, and avoiding it means avoiding specificity.

#### Congruence: when words and actions diverge

A March 2023 passage adds a condition that authenticity imposes on the writer's life rather than on the writing. Koe recommends observing society, "a very good practice," and notes that many public figures in health are not healthy. If someone is unhealthy but talks about balance and their own food philosophy without claiming to prioritize health, their actions align with their words. But if someone has the credentials of a health person and frames themselves that way, so that others "share minds" with them, while their actions do not align, the result is dissonance. Followers' actions will not align with that perspective either, which creates a tension that leaves them lost, negative, overwhelmed and without results. The same happens in business. "This is why I've stopped selling specific courses, because I no longer do them": he tries to make what he talks about and sells match what he does, the goals he pursues and the shared goals with which he attracts people.

**Source:** Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md

The argument has a mechanism that ties it to the rest of the chapter. In Section 22.1 the writer's job was to "program" the audience to adopt a frame of goals and problems. If the writer's own behavior contradicts the frame, the program transmitted is contradictory, and the reader inherits the contradiction as confusion. Congruence is thus not a moral ornament but a condition for the content to work. Koe's decision to stop selling courses on things he no longer does is the practical consequence, and it is consistent with Kevin's "posting should be downstream of doing."

The section's two sides now fit together. Conviction without authenticity becomes the engagement-driven exaggeration Koe warns against ("do not abuse these for the sake of engagement"). Authenticity without conviction becomes the wishy-washy content that "isn't doing anything." The combination he recommends is a perspective one has actually built and actually lives, stated with enough confidence to polarize, and held loosely enough to change when the replies contain a better argument.

### 22.7 Making Your Interests Interesting

The chapter opened with Koe's claim that the niche is a worldview and that the writer's job is to "program the minds" of an audience to adopt it. It closes with the argument that makes this claim more than a slogan. If interests were innate, a writer with unusual interests would have to find the rare people who already share them, and a writer with several interests would have to pick the one with the largest pre-existing market. Koe denies the premise. Interests, he argues, are not innate but persuaded, so they can be created in others; the writer's task is to make an interest interesting; and the commercial form of this task is "customer creation through education." The section follows that chain from the principle to its writing techniques and then to its business consequences.

#### "It's your fault": interest is programmed

The earliest statement of the principle is from January 2023. Creators gloss over the fact that "people can become interested in anything." People tell Koe they do not know whether to write about a weird interest because nobody would care. His answer: "That's not their fault for a lack of understanding of the interest; that's your fault for not being able to make it interesting." He then sets out the chain of reasoning:

1. You were not born with that interest.
2. Gradual steps opened your mind, made you aware of it and made you aware of its importance.
3. So ask why it is important in your life and how you can pass that importance on to someone else.

It is not "one and done" with a single tweet or Instagram post; it is "a 10 year long game of introducing people and educating people on these interests." You become the person who introduces people to these interests and makes them an important part of their lives, because the interests gave you many benefits. "If your words aren't resonating with other people, it's not them, it's you."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The **10 year long game** sets the time scale for the whole project: making an interest interesting is not a single persuasive act but a sustained program of exposure, which is why repetition (Section 22.5) belongs to the same argument.

A July 2023 passage, from a video arguing that "niche down" is terrible advice for smart people, adds that the problem is not the audience but the writer's technique: "It's not that people won't be interested in your other interests"; it is that people "throw all writing advice and any other social media growth advice out the window when it comes time to writing about these things." And it states the reframing: "It's not about finding people that are already interested in it, it's about introducing and making people interested in it by making your interest interesting to other people."

**Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md

In March 2024 the principle receives its sharpest formulation and its slogan. The passage begins with the same answer to "what do I write about, market, sell, email, design?" that Section 22.2 quoted from a later video: the interest you cannot help telling others about, the books you cannot put down, the ideas flooding your search history ("not those ones," he jokes), the projects you dream of building but cannot find time for. Then: "You don't find a profitable niche, you create one through persuasion." Nobody can tell you which niche to choose; they can only tell you the one they went into. If you understood the future-proof skills and human nature, you would understand that "you control the perception of your interest." The core claim:

> "Interest is generated, interest is programmed": you are interested in things because of how you were raised and the information you were exposed to, so others can become interested through well-placed writing on social media.

It has happened to you, Koe says: scroll and see something change your behavior. You spend time, attention and money on your interests, so others will spend them on you if you are valuable enough. "Free people don't find a niche, they create one."

**Source:** The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md

A July 2024 version restates the claim in two compact passages. The first gives the full definition of the method: "You don't find a profitable niche, you create a profitable niche through persuasion. You write persuasive arguments for why your interests benefit others' lives, you sell a product that aligns with that interest, you deliver both through technical skills like social media, email and design." And: "If you understood the future-proof skills, and therefore human nature, you would understand that you can control the perception of your interests."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The second adds the mechanism and an example. "Interest is generated, interest is programmed. You're interested in specific things because of how you were raised and the information you were exposed to; you're interested in them for a reason. That means others can become interested in them with well-placed writing on social media." "This has happened to you before. Go scroll the timeline and tell me something doesn't persuade you to change your behavior." If you have a weight problem and the right post about fitness reaches you, you become interested in fitness. The method: "You generate interest by targeting problems people are facing in their lives and introducing them to potential solutions that you've done yourself." And again: people spend time, attention and money on their interests, so they will spend them on the one "showing the interest or teaching about the interests." The slogan this time is "free people don't niche down."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The coined phrases **interest is generated / interest is programmed** and **free people don't niche down** (in its March 2024 variant, "free people don't find a niche, they create one") carry the whole argument. Two connections deserve emphasis. First, this is the positive reversal of Part I. There, programming was what the Matrix did to people: conditioning them into goals and desires they did not choose. Here Koe applies the same mechanism to the writer's advantage, and the theory is identical: interests are the product of exposure. The ethical question this raises (what distinguishes persuading people into an interest from programming them) is not addressed directly in these passages; the implicit answer, developed in Section 22.6, is congruence and genuine benefit: one introduces people to interests that "gave you many benefits," and the method targets "problems people are facing in their lives" with "solutions that you've done yourself." Second, the claim depends on Section 22.2: the interests one can make interesting are the ones whose importance one has lived, because the material of persuasion is the writer's own story of becoming interested.

August 2024 adds a sarcastic restatement aimed at the niche-down orthodoxy: "People have multiple interests. Insane." "People can adopt new interests. Oh my god." And the method: "You can make your interests interesting so people become interested in them. You do this by highlighting the benefits of achieving the goals of your brand and making people aware of how to solve their pain points with your skills and interests."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

A May 2024 formulation extends the principle to the generalist's breadth. People are interested in more than one thing, and they can become interested in more than one if one is persuasive enough and knows how to build an audience. If one believes success requires talking about a single skill, "you don't know how to build an audience at all and you need to study that."

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

In the same May 2024 video, immediately after the four-step post method of Section 22.1, Koe explains why the method focuses on illustrating the why: people "why a lot and experiment very little." (The phrase appears to mean that people need reasons before they will try anything; the transcript gives no further gloss.) When people ask how to talk about interests nobody cares about: "That's not their fault, it's yours. They're not interested in your interest because you're not making them interesting." If you became interested, there is a series of thoughts and ideas that can make the interest important to someone else: "You weren't born with that interest; you were programmed into it. You were persuaded of its importance and adopted it to help you achieve your goals." And the general conclusion: "The why... is what makes all content good."

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

#### The compelling why

This last claim turns the principle into a writing technique. The **why** (or **compelling why**) is Koe's term for the illustrated reason a reader should change their behavior, and he treats it as the core of good content.

The fullest statement is from March 2026:

> "The biggest problem beginners make is not illustrating the importance of the idea they are trying to convey. They have interesting ideas, but they can't make it interesting to other people."

"To make an idea interesting to someone else, you need to provide a compelling why. You need to give them a reason to change their behavior, because if you're the one who changes their behavior, they will remember you as the person who, quote unquote, changed their life, and they will start to trust you over any human or AI." The formula: "The secret to writing content is to (1) have a good idea and (2) have a pain point it solves or a benefit it gives, and you need to illustrate that reason well."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

The 2026 version adds an argument about trust in the age of AI: the writer who changes a reader's behavior becomes the trusted source, ahead of other humans and of AI. This is the same competitive logic as "niche in spirit" in Section 22.5: what AI cannot easily replace is the relationship formed when a specific person changed one's life.

In the same video Koe moves the why earlier in the process, to the moment of capture. "If you're reading something and you have an idea, or you're reflecting on your life and you have an idea that you know you want to post... that's kind of how creators act: they're just constantly turning ideas into something that they can post. It's just automatic for them to have an idea and immediately write it down. But what you do during that step as well is think of what's a pain point or a benefit of this that helps other people or that people actually want, and then you have to bake that into the idea."

**Source:** How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md

This modifies the capture practice of Section 22.4. In 2022, the criterion for capture was the writer's own excitement. In 2026, capture includes a second question asked on the spot: what pain point or benefit does this idea offer someone else? The golden nugget is no longer noted raw; the why is "baked in" from the start.

The technique has an earlier and more concrete form. In February 2023 Koe presents three post styles for building authority (actionable principles, the importance of a topic, and calling out common mistakes); the second, **importance of a topic**, is the operational form of the why. It resembles actionable principles but illustrates the why, giving people a reason to become interested. "This is where most people suffer," because they do not know how to make their interests interesting to others; continually illustrating importance is what really changes human behavior. His example tweet:

> "Marketing is the greatest skill you can learn. Why? It pairs with any other skill / it teaches you human psychology / it helps you monetize a niche interest. Only a fool expects to make an income without learning the skill that turns scrollers into customers."

The structure is a hook, three lines of importance, and a conclusion.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

In the same passage he supports the claim that the why changes behavior with a personal story. He did not go on walks, even though he knew the benefits and many people had told him about them. When he was trying to cut down for the summer, he came across the right why: he did not need twenty minutes of intense cardio; he could simply walk thirty minutes twice a day. He kept walking, learned that it is "kind of a game," started to enjoy it, developed a philosophical sense of mastery behind it, and now it is part of his day.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The story is an anecdote, not evidence for a general law, but it isolates the mechanism precisely. Knowing the benefits did not change his behavior; a reason that fit his current goal (cutting for the summer) and removed an obstacle (intense cardio) did. A "why," in Koe's sense, is not a list of benefits but a benefit illustrated through the reader's own goal, which is the definition of value in Section 22.1 applied to persuasion.

He also analyzes a third party's post as a case. A tweet by Taylin Simmons reads: "Few habits will make you feel as good as daily yoga. A daily practice will: calm your mind, increase your flexibility, teach you to be present, give you kinky sex positions to try. Try it — within 30 days you'll feel 10 years younger." Koe's reading: yoga is an interest some people may not find interesting, and the post introduces its importance. The **curveball** (the sex positions) makes people rethink trying yoga and at least "puts it on their radar," which he relates to the stages of customer awareness. As more content comes from the author, they are introduced to yoga and may buy a tutorial, a product or a routine. The post also leverages benefits: many people want to feel ten years younger.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

**Curveball** is a common word with Koe's sense: an unexpected element that places an interest on the reader's radar. The case shows that making an interest interesting is not only argument (three reasons) but also surprise; the curveball breaks the reader's pattern of dismissing the topic.

**Complementary context:** The "stages of customer awareness" refers to Eugene Schwartz's levels of awareness in *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), which classify prospects from completely unaware of their problem to fully aware of a product. Koe relies on this framework throughout his marketing material; Chapter 32 treats it.

#### Broad and beginner

If making an interest interesting is the challenge, how should a writer begin? Koe's answer is consistent from 2023: start broad and at beginner level.

In January 2023 he says anyone can become interested in fitness "if it is the right tweet," and "that's the entire challenge: how do I introduce you to the importance of a topic." A post such as "10 reasons you need to get into the gym" applies to anyone; if the reasons are good, it is shareable and people follow. The same works for business: "here's 10 reasons to start a one-person business." One has to start broad and at beginner level, because that is top-of-funnel social media, and that is how one grows.

**Source:** The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md

In the same month he answers the fear that adding a new interest will hurt engagement ("what if it doesn't get good engagement?") by doing his own Q&A. Low engagement on a post about a new interest is not because the audience dislikes fitness; it is because the writer did not make it interesting. They did not start **broad and beginner** and introduce the topic's importance before diving into "advanced stuff nobody cares about." Going broad and beginner still attracts advanced people: even if one's offer is advanced and one must educate the audience up to it, advanced people follow beginner advice because they know how important the fundamentals are. Koe gives himself as an example: he considers himself advanced in fitness, yet the people he follows talk about diet fundamentals and remind him. "Most people don't need something new; they need to be reminded of what works."

**Source:** The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md

The last sentence joins this section to Section 22.5 explicitly: the reminder principle and the broad-and-beginner principle are the same principle applied to two different problems (what to repeat, and how to introduce). It also qualifies, in Koe's own words, the role of novelty: novelty catches attention (Chapter 18), but most people need reminders more than novelty.

In August 2024 he warns against a rationalization that blocks beginner content: "Don't fall into the trap of 'they can just learn this information somewhere else online.' You have to assume they don't have the drive to learn elsewhere and that you have to give them the information. Also realize the massive difference between organic content and intentional searches. People on social media aren't actively looking for education. No, they don't already know it. No, they can't just search for it, because they don't have a reason to. Show them that you are valuable enough to follow and they will solely learn from you."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

The distinction between **organic content** and **intentional searches** explains why the why is necessary. A person searching Google for "how to lift weights" already has the interest; a person scrolling a feed does not. The writer's job on social media is to supply the reason to search, which is exactly what making an interest interesting means.

#### Viewers, fans, super fans

The same August 2024 video describes what happens to an audience when a writer talks about several interests well. Highlighting benefits and pain points, Koe says, "is what turns viewers into fans into super fans." His illustration is Dwayne "The Rock" Johnson:

- Someone watches one of his movies and follows him: they are a **viewer**, attached by one interest.
- He posts about fitness and nutrition, sharing another interest of theirs or introducing them to something new: they become a **fan**.
- They discover the beliefs and values that compose his mindset, such as gratitude and hard work: they become a **super fan**.

"Talking about more than one interest is how you become irreplaceable."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

The progression maps onto the layers of raw material this chapter has described. The first interest is the entry point (often an evergreen or validated topic, as in Section 22.4); the second interest deepens the relationship (the generalist's breadth, Section 22.3); and the beliefs and values make the relationship durable (the contrarian spine, the story and the alignment of values of Sections 22.2 and 22.6).

He also argues the converse, "flipping this on its head": what happens if one talks only about the interests or skills that make money? Three things follow. First, low engagement: "If your content doesn't get shared, how are you going to grow so that you have more people to promote to?" Second, no trust or authority: "Stop thinking like you have to monetize right now and start thinking that you're going to monetize in 12 months, and you'll monetize faster now by doing so." Third, people do not like you. And if one ever wants to pivot and sell something else, one cannot: "You're trapped in whatever niche you always sell to."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

The paradox in the second point ("think you'll monetize in 12 months, and you'll monetize faster now") is characteristic: the patient strategy is presented as the faster one because it builds the trust that the impatient one destroys. Chapter 19 made a related point about broad content and specific products.

#### Customer creation through education

The final step takes the argument from audience to business. If interests are persuaded, then customers, whose desires are also interests, can be created rather than found. Koe's term is **customer creation through education**.

He introduces it in September 2023. Almost every business can benefit from adding a layer of education, so that it can "create customers — remember that, create customers." When one prioritizes education in one's brand through courses, content, cohorts and coaching (all optional), "you unlock the ability to create your own customers." The reason: "Humans are learning machines." Why does a starving market desire what it desires and pull out the credit card, and why is one told to niche down? "Because of social conditioning, learning and education." One targets a specific person "because they are educated to have that identity before they even come across your brand." People are educated to a specific point of wanting to buy something.

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

He then contrasts two marketing strategies. Most marketers "incept someone at a specific level of education," capturing people who have already been educated by others to want a specific thing. The alternative is "attracting a much broader market at the bottom and educating them into the specific and hopefully holistic and purposeful worldview" one puts across to sell products. The second approach extends the journey and the nurturing of the customer or reader and changes their life at a much deeper level, "because you're guiding them through the entire actualization journey." The mechanism runs through identity: a person's identity shapes what they desire, through whom they surround themselves with and what they see as opportunity. "When you create identities through education by leading people towards your vision through your narrative, people grow to desire the product that will help them reach that goal."

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

The coined terms are **incepting** (capturing someone at a given level of education rather than educating from the bottom) and the **actualization journey** (the full path along which a customer is guided as they are educated into a worldview). The passage is the clearest junction in the corpus between the identity model of Part II and the business model of Part X: desire follows identity, identity is formed by education, so a writer who educates is forming the identities that will desire the product.

**Complementary context:** "Incept" alludes to the film *Inception* (2010), in which an idea is planted in a sleeping mind. Koe uses the verb loosely, for meeting a prospect at an already-formed stage of desire.

In June 2024 he applies the concept to lead magnets (free products). Their first purpose is "customer creation": "Your job is to educate your audience to the point of becoming customers for your other products." This, he says, "goes over most people's heads": it is not direct response or paid ads; on social media, content and free products literally educate the audience into customers. His own case: he teaches writing, so on social media he talks about the importance of writing, about the mindset of changing one's life and improving life in general, and then presents writing as a way to do that; people like it because it ticks all the benefit boxes.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

The distinction from direct response matters for Koe's trajectory. The corpus records that he learned direct response marketing early, later kept its psychological principles while abandoning its tactics (countdown timers, exaggerated promises), and by 2024 said customer creation "isn't direct response." Education replaces pressure as the means of persuasion.

In August 2024, answering the objection that followers are "not hot leads," he gives two further answers. First: "They're not supposed to buy from you right away. That's the entire purpose of writing content. You help them go from beginner content to advanced product with time. You create customers by raising them up the levels of awareness. You attract with social media, educate with newsletters, emails or free downloads, and help them implement with your product or service." Second: "Nobody is a useless follower. You are just so narrow-minded and indoctrinated with outdated business dogma that you don't understand that people can learn something new that improves their life." One can read a post and become interested in a new topic. "You aren't just born with specific interests. You are persuaded of their importance," as lifting weights can give confidence, improve health and make one look good, so that one slowly adopts it as an interest and becomes part of that niche. "Your job is to argue why your interests are valuable to adopt. All of your content is persuasive arguments," just as that very video argues that "you are the niche": "You aren't targeting a niche. You are a niche, and your job is to persuade people to join it."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

**Persuasive arguments** is the coined name for what all content is, on this view. The sequence attract, educate, implement (social media, newsletter or free downloads, product) is the same triad as in Section 22.1 (attract to the goals, help with content, implement with the product), now laid out across channels. The last sentence also closes the circle with the chapter's opening definition: the niche is not a group of people one targets but a worldview one invites people to join.

In October 2024 Koe names the principle in a single phrase: **education is the new marketing**. One cannot write or sell whatever one wants, he says, if one does not understand how becoming interested works. "People aren't just born interested in something; they're slowly exposed to ideas from beginner to advanced." The sequence of exposure has three steps: first, why they should care; second, how a pain point or problem affects their life; third, a solution or goal that creates a desire to change. People do not find one's content interesting because one does not talk in problems, goals, examples, benefits, processes and concepts. When starting a new topic, then, establish interest by writing about why it is important to your brand's vision; making people aware of pain points; giving actionable steps to overcome them; creating a free guide that covers all of that; and turning parts of the guide into posts, threads, newsletters and videos.

**Source:** The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md

This passage is the most procedural version of the whole section, and it shows how the parts fit: the why (importance to the vision), the pain point, the actionable step, the free guide (the education layer) and the repurposing (Chapter 24).

In September 2025 Koe applies the logic to a type of creator he calls "brilliant nobodies," intelligent people who disdain simple content. They do not understand that "shallow content has a place." There are levels of development: "you don't start out this hyper-intellectual," and 99% of social media users are not. "You have the value to offer, but you need to get on their level in order to lead them to the value." "The shallow content has the point of educating people to the point of receiving the depth or wisdom or value." And a sales funnel, which such creators refuse to study because it seems sleazy, "is an education funnel": the process of getting people to understand, or be ready to fully benefit from, one's product.

**Source:** The Death Of Social Media (& The Future Of Content Creation).md

The **education funnel** rehabilitates a concept Koe had earlier rejected. In a passage outside this chapter he urged creators to "build a world, not a funnel"; here he accepts the funnel when it is understood as an educational process rather than a manipulative one. The two positions can coexist: he rejects the funnel as a pressure device and accepts it as a sequence of learning. The figure of 99% of users not being "hyper-intellectual" belongs to a family of estimates in the corpus (90% or 95% of the market are beginners) whose exact value varies but whose point does not: most of the audience is at the beginning, and content must start there.

The economic argument behind customer creation appears in December 2022, in "The One Person Business Roadmap," and is repeated in the February 2024 compilation. In about three years in the creator game (after freelancing, which gave him a head start), Koe has seen small and large accounts fall off, small accounts making $100,000 a month, and huge accounts making under $1,000 a month. People see one case or the other and conclude that the opposite is bad. The classic advice is not to aim for followers or fast growth, because generic followers cannot be monetized. Koe disagrees: "If you understood marketing to any extent, you can raise the awareness level of that audience to the point of being a buyer." Staying small, in a cycle of manual outreach or in a client-acquisition system outside what one has built, is not sustainable. The aim is to grow and evolve until one controls one's time, income and projects while providing value, without getting trapped in one stage. "Social media is a skill," not luck. And one can build an **offer ladder**, a ladder of valuable products and services, preferably around what one enjoys, while decreasing the time spent fulfilling them.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The figures are Koe's observations of accounts he has seen, not a survey. The argument they support is that audience size and monetization are not in tension once one can educate an audience up the levels of awareness; the generic follower is a future customer at an earlier stage.

The most recent statement, from August 2026, connects customer creation back to the model of the mind from Part III. "In order to fix this or... course correct, you need the awareness of the problem." If the video registers, Koe tells his viewers, "a new goal is starting to form in your head, and your behavior and learning are starting to pick up and notice opportunities in that direction. And by simply having awareness that this could be a problem, that's going to direct your behavior." He adds: "This is largely marketing as well. This is a better form of marketing," based on a deep understanding of psychology, learning and behavior, "not the surface-level psychology that goes viral on Instagram." He announces a future video on what he calls **anti-marketing**.

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

The passage shows the full mechanism the chapter has been building. Chapter 6 described the mind as a goal-seeking system that notices what is relevant to its goals. Content that makes a reader aware of a problem plants a goal; the goal reorganizes the reader's attention; the reader then notices opportunities, including the writer's product, in that direction. Writing about one's interests, framed through a goal and illustrated with a compelling why, is therefore not a preliminary to marketing. On Koe's account, it is marketing in its "better form," and it is the same act as the "programming" of a worldview with which this chapter began.

What remains is the question of form: how an idea, once chosen, framed and given its why, is structured so that a reader actually reads it. That is the subject of Chapter 23.

### Exercises

1. **Apply the definition of value.** Take three ideas you have recently found interesting (from a book, a conversation or your own experience). For each, write one sentence stating the goal or problem through which you would frame it, in the way Koe frames psychic entropy through "becoming future-proof." Then identify which of the three would lose the most if written without the frame, and explain why.

2. **Excavate your raw material.** Answer in writing the three excavation questions and the three contrarian-spine questions of Section 22.2. Choose the answer that "felt best" and draft the first post it suggests. Then reflect: which of the six answers was hardest to produce, and what does that difficulty suggest about where your material is buried?

3. **Compare two planning tools.** Build a topic tree for yourself (you at the top, three broadened interests, topics, subtopics) and a paper map from point A to point B (two to three pillars, subtopics, pain points). Which of the two generates more usable post ideas for you, and which is closer to your actual mission? Use your answer to take a position on Koe's January 2026 claim that you could skip both and "just focus on the ideas that are important to you."

4. **Diagnose an outlier.** Choose a creator you admire and filter their content first by "most popular" and then by "oldest." Identify one anomaly that appears to have caused a growth spike.
