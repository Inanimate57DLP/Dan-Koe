## Chapter 22 — What to Write: Ideas, Raw Material and Perspective

Chapter 21 established why writing is the base skill: it is the foundation of online media, the most accessible form of leverage, the apparatus with which a mind thinks, and the daily practice that turns a life into a public body of work. That argument leaves the most common question a beginner asks Koe unanswered. In his own recurring phrasing, the question is "Dan, what do I write about?" This chapter collects his answers to it across five years. They are numerous, they overlap and they do not always agree, but underneath them runs a consistent logic: the material of writing is not a topic chosen from a market list but a perspective, and a perspective is made of a goal, the problems one has lived through on the way to it, the beliefs formed in the process and the ideas one has hunted down and made one's own.

The chapter proceeds in seven steps. Section 22.1 fixes the criterion of value that governs everything else: "value is ideas written from the lens of a specific goal or problem," and the niche is not a market but "a perspective or worldview." Section 22.2 turns inward to the writer's raw material: the topic one cannot stop talking about, the excavation questions, the "contrarian spine," and the thesis that one's story is one's brand. Section 22.3 presents the tools Koe has used to organize interests into something that can be written about for months: the topic tree, the Domain of Mastery, content pillars and the paper map from point A to point B, together with his later doubts about all of them. Section 22.4 describes the hunt for ideas: golden nuggets, "modern hunting," the idea "you wish you wrote," the study of high-performing and outlier content, the balance of performance and excitement, and a guest's system, Justin Welsh's content matrix. Section 22.5 defends repetition against the demand for novelty: "people need to be reminded more than they need something new," the evergreen "small-scale book," saying one thing a thousand ways, and the value of perspective over prescription. Section 22.6 addresses the stance from which one writes: confidence and conviction, picking a side, authentic polarization and congruence. Section 22.7 closes with the claim that makes the whole chapter coherent: interests are not innate but persuaded, so the writer's task is to make an interest interesting, which is also how customers are created through education.

The chapter depends directly on Chapter 19 (you are the niche), whose thesis it converts into a writing practice, and on Chapter 20 (the generalist), whose multiple interests it organizes. It also depends on the model of the mind built in Parts II and III: the goal as a lens (Chapter 6), identity as a set of beliefs that can be reprogrammed (Chapters 3 and 4), and the idea of the "program" or conditioning (Chapter 1), which here returns in a positive form, as the writer's ability to program interest in others. It prepares Chapter 23, which deals with how the material chosen here is structured to hold attention, and Chapter 24, which deals with the weekly process and the content ecosystem.

### 22.1 Value Is Ideas Through the Lens of a Goal

#### Content is limited to value, not to topic

The foundational answer to "what do I write about?" is a definition, and it arrives before any technique. In a March 2023 video, Koe tells his audience that they can pivot whenever they like, because their content "shouldn't be limited to a topic or niche; it's limited to value." He then defines the word: "value is ideas written from the lens of a specific goal or problem," regardless of topic.

The definition does two things at once. First, it demotes the topic. Topics, in this view, are useful only for idea generation: if one writes about self-improvement, business and spirituality, those labels tell one where to look for material, but they are not what the reader is buying. "In essence nobody cares about the topic," Koe says; people care about how anything and everything will benefit their life, because "we are selfish creatures." Second, the definition promotes the lens. An idea becomes valuable when it is written through a goal (something the reader wants to achieve) or a problem (something the reader wants to escape). The same idea about, say, the psychology of habits can be worthless as abstract information and valuable when it is shown as a way to reach a goal the reader already holds.

**Source:** The Best Online Business To Make $1 Million In 3-5 Years.md

This definition is one of several Koe gives of "value" across the corpus, and it is worth situating it among them. Elsewhere (in material treated in Part XI) he defines value as "the relationship between what you do, why you do it, how you do it and who benefits," as "positive behavior change," and as "structured communication or sense making to the mind." The 2023 formulation used here is the one specific to content: it tells the writer what to do with an idea before publishing it. It also connects back to Chapter 6, where the goal was presented as the lens through which the mind filters reality. Here the same lens is turned outward: the writer filters ideas through a goal so that the reader can see them as relevant to their own goals.

#### Point A and point B: content as programming a video game

In a June 2026 video aimed at people with multiple interests, Koe gives the definition a spatial form through a metaphor he uses as an explanatory device:

> "You treat content like you're the programmer of a video game. You have point A, where your audience is now, level one, and then you have point B, where you can help them get to, which can be level 100, or 50, or 10, or even two, just one level ahead of them."

The criterion that follows is permissive by design: "Any idea or content is free game if it lands between your point A and point B." A writer with ten interests does not need to decide which of them is "the niche." The question is only whether a given idea helps move a reader from where they are toward where the writer is leading them.

The metaphor carries a second point about the writer's own position. Koe adds that "even if you aren't level 100, you're allowed to grow. You should grow. Actually, you're the leader. That's why you attract followers... because entrepreneurship is self-improvement in disguise." The coined phrase **"self-improvement in disguise"** condenses an argument developed in Part X: building a one-person business forces the builder to develop, so the writer is not a finished expert dispensing knowledge from level 100 but someone a few levels ahead who keeps climbing in public. This anticipates the advice in Section 22.2 to write "from your current position."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

#### The map and the world

In the same video, immediately before the video-game metaphor, Koe adds a second image. "If your brand is the transformation, your content is the map that is built over time. It's the world." He compares it to the Marvel Cinematic Universe: Iron Man, the Avengers, Spider-Man, "built up over time in this like bingeable fashion," with spin-offs, films and action figures, "an entire lore behind it. That's kind of what you're building over time."

The two images fit together. Point A and point B define the transformation (the brand); the content is everything placed between them, and over time it accumulates into a navigable territory that a newcomer can explore from many entry points. The emphasis on the "bingeable" quality recurs elsewhere in the corpus: in 2025 Koe speaks of building "your own small-scale Netflix that people can binge watch or binge read" (Section 22.4). The practical consequence is that a single piece of content is never judged alone; it is judged as a location on a map that is still being drawn.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

#### Frame the idea through the mission

The definition of value becomes a working method when the "goal" is specified as the writer's mission. Koe describes his own: his brand "is to help people become future-proof... That's my niche, so to speak." His content goal is to find great ideas he wants to talk about "and just frame them through the lens of becoming future-proof." He explains that the very video in which he says this was born from such a question: faced with the topic of multiple interests, he asked "how does that help you become future-proof? And this video was born."

He then gives a worked example. Studying the psychology of flow, he finds an idea he loves: "how psychic entropy causes the mind to slowly become disordered with time unless effort is put into maintaining a meaningful hierarchy of goals." He asks: "How does this apply to becoming future-proof?" And he draws the conclusion:

> "Through that question alone, I now have a unique piece of content. I'm not copying the idea one-to-one, I'm applying it to a new domain from my own voice and perspective. And that is content creation in a nutshell for people who don't want to dance in front of a camera or be on TikTok."

The coined instruction is **"you frame the idea through the mission."** The method has three parts: hunt for ideas in one's interests (Section 22.4 develops this), have a mission, and tie the two together, checking against the map how the idea helps people get from A to B.

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

**Complementary context:** "Psychic entropy" is a concept from Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's work on flow, where it names disorder in consciousness caused by information that conflicts with one's goals. Koe introduces it here without naming the author, as "flow psychology"; Chapter 5 of this book treats the concept and its sources in detail. The example is useful precisely because it shows a borrowed idea becoming original by being transposed into a new domain, which is the same mechanism Chapter 18 described as synthesis.

#### Filter it through your big goal: a post in four steps

An earlier version of the same method, from a May 2024 video on the generalist, is more procedural. Koe gives four steps for writing a post:

1. Make a list of content ideas based on your interests.
2. Focus on the how and the why.
3. Write the post.
4. **Filter it through your big goal.**

The fourth step carries the nuance. One does not need to make the goal explicit ("hey, we're achieving this goal together"); people infer it. The writer should "just write good content people want to read," and a small addition of the big goal is what makes the post impactful and desirable enough to follow.

His example illustrates the mechanics. Take a psychology topic: how do I manage my emotions? The "how" yields a list of steps; the "why" yields compelling reasons drawn from experience; the post is then framed through financial freedom. The resulting post reads roughly: become aware of negative reactions, pause before acting, stay quiet or make a better decision; repeating this reprograms your mind to be less negative; and "life, money and relationships will flow with ease." The last clause is the filter: a post about emotional regulation becomes, by one added line, a post about the goal the brand pursues.

**Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md

The 2024 "filter it through your big goal" and the 2026 "frame the idea through the mission" are the same operation described two years apart; the later version names the goal more precisely (a mission rather than a "big goal") and attaches it to the explicit map of point A and point B. The step "focus on the how and the why" also anticipates Section 22.7: the "why" is, for Koe, what makes all content good.

#### The niche as a perspective or worldview

If value is ideas through the lens of a goal, then the niche cannot be what conventional business advice says it is (a market segment defined by demographics or a single skill). Koe draws this conclusion explicitly. In an August 2024 essay-video, "Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche," he writes: "A niche is a perspective or worldview." He follows it with a short manifesto:

> "Become a nobody. Become everything. Become a designer, writer, marketer, socializer, runner, bodybuilder, philosopher, scientist, psychologist, and polymath who knows how to sustain your obsessive curiosity. The universe rewards those who don't impose their own limits."

Later in the same video he gives the fuller definition that this chapter treats as the anchor of what to write:

> "Your niche is the frame of big goals and burning problems that compose your worldview. Your job is to program the minds of your audience to adopt this frame or worldview, pursue those goals, and solve those problems. This is a massive yet fulfilling undertaking to live a life of purpose."

And he reduces the business to three roles: "you're attracting people to the goals. You're helping them solve the problems with your content. And your product is the implementation. It's how they actually go about solving the problem by changing their behavior."

**Source:** Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md

Three features of this definition matter for the rest of the chapter. First, "niche" is a common word used with a meaning of Koe's own: not a segment one targets but a frame one holds. Second, the writer's job is described with the verb "program," which reverses the vocabulary of Part I. There, conditioning was the problem: the Matrix programs minds toward goals they did not choose. Here, the writer deliberately offers a program, a set of goals and problems, which the reader may adopt. Section 22.7 returns to this reversal when Koe argues that interest itself is programmed. Third, the triad of goals, problems and implementation is the same structure as Chapter 19's "four pillars" (goals, problems, systems, benefits), viewed from the side of content.

The earliest version of the idea in this cluster dates from November 2023. In a video on productizing one's mind, Koe argues that perspective matters because "your perspective shapes the entirety of your brand, that is your niche," and he announces a future video called "The Anti-Niche," glossed as "the most profitable niche is you." He then decomposes a perspective into three parts (a goal, a problem, and the clarity, path, skill or knowledge needed to achieve the goal) and says: "that's all a brand is." The writer leads people toward a goal, helps them with skill set and mindset through content, and gives them a system or clarity through a product. "That's turning yourself into the business, turning your mind or perspective into the business, that's earning with your mind." He closes with a characteristic generalization: "Once you peel back to just what the foundation of life is, life and business becomes pretty easy."

**Source:** Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md

The term **anti-niche** is one in a series of labels for the same thesis that Chapter 19 traces ("niche of one," "the most profitable niche is you," "you are the infinite niche," "nicheless," "mission as niche"). What this section adds is the consequence for writing: if the niche is a perspective, then what to write is anything that expresses, applies or extends that perspective, and the remaining sections of the chapter describe where the perspective comes from (22.2), how to organize it (22.3), how to feed it (22.4), how often to repeat it (22.5), with what stance to express it (22.6) and how to make others adopt it (22.7).

### 22.2 Excavating Your Own Raw Material

#### The raw material is already inside you

If the niche is a perspective, the next question is where a perspective comes from. Koe's most developed answer comes late, in a June 2026 video titled "How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You," but it gathers threads that run through the whole corpus. He begins with a diagnosis. Most people love the idea of a personal brand, of a creator's or YouTuber's life, but get stuck fast. They look for "the quick dopamine hit," searching for the best niche to make six figures, "rather than digging into the value they already have from years of accumulated experience and story that they think is worthless because it's normal to them."

The thesis follows:

> "The raw material for your life's work is already inside you, buried under years of being told to specialize, to be practical, to stop asking so many questions."

The phrase **raw material (for your life's work)** names the accumulated experience a person discounts because it is familiar to them. Koe explains why it is buried: most of what makes a person interesting has been "trained out" of them. Curiosity was treated as distraction; varied interests were labeled lack of focus; "the system wanted you an obedient worker." This is the diagnosis of Part I (the school as an institution of the default path) and of Chapter 20 (the critique of specialization) applied to the question of content. The process he is about to describe "isn't meant to give a novel idea but to show what you already have," and content, he says, only works if it comes from material that is actually yours.

The passage also contains a product reference: Koe mentions that the onboarding of Eden, his software, builds "your identity" so that AI can speak like the user, "not just voice replication... your mind." The pitch is incidental, but it shows how he frames the stakes in 2026: in a period when AI can produce generic content without limit, the excavated material is what the machine does not have.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

#### A topic you can't shut up about

Before the 2026 excavation exercise, Koe's first step for beginners was simpler and remarkably stable. In August 2022, May 2023 and again in a February 2024 compilation, the first step of his writing process is the same: **choose a topic you cannot shut up about**.

The 2022 version describes where to find it: your favorite books, the ones you enjoy and "get sucked into"; your YouTube watch history; what you are genuinely interested in, love learning, and love teaching to friends; and your favorite social media accounts that are "not meme accounts but value creators giving good advice." It then explains the mechanism with a scene. You tell a friend, "I'm learning how to code... have you ever heard about HTML, CSS?" and the friend does not care. "Your friends don't really give a [damn] about that, but there's an audience online that does and they want to hear you talk about it no matter your experience, because that's all perspective based."

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md

The May 2023 version, the first of nine steps in the video "I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day," compresses the procedure into a list to write down: favorite books, YouTube watch history, favorite social media accounts, and what you would write about "if money wasn't an issue." The last criterion is new and functions as a test: it separates a genuine interest from one chosen because it seems to pay.

**Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md

The 2024 compilation repeats the 2022 passage almost literally and adds the element that resolves the beginner's typical objection ("but I have no results to show"). If you cannot claim "$40–60K a month writing," talk from a different perspective, for example "seven things that I learned about impactful writing," which builds authority without faking it. The principle is the coined phrase **"brand is what you do"**: whatever you are doing right now is how you write. If you are learning, write as someone learning, and teach what you learn. "You just be the guy that is learning; brand is what you do."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

A July 2024 formulation gives the same answer in more lyrical form, in response to the broader question "what do I write about, what do I market and sell, what do I email, design or leverage technical skills with?" The answer: "the interest you can't help but tell others about, the books you can't pull yourself away from, the ideas that flood your search history, the projects you dream of building but can't seem to make the time to build." The question is wider than writing (it covers marketing, selling and technical skills), which shows that for Koe the topic one cannot shut up about is not just an editorial choice but the core around which the whole one-person business is built. Section 22.7 shows that the same passage, in a March 2024 version, leads directly into the thesis that one does not find a niche but creates one through persuasion.

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The earliest statement of the underlying rationale is from October 2022: "Post the things that are interesting to you because they're going to be interesting to someone else." If a line in a book catches your attention, other people will think it too; distill it for your audience, synthesize it with your interests or what you are trying to achieve, and it will interest them "because they are similar to you." The logic is the same as Chapter 19's "you attract who you are": the writer's own attention is treated as a sample of the attention of a population of similar people.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

This last heuristic is the starting point of a change Koe made later. In 2022 and 2023 the writer's own interest is presented as a sufficient criterion ("post the things that are interesting to you"). By 2026, as Section 22.4 shows, he says that "the best creators don't just post what's on their mind," and asks for an intersection between what interests the writer and what has been shown to interest others. The two positions are not strictly contradictory (the 2022 advice already asks the writer to "distill" and "synthesize"), but the emphasis moves from self-expression toward market research.

#### Step one: three questions to excavate the raw material

The 2026 excavation is presented as an exercise with precise instructions. Set aside 15 minutes, close all tabs, open a blank document and answer in writing. If no answer comes to a question, move on and let it sit in the subconscious. Then answer three questions:

1. **"What do you know too much about for it to be an accident?"** That is: what topic have you researched across dozens of sources, for years, without being paid?
2. **"What problem did you solve for yourself that you assumed everyone else had figured out?"** And, as a variant: what comes naturally to you that seems to break everyone else? Koe adds: "Hint hint, that's your product." One solves a problem for oneself and then for someone else.
3. **"What did you get in trouble for as a kid that was actually just early taste?"** That is: what did you obsess over before anyone told you it was impractical?

Then take one answer, the one that felt best, and start there. Koe adds explicitly: ignore niche and content pillars.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

Each question targets a different layer of the buried material. The first targets accumulated knowledge, using unpaid research as evidence of genuine interest (the same test as "what you would write about if money wasn't an issue"). The second targets solved problems, which connect directly to Chapter 19's "build, write and sell to yourself": the product is the solution one already built for one's past self. The third, with the coined term **early taste**, targets what the school and family environment labeled as misbehavior. The question presupposes the diagnosis of Part I: what the default path suppressed may be precisely the interest that defines a person's taste (a concept treated in Chapter 18). The instruction to "ignore niche and content pillars" is significant, because it is Koe in 2026 telling his audience to set aside tools he himself taught earlier, a shift Section 22.3 examines.

#### Step two: the contrarian spine

The second step of the same exercise begins with a sentence that summarizes Koe's late position on originality: **"Nobody needs another person repackaging common sense."** Content needs a perspective that only the writer sees, and that perspective comes from "the one thing you believe that the mainstream gets wrong." He calls this the **contrarian spine**. Three more questions are meant to locate it:

1. What piece of mainstream advice actively made your life worse, and what did you have to unlearn to become functional?
2. What do you believe about your domain that experts would call stupid or naive, but you can't stop thinking about?
3. What is everyone in your industry pretending not to see, or not talking about?

The six answers from steps one and two, he says, "gave you the six fundamental types of posts." The coined phrase **six fundamental types of posts** refers to these six answers as the starting point of "proven posts" that generate authority and trust rather than simply chasing virality. The instruction is to pick one of them as the first post.

**Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md

The metaphor of the "spine" suggests structural function: the contrarian belief is what holds a body of content upright and gives it a recognizable posture. It connects to two later sections of this chapter. In Section 22.5, Koe's earlier and repeated advice that "people need to be reminded more than they need something new" sits in visible tension with "nobody needs another person repackaging common sense." In Section 22.6, the contrarian spine is the content of the conviction with which one is asked to write: one cannot "pick a side" without a side to pick.

#### Your story is your brand

The second layer of raw material is the writer's story. The phrase **your story is your brand** appears across the corpus from 2022 to 2026, and its strongest justification is a metaphor from April 2023:

> "From a multiverse perspective": there are a million different actions you could take in this very moment, and all of those split off into infinite different realities. You take one specific action, then there are a million more, you take one, and so on, and everyone is doing that every second of the day. "That's what creates your story and your story is your brand and that's what makes you unique and that is why every single person on this Earth can have a business that does not have any competition."

**Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md

The multiverse image is an explanatory metaphor, not a claim about physics: it dramatizes the combinatorial uniqueness of any life history. Its practical point is that competition is a property of offers made from generic positions; an offer made from a specific history has no exact competitor.

A January 2025 passage, from a conversation about growing from zero followers, answers the objection that follows naturally: "other people are talking about this too, how do I stand out if I'm giving the same information?" Koe's answer uses a homely metaphor: "There's maybe a few ways to weave a basket, but let's say there's one way to weave a basket. What makes me different from the next person talking about this?" And: "It would be your story and who you relate with." Even when one talks about a single thing, what makes it unique "is how it applied to your life and how it helped you." He calls the story "the glue between these different perspectives," and gives a combined formula for the niche: "interests combined with the pain points in your life that those solved, and led to the story that you have now."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md

The basket-weaving metaphor is useful because it concedes the strongest version of the objection. Koe does not claim that the information is different; he grants that there may be only one correct way to weave a basket and locates the difference elsewhere, in the application and in the reader's ability to relate to the person explaining it. The formula "interests + pain points they solved + the resulting story" also shows that the story is not an extra ingredient but the connective tissue between the interests (Section 22.3) and the problems (this section).

To recover the story, a January 2026 passage gives a reflective exercise. Spend a day, or go on a walk, and think: Where did you come from? What is the lowest point you have been? What other lows? What have you overcome? What is your actual story? What skills have you learned? What traits have you developed? What are some of your weird and extreme beliefs? What do you think that other people don't? "What are your contrarian takes? What makes you different?" The final questions overlap with the 2026 contrarian spine, which suggests that, for Koe, story and contrarian belief are two faces of one material: the beliefs are what the story taught.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

He anticipates resistance in the same video: "The difficult part for a lot of people, and this was myself included, is that you don't really think your story is worth telling because you think it's boring or you haven't reflected on your growth. But it is, and you'll come to realize that." The warning matches the 2026 diagnosis of raw material that seems "worthless because it's normal to them." The obstacle is not the absence of a story but the absence of reflection on it.

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

#### Story and core beliefs as topics and as reframes

A June 2025 video on personal branding turns story and beliefs into a method with two uses each. Koe's stated aim is to "replicate authenticity" by illustrating one's story and **core beliefs** (beliefs held with conviction) often. Each can serve as a topic or as a reframe:

| | As a topic | As a reframe of a validated topic |
|---|---|---|
| **Story** | "How I failed at seven different business models" | Take a high-potential topic such as "how to build a personal brand" and make it unique by opening with one's own story, as Koe did at the start of that very video |
| **Core beliefs** | "Digital products are the best way for beginners to start," with one's reasons, while others disagree | Learn how Steve Jobs builds a product and frame it under a new lens: for digital products or creators rather than for a Fortune 500 company |

His comment on his own example is the key: "I started with a story and that's what makes me talking about personal branding unique." The distinction between topic and reframe matters because it reconciles two pieces of advice that otherwise pull apart. Section 22.4 recommends taking topics that have already proven to work; this section insists on personal material. The reframe joins them: the topic is borrowed and validated, the lens is personal.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

To do this well, Koe gives an exercise: write out "where you were, what sparked change, and where you are now," and for each topic or theme list the **core principles** you hold with conviction. He warns that this is hard in front of a blank screen, because beliefs "aren't just all sitting in your conscious mind. You have to dig them up from your unconscious." He suggests thinking about it for about a week, listening to content and letting it bring up ideas to write down. As an alternative, he offers an AI prompt that guides the user's thinking and extracts their story and beliefs into a single document.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

The week-long excavation and the claim that beliefs live partly in the "unconscious" echo Part II's model of identity as a structure of mostly unexamined beliefs. The difference is that, in Chapters 3 and 4, those beliefs were examined in order to change them; here they are excavated in order to publish them.

In the same video, Koe adds a third lens to story and belief: reframing a topic "by reframing through a novel idea." If one follows what he calls the growth pillar, one is constantly researching ideas, and some of them can serve as a new frame. His example is Naval Ravikant's ideas that "ideas are the new oil" and that "this generation is getting rich in idea space, not physical space." These let Koe introduce personal branding more compellingly: traditional business models needed a physical location and so on, "but with an online business, with a personal brand, you just need ideas." The term **new lens** names this operation; "idea space" is Naval's phrase, adapted. The example also shows the attribution chain: Naval provides the frame, Koe applies it to a topic Naval did not address.

**Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md

**Complementary context:** Naval Ravikant is an investor and essayist whose aphorisms on wealth, leverage and "specific knowledge" circulated widely on Twitter; Koe has said that Naval's philosophy "became mine without realizing it." The "idea space" image belongs to that body of aphorisms. Chapter 18 treats the related metaphor of idea space as a map of light and dark.

#### Three sources of topics and five lived problems

For beginners, Koe has also offered a plainer inventory. In a December 2024 video, he tells viewers to break the topic of their product or service into three sources:

1. **Your personal story**: where you were before, in the middle and after, in relation to the topic; your transformation.
2. **Pain points people face**: this is "how you start 80% of the content that you write," in hooks and introductions. He points out that the introduction of that very video is a pain point.
3. **Content topics that already did well**: search YouTube, note the best-performing videos, watch them for ideas and note ideas as you go.

The practical instruction is to create a document with five to ten points for each source, and to worry about how to write later: "act like you're texting a friend and they're asking you questions about your topic of interest; write your answers in public."

**Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md

The figure "80%" is a rule of thumb about his own practice, not a measured statistic. The list places personal material (story) beside external material (topics that already did well), with pain points as the bridge between them, since a pain point can be both lived and widely shared. The "texting a friend" instruction addresses the paralysis of form; Chapter 23 deals with form properly.

The most compressed version of the same idea is a one-line exercise from July 2026: "Name five problems you've experienced in your life. Seriously, do that... and boom, now you have five content ideas that you can go and post, and they're going to be a lot better than if you just thought of them from scratch." The claim that lived problems produce better ideas than ideas "thought of from scratch" is the whole thesis of this section in a sentence. In the same passage Koe mentions a prompt he built that acts as a persuasion coach, teacher and writer: run it, it asks what you want, and you practice with it to learn human nature and persuasion faster, whether writing posts, newsletters, scripts, talks or a book.

**Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md

Taken together, the material of this section answers "where does a perspective come from?" with four sources: interests one cannot stop talking about, solved problems, a story of transformation, and beliefs that contradict the mainstream. None of these has to be invented; all of them have to be dug up. What remains is to organize them so that they can sustain months of writing, which is the purpose of the next section.

### 22.3 The Topic Tree: Organizing Interests With Yourself at the Top

The raw material of Section 22.2 is personal and unordered. A writer who has excavated five interests, three solved problems and a contrarian belief still has to decide what to write on Monday, and then on every Monday for the next year. Between 2022 and 2026 Koe offered several tools for this: the Domain of Mastery, the topic tree (earlier called the "content pyramid"), content pillars and the content map, and a sheet of paper with point A and point B. They share a structure (a small number of broad interests, broken down into smaller units that become individual pieces of content) and differ in emphasis. This section presents them in roughly chronological order, because the order shows a real change in how much weight Koe places on planning, and ends with his own later relativization of all of them.

#### The Domain of Mastery (2022 and 2024)

The earliest of these tools comes from Koe's course Digital Economics, which he cites in an October 2022 video as his answer to "Dan, what do I write about?" He calls it the **Domain of Mastery**. The instruction is to pick three interests:

1. One that makes money. Almost any interest can, and this is the **priority interest**.
2. One that excites you.
3. One that is development-based: psychology, self-improvement, self-development, metaphysics, philosophy, spirituality. Its function is to help one understand "the progression pattern of every single thing anyone could ever do," so that one can have a unique perspective by applying spirituality, philosophy or psychology to the other two.

Each interest is then broken down into components. In the 2022 version, these are four: **principles** (the fundamentals of getting results with that interest, in relation to one's goal), **topics**, **mentors** (people one follows and takes advice from, as inspiration) and **connections** (between all of them and real-world problems). These, Koe says, are "the starting places for effective, educational, valuable content."

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md

The same passage reappears almost word for word in a February 2024 compilation.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

In July 2024 Koe teaches the framework again, with three refinements. First, he insists that the interests overlap: "all of these overlap, these aren't like hard interests." Second, he separates "real world problems that they can solve" into its own component, so the breakdown now has five parts: principles ("the things that really matter within that interest"), "topics you can write about outside of that," "mentors you can study and emulate," "connections that can be made between them," and real-world problems. Third, he states the purpose of the whole exercise in terms of this chapter's definition of value: "Principles, topics, mentors, connections: those are all ideas that can be filtered through a goal of what you want to achieve in life and then be written to help people achieve the goal."

**Source:** The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md

The framework can be reconstructed as follows:

| Layer | 2022 version | 2024 version |
|---|---|---|
| Interest 1 | Makes money (priority interest) | Makes money |
| Interest 2 | Excites you | Excites you |
| Interest 3 | Development-based (psychology, self-improvement, metaphysics, philosophy) | Development-based (philosophy, spirituality, self-improvement, self-actualization) |
| Relation among interests | Not stated | "These overlap; these aren't hard interests" |
| Breakdown of each interest | Principles, topics, mentors, connections (to each other and to real-world problems) | Principles, topics, mentors, connections, real-world problems |
| Purpose | Starting places for educational content | Ideas filtered through a life goal, written to help others achieve it |

In all three appearances Koe refers to a graphic shown on screen that the transcripts do not reproduce. The components and their order can be recovered from the narration, but the exact visual layout (whether the interests are drawn as overlapping circles, for example, or how the components hang from each interest) cannot be reconstructed with certainty. The 2024 change is a refinement rather than a contradiction: the earlier version already mentioned real-world problems inside "connections"; the later one makes them a component and makes the goal filter explicit.

The Domain of Mastery is the most deliberate of Koe's tools in one respect: it builds an economic criterion into the selection of interests (one must make money) and a developmental one (one must explain the "progression pattern"). The development-based interest is what gives the writer a vantage point on the other two. This is, in miniature, Koe's own brand: business as the money interest, writing or fitness as the exciting one, and philosophy, psychology and spirituality as the development lens through which he discusses the others. The corpus also uses "Domain of Mastery" for a different map (entrepreneurship as vessel, marketing as message, writing as medium, and so on), which belongs to Part X; the version treated here is the one about choosing interests to write about.

#### Filter interests through your ideal future and zoom out (2023)

A February 2023 video, "How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101)," gives the most complete early process. It opens with a precondition. If one does not know what to write about, or whether to write about a specific interest, one needs a goal for one's ideal future: what are you working toward, and which interests help you get there? Interests are then filtered through that goal.

Koe's example comes from a viewer's dilemma: wanting to talk about spirituality while having a marketing-based product. His reasoning connects the two through an intermediate field. Marketing connects to psychology; spirituality is "like a philosophical psychology in a sense"; and if the end goal is to live autonomously and be sovereign, spirituality plays a huge role, so it makes sense to incorporate it. The first step, then, is to choose two to three interests to write about.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The example shows that the goal filter is permissive rather than restrictive. It does not exclude spirituality from a marketing brand; it finds the path by which spirituality serves the brand's goal. This is the same operation as "frame the idea through the mission" (Section 22.1), applied at the level of whole interests rather than single ideas.

The same video then warns against the opposite error. Most people, asked to write down their interests, write hyper-specific ones, such as "biomechanical movement for holistic health people." When few people turn out to be interested, they get stressed, and the stress damages their ability to write good content, because they think that if they do not talk about that one specific interest nobody will buy. "That's not how it works, my friend. Zoom out." The phrase belongs to a family Koe uses elsewhere ("when in doubt, zoom out," where the solution to struggle is perspective); here it means broadening the interest until there is room to write in it.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

#### Broaden and break down

The second step of the same 2023 process gives zooming out a procedure, under the coined name **broaden and break down**. Koe uses three example interests: web design, spirituality and weightlifting.

First, broaden each into its overarching market. Web design becomes online business, or business. Weightlifting becomes bodybuilding, or health. Spirituality becomes self-actualization or self-improvement. Koe adds that all three could be gathered under the single umbrella of self-improvement or self-actualization, even web design, "since skill acquisition plays a major role in self-actualization."

Second, break the three broad markets down into sub-markets or subtopics to write about over a long period. "This isn't like one week of content and your brand just takes off and explodes; this is like six months of content," enough to introduce people to everything one talks about and knows.

He contrasts this with a different logic of selling: personal branding is a long-term game, not a quick direct-response Facebook ad trying to make someone buy immediately. "Facebook ads and personal branding are a lot different."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The movement has a clear rationale. Broadening solves the problem of the hyper-specific interest (too few people care); breaking down solves the opposite problem (a broad market is too vague to write a post about). The result is a structure that is wide at the top for audience and narrow at the bottom for individual pieces. Chapter 19's "Eternal markets" (health, wealth, relationships, happiness) are the same broadening carried to its limit.

#### The topic tree

The result of broadening and breaking down is what Koe, in the same video, names the **topic tree**. He presents it as a graphic, which can be reconstructed from his narration:

- At the top: **you**, the "niche of one."
- Below: three interests (his example: health, improvement, business).
- Below each interest: topics (for health: nutrition, bodybuilding, lifestyle).
- Below the topics: subtopics (best exercises, recipe ideas, training programs, intermittent fasting, daily routine, sleep tips, which he says are "all just made up" for the illustration).

The writer's job is to cover everything written down in the tree over the six to twelve months of writing content. He comments on the name: "I call this the topic tree. I've called it many things in the past, the content pyramid," but he prefers "topic tree." The transcript does not specify exactly which subtopics hang under which topic; the example subtopics are mostly health-related, but the narration does not assign them one by one.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

Placing "you" at the top of the tree is the visual form of Chapter 19's thesis. In a conventional content plan the top node would be a market or a topic; here it is a person, and the interests hang from that person rather than the person being defined by one interest. The tree is therefore a tool for the generalist of Chapter 20: it gives multiple interests a single root.

A September 2023 variant adds a level above the three interests. "You want to create a topic tree with three broad interests," Koe says, but he argues that most people should first "umbrella under the one interest of self-improvement," self-development or personal development, "because we're trying to improve other people's lives." Then they break that umbrella into three (or more) broad interests, for example productivity, creativity and business. These serve as starting points for research: buy books, look at content, generate ideas from what others have posted on YouTube, Medium or elsewhere, "so you can create that content too."

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

The difference from the February version is the source of unity. In February the three interests are unified by the person at the top; in September they are unified first by a shared market (self-improvement) and only then by the person. The later variant also gives the tree a new function: not only a list of topics to write about but a list of fields to research, which links it to the idea hunting of Section 22.4.

A third mention, from September 2024, is brief. To decide what to teach and sell (what one's content and offer are composed of), Koe says he has taught the topic tree: three big, broad topics that "allow you to maneuver within them" and create many subtopics and content topics underneath, which one can fill out in one's own time. In that video he sets it aside in favor of a simpler plan he calls "straight-line to $1,000 in 60 days," which belongs to Chapter 29. The graphic, again, is not reproduced in the transcript.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

#### The authentic content process in six moves

At the end of the February 2023 video, Koe recaps the whole process in six moves, which place the topic tree inside a larger sequence:

1. Incorporate your interests to make your brand authentic.
2. Create a topic tree you can master in six to twelve months through writing.
3. Write multiple list-style pieces to establish authority in your main interest.
4. Broaden your writing if you don't think it will be shared.
5. Study the best content structures and use them "as training wheels."
6. Branch off what you have already written (repurpose).

Paired with his earlier video "The Most Profitable Niche Is You," he says, this is a start in building leverage. The next steps are networking and growth, then marketing, sales, advertising, promotions and product creation, toward a holistic brand and "potentially your life's work."

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

The recap is useful as a map of where this book places each element. The first two moves belong here. The fourth ("broaden your writing if you don't think it will be shared") anticipates the "broad and beginner" principle of Section 22.7. The fifth, structures as **training wheels**, is the subject of Chapter 23. The sixth, repurposing, belongs to Chapter 24. The study of structures through Twitter search, which the same video describes, is treated in Section 22.4.

#### Two to three intersecting interests: the generalist's niche (2024)

In 2024 Koe restates the selection of interests in the vocabulary of the generalist. In a June 2024 video, step four of a larger plan is to choose two to three **intersecting interests** as one's niche and not to over-specialize, "yet or at all ever." He refers back to his earlier videos "The Most Profitable Niche Is You" and "The Age of the Generalist." The argument has two parts. First, the writer needs to attract people toward the vision of the future they are leading them to, with content and product, and help them achieve goals aligned with that vision. Second, people niche down because they are specialists going toward a specific goal with one skill, which sets them up to be replaced, overtaken or out-competed; a generalist has a unique combination of skills.

His example makes the uniqueness concrete. If two people both seek financial freedom, one may go the budgeting route and the other the online business route; each pivots, learns different skills, and passing that knowledge on contributes value to humanity. The unique intersection of interests is what makes each of them unique in relation to the shared life goal.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

In the same video the next step turns the intersection into content. Break the two to three broad interests into subtopics, which lets one research more effectively for idea generation, and then break those further into post, newsletter, podcast and other media ideas. Start by writing to attract an audience: "start writing to attract an audience, not video, not something else where you need more skills." Repurpose the writing later, once ideas are validated. The purpose is to create media content around the interests and monetize it via a digital product.

**Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md

A week later, in "Ideas Are The New Oil," Koe compresses the same logic into four steps for finding one's niche as a generalist: (1) think about what you are trying to achieve in your life; (2) write down the interests and skills you have learned to get there; (3) attract others trying to achieve the same goal; (4) use your combination of interests and skills to make yourself unique. He supplies the definition behind it: "This is the definition of a generalist: a person who pursues a goal and learns multiple interests or skills in order to achieve it." Put differently: what are you trying to do with your life, what are you learning along the way, and how can you pass it down? "That's literally your niche." He adds that most big creators, and many small ones making good money, work this way.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

**Generalist** is here a common word with Koe's own meaning: not someone who knows a little of everything, but someone who learns whatever a goal requires. The four steps make visible that the 2023 goal filter and the 2024 generalist definition are the same structure: a goal first, then the interests that serve it, then an audience defined by the shared goal. Chapter 20 develops the generalist in full.

#### The paper map: pillars between point A and point B (2026)

In June 2026, in the same video where he introduced point A, point B and the map (Section 22.1), Koe returns to a planning tool. "Quite literally pull out a piece of paper, draw point A on one side, point B on the other, and then you can start filling in the middle with topics and ideas." The filling has three levels:

- Two to three broad topics, such as health, psychology or business: "these are your content pillars, or your main interests broadened up a bit."
- For each topic, two to three subtopics.
- For each subtopic, two to three pain points, principles, how-tos "or other types of content styles that you've seen."

**Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md

The **content pillars** are the topic tree's broad interests under another name, and the descending structure (topics, subtopics, units of content) is the same. Two things are new. First, the map is drawn between A and B rather than under "you": the organizing principle is the transformation, not the person. This matches the corpus's late shift, traced in Chapter 19, from "you are the niche" toward "your mission is your niche." Second, the lowest level is not "subtopics" but types of content (pain points, principles, how-tos), which brings the planning tool closer to actual posts.

#### From method to option: "just share the ideas that are important to you"

Five months before that video, in January 2026, Koe had relativized the whole family of tools. Others, he says, teach focusing on two to three content pillars, maybe one, broken down into topics and subtopics, and he has taught the same in the past (for example, his "content map," which creates a web of content ideas). "That's very useful, but if you want to avoid all of that, just focus on the ideas that are important to you and share those. That's it. That's your entire content strategy."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

This is a change in Koe's position, and the corpus does not resolve it into a single line. The sequence, as far as the dated material shows, is as follows:

| Date | Position | Source unit's video |
|---|---|---|
| Oct 2022 (repeated Feb 2024, refined Jul 2024) | Domain of Mastery: three interests broken into principles, topics, mentors, connections, problems | One-Person Business Model; The Future Of Work |
| Feb 2023 | Topic tree (formerly content pyramid): you → three interests → topics → subtopics, mastered in 6–12 months; filter interests through the ideal future; avoid hyper-specific interests | How To Write Authentic Content |
| Sep 2023 | A single umbrella (self-improvement) above three interests | How To Reprogram Your Mind |
| Jun 2024 | Two to three intersecting interests; break into subtopics and media ideas | Change Your Life In 365 Hours; Ideas Are The New Oil |
| Sep 2024 | Topic tree mentioned, then simplified into a 60-day plan | The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business |
| Jan 2026 | Pillars and content map are "useful," but optional: "just focus on the ideas that are important to you and share those" | If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years |
| Jun 2026 (June 13) | The paper map with 2–3 pillars, subtopics and pain points between A and B | If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business |
| Jun 2026 (June 28) | "Ignore niche and content pillars" and start from the excavated raw material | How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You |

Two readings are available, and the corpus supports both. On the first, pillars move from method to option: they remain useful scaffolding, but the criterion for selection loosens from a deliberate intersection of interests to the writer's own judgment of importance. In a July 2026 passage outside this chapter's core material, Koe goes so far as to say that if one finds an idea important, "that is all the niching down you need." On the second reading, the two kinds of advice address different writers. The map serves the person who needs structure in order to generate ideas at all; "share what's important to you" serves the person who already has a collection of ideas, a goal, a mission and a formed style. Koe does not state this distinction himself, but the fact that he returns to the paper map in the same month in which he tells people to ignore pillars suggests that he does not regard the two as exclusive. The reason he gives for the simplification is to avoid overcomplicating the start.

What remains constant across every version is the structure the previous sections established: a goal or mission that gives the lens, a small number of broad interests that give room, and a descent from broad to specific that turns interests into individual pieces of content. The tools differ in how much of that structure they ask the writer to make explicit on paper before writing.

### 22.4 Hunting for Ideas

The previous two sections located the writer's material inside the writer. This one looks outward. A perspective does not produce posts by itself; it needs ideas to work on, and Koe's consistent position is that ideas are not waited for but hunted. The section first describes the hunt as a state of attention (golden nuggets, "modern hunting," the idea one wishes one had written), then as a research practice (studying high-performing and outlier content, swipe files, the archives of other creators), then as a selection criterion (performance and excitement), and finally through a guest's system, Justin Welsh's content matrix, which solves the same problem from a different direction.

#### Golden nugget ideas: capturing what excites you

The earliest version is from October 2022, in a video on "the value creator." Step three of a process for researching one's obsessions is to note down **golden nugget ideas**. If one is researching topics one is genuinely curious about, it should be "near impossible to not write down your discoveries." Koe recommends keeping a running note on one's phone and capturing ideas, anecdotes, stories, even statistics, anything that made one think "wow, that changed my mind" or "aha, that's it." He admits it is hard to put into words, but "when you get that excitement, that's what you write down."

The function of these notes is structural. When writing content, a video or a podcast outline, the notes become **building blocks**: one copies them over and structures an argument around what one wants to talk about. They serve as the foundational ideas of the piece. And the justification is the same as in Section 22.2: what stuck with you will interest someone else.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The passage reappears almost literally in the February 2024 compilation, with a plug for his free creative challenge as the place to keep the running note.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The criterion for capture deserves attention because it is affective, not logical. Koe does not tell the reader to note ideas that are true, useful or important by some external standard; he tells them to note what produced excitement. The feeling is treated as a signal. Chapter 18 described novelty as what catches attention and the "good dopamine hit" of a new idea; here that signal becomes the trigger of a writing practice.

#### Modern hunting

In September 2023 Koe gives the practice a name. When you feel the excitement, "or dopamine," that comes from discovering a novel idea, write it down, refine it and post it to your profile. "This is just one huge content hack in general: sharing what excites you." That is what one is hunting for when one reads: "This is modern hunting," finding something new, being excited, and thinking "I need to share this with someone else," because that is how one attracts people interested in the same things. He states the principle that links capture to value: "The first step in value creation is capturing an idea so that you can make it your own and give it to someone."

**Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md

**Modern hunting** is a metaphor with a specific implication. The hunter does not produce the game; the hunter finds it, takes it and brings it back to share. The writer, likewise, does not need to generate ideas from nothing; the work lies in attention, capture and transformation ("make it your own") before giving. The mention of dopamine should be read as Koe's shorthand for the felt excitement of novelty rather than as a neurochemical claim he supports in this passage; Chapter 11 discusses how he uses dopamine more broadly.

A December 2025 formulation, from a video on articulation, describes the hunt as a deliberate activity for when one has no idea:

> "You need to hunt for them. You need to read old books, go down rabbit holes on a topic, listen to a new podcast, or just sit with your thoughts and follow them until you reach a compelling insight. When you hunt for an idea, you aren't just letting the information go in one ear and out the other. You are listening intently for an idea that you wish you wrote. Then you jot it down so you don't lose it. Then you articulate it in your own words using these frameworks so it takes a new shape."

**Source:** How To Articulate Your Thoughts Intelligently (Talk Like This).md

The sequence (hunt, recognize, capture, re-articulate) is the complete practice, and its last step points to Chapter 23, where the "frameworks" are discussed.

#### The idea you wish you wrote

The recognition step has its own name in the corpus: **an idea you wish you wrote**. It first appears in September 2023. Koe argues that the feelings produced by meaningful events (excitement, fulfillment, gratitude) "should be seen as an opportunity to document the important parts of your life that you can share with others." When a good book sparks excitement, write it down, "because if you plan on giving any form of value, it's going to be the stuff that you find important in your life." He then describes his own practice: "My best tweets are the things where I read something and I'm like, that's really good, I wish I wrote that, and then I rewrite it." Now he has something to write; he shares it, and it resonates with people similar to him.

**Source:** How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md

A February 2024 passage adds the mechanism that makes the practice self-reinforcing. When Koe knows he will teach what he learns, reading changes. He sees an idea in a book and thinks "damn, that's one that I wish I wrote, and I take it": "I need to write content about this because I'm ready to teach it." The general observation is: "My mind takes more care in life when I know that I'm going to... do something with any of the information that goes into my head."

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md

**Complementary context:** The observation that people learn better when they expect to teach is known in educational psychology as the "protégé effect." Chapter 14 discusses how Koe uses it in his "build to learn" approach. Here it explains why the intention to publish turns reading into hunting: the expectation of teaching raises the quality of attention.

The phrase "I take it" and "then I rewrite it" raise the question of where hunting ends and copying begins. Koe's answer, spread across this section and the next, is that what is taken is the idea and sometimes the structure, and what must be added is one's own articulation and frame. The December 2025 passage says the idea is articulated "in your own words... so it takes a new shape"; the 2026 example of psychic entropy in Section 22.1 says "I'm not copying the idea one-to-one, I'm applying it to a new domain."

#### "Don't consume, research"

A July 2023 passage generalizes the hunting attitude into a rule for all consumption: **"Don't consume, research."** The best creators, Koe says, consume "from the lens of wanting to learn and apply," not for entertainment, and "this perception shift alone will turn you into an idea generation machine." One reads with the lens of turning the material into one's own, "looking for creative firepower." The coined phrases **idea generation machine** and **creative firepower** name what the consumer becomes once the lens changes.

He adds an emotional dimension: "Fall back in love with learning outside of the traditional education system that made you hate it": books, podcasts, blog posts and newsletters aligned with one's goals. The practical instruction is to use a notebook app or an idea development system (he mentions his free course "Seven Days to Genius Ideas"), because "no, you won't remember them; this step is absolutely critical." One notes the ideas that raise dopamine, rivet attention, change perspective or bring clarity, and then contemplates their angles and how they apply to one's vision, goals and problems. The step closes with a sentence that restates Section 22.1: "You're not writing about topics, you're helping people overcome problems in their life towards their goals." If a spiritual idea helped you build a business, it will help someone else.

**Source:** The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md

The insistence on capture ("you won't remember them") has one declared exception in the corpus. In a March 2026 video on recovering "creative genius," Koe proposes a seven-day protocol in which one does not write ideas down ("if it's important it will come back") and does not share what one creates for 24 hours, and he acknowledges that this is "antithetical to everything that I preach." He limits it explicitly to that protocol, so it does not replace the everyday practice described here; it is noted so that the reader does not take capture as an absolute rule in Koe's own view.

#### Naval: hunt for ideas, share ideas

An August 2024 passage attributes a version of the practice to Naval Ravikant. "Naval treats Twitter as his note-taking platform": he reads a book, finds an idea, and shares it from his own perspective; he finds it impactful, shares it, and those who also find it impactful follow him. Koe draws a definition from the example: "That's what an idea is: an important idea that you find important helps you achieve a goal." And he gives a radical simplification for anyone who does not yet know what goal to help people with: "Just focus on ideas... stop thinking about your niche... get ideas, share them, that's it, that's how you build a business. Simple: hunt for ideas, share ideas, a beautiful flow of information that benefits both yourself and others."

**Source:** This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md

The attribution needs care. The observation about how Naval uses Twitter is Koe's description of Naval's behavior; the slogan "hunt for ideas, share ideas" and the conclusion about building a business are Koe's adaptation. The passage is also one of the clearest anticipations of the January 2026 simplification discussed at the end of Section 22.3 ("just focus on the ideas that are important to you and share those"): already in 2024, for someone without a defined goal, Koe tells people to stop thinking about the niche and simply circulate ideas they find important.

#### Hunting high-performing ideas

Alongside the hunt for ideas that excite the writer runs a second practice, aimed at ideas that have already proven to interest others. Koe's language for it is deliberately blunt: steal or swipe **high-performing ideas**.

The earliest version is from December 2022, in "The One Person Business Roadmap." Creators are encouraged to steal or swipe high-performing ideas; this is "not bad, just overused," and Koe says he does it all the time. His sources:

1. Go to a channel you aspire to be like and filter its videos by "most popular."
2. Go to Medium and skim the homepage; its algorithm serves content around your interests, with "incredible ideas."
3. Look at top tweets using Twemex or Tweet Hunter, filtering any account's tweets to see its highest-performing topics.

"People sleep on Twitter," he says: a good tweet that went viral is "the perfect newsletter, blog, YouTube headline," or the start of a newsletter, with more value in the newsletter itself. The problem, he adds, is that the practice is overused, and his fix comes in what the Roadmap calls stage three: synthesis.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md

The passage reappears in the February 2024 compilation, with the tool name transcribed differently.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The mention of synthesis matters. In the same Roadmap, Koe warns elsewhere that those who copy without synthesis become commodities and that copying others' ideas without novel perspectives will not last. Stealing is presented as a beginner's stage, to be superseded by the synthesis treated in Chapter 18, not as the destination.

The same Roadmap places the study of high-performing content at the very start of the creator's development. Everyone starts at stage one (assuming no prior business experience), and the first thing to understand is that social media growth is a skill that can be practiced, not luck. One cannot post whatever one feels like, or something because it is clever or artsy; one has to understand what leads to growth. Koe adds a sentence that assigns responsibility: "If you aren't growing, it's not the reader's fault... it's because you lack the ability to make your message understandable to them." The method: filter your favorite channel's videos by most popular and ask why they did best; study top tweets; follow accounts you aspire to be like and see which posts outperform; use Tweet Hunter or Twemex; note common patterns, understand why, and emulate.

**Source:** The One Person Business Roadmap (99% Make This Mistake).md

The principle that failure to grow is the writer's responsibility, not the reader's, returns in Section 22.7 in a stronger form: if nobody is interested in your interests, "it's your fault."

An April 2023 passage lists four sources and names a favorite:

1. Filter your favorite YouTubers' videos by most popular and study the titles.
2. Sign up for Medium, select your interests and study the homepage headlines daily.
3. Read books until a novel idea catches your attention, write it down and use it in writing, videos or speaking.
4. Study top tweets, Koe's favorite, "because tweets are just ideas, right? They're digestible ideas and they're time tested." He uses Tweet Hunter (also his tool for scheduling) to type in accounts he likes and see their top tweets for idea generation, "to make sure that your ideas are validated before you go and write them in your own words."

**Source:** The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md

The phrase "validated before you... write them in your own words" introduces a concept that runs through Koe's later work, **validated content** (or validated ideas): ideas that have already proven to generate engagement, rewritten from one's own perspective. Chapter 26 treats validation as a growth strategy; here it functions as a filter on what to write.

Two more versions add specific techniques. In February 2023, in the "authentic content" video, Koe shows how to study structures without any paid tool: type into Twitter's search "from:[username] min_faves:1000" (his own example is "from:thedankoe min_faves:1000"), which shows only that account's tweets with at least 1,000 likes. Look at "your tribe of mentors," the people you aspire to write like, study their top tweets and how they are structured, then plug similar topics into those structures and rewrite them.

**Source:** How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md

In September 2024 he lists four ways to turn chosen topics into posts: (1) break them into common pain points and write about how to solve them; (2) research other accounts' top tweets with a tool such as the Tweet Hunter X sidebar (a Chrome extension) or SuperX, and rewrite them from your own perspective, since in one view you see an account's most popular tweets, "pretty much proven to do extremely well if you can get the traffic kickstarted"; (3) read books and use social media as a place to take notes, "but write those notes with a hook, body and conclusion," in your own words (he was then reading *The Systems View of Life* and turning passages into posts); (4) filter favorite YouTube channels by most popular and use their titles as starting points for hooks and posts.

**Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md

**Complementary context:** *The Systems View of Life* (2014) is a book by the physicist Fritjof Capra and the biochemist Pier Luigi Luisi that presents living systems, cognition and society from a systems-theory perspective. Koe cites it as an example of a dense book whose passages can be turned into short posts.

#### The author's own process: from a proven title to an argument

Koe illustrates the method with his own work at two moments four years apart. In October 2022 he describes how he chooses YouTube topics: he watches good YouTube videos, knows what performs well in the algorithm, and takes the title. His example in progress is "The Power of Being Alone," a hot topic he was also writing about in his newsletter. He was listening to videos on being alone to study the topic so he could "come with a credible argument, because I have experience" to back it, but he still needed a way to structure the argument.

**Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md

The same passage reappears in the February 2024 compilation.

**Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md

The example shows the division of labor that the rest of this section formalizes: the title (the topic and its packaging) comes from what already performs; the credibility comes from the writer's experience; and the remaining problem, structure, is the subject of Chapter 23.

In July 2026 he describes the origin of a specific video. Its inspiration was a video with about 700,000 views called "The notebook system that saved my brain." He asked: "What about the writing system that saved my brain? The writing habit that saved my brain?" That gave him a good starting point for an idea to write about; the title and thumbnail could be crafted later if a better idea came, "because none of this is guaranteed." The resulting video is the one in which he tells this story.

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

#### Swipe files and outlier research

From 2025 onward, the practice acquires a vocabulary borrowed from marketing and a set of tools Koe himself builds. A **swipe file** (a common marketing term he adopts) is a collection of high-performing posts, titles and ideas to study and reformulate. In February 2025 he presents building one as the first way to practice structure. "Keep your finger on the pulse of the market": "scroll the timeline as a researcher, not a consumer," "scroll to hunt for ideas." When you see a good post ("damn, I want that under my brand"), copy the link and paste or embed it in a capture tool; at the time, his own product, Kortex, whose mobile version he says aims to be "faster than Apple Notes."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Scroll as a researcher, not a consumer** is the 2025 form of the 2023 "don't consume, research." The same shift of lens is applied to the feed itself, the place where most people consume most passively.

In July 2026 Koe walks through the practice in more detail. Capture is free and can be done anywhere (in notes or any capture place); he shows it in Eden, his newer product. Paste links into a library; on the phone, quickly capture an idea or a social link while scrolling; save videos that are doing well, or ideas worth saving, where you can find them later; "if it disappears, that's fine." Next, use an **outlier tool** (for example 1of10, for what is trending on YouTube, or Eden's discover feed) to research the market. His favorite move: go to a specific creator's account and "filter their content by top liked," especially for YouTube titles and angles people already pay attention to (top videos, posts, notes, articles, tweets, reels, carousels). Then "reverse engineer" it: "I want to recreate this with my own idea."

**Source:** The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md

A May 2026 passage, inside a pitch for the relaunched Eden, describes his full research workflow:

1. **Boards** where he pastes links to social posts or websites, uploads PDFs, and creates notes or documents to write in, for example a "YouTube swipe file" and a "post swipe file."
2. A **discover feed**: set your content pillars, scroll, filter by platform (only tweets, or only YouTube), and add what catches your eye to a board. "If you don't know what to create next, just scroll through here, add it to a board, and then when it's time to actually create, go to your board, create a note in there, and then start writing."
3. **Search a specific creator** across platforms and filter by top-performing content, including his own: "I can just see... my own good ideas, so I can iterate off of those, and I do that all the time. That's really how I continue growing at this point."
4. **AI over a board**: reference a board in chat and ask for variations, or add a creator's top YouTube videos and ask: "Break all of these down. What is this person's worldview? How does he structure his videos? How can I apply this to the next video?"

He mentions in passing that the old Eden pivoted after they "went broke thanks to AI credits."

**Source:** How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md

Three features of the workflow are worth drawing out. First, the content pillars of Section 22.3 reappear as search filters: the planning tool becomes a research tool. Second, Koe's most valued source by 2026 is his own past outliers: the writer studies their own archive for what has already worked and iterates on it, which connects to "say one thing a thousand different ways" in Section 22.5. Third, the AI questions ask for a creator's worldview and structure, not their sentences, which is consistent with what Koe says may be taken (topics, angles, structures) and what must be one's own (articulation, perspective).

The tools change across the corpus: Tweet Hunter, Twemex and Medium in 2022–2023; SuperX and the Tweet Hunter sidebar in 2024; Kortex (also spelled Cortex) in 2024–2025; Eden and 1of10 in 2026. Several of these are Koe's own products, and their mention often accompanies a promotion. The mechanics, however, do not change: filter a creator's output by what performed best, save it, ask why it worked, and recreate it with one's own idea.

A February 2025 passage gives the research a second target. Once one knows one's topic, research five to ten accounts that talk about it, and notice what content they repeat weekly or monthly and what they all talk about. Make those topics a consistent part of your brand: create multiple free guides around them, create content on one of them at least once a day, and write short, medium and long-form content (posts, threads, carousels, which he glosses as Instagram's swipeable sets of about ten images, and newsletters). The goal is to "build a world that people can sink their teeth into," "your own small-scale Netflix that people can binge watch or binge read."

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The **small-scale Netflix** echoes the 2026 image of content as a world and a lore (Section 22.1), and the topics that "they all talk about" are what Section 22.5 calls evergreen ideas.

#### Study what creators did to grow, not what they do now

The study of other creators has a trap, which Koe describes in February 2025. He calls the beginner's predicament "beginner hell," and says it can last three months or thirty years: "that's a starving artist," someone who does their art, never thinks about the business side and cannot sustain their life's work. Creators who never progress try to do what advanced creators are doing now "without realizing that that's not what the advanced creators did." Beginners post philosophical quotes or try to be "the next Marcus Aurelius when nobody knows who they are." His correction is blunt: "You aren't a Roman Emperor; you aren't respected because nobody knows who you are." If social media is one's path to one's life's work, one must build authority, building up to being respected by a group of people.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

**Starving artist** is a common phrase Koe uses with a specific sense: the creator who refuses the business side of creative work and therefore cannot sustain it. It recurs below in the discussion of performance and excitement.

The remedy, in the same video, is to study creators' archives. You are probably copying "feel-good content" from accounts you follow, but they already have an audience, baseline engagement and recognition. Study what they created in order to grow. On YouTube, filter their profile by "oldest"; look through all of it and find "which one created that spike," because "social media growth comes in waves, it's not just linear." Scroll to the bottom even if it takes an hour, copy the content that started to do well ("that was an anomaly") into a capture tool, and use it as a framework.

**Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md

The **anomaly** (or outlier) is a piece that performs far above a creator's normal level and causes a growth spike. Studying anomalies in the oldest content corrects the beginner's error: it shows what worked when the creator had no audience, which is the beginner's actual situation.

A June 2024 version frames the same study as becoming an **amalgamation**. To change from consumer to creator, study the ten accounts you follow, enjoy and want to become like, and "become an amalgamation of all of them," taking little pieces from each. Ask why a piece of content did well, why a product sells, why a profile picture works, what their best ideas are and how to write your own version; if they went viral, your spin may too. Crucially, study what they did at the beginning by going back into their archives: were they writing the single sentences that get engagement now, or giving actual value in a different way? People copy what big creators do now without seeing what got them there. "Social media is a skill, not random luck," and people blame luck without spending even an hour studying the skill.

**Source:** Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md

The earliest and least technical version, from October 2023, says one does not even need a course. Write what you want, talk to whom you want, "get your name in front of people by not being a ghost," and get eyes on your writing so it spreads. For an extra push, look at people you have followed for a long time: observe what they write about, how they write it, who they interact with and what they sell to make a living; piece the dots together and begin emulating them. If you lack an answer, look it up, and accept that you will not get all the answers instantly. "If you know you are meant for more, start acting like it." Koe also calls writing "the fundamental mode of communication that has survived throughout human existence," and a great starting point to discover what one truly wants.

**Source:** If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md

Read together, these passages describe imitation as a learning method and not as a betrayal of authenticity. The corpus records that Koe's position on this moved over time: he first spoke of a "consistent authenticity" that takes thousands of hours, then of "intelligent imitation," and in 2026 he argued against "authenticity bros" that "you imitate because you learn." The change, in the corpus's own summary, is that authenticity stops being the opposite of imitation and becomes imitation in the service of one's core. Section 22.6 returns to authenticity from the side of the reader's experience.

#### Performance and excitement: a balance of art and business

The practices above could suggest that Koe recommends writing only what has already worked. His 2026 formulation corrects that impression with a selection criterion that combines the two kinds of hunting. In January 2026:

> "The goal of curating ideas to include under your brand should fall at the intersection of one, performance, which is ideas that have the potential to do well, and this is a measure of how much other people will care, and two, excitement, which is the ideas that give you a sense of excitement to write about them. This is a measure of how much you care."

"It's a balance of art and business." On the side of performance the questions are: will others care, has this done well before, do I have a good hook? On the side of excitement: what do I care about? "It's not blatantly copying what works. It's doing something unique, but also taking into account the principles that are actually going to capture attention."

**Source:** If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md

In August 2026 he repeats the criterion as step one of writing a long piece (a Substack article, a YouTube script or an X article; long form is growing, he says, "because they're tired of brainless short-form"). Choose a topic at "the intersection of performance and excitement." Performance means "topics that have already proven to do well": save viral posts or use an outlier research tool to study creators' top posts, "this is just what you do." He supports this with an analogy: do musicians become famous "by just making little sounds, or did they have a structure... 'How will the market respond?'" And he names the failure mode: "A lot of artists skip the business part, and that's why they become starving artists... that is their downfall." Using proven principles "isn't sacrificing your voice... because it still runs through your taste filter." The **taste filter** is the coined term for the writer's judgment through which any borrowed principle passes, which preserves the voice.

His example is the very video: its title includes "how to remember everything you read" because hundreds of videos with that phrase went viral, yet the video argues the opposite. Excitement, in turn, means "topics and ideas that you want to explore more out of curiosity," and the tactic for joining them is: "pair the topic you want to write about that probably wouldn't do well on social media with a topic that has already proven to do well."

**Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md

The framework can be summarized:

| | Performance | Excitement |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | How much other people will care | How much you care |
| Questions | Will others care? Has it done well before? Do I have a good hook? | What am I curious about? What do I want to explore? |
| Sources | Viral posts, outlier tools, creators' top content | Interests, curiosity, the idea you wish you wrote |
| Failure if alone | Blatant copying; a voice lost | The starving artist |
| How they join | Pair an unproven personal topic with a proven one; run proven principles through the taste filter | |

This criterion marks a shift in Koe's emphasis. In 2022–2023 the writer's own interest was enough ("post the things that are interesting to you"; elsewhere in 2023, "write about whatever you want in a way that's interesting"). By 2024–2026 he says that if one's goal is income, content should be driven by the offer, and in May 2026 that "the best creators don't just post what's on their mind... that's not a strategy." The intersection of performance and excitement is the synthesis: interest is necessary but not sufficient, and market research is necessary but not sufficient. The reason he gives is that treating social media as one's life's work implies strategy, and that artists who ignore business end up starving. He also acknowledges that he himself sometimes simply posts what he is thinking, so the two attitudes can coexist depending on the goal (expression or income).

#### A guest's system: Justin Welsh's content matrix

A December 2021 interview with Justin Welsh, a former SaaS sales executive who built a one-person business on LinkedIn and Twitter, contains a system that solves the problem of what to write by combination rather than by hunting. The system is Welsh's, not Koe's, and is presented here as a guest's contribution that Koe later cites; Koe adapted several of Welsh's other ideas elsewhere in the corpus (Chapter 19 traces the "niche of one" to him).

Welsh describes the problem plainly: he used to stare at a blank white screen thinking "what do I want to write today?", and "that's never very fun." Needing "ways to ideate rapidly," he built what he calls "the content matrix, for lack of a cooler or better name." Its components:

1. **Rows**: all the topics he normally likes to write about, listed down the left side.
2. **Columns**: his favorite structures across the top: "observations, step-by-step guides, how-tos, analytical, X versus Y, past versus present... listicles."
3. **The session**: once a week, Saturday morning over coffee, "when I'm feeling really energized, I got tons of free time."
4. **Pairing**: "Topic, structure. Pair, pair, pair... quickly, quickly. The idea is to just ideate quickly."
5. **Headlines**: as soon as a pair produces an idea, he writes the headline.
6. **Output**: "10 headlines right away. Now I've got at least 10 starting points."

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

A schematic version of the matrix (the specific topics are illustrative, since Welsh does not list his) looks like this:

| Topic \ Structure | Observation | Step-by-step | How-to | Analytical | X vs. Y | Past vs. present | Listicle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Topic 1 | headline? | headline? | … | … | … | … | … |
| Topic 2 | … | … | … | … | … | … | … |
| Topic 3 | … | … | … | … | … | … | … |

The matrix turns ideation into combination: the blank page is replaced by a grid of cells, each of which is a prompt. Welsh notes that this is his system at the "10k and beyond" stage, "more robust and... more systematized" than at the start.

The headlines then enter a production pipeline. Welsh plugs them into tools ("I think it's called Typeform, and there's another, there's Hypefury"), using their "inspiration and templates" to make sure the writing is "formatted correctly." The name "Typeform" is uncertain in his own words, and it is not clear which tool he means beyond Hypefury. He runs the text through the Hemingway app, which tells him "if it's grade one, grade two, grade three," that is, whether sentence structure is clear and easy to read. He loads it back into Hypefury. The target is to "create 10 pieces of content sitting down for 2 hours." And "because I've created 10 pieces of content, I might as well put them everywhere that's relevant": LinkedIn, Twitter (through Hypefury), Instagram posts and his website.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

**Complementary context:** Hemingway is a text editor that estimates the US school grade level needed to read a passage and flags long or complex sentences. Hypefury is a tool for scheduling and templating social media posts. Grade one to three is an unusually low target; it reflects Welsh's priority on readability in a scrolling feed, a theme Chapter 23 develops under legibility.

Two further pieces of Welsh's system concern what happens after publishing. Once he started to stand out, he "started to just do things that I had done in my sales leadership job. Analyze the data. What's working? What isn't working? Double down on what does, cut what doesn't." Through this he learned "about formatting, right? Being compelling, being polarizing." The operator's data loop from SaaS sales transferred to content.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

And he is candid about the motive that sustains the loop: "The interesting thing about getting attention online is that it's addicting. And anyone who says that it isn't is full of [it]." Once you get attention, you want more; to get more, "you got to study more." So his cycle has been "writing, analyzing, studying, writing again, repeat." Koe later echoes the idea when discussing paid ads: after "getting kind of addicted to the growth" on organic platforms, he did not feel he needed ads.

**Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md

The content matrix and the data loop complement Koe's own approach rather than repeating it. Koe's hunting starts from the idea and asks how to frame it; Welsh's matrix starts from known topics and known structures and generates ideas by crossing them. Koe's study of outliers looks at other people's data; Welsh's loop looks at one's own. Welsh's frank remark about addiction is also a useful counterweight to the rest of this chapter: the same attention economy that rewards repetition and outliers can capture the writer, a risk Chapter 11 discusses in its own terms.
