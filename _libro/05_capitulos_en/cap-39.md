## Chapter 39 — Happiness, Struggle and the Chapters of Life

Everything in this book so far has been about building something: a self that is chosen rather than assigned, a mind that holds a goal, a body of work, an audience, a business, an income that does not depend on permission. This chapter asks what all that building is for and what it feels like from the inside while it is happening. It gathers Dan Koe's answers to four questions that run underneath the whole corpus. What is happiness, and can it be pursued? Why is life difficult, and what should be done with that difficulty? How do you hold the drive to progress together with the capacity to be at peace? And why does life seem to move in cycles, so that clarity is found, used up and lost again, and what should you do in the stretch where you are lost?

Three cautions govern the presentation.

First, Koe uses ordinary words in special senses here. "Happiness," "enjoyment," "suffering," "pain," "balance," "freedom" and "autonomy" each carry a definition of his own, and in two cases (suffering and balance) the definition changes over the years. The chapter gives each definition where the word first matters and marks the changes.

Second, much of this material is philosophy of life rather than method, and Koe draws on spiritual traditions (Buddhism, Alan Watts, teachings on presence), on physics used as metaphor (the harmonic oscillator, entropy, exploding stars) and on his own biography. None of these frames is offered with experimental evidence. Where he speaks of neurotransmitters he relies on a popular book (Daniel Z. Lieberman's *The Molecule of More*) and simplifies it. The chapter marks frames as frames and anecdotes as anecdotes.

Third, the model of life as a sequence of chapters and phases appears in six versions between 2022 and 2025, with different names, different numbers of phases and a different place for "feeling lost." Section 39.6 presents them side by side instead of choosing one silently.

The chapter depends on almost everything before it: on identity as a structure that defends itself (Part II), on entropy, attention and the goal as a lens (Part III), on anti-vision, vision, purpose and flow (Part IV), on dopamine and the 4-Hour Workday (Part V), and on the one-person business as a vessel (Part X). It prepares Chapter 40, which turns from the shape of a life to its contents: body, relationships, spirituality and collective development.

### 39.1 What Happiness Is

Koe never gives a single, fixed definition of happiness. He gives a formula that he refines over three years, a lens (connection instead of comparison) that explains why some experiences feel good and others feel like suffering, and a classical triad (the good, the true and the beautiful) that describes what a good life is made of. Each of the three answers a different question. The formula says where the feeling comes from. The lens says how perception produces or destroys it. The triad says what a life has to contain for the feeling to be more than a passing state.

#### Happiness as resistance overcome: a borrowed line that becomes a formula

The first version is a quotation. In October 2023, explaining how to "reprogram" a life that feels bad, Koe concedes that you cannot solve every problem or escape your situation immediately. What you need is a plan and an aim. If you stick to them, he promises, the journey will be more enjoyable than the outcome, and he supports the promise with a line he attributes to Nietzsche: "happiness is the feeling that power increases that resistance is being overcome." **Source:** If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md.

**Complementary context:** The line comes from Friedrich Nietzsche's *The Antichrist* (section 2), where happiness is defined as "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome." In Nietzsche it belongs to a polemic about "will to power"; Koe uses only the psychological core, the idea that the felt quality of progress against an obstacle is what we call happiness.

The logic of the quotation in that video is simple and important. If happiness were the possession of an outcome, a person far from the outcome would have to wait to be happy. If happiness is the feeling of resistance being overcome, a person with a plan and an aim can feel it every day of the journey, long before any outcome arrives. This is the same move Chapter 8 made with goals: the goal is not mainly a destination but a lens and a source of daily direction.

By early 2025 the quotation has become a formula of his own, with a second term added. "If happiness comes from overcoming resistance and a connection to something greater than yourself, creativity is the vessel for both." **Source:** The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md. The two terms do different work:

| Term | What produces it | Where it appears in Koe's system |
|---|---|---|
| Overcoming resistance | Solving problems; making progress; the absorption of flow while working on something hard | Progress, flow, the game (Chapter 10) |
| Connection to something greater than yourself | Passing what you have learned or built on to others; contributing to a cumulative human project | Contribution, purpose, value creation (Chapters 31 and 35) |

Creativity is "the vessel for both" because a single act of creation satisfies both terms. When you create, you solve a problem, which gives you the first term. When you hand the creation to someone else, you solve their problem, which connects you to something larger than yourself. In February 2025 he puts it in nearly the same words: "Happiness stems from resistance being overcome and a connection to something greater than yourself, in other words creativity and contribution." When you become a person with value to give and give it consistently, "you learn what it means to be happy." **Source:** The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md.

He uses the formula to explain his own choices, which makes it more than a slogan. He writes and makes content instead of pursuing business models that would make "far more money." He builds Cortex, a note-taking application, in a category where most products fail, instead of a niche vertical software product or an agent business that could, by his own estimate, make more and grow ten times faster. The reason he gives is that he enjoys the creative challenge. **Source:** The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md. In other words, he presents a trade of expected money for expected happiness, and the formula is his justification for the trade. The reader should notice that this is a personal decision explained by a personal theory; Koe does not claim that everyone should make the same trade.

In the same video he widens the second term with an image. Any product or service passes knowledge down: "this water bottle is a deposit of knowledge." The bottle in his hand embodies centuries of accumulated know-how about materials, manufacturing and design, and whoever makes something adds a deposit to that cumulative progress. That contribution is what gives purpose, "the other end of the equation." Solving problems gives you the flow state; passing down knowledge gives you meaning. **Source:** same.

The third appearance, in February 2026, shows the formula drifting away from its source. Koe says there is "a niche quote" on happiness that he cannot recite exactly, "something like happiness is the feeling of resistance being overcome, of progress being made," and asks viewers to post it in the comments. "This insight isn't anything new. We just need to be reminded of it." **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md. The transcript's "niche" may be a mishearing of "Nietzsche"; it is ambiguous. Either way, the quotation that was explicitly attributed in 2023 has, by 2026, become something he half-remembers and has absorbed into his own framework of progress and contribution.

The evolution, then, has three steps: a borrowed line (2023), an expanded formula of his own with a contribution term and with creativity as its vehicle (2025), and a half-remembered quotation folded into a larger theory of meaning (2026). In parallel, the corpus contains a quieter definition that does not fit the formula: in 2025 he says, answering the objection that happiness surely means going out and having fun, that happiness "is being fine... calm... in a good mood. It's not euphoria." The two definitions can be read as belonging to the two poles that Section 39.4 calls progress and peace: the Nietzschean formula describes the happiness of progress; the "calm" definition describes the happiness of peace. Koe himself does not reconcile them explicitly.

#### Comparison and connection: why perception produces suffering or enjoyment

The formula explains where happiness comes from. A different strand, older and more explicitly spiritual, explains how the way you look at the world produces suffering or enjoyment.

In November 2022, drawing on Buddhism, where suffering is the central word (something not necessarily eliminated but seen in a different light), Koe proposes that "suffering happens when you view the world from a lens of comparison." The lens of comparison highlights differences: why things are not the same, what separates you from others, what makes you different. Tension arises from those differences. His example is religious: a Christian and a Catholic, each disliking the other because the other "won't transition over to you." He adds that this is only one of "a billion different examples." **Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md.

Enjoyment, by contrast, happens when you view the world from a lens of connection: zooming out, gaining "meta perspective," seeking to understand the big picture with an open rather than a closed mind. The formula he offers is "sameness over difference," but he immediately qualifies it: understanding differences is also good, and you have to hold both to see the whole. The point is not to deny that things differ. It is that a mind fixated on difference lives in tension, while a mind that can also see what things share can relax into the whole. **Source:** same.

In the same passage he explains a word choice that runs through his later work. He prefers "enjoyment" to "love," because "love" has been "twisted and manipulated so much in our language." Enjoyment, in his sense, is the feeling that comes from doing the things you are connected with. It arises from connection, understanding and clarity about what you are doing. His model is a game in which you understand every aspect and your skill matches the challenge the game presents, so that playing becomes so enjoyable you become obsessed. "Enjoyment and obsession is kind of my word for what the spiritual teachers or the mystics would call love." **Source:** same. This is a word used in a sense of its own and should be read that way: when Koe says "enjoyment" in a philosophical passage, he does not mean pleasure or entertainment. He means the absorbed, connected state that Chapter 10 described as flow, here given a spiritual reading.

He also borrows a formulation from Leo Gura's channel Actualized.org. "If you want to get very spiritual," he says, there is a clip called "love is connection." It is not about "the ooey gooey modern notion of love" but about selfless love for everything: "Selfless love for everything is connection," which means finding shared perspective, connecting the dots, seeing how reality moves together. Koe says in October 2022 that this is "what has changed the game for me with my newsletter," and he repeats the passage in a February 2024 compilation, where he names the concrete change: he started connecting fields, noticing that neuroscience and philosophy were "saying the same things." **Source:** The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md; The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md.

The three ideas fit together into one argument. Comparison isolates and produces tension; connection relates and produces enjoyment; enjoyment is what mystics call love; and the practical expression of love-as-connection, for a writer, is synthesis, the work of seeing that two fields describe the same reality. This explains why synthesis is so central to Koe's model of writing and of the generalist (Chapters 20 and 22): it is not only a content strategy but, in his framing, a way of practicing the perceptual stance that produces enjoyment.

One interpretive note. "Connection" appears in two places in this section: as the second term of the happiness formula (connection to something greater, meaning contribution) and as the lens opposed to comparison (connection as seeing relations). Koe does not explicitly join them. A reasonable reading is that they are two faces of the same stance, outward (giving to a larger whole) and perceptual (seeing yourself as part of it), but that reading is the book's, not his.

#### The good, the true and the beautiful

The third answer comes from a 2025 video and concerns the content of a good life rather than the feeling of happiness. "A good life stems from the pursuit of the good, the true and the beautiful," which he glosses as the pursuit of truth as a whole. He uses the triad to justify a set of four daily "focus habits," which he presents as the "offense" of a changed life; the habits themselves are developed elsewhere in the book and are not reconstructed here. **Source:** You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md.

He maps each term to a kind of truth:

| Term | Kind of truth | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| The good | Intersubjective truth (the transcript reads "inner subjective") | What is good for humanity or for a community; doing what is right |
| The true | Objective truth | What scientific study finds; science derives data, data is experience, and experience is physical, mental and spiritual; learning how things really are |
| The beautiful | Subjective truth | The realm of aesthetics; noticing and creating beauty around you |

**Complementary context:** The triad of the good, the true and the beautiful goes back to Plato and runs through Western philosophy. The mapping of the three onto intersubjective, objective and subjective truth closely resembles Ken Wilber's "Big Three" (We, It and I), a framework Koe uses elsewhere (Chapter 38). In this passage Koe does not attribute either the triad or the mapping.

He then applies the triad to work, which he calls the central pillar of life. Your work or project represents the good because "entrepreneurship is the art of solving problems in other people's lives": you contribute to a community or audience by filling a role that improves their lives. Work also has a bad side, which he describes as working for someone else while unconscious of the work's effect on society; his example is an employee at a desk job who never notices that the company coordinates with another company that drops bombs in another country. Work integrates the true because you must learn and produce objective results for others. It integrates the beautiful because you express the depths of your personality, and the best businesses and brands have personality. His summary: "Work that doesn't feel like work is the intersection of what the world needs (the good), what you have experience with (the true), and what you deeply care about (the beautiful)." Work done right "is synonymous with life," the pillar everything else rests on. **Source:** same.

This passage closes a loop with the happiness formula. The good corresponds to the contribution term (connection to something greater); the true corresponds to resistance overcome (learning, results, skill); and the beautiful adds something the formula lacked, the expression of the self. Taken together, they explain why Koe treats the one-person business not merely as a way to make money but as the vehicle of a good life: it is the place where all three can be pursued at once.

### 39.2 Expectation, Suffering and Chosen Struggle

If happiness is resistance overcome, then resistance is not an accident in a good life but one of its ingredients. This section follows the chain of reasoning Koe builds around that idea. It starts with a diagnosis (the gap between expectation and reality creates tension), moves to a reinterpretation of discomfort (the same heat is unbearable on a walk and pleasant in a sauna), refines the vocabulary of suffering (from "suffering is good" in 2023 to "pain versus suffering" in 2026), turns the argument into a choice (since you will suffer anyway, choose what you suffer for), generalizes it into a principle about reality (struggle at every scale produces something new), and ends with Koe's 2026 theory of where meaning comes from.

#### The gap between expectation and reality

A March 2023 video opens with the thesis that organizes the rest of the section: "the space between expectation and reality creates tension that causes pain." He announces three perspectives from which to understand this: expectation versus reality, concept versus experience, and what is versus what isn't. **Source:** Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md. This section develops the first; the distinction between concept and experience belongs to Chapter 40.

An expectation, in his definition, is "the assumption that something should or will happen." Expectations are beliefs, and most were conditioned from a young age. Western children are told they should get a high-paying job, become a doctor or a lawyer. Children are told they will go to church on Sunday, and they either believe it or rebel; the rebellion creates tension, overwhelm and uncertainty, and it can lead to a different (hopefully better) path if the person can zoom out and think critically. **Source:** same. This is the conditioning described in Chapter 1, seen from the angle of its emotional cost.

The mechanism he describes is a narrowing. Expectations narrow the mind toward a predictable future and can close it to other possible futures, because the future feels "already set." The standard Western expectation (school, job, retirement at 65) leads people to outsource their capacity to act on anything else. Their actions follow the expectation, and that usually leads to more suffering, though not, he specifies, to absolute misery. The chain runs from expectation to perception to choice: a narrow perspective created by expectations influences how you perceive a situation; perception influences choices that stay narrowly aligned with the perspective; and "choices are the bricks that build your future self and life." **Source:** same. The image of choices as bricks connects this diagnosis to the identity model of Part II: an expectation you never chose becomes, through a long series of small choices, a self you never chose.

The same video describes a second source of tension: projection. The mind's flight out of the present moment creates tension (he hedges: "not all the time"). Unless resolved, the tension lingers. It produces the familiar state of feeling lost, overwhelmed or uncertain about the future, in which you do not know what to pursue next because you lack the clarity "that discomfort provides with time." Instead of leaning into the discomfort to reach clarity faster, we fight it, and so prevent the discomfort from "collecting into the clarity that we seek." Being lost, he says, means missing a piece of a puzzle, and "that puzzle can only be found with aligned movement": moving forward in a way that serves a goal and gathering, along the way, the pieces that create clarity. **Source:** same. The phrase "aligned movement" is his: motion in the direction of a goal, which produces the information that thinking alone cannot. It anticipates Section 39.7, where the way out of the lost phase is exposure and experiment rather than reflection alone.

The prescription comes from a February 2023 video. "If you were to change your expectations to actually align with what reality is and what is happening in the situation that you are in, and be with it, flow with it, you would then start to realize that discomfort is a part of the human experience, and you would also find that the interpretation of your thoughts is the culprit of your suffering." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md. Two claims are packed in the sentence. Discomfort is a normal part of being human, not a sign that something has gone wrong. And the suffering added to discomfort comes from interpretation, from what the mind says about the discomfort.

#### "What should be": the sauna, the cold and radical acceptance

The same video gives the clearest illustration of how interpretation turns discomfort into suffering. "We live in a delusion that we want to make progress without it being hard, so we become slaves to what should be, and our should be is easy." People "go insane at the thought of going on a walk outside when it's too hot, but then they will go into a sauna without complaint, and the difference here is expectation and intention." Koe grants that a hot walk and a sauna are not the same experience, but insists that "you can go and walk in hot weather without complaining and causing unnecessary pain for yourself." Cold works the same way: the automatic reaction is to fight, shiver and complain, which makes sense because cold is uncomfortable, "so we focus on the compulsive negative thoughts that flood our brain and we get stuck in this loop of thinking." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md.

"What should be" is a phrase he uses as a term: the expectation of how things ought to go, which for most people means "easy," and which enslaves them because reality rarely matches it.

The sauna image had appeared a year earlier, in July 2022, as Koe's own way of painting an idea from Alan Watts. He first quotes Watts, warning that the quote "may not make sense to a lot of people until you directly experience it": when you are out in the cold you start shivering because you are resisting the cold, tightening your muscles against it, and you get "the staggers"; instead, "take it easy, go with the cold, relax." **Source:** Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md. Watts's point, as Koe uses it, is that resistance amplifies discomfort, and that not resisting the inevitable waves of life is a way of flowing with them.

Then Koe extends it. Think of the sauna as a struggle in your life, "a period in your life where you are being put in the furnace of life to be refined by it," and learn the lesson from it. Going into a sauna is intentional: you know it will be hot. If you were outside, not expecting heat, not intentional or mindful about it, you would complain the whole time and get upset. "You don't get upset in a sauna. You go in the sauna, it's hot, you know it's gonna be hot, and you calm down and you let it do what it's supposed to do." Applied to life: "you let life teach you the lesson it's supposed to teach you." **Source:** same. The mechanism is precise. The physical stimulus is the same; what changes the response is expectation (you know it will be hot) and intention (you chose to go in). A struggle entered deliberately is a furnace that refines; the same struggle met with the expectation that life should be easy is merely an injustice to complain about.

The *Art of Focus* summary (January 2024) compresses the idea into two of its principles. Principle 11: "The cause of most people's suffering is the expectations people have compared to the reality of a situation. The present moment isn't supposed to be anything other than what it's being." Principle 12: "When you practice radical acceptance you begin to see situations for what they are; there is nothing you can think that will change the situation." **Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md. "Radical acceptance" here means seeing a situation as it is, without the overlay of what it should be. It does not mean passivity: elsewhere in the same system, acceptance is the precondition for effective action, because a mind fighting reality has no attention left to change it.

#### Pain and suffering: how the vocabulary changes

Koe's use of the word "suffering" changes between 2023 and 2026, and the change matters because it alters what he recommends.

In May 2023 he says "suffering is a very good thing when you perceive it that way." As a side note, he adds that one thing he does not much like about Buddhist teachings is the phrase "life is suffering"; he knows it is one of their teachings, but people "misconstrue it all the time." Suffering is part of life; you are not trying to get rid of it, you are trying to flow with it, "and that's what some Buddhist teachers are saying." Suffering is not bad, and it is not that it isn't supposed to be there. **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md. A month later he goes further: struggle or suffering is necessary for progress (developed below under "novel emergence").

In May 2026 he draws a distinction that redistributes the meaning. Among the things to do in a hard period, the first is to keep increasing the gap between impulse and response, to "work your psychic muscle," to expand and contract the mind and become familiar with those states of consciousness (the practice is described in Section 39.4). The second: "Distinguish pain from suffering. Pain is a feature of life. Suffering is the identity's refusal to accept the event." Becoming more conscious and open-minded "doesn't remove pain, but it removes the second layer." His example is a businessman whose revenue drops. He "still feels the punch," but he does not need to feel the spiral that follows: "this shouldn't be happening to me. I feel like a failure. My life is ruined." **Source:** How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md.

| | 2023 usage | 2026 usage |
|---|---|---|
| The inevitable difficulty of life | "Suffering" (good when perceived as such; flow with it) | "Pain" (a feature of life) |
| The added layer of resistance and interpretation | "The interpretation of your thoughts" (culprit of suffering); "what should be" | "Suffering" (the identity's refusal to accept the event; the second layer) |
| Recommendation | Accept and flow with suffering | Accept pain; reduce suffering by widening the gap between impulse and response |

The two positions are compatible if the "suffering" of 2023 is read as the "pain" of 2026, and the 2026 unit itself is presented as complementing the earlier one rather than retracting it. What changed is the label: the word "suffering" moved from the unavoidable part to the avoidable part. Koe gives no reason for the change; the 2026 version rests on the identity framework of Part II (the identity reacts to events that threaten it as if they threatened the body) and on the gap between impulse and response that the book has used since Chapter 6. A reader moving between his videos should check which sense is in play, because "suffering is good" (2023) and "suffering is the avoidable layer" (2026) sound contradictory and are not.

**Complementary context:** A similar distinction is common in contemporary Buddhist teaching, often summarized as "pain is inevitable, suffering is optional," and the Buddha's parable of the "second arrow" (the first arrow is the painful event; the second is our reaction to it) makes the same point. Koe does not cite either.

#### Life is suffering: choose what you suffer for

If difficulty cannot be avoided, the practical question is which difficulty to accept. A November 2023 video gives Koe's answer, and it begins with an observation about routine. "Routines are comfortable and the mind craves order. Routines allow you to focus your attention when conditioned and programmed over time to make action seamless. This is why working a job sucks, but not enough to make you quit and pursue something that sucks more until it sucks less." **Source:** The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md. The phrase "something that sucks more until it sucks less" describes the entrepreneurial path precisely: harder at first than the job, easier and better later. The job persists because its discomfort is moderate and its routine is comforting; the alternative requires accepting more discomfort up front.

Then the central claim: "Life is suffering, and you have the ability to choose what you suffer for every single day. Your goals are the axis of your suffering, and most people are pursuing goals that aren't their own; their suffering doesn't bring fulfillment." **Source:** same. "The axis of your suffering" is his term: your goals determine what you suffer for. Since you will suffer regardless, the only real choice is whether the suffering serves a goal you chose or a goal assigned to you. This ties the argument back to the opening thesis of the book (Chapter 1): you are always pursuing goals, chosen or assigned, and the assigned ones produce suffering without fulfillment.

Every identity carries its own trade-offs. A professional gamer must accept a possible decline in health, "subtracting from other areas of your life so that you can succeed in another." A bodybuilder for whom healthy eating is easy may find that it subtracts from social life, going out and letting loose. "Certain people value certain things based on their identity and where they focus their attention." **Source:** same. The point is not that one trade-off is right. It is that every chosen life costs something, and a person who expects a life without cost is still operating under the expectation of "what should be."

He states the corollary bluntly in a March 2023 video. One thing is guaranteed: it will be difficult; reality does not remove difficulty. Expecting it to be easy because you watched "a little YouTube video on the exact strategies I took to get to $1 million," without accounting for the years of effort before that, is unrealistic. "Ground yourself in reality": there will be days you have to show up and work even if you don't feel like it, "because it isn't supposed to be any other way." And: "those that think it is supposed to be easy are the ones that don't get results." **Source:** Your Future Will Be Determined By 3 Things.md. This is the practical form of the expectation-reality thesis applied to his own audience: people who consume success content form an expectation of ease, and the expectation itself becomes the reason they quit.

Two images from June 2023 describe what chosen struggle demands and what it can produce. The first is a hot air balloon with bags hanging from it on strings. You either go down because you carry too many attachments or go up by letting go of them, and letting go demands sacrifice and change. Koe connects the image to what he calls the Law of Equivalent Exchange: for anything you receive, something of equal or greater value must be given. **Source:** Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md. **Complementary context:** "Equivalent exchange" is a principle associated with alchemy and is best known today from the Japanese manga and anime *Fullmetal Alchemist*, where it is the central law of alchemy. Koe uses it without naming a source.

The second image is also alchemical. At the climax of an inner mental battle, which is a natural low point, you can use the low point through "emotional transmutation." "Transmutation is the action of changing or the state of being changed into another form." Alchemy is often read literally, as turning dirt into gold, just as the concept of God is often read literally, but "all understanding is metaphorical," and metaphorically "you can turn dirt or a low point in your life into gold," something better. He adds a warning against narrow-mindedness: stop trying to disprove things as literally false when their value lies in what they point to. **Source:** same. Emotional transmutation is the inner counterpart of the sauna: the sauna changes how you meet the difficulty; transmutation changes what the difficulty becomes afterward.

Finally, chosen struggle has a moment that makes it bearable: the "breakthrough point." In the kayaking story told in full in Section 39.3, the moment of seeing the shore gave the exhausted group "a huge burst of inspiration." Koe maps it to "the breakthrough point that so many successful people talk about, where you go through all this trial and error for so long, but eventually you get to this point where you see the shore, you have that breakthrough," and you think, "okay, yeah, I can actually do this." **Source:** Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md. The breakthrough does not end the struggle. It comes after hours of giving up and regaining hope, and what it provides is the fuel for the final, most intense push.

#### Struggle as a universal principle: novel emergence

Koe does not treat struggle as a fact of human psychology only. He treats it as a principle of reality, visible at every scale, and this is where his philosophy of life takes its most cosmological form.

In January 2023: "This is supposed to be difficult. Life is difficult." Struggle and conflict are "baked into the system," visible at every level "going up and up the ladder of everything": not only in "your little brain" but on a cosmic scale, where stars explode all the time, and on a social scale, where wars are being fought now. "Struggle is a universal thing," and it is very unwise to think it should not be part of your life. Once you realize that it is, and that it is the secret to unlocking the profound lessons that are born after the battle is resolved, "the creative emergence after that," also reflected at all scales, "that's when things get good." His recommendation: fall in love with the struggle or challenge by leaning into it; it gets easier with time. **Source:** How To Do Whatever The Fck You Want.md.

In June 2023 he formalizes the idea as the first of two principles that underlie his four-phase model of life (Section 39.6), and gives it a name: novel emergence. "After every battle or conflict, struggle at any plane of existence, physical, mental, cosmic, animalistic... when that tension is released, something new emerges." He gives three examples at three scales:

- Cosmic: a star accumulates tension and eventually explodes, creating atomic nuclei that become "the resource for creation in the cosmos."
- Societal: after a world war, many perceive the event as bad, and there are obviously bad things in it (destruction), but "on the other side there is medical advancements that have changed the path of human evolution."
- Personal: in our minds there is conflict, struggle, "this deep emotional trench where we fight with ourselves until the tension finally resolves itself by us letting go, and we let the vision emerge, we let the insight crash into our psyche."

**Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md.

The lesson he draws is direct: "struggle or suffering is a necessary part of life. Every single person experiences it, and it is supposed to be there, because without it progress would not be made, we wouldn't evolve, we wouldn't be here, we would have died off already." Without struggle "there's no problems to solve to move forward, there's nothing to focus our attention on to build and create towards." **Source:** same.

These cosmic and historical examples should be read as illustrations of a pattern Koe perceives, not as scientific or historical arguments. **Complementary context:** It is true that heavier chemical elements are formed in stars and dispersed by stellar explosions (supernovae), and that some medical techniques advanced rapidly in wartime. Neither fact establishes a general law that conflict produces novelty, and Koe does not claim to have demonstrated one; he uses them as images of the inner process he cares about, the "emotional trench" from which insight emerges. The closest scientific relative of the idea in his corpus is Ilya Prigogine's notion that ordered structures can emerge from systems pushed far from equilibrium (Chapter 5), which he cites elsewhere.

A March 2024 passage turns the principle into a description of daily life. "Problems don't ever go away; they just get better and they get more fun to solve." "All we're doing here is we're just constantly solving problems or avoiding problems, and that's the cause of pain." At any moment you are in one of three states: "we're either leaning into the battle, the conflict, the problem, or we're sitting back and we're marinating in it and we're just not moving, or... we are in the present moment or we are meditating and we are practicing and getting ready for that next round of heat, which is problem solving." **Source:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md.

| State | What it is | Its effect |
|---|---|---|
| Leaning into the problem | Active engagement with the battle | Progress; the problem gets "better" and "more fun" |
| Marinating in it | Sitting back, not moving, avoiding | The cause of pain |
| Present / preparing | Rest, meditation, practice before the "next round of heat" | Recovery and readiness |

A terminological caution: here "marinating" is the passive state that causes pain. Elsewhere in the corpus Koe uses the same word approvingly, for sitting with pain long enough to become fed up and change, and in Section 39.7 "marinate your mind" means immersing yourself in information about a goal. Context distinguishes the three uses.

The three-state model also shows why struggle is not the same as constant effort. The third state, presence and preparation, is a legitimate part of the cycle. This anticipates the balance of peace and progress (Section 39.4) and the idea that a lost phase is preparation for the next round (Section 39.6).

#### Meaning generators: struggle, curiosity and status

The argument about struggle culminates, in 2026, in an explicit theory of where meaning comes from. It has a 2023 antecedent in a single image: "If life feels meaningless, it's because you aren't training your responsibility muscle. You haven't lifted the emotional weight to add more to the bar and see progress on a deeper level than muscle." **Source:** A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md. Responsibility, in this image, works like resistance training: meaning comes from progressively heavier emotional loads that you choose to carry, and a life without load feels meaningless for the same reason a body without training feels weak.

In February 2026, in a video about the future of work, Koe presents three "meaning generators." **Source:** The Future Of Work (& The New High-Income Skill Stack).md.

1. **Struggle** is the engine of progress. It is "what you choose to struggle for, because that defines what your purpose is." Not struggle imposed on you, not an assigned goal, but what you consciously choose: the creative who chooses to master a craft, build an audience, say something true. "That struggle generates meaning." In practice, it means making deliberate choices, rejecting conformity, and choosing your own problems to solve.
2. **Curiosity** is the direction of progress. Koe defines it as "nonlinear attention," "following threads that don't make sense yet." AI cannot be curious, he says, and curiosity is how you solve problems or discover ideas no one else is thinking about. His recurring example: two people reading the same book will not highlight the same lines. One may find it trash and the other the best book ever, because each has a situated point in life, a perspective and accumulated experience that create a unique way of viewing the world, "that we don't think is unique because we live in our heads." That uniqueness is your edge, and you have to learn to channel it. In practice: filter signal from noise and follow the threads that interest you, especially when everyone else is chasing trends.
3. **Status**, which "gets a bad rap," so he prefers to call it **recognition**, for your work or contribution. "It's the signal that your struggle mattered to someone," that "you're not here alone in this void." It is the proof of contribution. In practice, money becomes a tool for agency rather than just a productivity metric.

| Generator | Function in the theory | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Struggle | Engine of progress | Choosing your own problems; mastering a craft |
| Curiosity | Direction of progress | Following threads that don't make sense yet |
| Status / recognition | Proof of contribution | Being recognized for work that mattered to someone; money as fuel for agency |

The three generators, he argues, are "quite literally the foundation of a story": a character who struggles toward something, a direction chosen by curiosity, and a recognition that the struggle mattered. Stories matter going forward because "your brain is a story engine": we crave novelty, drama, myth and meaning, especially when not in survival mode, in leisure, when jobs have been replaced and basic needs are met. That is what people will seek and pay for, because they will not be worried about bills; "they're kind of just lost." Those who do not understand this, he warns, will end up like the character in the film *WALL-E* in the hover chair with the soda and the screen, 300 pounds and unable to move. **Source:** same.

This theory connects the present chapter to Chapter 36's "meaning economy": if AI removes the need to struggle for survival, people will need to choose their struggles, and the generators of meaning become both a personal necessity and a market. It also reorganizes the happiness formula of Section 39.1. Struggle corresponds to "resistance overcome"; recognition corresponds to "connection to something greater"; curiosity, the new element, explains how a person finds which struggle to choose. The change from 2023 to 2026 is a systematization: scattered formulations (the responsibility muscle, "the meaning of human existence is to create," the claim that status is not bad when it is conscious) become a three-part framework, with status renamed as recognition and promoted to an explicit source of meaning.

### 39.3 Polarity and Impermanence

The previous section treated difficulty as something to choose and use. This section treats the alternation between good and bad periods as a structural feature of life. Koe builds it from four related ideas: highs and lows follow each other as in an oscillating system; a life without lows is not happier but emptier; every quality exists only in relation to its opposite (polarity); and nothing lasts (impermanence). The section ends with the story he uses as the master illustration of all four, a kayaking trip through a storm, and the framework he draws from it.

#### Highs and lows: order from chaos and the harmonic oscillator

The earliest formulation, from June 2022, is compressed and abstract. "Clarity stems from chaos, order stems from chaos." There is the ebb and the flow, reflected in "the highs and the lows... in frequency of information." When you hit a high in life, it is "almost inevitably going to go into a low point." Labeling these periods as positive or negative, good or bad, "is really just sense or nonsense, or order and chaos," and projecting your feelings and emotions onto them brings that energy and experience into the present. **Source:** How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md. The claim has two parts. Descriptively, lows follow highs. Evaluatively, calling a low "bad" is a projection, and the projection makes the present worse by importing into it the emotion attached to the label. The phrase "order stems from chaos" is also the first sign of a shift that the book traced in Chapter 5: entropy and chaos, first described as the enemy, become in later years the precondition of a higher order.

A month later he gives the pattern a model borrowed from physics. He offers what he calls "the 80/20" of the harmonic oscillator: a system that tries to maintain equilibrium. If it is displaced downward, it tries to return to equilibrium but overshoots in the opposite direction, so a low brings "an inevitable but somewhat equal high," and the movement continues until the system reaches equilibrium again. "Our mind is a harmonic oscillator." There are aspects of life you cannot control, and the model explains why changes produce swings: a high will be followed by a counter-force low, and a low by a high, before things settle. **Source:** Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md.

**Complementary context:** In physics, an ideal harmonic oscillator (a mass on a spring with no friction) swings forever with the same amplitude; it is a damped oscillator, one that loses energy to friction, that swings with decreasing amplitude and settles at equilibrium. Koe's "80/20" version, with swings that eventually settle, is closer to the damped case. As elsewhere, he uses the physics as a metaphor for a psychological pattern, not as a claim that the mind obeys the equation.

The practical use of the model is to expect the counter-swing. A person who has just changed something and feels a surge of motivation should expect a dip; a person in a dip after a change should expect recovery. Neither swing is a verdict on the change.

#### High highs and low lows: why a flat life is the real misery

From the oscillation Koe draws a conclusion that runs against the usual picture of happiness. In October 2023, observing society, he says many people are not happy, and he means "miserable" in a specific sense: their life is "so dull, it's so flat and predictable and mindless." They may even be happy in some cases, "but they aren't experiencing the high highs and low lows that... contrast that actually brings meaning to life. That alone is miserable." **Source:** How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md.

He calls it "a dangerous trap," because "you don't think you're miserable, you're comfortable, but you are so much more miserable than the people that look miserable in the low lows," since those lows are what make the high highs possible. "You can't have a high high without a low low," he says, and he calls this "a universal principle." His metaphor is a plucked string: "when you pluck a string... it goes up and it goes down, but if you don't pluck the string, if you don't add risk or stress or throw yourself into the unknown, that event, that traumatic event that causes growth, then you're not going to live a meaningful life." The principle, he says, is everywhere: in music (highs, lows, chorus, melody; "the universe, uni-verse, one song, everything happening in harmony"), in the climaxes of stories, in games. "Everything you see is pointing at meaning, and that dull robotic mechanical mediocre existence is not meaningful." The consequence is a fork: either you eventually break out "to live that uncertain and chaotic life that brings meaning," or you stay stuck "slowly making progress up this corporate ladder that others assign to you." **Source:** same.

A month later, in a video about writing as a high-income skill, he states the implication for happiness directly. Emotions with highs and lows give people meaning and feeling. "Happiness is kind of a false metric to pursue": if you are happy all the time, "that's not happiness, you're just kind of stuck there, it's normal equilibrium, homeostasis." It balances out to normal or boring. At a constant level, "your baseline increases to that and you are no longer happy," so you must pursue more and more. Human understanding is based on highs and lows, and "we need to bake that into whatever we write, speak or communicate." **Source:** The Most Important High-Income Skill To Learn (In The Next 10 Years).md. The last sentence explains why this philosophical point appears in a video about writing: stories and persuasive writing work by contrast (Chapter 23), and they work because human understanding works by contrast.

**Complementary context:** The phenomenon Koe describes, in which a constant pleasant state becomes the new normal and stops being felt as pleasant, is studied in psychology as hedonic adaptation (the "hedonic treadmill," a term introduced by Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell in 1971). Koe does not cite this literature.

There is a tension here that Koe does not resolve. In February 2023 he titled a video "Happiness Is A Skill," and in mid-2023 he repeated that happiness is something you create and practice. In November 2023 he calls happiness "a false metric to pursue." The two can coexist if "happiness is a skill" is understood as the skill of handling highs, lows and impermanence (which is, in fact, what that video teaches, as the next subsections show), and "false metric" is understood as a rejection of pursuing a constant state. That reconciliation is the book's reading; Koe leaves the two statements side by side.

#### Polarity: nothing exists without its opposite

The principle behind "high highs and low lows" is what Koe calls polarity. In a June 2023 video he says polarity deepens the understanding of meaning: happiness cannot exist without a sad reference point, a cup without a hand, life without suffering. The climax of a story is most memorable when the battle peaks, the future is uncertain, the villain is about to win, and the hero triumphs. If there were no low points, life would become "flat, dull, linear, lifeless, mechanical, robotic, mindless, mediocre." Life is a dance between order and chaos, yin and yang, push and pull, masculine and feminine, across personal, social, cultural, cosmic and universal planes. "You cannot achieve new heights... if you never accept the battle, lose your ground and propel in a new direction." The low point, the climax of the inner battle, is supposed to be there. **Source:** Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md.

The "Happiness Is A Skill" video (February 2023) draws the consequence for happiness itself. "When we try to isolate and sustain and make sense of this thing called happiness, it ceases to make sense, because it can't make sense without an unhappy reference point." He gives his own example: "I wouldn't be able to set a goal for the future without having a previous experience like breaking up with my girlfriend in Costa Rica and wasting a lot of money and having to fly back." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md. The "unhappy reference point" is a term of his: a low that gives a later high its meaning, and that also supplies the information from which a future goal is set. This is the anti-vision logic of Chapter 7 restated as a philosophy of happiness: you learn what you want by experiencing what you do not want.

From the same premise he criticizes what he calls "the new age positivity movement." "You can think positive thoughts all day long, but what people fail to realize is that every positive thought holds a negative seed, and this is why almost every great success comes after great failure." **Source:** same. The criticism is not of focusing on a goal; elsewhere (2024) Koe defends goal focus as a kind of positive thinking, provided it is not delusional. It is of positivity used as a way to avoid the negative pole, which, in his frame, cannot be avoided because it is half of the same thing.

What should be done with negative emotions, then? Koe gives two answers, and both reject repression.

- **Use them.** In December 2022 he says negative emotions "aren't bad, they exist, they are what they are, and they should be used, not suppressed, but used for good." **Source:** How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md. This is the premise that makes the anti-vision work (Chapter 7): the anger and discomfort it generates are fuel rather than something to avoid. (The unit records that he illustrates the point with a treadmill example; the details are not recoverable from the material.)
- **Let them go so their opposite can appear.** In June 2023 he says most people think hate and negative emotions should be repressed, forgotten or should not exist. But by polarity, hate is the opposing end of love, and you would not understand love without hate; at the same time, "hate is the obstruction of love," and by letting go of it, love reveals itself. The same holds for many emotions and things in life. **Source:** Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md.

The two answers are in mild tension: one treats the negative emotion as fuel to be burned, the other as an obstruction to be removed. Koe does not reconcile them, and the most natural reading is that they apply at different moments. Anger at a life you refuse to keep is useful fuel at the start of a change; hate that persists after it has done its work blocks the positive state it was the opposite of.

#### Impermanence: the supreme law

The second principle underlying Koe's model of life phases (the first was novel emergence) is impermanence. In June 2023 he introduces it as "impermanence and entropy," "both a Buddhist teaching and a systems principle, where things just fall apart with time and nothing lasts forever and nothing is permanent, no matter how permanent it may seem." It goes beyond physical and material things to the phases of your life, which you can notice as patterns "if you choose to reflect consistently on your life." So: the lost phase is not permanent; the phase of absolute progress "where I'm on top of the world is not going to last, it's going to come crashing down"; and the limbo of maintaining consistency will not last either. "Nothing's gonna last, and you have to be ready to observe and move within that." **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md. He then plays on the word universe, apparently glossing it as "uni-verse," "one song," and saying that is what you are "smack in the middle of"; the transcript is ambiguous, but the image matches the "one song" of the October 2023 passage above.

At the end of the same video he calls it "the supreme law": "nothing is permanent. Feeling lost isn't permanent, intense progress isn't permanent, even happiness isn't permanent." These phases come and go, "but you can trust that if you seek to understand them year after year, you will get holistic results with time." The warning: "If you grasp at any phase of existence and attempt to make it permanent, you will experience chaos." And the instruction: "Don't fight with reality, flow with it." **Source:** same.

The "Happiness Is A Skill" video explains, in four steps, why grasping causes unhappiness.

1. **The teaching.** "The main Buddhist teaching is that of impermanence, that is, nothing can be isolated and held static by the mind. This includes ideas, feelings, emotions, people, locations and everything else that we try to latch onto and expect to stay the same forever." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md.
2. **Koe's application to identity.** "The identification with ideology, dogma and other static mental constructs is the source of our suffering. When those things are threatened, or attachment to them is threatened, like the string is plucked, then we are physically threatened." **Source:** same. Here the plucked string, which in the October 2023 passage was the source of meaning, is the threat: when an idea you identify with is challenged, the identity reacts as if the body were in danger. This is the identity mechanism of Chapter 3 described in Buddhist vocabulary.
3. **The mechanism of unhappiness.** "Thinking is not bad... especially when it is critical, but when gone unchecked, compulsive thoughts can create a personal reality of inescapable turmoil." "When one is still, their mind wants to move; when one is moving, their mind wants to be still. Without deliberate practice the mind will try to latch on to the past or the future in an attempt to make the impermanent permanent. This is the cause of unhappiness." **Source:** same.
4. **The secret.** "The secret is to engage the mind with the impermanence of life and flow with it, rather than the mind trying to fit the impermanence of life into some form of permanent structure." **Source:** same.

And what to do with unhappiness when it comes: it "is not to be demonized, because that is against the point. When you feel unhappy, observe that feeling, lean into it, let it point a finger at the moon and present the lesson that life is trying to teach you from that low, [and] lean into the coming high." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** "The finger pointing at the moon" is a traditional Buddhist image, especially common in Zen: the teaching is like a finger pointing at the moon, and one should not mistake the finger for the moon. Koe uses it without attribution and in a slightly different way: the unhappiness is the finger, and the lesson it points to is the moon.

This is where "happiness is a skill" gets its content. The skill is not producing a constant pleasant state. It is a set of moves in relation to impermanence: not demonizing the low, observing it, extracting its lesson, and leaning toward the high that the oscillation will bring. The happiness that results is the capacity to move through the cycle without grasping either end.

#### The waves of life: the Catalina Island storm

Koe's master story for all of this comes from his childhood, and he tells it at length in July 2022 "to paint a life concept." **Source:** Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md.

As a boy scout, he went to weekly meetings at his church, recited the scout oath, and went on monthly camping trips plus one big trip a year (one was to Moab, Utah, for mountain biking, rappelling and white-water rafting). The trip he tells is to Catalina Island, off the coast of California. They drove from Arizona in his father's truck, with a television placed in the middle seat and an Xbox hooked to a portable power charger, playing *Halo 3* survival mode the whole way. On the ferry, while the adults drank Bloody Marys, the children played cards; he had a virgin piña colada. They arrived late and rushed straight to the kayak rental, strapping their bags to the kayaks in order to paddle around the island and camp at different beaches.

For the first hour or two the water was beautiful; dolphins or seals swam beside them. Then a storm hit. Rain, waves of "at least like 10 feet in difference," kayaks flipping over at the bottom of the waves. The group split up. From the top of a wave they could see only the scout leaders' orange oars ahead; they lost sight of the people behind them. His bag fell off. They gave up several times ("just throw us into the side of the thing") and then got bursts of inspiration near the top of a wave, when they saw they were closing in on the leaders. "It was a cycle of going fast, going slow, giving up, losing hope, thinking we're gonna make it, and all of these highs and lows that emulate a very good story." After two or three hours in the storm, during which he truly thought his life would end, they saw the shore, felt "a huge burst of inspiration," paddled as hard as they could, crashed onto the beach, rolled out of the kayaks and lay shivering in the sun. Those who had fallen behind were brought in by security staff navigating the storm. His food had been soaked; the next morning birds had eaten it and scattered it across the campsite. **Source:** same.

The story contains, in miniature, most of the ideas of this section and the previous one: a calm that gives no warning of the storm; a struggle that cannot be controlled; a cycle of hope and despair that repeats within a single afternoon; a breakthrough point (seeing the shore) that fuels a final push without ending the struggle; and an aftermath that is anticlimactic (soaked food, birds). Koe stresses that the highs and lows of the paddling "emulate a very good story," which links the story to his later model of life as chapters (Section 39.6).

From the story he draws a framework, which he presents with a graphic that the transcript does not allow us to reconstruct in detail. The elements he names are the present, the future, and the waves of your life before this moment, which you can remember and "tap back into." In the present there are "the highs, the lows, the inevitable cycles of life that nature provides," like the storm, which could not be controlled. The key point of the graphic is a separation between two things:

1. **The inevitable waves of life**, which you do not control.
2. **A mindful response to those waves**, which you do.

As you go up a high, your mind can take a step back and become aware that you are in a cycle of life. "Your emotions do not have to match those waves." You do not have to go deep into a particular wave and "lose yourself in that moment." **Source:** same. The exact visual layout of the graphic is ambiguous in the transcript; what can be reconstructed with confidence is the distinction itself.

He closes the video with a warning that turns the story into a forecast. Reinvention "is not an easy process": you are either in a kayak going through a storm or on easy waters right now, "but the storm is eventually going to come, and it's going to be inevitable." **Source:** same.

The waves-of-life framework (2022) is the first of the phase models that Section 39.6 traces. It already contains the two ideas that every later version keeps: the alternation is not optional, and what you can choose is your relation to it. What it lacks is names for the phases and a description of what to do in each, which is what the later models add. It also contains a version of detachment (your emotions need not match the waves) that Koe will qualify in 2026, as Section 39.4 shows, by warning that detachment cannot be forced.

### 39.4 Peace and Progress

Koe's short name for the balance he seeks is "peace and progress," and he calls it his motto. Progress is the outward pole: doing, pursuing, building, overcoming resistance. Peace is the inward pole: being, stillness, appreciation, presence. This section assembles the pieces of that idea in a logical order. It begins with the two ways of being fully present (presence and flow), then explains, using Koe's adaptation of a neuroscience framework, why progress alone cannot satisfy and how satisfaction after achievement is cultivated. It then presents the being/doing distinction, the three successive formulations of "balance" (containment, polarity, collapse into one), the personality trait that holds both poles (intense and unbothered), and the 2026 warning that winning the game of progress is often how you discover it was the wrong game.

#### Presence and flow: the goal in the back of the mind

In July 2024 Koe distinguishes two states that are often confused. Flow is "quite similar to being present," which is what spiritual teachers preach. But "presence comes from an open state of focus: you release your mind from attaching to any given thought and become one with life itself." "Flow, on the other hand, comes in handy when in the pursuit of a lofty goal: you become one with the task in front of you, which leads to the actualization of a meaningful goal." **Source:** Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md.

| | Presence | Flow |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of focus | Open: no attachment to any given thought | Narrow: absorbed in a task |
| What you become one with | Life itself | The task in front of you |
| What it serves | Peace | Progress toward a meaningful goal |

The bridge between them is a formula that recurs throughout the corpus: focus on the present while holding the goal in the back of your mind. In March 2023 Koe attributes a version of it to Greek philosophy, which distinguishes "telic" and "atelic" activities ("telos is the Greek word for goal"). He describes them as different states of being, doing nothing versus doing; this rendering is ambiguous in the transcript. When doing, he says, you gain fulfillment by "focusing still on the present moment while holding the goal in the back of your mind": you are still moving toward something, but you are focused on the reality of the situation rather than on the expectation. He adds that everything evolves, and once you actualize a goal you will set another. **Source:** Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md.

**Complementary context:** The terms "telic" and "atelic" are used in contemporary philosophy, notably by Kieran Setiya, to distinguish activities aimed at completion (telic: finishing a project) from activities that are complete at every moment (atelic: walking, conversing), building on Aristotle's distinction between movements aimed at an end and activities that are their own end. Koe's rendering as "doing" versus "doing nothing" is looser than this, but his conclusion (live the telic pursuit in an atelic way, attending to the present) is close to Setiya's.

The same structure appears in the first of what Koe's book *The Art of Focus* calls the three pillars of the good life: focus, energy and experience. He quotes the book: "The ability to focus your attention on a meaningful goal, holding that in the back of your mind as a distraction repellent, knowing exactly how to achieve it through prior experience, and refocusing on the choice in front of you is a superpower." With ample experience, "the feedback loop of focus and energy tightens," and results compound because what you filter from the flood of information is more conducive to meaningful action. **Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md. The goal held "in the back of the mind" acts as a "distraction repellent": it does not occupy attention, but it filters what attention picks up. This is the goal as a lens (Chapter 6) applied to the moment-to-moment experience of a day.

A 2026 passage adds an unexpected route into flow: boredom. True boredom makes you "more likely to enter this intense period of flow, like this monk mode... where you're just making progress nonstop. Because in order to do that, you do need a creative idea... idea flow... something to build." He lists three "flow triggers" that boredom sets up: deep embodiment ("being present with discomfort"), novelty ("boredom forces you to seek new, healthier stimulation") and unpredictability ("not knowing what will emerge from the void"). "So if you don't know what you should do with your life, maybe you should do nothing. And I mean actually nothing. Not the default nothing that everyone else falls into." **Source:** If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md. The "default nothing" is the distracted nothing of scrolling and cheap stimulation; "actually nothing" is the empty time from which, in Koe's experience, ideas emerge. **Complementary context:** "Flow triggers" is a term popularized by Steven Kotler; Koe uses it without attribution, and his list of three does not reproduce Kotler's longer list.

The sequence in this subsection matters for what follows. Presence and flow are both forms of being fully in the present; one serves peace and the other progress; and the goal held lightly in the back of the mind is what lets a person pursue progress without leaving the present.

#### Why progress alone does not satisfy: the here-and-now chemicals

Koe's explanation of why achievement leaves people empty is borrowed, and adapted, from Daniel Z. Lieberman's *The Molecule of More*, which the book discussed in Chapter 11. In a November 2024 video he presents two kinds of neurotransmitters that provide feedback on behavior and perception. **Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md.

The "down chemicals," or "here and nows," correspond to what you see when you look down: your peripersonal space, the things you can grab, possess and control. These chemicals let you "savor, enjoy, or fight and run away from" what you are experiencing. He lists oxytocin, norepinephrine, adrenaline, serotonin, endorphins and endocannabinoids.

The "up chemical" is dopamine alone. It corresponds to what you see when you look outward: the sky, shop windows, television ads, "this constant feed called social media," even memories of a past vacation that make you want it again. What these have in common is that they are "things that you don't have, things that are in your extrapersonal space." Dopamine "motivates you to pursue, to control and to possess what you don't have but want, because it could be useful for your survival." It may not be useful now, but it was in the past: gathering more resources, having more sex and offspring to push your bloodline into the future or go extinct, getting a horse and a sword to fight battles and survive.

| | Down chemicals ("here and nows") | Up chemical |
|---|---|---|
| Molecules (2024 list) | Oxytocin, norepinephrine, adrenaline, serotonin, endorphins, endocannabinoids | Dopamine |
| Space | Peripersonal: what you can grab and control | Extrapersonal: what you do not have |
| Function | Savor, enjoy, or fight/flee what is present | Pursue, control, possess what is absent |
| Typical triggers | What is in front of you | Shop windows, ads, feeds, memories of what you lack |

The list of "down" molecules changes across the corpus. In October 2022 it is oxytocin, serotonin and a word the transcript renders as "neuropanephrine"; in July 2023 serotonin, oxytocin, endorphins and endocannabinoids; in November 2024 the longer list above. This is a refinement of the same borrowed framework, not a contradiction. One terminological slip should also be flagged: in a February 2023 passage (discussed below) Koe speaks of "peripersonal neurotransmitters like dopamine," which inverts the mapping of the 2024 framework, where dopamine is precisely the chemical of extrapersonal space. The 2024 version is the more careful one.

**Complementary context:** Lieberman's book popularizes a real research distinction between dopamine's role in anticipation and pursuit and the role of other systems in present-moment satisfaction, but the clean division into "up" and "down" chemicals is a simplification for general readers. Koe simplifies further. The framework should be read as a useful model of the experience of wanting versus having, not as a precise account of neurochemistry.

In a July 2023 video Koe draws the practical conclusion. While dopamine rises when you desire something outside you that you lack, "there are other neurotransmitters that are called the here-and-now neurotransmitters, like serotonin, oxytocin, endorphins and endocannabinoids, that give you satisfaction and appreciation for what's in front of you," the things in your personal space. Some people reach these states with drugs (he names MDMA, marijuana and alcohol, says he uses them occasionally and is not judging), but there are more sustainable ways to bring satisfaction, fulfillment and enjoyment into everyday life with what you already have. **Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md.

His principle: "The key to the good life is a disciplined balance between meaningful dopamine sources and creating satisfaction when you achieve those goals." **Source:** same. Dopamine drives the pursuit; satisfaction practices sustain enjoyment after the acquisition. The principle leads to "choosing your dopamine sources wisely," which is the subject of Chapter 11. Here the emphasis is on the other half, the satisfaction that most people never cultivate.

The cost of neglecting that half is illustrated in a February 2024 passage about the lifestyles promoted online. Their glamour is "for dopamine": you see something you want, and once you get it, "if you don't appreciate it and make it a part of your life, then that is going to fade very quickly." What social media shows is a highlight reel. Because these glamorous things are not truly sustainable, he frames his advice around moderation and sustainability, and proposes a business model that lets you build your ideal lifestyle "through practice, experimentation and iteration," a creative income from your skills, interests and expertise. **Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md. (His example is the "digital nomad" image of working from a beach.)

Koe describes three practices for creating satisfaction after acquisition. All three come from the July 2023 video. **Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md.

**1. Mindfulness and detail.** Most people live in a superficial state in which everything around them is normal. The practice is to notice what your senses provide. On a walk, see details you have never seen: the leaves of trees, a bush you never looked at closely, the concrete under your feet, blades of grass, the shine of light on passing cars. When you cook, notice differences in texture, taste and feel; pick the ingredients apart. When you listen to a song, dig for the subtle layers in the background: "those were intentional by the creator, they want you to notice them." This is why food critics, music lovers and yogis enjoy their activities: they are immersed in the present and flooded with here-and-now neurotransmitters, "much more sustainable than constantly pursuing that cheap dopamine hit." It is getting in touch with reality, which is "an ever flowing quality of experience, and we're missing out on the depth of that experience every single day." Applied to possessions: when something you acquired is "borderlining boredom" and its shine fades, you can learn to enjoy it by noticing detail, "far beyond just your senses as well."

**2. Developing an intrinsic philosophy.** His case is a young bodybuilder who started going to the gym for vanity, "but they stayed for the therapy." They wanted a strong, aesthetic physique to attract a partner and command respect. After a year or two, progress slowed drastically. They had built a good body; what next? It had not really changed how many people they attracted. Perhaps it bumped them up the social hierarchy a little and brought a little more attention. Was it still worth seven or eight hours a week? So they looked inward: What has this brought to my life? Why do I keep showing up? Where would I have spent that time otherwise? How has it affected the people I meet, the conversations I can have, the career opportunities open to me? They realized that "their material pursuit was deeply immaterial, as are most," a fact people glance over. The pursuit had given them an identity that shaped good decisions and kept them from being like "the mindless masses who pursue nothing."

A 2022 version of the same arc, from a video about reinvention after a breakup, shows it from the inside. You start going to the gym; many do it for vanity, but eventually it becomes a passion because they see the depth in it: they appreciate it for what it is, appreciate the mechanics of learning to lift, appreciate the progress and the strength gains, and "the survival mechanism of their brain rewarding that with dopamine because they are making progress by pursuing their own goals." Later, in the gym, they become aware of what the problem actually was (a vanity or self-esteem problem) and can slowly fix it through self-education, questioning and following curiosity. **Source:** Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md. The two passages describe the same movement, from an external motive to an internal one, but they emphasize different mechanisms: the 2022 version emphasizes depth and the dopamine of self-chosen progress; the 2023 version emphasizes deliberate reflection on what the pursuit has given.

**3. Learning and education.** Ninety-five percent of people, Koe says, would scoff at someone who buys a Porsche GT3, because they see only the surface and cannot see that a person's life and identity are more than a snapshot. What if the buyer were a mechanic who derives deep satisfaction from digging into the machinery? And what if an ordinary person made the same purchase but decided to learn, to "learn their way into a deeper crevice of reality through education": study the car's parts and history, learn to drive it better, start going to the track? The car becomes a consistent part of their lifestyle, bringing enjoyment and flow. "They construct a house of meaning under the foundation of material. Most people keep laying a foundation and never build anything worth keeping." **Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md. "House of meaning" is his term for the structure of understanding, skill and relationship that turns a material object into a source of lasting satisfaction.

A February 2023 passage states the general principle behind the three practices and places it in Koe's view of wealth. The "new rich," he says, are not about billions, fancy cars and mansions. Those are not bad: they can be useful goals for exposing yourself to more experience, and "a superficial pursuit of buying a car is better than sitting around and doing nothing." But once you have them you learn quickly that "you need a philosophical pursuit of mastery behind the possessions that you acquire in your life," whether they are skills or material things. The mechanism: wanting and acquiring run on dopamine (here he says "peripersonal neurotransmitters like dopamine," the inversion noted above); once the pursuit is over and the dopamine fades, can you shift to "the here-and-now neurotransmitters, aka the present moment," and appreciate what you have by developing a relationship with it? Will you study mechanics, work on the car, keep your awe for its craftsmanship, study that craftsmanship so you appreciate it more, or will it become "a hunk of metal in your garage" once the dopamine of the pursuit is gone? **Source:** Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md.

This position on material things is itself a refinement. In early 2023 Koe says you don't want the mansion but "what's underneath it," and looking at it is cheap dopamine. Later in 2023 he says "money doesn't buy happiness" is a half-truth: material goods have real utility if they are not superficial pursuits. The philosophical pursuit of mastery is the condition that makes the difference.

The November 2024 video reduces all of this to one habit. "Dopamine is the anticipation molecule, so if we want to enjoy the things we have we must train our minds away from the future-oriented and move back to a sustained state of present-oriented": soak in the present moment, adopt a mindfulness habit, go for a walk, "stare at a leaf and find joy in the translucent web of life encapsulated in such a small green object. That's the main habit you're probably missing." Dopamine detoxes help for this reason (you remove every external "more," even the possibility of desiring anything, get stuck in boredom and are "forced to find the appreciation in the boredom"), "but you don't have to do a dopamine detox, you simply have to change how you approach, use and leverage the technology that is destroying most people's lives." **Source:** The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md. Eleven months later Koe published a full thirty-day detox protocol, and in 2026 a reduced one-week version; the position moves from "optional tool" to "recommended periodic reset" (Chapter 11 develops this). The habit itself, retraining attention from the future to the present, is constant.

#### Being and doing: two modalities of pure living

The neurochemical framework explains why progress needs a counterweight. The "Happiness Is A Skill" video (February 2023) names the two poles as states of mind. "There are two states of mind to become aware of: being and doing, or from another perspective, peace and progress, or stillness and movement. When your attention is focused on either of these modalities of pure living, you tap into the present moment: so pure peace or pure progress, where your attention is fully engaged in either the task at hand or no task at hand." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md.

"Pure living," in this passage, is a term for full engagement of attention, and its two modalities correspond to the presence and flow of the first subsection: pure peace is attention fully engaged with no task; pure progress is attention fully engaged with a task. What the two have in common is that both put you in the present. What they exclude is the middle state of distracted half-attention, which belongs to neither.

Which activities produce these states is personal. "The activities that launch you into these blissful states of mind, either being or doing, are personal. You must observe, hypothesize, experiment, fail, learn and repeat. This never-ending process, that is, even once you've reached some form of objective success, because even that isn't permanent." **Source:** same. The method is the scientific method applied to one's own life, and the reason it never ends is impermanence (Section 39.3): even a successful configuration stops working.

The same video gives a heuristic for diagnosing which pole is missing: "If you aren't tired when you go to bed and excited when you wake up, you need intensity and goals." **Source:** same. The heuristic tests for a lack of progress; the rest of the corpus supplies the opposite test (restlessness, inability to enjoy what you have, the "binary" compulsion described below) for a lack of peace.

He also distinguishes two types of happiness that correspond to the poles. "There are two types of happiness: one is derived from achieving goals, the second is derived from achieving nothing. But this balance of peace and progress has to be upheld by a sense of mastery, otherwise it becomes a shallow pursuit that will leave you disappointed." **Source:** same. The "sense of mastery" is the same condition as the philosophical pursuit of mastery above: progress without it becomes a shallow chase of the next acquisition, and peace without it becomes mere idleness.

#### Balance: three formulations

What does it mean to balance the two poles? Koe's answer changes over about six months, and a guest pushes him to change it.

**First formulation: containment (November 2022).** Discussing the value of time alone, Koe defines the word. Balance "does not mean split down the middle. Balance means a conscious effort to prevent either extreme from taking over." His application: if you want to make a dent, become a value creator, start a one-person business, or be more original and creative, you will inevitably have to expose yourself to people, because business is transactional (someone gives you money in exchange for value). But you must also create time for being alone: sitting, contemplating, meditating on the ideas you want to put out. **Source:** Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md. "Balance" here is a word used in a sense of its own: not equality between the poles but vigilance against either one taking over.

**The motto and the "spiritual materialist" (March 2023).** In a long conversation with Dickie Bush, Bush names progression as a core value: "anytime I feel that I'm not progressing it means I need something new to take on." Koe answers: "the balance, peace and progress, that's my motto lately." Progress "feels amazing" but can become extreme without peace to balance it; his examples are skipping a week in Sedona or giving up the daily walk. He says he wants to write about "the spiritual warrior or the spiritual materialist." He does not see getting rich, fancy cars and a lot of money as "the picture of absolute evil," but if they are unbalanced or pursued only for status, "then why?" He pursues bigger things because "it's taught me so much... it's led to me refining my lifestyle." It is "not absolute progress like a workaholic, but it's not absolute peace like a monk," and he feels "those two polar ends are collapsing as we move forward, because there's so much information... that we're able to synthesize it and understand life from this balanced perspective." **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. "Spiritual materialist" and "spiritual warrior" are his coinages for a person who pursues large material goals without either treating them as evil or making them the point.

**Dickie Bush's counter-proposal: balance through extremes.** Bush pushes back. "Finding that balance through extremes is how I've thought about it, where I try to avoid operating in the middle of... peace and progress, because that's where all the horrible results come." "A little bit of progress but a lot of peace" yields bad results. "Work-life balance never made sense to me... I always think of it as polarity": either heads-down fifteen-hour days for long stretches or completely off-grid with the phone in airplane mode. "Any kind of intensity: bring it or totally shut it off." He applies this at every time scale: macro (work very hard for twelve weeks, then take a week off), micro (fifty minutes of work, ten minutes of break), daily (intense work periods, then complete rest). The opposite, spending most of the day, week and year "kind of in the middle," means "you look up five years later and you're in the same spot." **Source:** same. This is Bush's idea, not Koe's.

Justin Welsh, interviewed in December 2021, describes the risk on the other side of Bush's polarity. He calls himself binary with food, drink and work: "I'm usually zero or one." Sit him in front of a computer and he will stay there all day: Twitter, LinkedIn, blog posts, fixing the website, changing processes. "I feel like every moment of the day where I'm not working on my business is a wasted moment, which is a problem." Walking is the deliberate mechanism that "gets me away from that." Koe, who says he takes "five, six walks a day, they're not that long," frames walking as crucial not for health but "for idea generation and just being able to perform well," and Welsh answers "100%." **Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md. A "binary person," in Welsh's phrase, is someone who operates only at zero or one. Bush's polarity requires the "zero" to be real; Welsh's confession shows how easily the "one" swallows it.

**Second formulation: "even moderation" (March 2023, same conversation).** Koe answers Bush with a saying whose source he does not know ("I actually don't know if anyone said this"): "everything in moderation, even moderation." "The key word there is even moderation, meaning you aren't in the middle, you're moderating how much time you expose yourself to each extreme, and letting that... teach you the lessons of life." **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md. This reconciles the two views: you live at the extremes, as Bush says, but you moderate how long you spend at each, which preserves the containment of Koe's 2022 definition.

**Third formulation: the duality collapses into one (May 2023).** "To change who you are, you must strike a balance between peace and progress." Progress pushes outward and peace inward: "nothingness, doing and being." This duality "must collapse into one." His reason: "certain goals require a certain character to be able to even know that that is a goal and pursue that goal," as in a video game where "you have to be level 50 to go into whatever dungeon or accept whatever quest"; you have no idea that the goal, and the potential it represents for your life, even exists until you reach the level. **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md. The argument is subtle. Progress is needed to reach the level at which new goals become visible; but reaching that level is a change of character, which requires the inner work of peace. The two poles are therefore not two activities to alternate but two aspects of a single process of becoming.

| Peace and progress / spiritual materialist | 2023-03 | Neither workaholic nor monk; the poles "collapsing" through synthesis | Koe |

The formulations can be read as applying at different scales (containment describes the aggregate over a life; polarity describes how each block of time is lived) or as stages (first balance, then alternate, then integrate). Koe presents "even moderation" explicitly as a reconciliation with Bush, and he justifies the collapse into one with the level-50 argument; he does not otherwise say that one formulation replaces another. The idea returns in 2025 as "true balance" in contrast, in the context of work hours (Section 39.6).

**Peace and progress as habits.** Later in the same conversation with Bush, Koe makes the motto concrete. He frames his future not in terms of specific things but in terms of peace and progress: "what's the one habit that is going to result in progress, which is writing; what is the one that's going to result in peace, which is... walking." The principles of his lifestyle are what he does now: writing, walking, reading, getting sun and good dinners. He mentions that he said on Noah Kagan's podcast that what is different now is that he likes fancy dinners; it is the one thing he is willing to spend on, roughly every two weeks with two or three friends and great conversation. **Source:** same. Defining a lifestyle by its "principle habits" rather than by its possessions is a direct application of the satisfaction argument above: possessions fade, habits that produce pure peace and pure progress do not.

**Peace and progress applied to vanity.** An April 2023 video, repeated in a March 2024 compilation, applies the motto to appearance and money. "There is a line where vanity can hurt your self-image. You can get trapped in that mental thought loop." But this is "just an aspect of living with purpose, and that can be learned and overcome through a mindfulness practice." The mechanism: "when you're pursuing anything, whether it be looks, money, whatever, there's always something you can compare yourself to when you're moving forward and making progress in the world. That is where suffering is born out of desire, and so you need to learn to manage that." Managing it "doesn't mean you just avoid it completely and not expose yourself to experience through actually moving through the world and making progress. You have to do both: peace and progress. Practice both skills." And: "you can look good and not be a narcissistic [expletive]." **Source:** Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md. The 2024 compilation adds an ordering: start with the body, "because you can start right now," with no need to wait for a skill set, and the benefits transfer to clearer thinking, a stronger mindset and more physical energy. "When you prioritize your body, your mind and finances follow, because you're improving the vessel from which those things come." **Source:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. The phrase "suffering is born out of desire" echoes the comparison lens of Section 39.1: pursuit creates a field of comparison, and comparison produces suffering, which peace must manage without abandoning the pursuit. The order body → mind → business is developed in Chapter 40.

#### Intense and unbothered

In May 2026 Koe names the personality that holds both poles. The goal of the video, he says, is to adopt the traits of a highly successful person, specifically "a person who works with tremendous intensity on things that matter to you, and more importantly, a person who is strangely unbothered when those things don't work out." Later he calls it "the greatest trait you can acquire." He warns that the first part of the video is dense and that many will drop off, and that if you try to understand it rather than "hoard the knowledge like you do every other video," your life will look drastically different "in 3 to 6 to 12 months." **Source:** How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md.

"The intense and unbothered personality is powerful. It is near the pinnacle of human development across both material and spiritual planes. They are a walking contradiction. They fear mediocrity enough to work hard, and they're unbothered enough to not collapse when things don't work out. It is the mark of a mature mind to be able to hold two opposing forces at once." **Source:** same. The phrase "intense and unbothered" is his; it is the 2026 name for the collapse of peace and progress into one, now described as a personality rather than a schedule. **Complementary context:** The final sentence echoes F. Scott Fitzgerald's well-known line that "the test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in the mind at the same time." Koe does not cite it.

The practice that builds this personality is what Koe calls "24/7 meditation." "The most impactful thing you can do is to practice some kind of a 24/7 meditation. You have to become the observer of the self that's trying to survive," operating from a higher, more expanded level of mind. The core skill is expanding and contracting the mind. You zoom out very far and hold many ideas at once (creativity); then you zoom in, work and get into flow (productivity). It is a spectrum, and the further you can expand and the deeper you can contract, the more focused work or deep conversation you get done ("zooming into reality right here, right now: what's the task that I'm working on?"). "It's just like training a muscle... eccentric, concentric." The problem: "most people just live in a perpetually zoomed-in state. We call it survival mode." The goal of the practice is a sequence: notice the impulse, do nothing about it, neutralize it, wait until calm arrives, and then make your own choice in the direction you want. **Source:** same.

| Movement | Mental state | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Expand (zoom out) | Holding many ideas at once | Creativity, perspective |
| Contract (zoom in) | Absorbed in the task here and now | Productivity, flow, deep conversation |
| Perpetual zoom-in | Unable to step back | "Survival mode" |

This is the "psychic muscle" of Section 39.2: the capacity to widen the gap between impulse and response. It is also the mechanism behind "pain versus suffering": the observer who can step back from the self that is trying to survive feels the pain of an event without being swept into the identity's refusal to accept it.

#### Winning the game is how you discover it's the wrong game

The same May 2026 video contains Koe's most important qualification of everything in this section. **Source:** How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md.

"If you're smart, you've noticed... that we've replaced one survival game with another. Meaning, we replaced one source of suffering with another." Stressing yourself into becoming a hyper-successful businessman or building an aesthetic physique is better, in his opinion, than the endless stress loop of being overweight and broke. But the business owner is unhappy until reaching a certain level of money, "like a hundred million dollars," and even then has the same identity, so unless the money keeps increasing, they remain unhappy. People who sell a business for a huge payout have an existential crisis: "I don't know what to do with my time. I'm depressed." The bodybuilder will not be happy until the professional card, and without a shift in who they are, they keep moving the goalpost until an existential crisis and a deep depression force a change. You think you want the lives of business and fitness influencers, but look closer: "they are covering up their lack of fulfillment with more work." "Winning the game is how you discover it's the wrong game." "You kind of have to experience it before you can transcend it."

The conclusion is not to stop playing. Play the survival game (there is fulfillment in continuous growth, and stagnation is bad), "but you must also transcend the game": remain largely unbothered when things do not work out.

And then the warning, which Koe says he hesitated to include. "You can't try to be unbothered. You can't try to be cool and lax, because the bothered self attempting to become the unbothered self is just another survival strategy. The person who says, 'I'm going to detach from outcomes' has just made not being attached to outcomes the outcome they're attached to. So it becomes the axis of their suffering." This is "a huge spiritual trap": you read one spirituality book, see a higher form of being, and act like that person so others perceive you as wise, "but you haven't put in the years of work to naturally become that." He calls it "premature transcendence." Most people "have to be deeply attached to something before their attachment becomes visible enough to see through." There is no quick fix. He was hesitant to say this because most YouTube viewers want "the higher form of the cheap dopamine hit," a video that promises success so they feel they know more or are progressing when they are not.

The argument is a chain worth seeing whole:

1. Progress games (money, physique) are better than the stress loop of stagnation, so play them.
2. But winning them without a change of identity only moves the goalpost, and winning big can produce an existential crisis.
3. So you must also transcend the game: stay unbothered by outcomes.
4. But you cannot reach unbotheredness by deciding to be unbothered; that is another survival strategy and makes detachment the new attachment, "the axis of their suffering" (the term from Section 39.2, now applied to spiritual ambition).
5. So the only path to genuine detachment runs through genuine attachment: you play the game fully, long enough for the attachment to become visible, and then see through it.

"Premature transcendence" is Koe's term, and it qualifies his earlier recommendations of detachment without retracting them. In 2022 he taught that "your emotions do not have to match those waves" and offered Alan Watts's advice to relax into the cold; in 2024 he taught radical acceptance. The 2026 warning does not deny any of this. It adds that these states cannot be performed or adopted from a book, that they require years of practice, and that they normally come after, not instead of, the experience of being attached. The earlier versions describe the practice; the later one warns against its counterfeit. The step also explains why the intense-and-unbothered personality is described as "near the pinnacle of human development" (an idea developed in the stage models of Chapter 38): it cannot be skipped to.

### 39.5 Autonomy and Confidence

A good life, in Koe's account, needs two inner conditions that are easy to misname. The first is what people call "freedom," which he argues is really autonomy: not the absence of structure but the authorship of it. The second is confidence, which he treats not as a personality trait or a by-product of competence but as a skill with four pillars, built on trust in your own future. The two are related. Autonomy is the external arrangement in which you write your own rules; self-trust is the internal condition that lets you live by rules nobody else gave you.

#### Freedom or autonomy

The idea develops over four years, beginning with a question Koe asks guests on his podcast. In December 2021 he opens his interview with Justin Welsh, as he opens every interview, with: "What is your life philosophy in one sentence?" Welsh answers: "to do what I want with whom I want whenever I want." Koe observes that, from asking the question many times, answers "go both ways": people either already know their philosophy or think about it, and "it comes out to something similar." He concludes that "it's all related to growth," and that people who have seen success or autonomy are after "that personal sovereignty that everyone's after." **Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md. The question is a compression exercise: forcing a person to state their core value in a single line. Welsh's answer, as Koe reads it, is a statement of sovereignty over one's own time, company and activity.

Welsh also explains why that sovereignty matters to him, through the story of his burnout. He took his first executive role at 33, as "a stretch hire," expecting to take the company to "a million, maybe two" in revenue; instead he grew it to about $51 million. "As it got bigger and bigger and bigger, I lost control." His thesis: "burnout is about loss of control. It's not about working hard. I could work hard all day. But losing control is what starts to really cause burnout." **Source:** same. This is Welsh's idea, and its implication is that the remedy for burnout is regaining control, not reducing effort. It anticipates the distinction Koe draws later between hours that are chosen and hours that are forced (Section 39.6).

In November 2022, in a video of lessons from his own business, Koe describes what full control looks like and what it costs. His lifestyle consists of researching his interests, writing about them creatively and producing valuable messages. He has full control over how much he works (zero, four or twenty hours a day; income can fluctuate, but he controls it) and over where he lives, and he builds products and services that help him and others. The main downside is the same thing: "you are in full control," with the uncertainty and discomfort that implies, because you have to manage every aspect of your life. But comfort and security, he argues, "are usually just illusions," and they are far more risky, uncomfortable and uncertain than pursuing things yourself and embracing the uncertain nature of life. **Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md. (He calls this "the first lesson" of eleven, and then labels traffic and offers "the first lesson" too, so the numbering in that video is ambiguous.)

In July 2023 the formulation becomes a distinction. It is ironic that people think they want to maximize freedom, "because that's the opposite of what they want. People don't want freedom, they want autonomy. They don't want limitations projected on them, they want to create their own limitations." **Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md. The reasoning follows from the need for ordered attention developed in Part III: total freedom means chaos, and a mind needs order; the question is only whose order it is.

In September 2024 he spells out the mechanism. "You want freedom, and now maybe you don't actually want absolute freedom, you want autonomy; there's a difference." "Freedom is just chaos; it's something that you think you want but you don't; you're mislabeling autonomy for freedom." "Autonomy is the ability to choose your own limits, to choose your own structure, because the mind craves order, it craves structure; you want that thing, but you need to be in control of it." And then the key observation: "You would love your own routine and being able to iterate and improve on that routine, but since you don't create your routine, because your routine is the source of your money, then you start to think that you don't want a stable lifestyle." **Source:** You Won't Look At Money The Same Again (How To Build Meaningful Wealth).md. People reject stability, in other words, not because they dislike structure but because the structure they know was imposed by their source of income. They confuse the imposition with the structure.

In September 2025 the argument reaches its sharpest form. "Life is so much better when you have a code to operate by, because most people have been tricked to think that they want freedom." "Absolute freedom, so no limitations, no restrictions, is synonymous with absolute chaos. The only reason you think you want freedom is because you're living by a set of rules you didn't create." When you feel you need to escape, you go on vacation; after a week, "the simulated honeymoon phase ends and the tourist novelty wears off," and you want structure again. "You don't want freedom. You want the freedom to create your own rules of the game." You don't want goals assigned by parents, teachers or employers, but you mistake that for not wanting structure, "so you crave the one thing, freedom, that will make your life substantially worse." **Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md. The vacation example is a small experiment anyone can recognize: total freedom is pleasant for exactly as long as it is novel.

| 2024-09 | Autonomy is choosing your own limits and structure; stability is rejected because it was imposed | Koe |
| 2025-09 | Absolute freedom is absolute chaos; you want "the freedom to create your own rules of the game" | Koe |

The change is a refinement, not a reversal. The 2021 "sovereignty" already implied choosing one's own rules; later versions make explicit that what is wanted is authorship of structure, not its absence.

The practical consequence, for Koe, is a business. In September 2024 he says, with deliberate bluntness, that whatever you call it ("a side hustle or your life's work or creative work"), "you need a business." A business "demands a much different character than a 9-to-5 job": there is no boss providing order for your life; you create your own schedule and manage yourself, "a lot more of an undertaking than it sounds." If you like stability and the comfort of a 9-to-5, you may not realize that a business is still better, "because you create your own stability, the one that you like." But most entrepreneurs don't; they drown in twelve-hour days with no focus or priorities. **Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md. Autonomy is therefore not a gift that comes with self-employment. It is a capacity that self-employment requires and that most people who leave a job have not built.

The rule that governs the arrangement comes from an April 2023 video. With a job, everything revolves around work. In entrepreneurship, "how I believe it should be is that work should revolve around your ideal lifestyle, and anything that compromises that, then entrepreneurship isn't worth it at that point." **Source:** Self Improvers Are Creating Their Own Careers (The New Economy).md. This is the "lifestyle first" principle that Chapters 12 and 28 applied to the design of the workday and the business; here it appears as the criterion of whether the autonomy is real.

Finally, a reminder that the capacity to choose is never actually lost. In October 2023 Koe cites Greg McKeown's *Essentialism* (the transcript renders the name as "Greg Macau") on the ability to choose: it cannot be taken away, "it can only be forgotten." **Source:** If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md. The transcript garbles the first half of the quotation, but Koe's use is clear: whatever your situation, you retain the ability to choose differently, and a person who feels they have no choice has forgotten it rather than lost it.

#### Self-trust: why people care what others think

An August 2023 video opens with a thesis: "stop caring about others' opinions when you trust yourself with your future." **Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md. The lack of confidence, Koe says, limits the potential of most people, because they care too much about what others think. Starting a business floods the mind: What will my parents, friends, spouse think? Will it replace my income? How long will it take? And more thoughts arrive once you are building. "The reason people fail is they can't lift the emotional weights required to achieve the goals they set": for every thought of a better life there are a hundred negative thoughts beating you back, and that is only about business, among the 60,000 to 80,000 thoughts humans average in a day (about making friends, about debt, "did I feed the dog this morning," about what people would think of you as a pet owner). **Source:** same. The figure of 60,000 to 80,000 daily thoughts is a popular estimate that Koe repeats without a source; it should be read as an image of mental noise, not as a measurement. "Emotional weights" is the same image as the "responsibility muscle" of Section 39.2: goals require carrying a psychological load, and failure is often a failure to carry it.

The modern environment makes this worse. "More information, more responsibilities and more options for the future turns confidence into success and anxiety into failure." Developing self-confidence in this environment is not easy, and the question becomes how to cultivate the self-trust that leads to it. **Source:** same.

#### The four pillars of self-confidence

Koe's answer, in the same video, is a framework he calls the four pillars of self-confidence. **Source:** You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md.

| Pillar | What it gives you | Core practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Perspective | The realization that nobody is special | When anxiety sparks: pause, zoom out, transfer your consciousness to the others in the situation |
| 2. Perception | The ability to act on opportunities | Enter situations in an open-minded state and maintain it |
| 3. Practice | The willingness not to shy away from challenge | Repetition in real situations |
| 4. Authenticity | Freedom from conforming to others' opinions | Doing what you want without interference from external beliefs |

The order matters: he says you cannot advance to the other pillars without first adopting the perspective habit. And authenticity is described later in the video as "how you implement the four pillars."

Before presenting the pillars, he criticizes the standard advice. When people seek confidence they are told "confidence equals competence," so they learn and learn, never act, never self-reflect, and never develop confidence as a skill usable in any situation; that, he says, is the most common outcome. There is depth behind the statement that people cannot see, but as general advice "confidence equals competence... has not fixed the biggest problem in the world, but it has made it worse." **Source:** same. The criticism connects to the learning chapters (Chapter 14): competence acquired by consumption without action produces neither competence nor confidence.

**Perspective.** When you enter a situation without confidence, anxiety shoots up and closes your mind to everything but your multiplying thoughts. You retract and "become a slave to your past," pulling old feelings into the present and remaining the same person. You break the cycle by consciously reprogramming your habitual reactions: when anxiety sparks, pause, zoom out to see that it is just a normal situation, and "transfer consciousness" to the people in it, adopting their perspective, because most people live the same experience you do. "Billionaires are people with the same problems; fitness models are people with the same problems." "Any person you mentally place above you has the same problems." The one thing that makes such people react negatively is being treated as if they were not normal people like you. And negative reactions come not from overconfidence but from its opposite: people sense that something is off, that you are unconfident or projecting into an imaginary future. This is a habit that requires practice and repetition in real life; one video will not fix it. **Source:** same. "Transfer consciousness" is a phrase Koe uses in several contexts (in writing it means imagining the reader's mind); here it means adopting the perspective of the others present, which dissolves the imagined hierarchy that produced the anxiety.

**Perception.** "How you perceive situations determines how you act." This pillar requires shifting into an open-minded state before the situation and maintaining it during. His example is freelancing: if, while scrolling social media, you do not perceive a business owner's tweet as an opportunity to reach out, you will not reach out, will not land the client and will not make money. Even if you see the opportunity, you may botch the message or not send it because you misperceive the person. The same holds for making friends and entering new situations. **Source:** same. The pillar is the goal-as-lens principle of Chapter 6 applied to social courage: opportunities exist only for a mind prepared to see them.

**Practice.** The framework names practice as the pillar that keeps you from shying away from challenge, and the perspective pillar already insists on real-life repetition. The material available for this chapter does not include Koe's detailed development of the third pillar, so it is not reconstructed further here.

**Authenticity.** Koe defines it as doing what you want without interference from the external thoughts, opinions and beliefs you feel the need to conform to. His example is his own swearing. He curses occasionally in videos and gets comments calling it unprofessional or ungodly. He was raised heavily religious, and those words were forbidden; when he questioned the rule, researched it and thought for himself, he concluded that swearing is not bad in any sense. If people are put off by one word because of their own connotations, they can leave; he does not care to be "professional," or to be offended by words that everyone interprets differently. "Confidence through authenticity is a mental game; you must play it daily": pause, gain perspective, perceive the situation, practice the skill it requires. "Authenticity is how you implement the four pillars of self-confidence." **Source:** same. "Authenticity" is a common word used here in a sense of its own: not self-expression in general but the absence of conformity to beliefs you never examined. It is the confidence counterpart of the autonomy argument above: in both, the problem is a structure (of rules or of opinions) imposed from outside and mistaken for your own.

The video adds a heuristic for the moment when the effort to become more confident itself creates anxiety, which Koe calls "the anxiety trap." Trying to become more confident brings anxiety, whether about approaching people or about the changes a business requires. When you feel it, pause (a conscious effort), zoom out and reframe: "the cure to anxiety is curiosity." Being anxious about starting a business means projecting so far into the future that clarity is nearly impossible; the mind cannot bring order to that much chaos. Bring your attention to what you can do now: get curious about what you can start learning, and learn it. In social situations, stop imagining every future detail of your interaction with the person and focus on the person in front of you: what catches your eye? Ask about it. **Source:** same. The mechanism is the same as in Section 39.2: anxiety is tension produced by projection out of the present, and curiosity is a way back into the present that also produces action.

Finally, Koe proposes what he calls an unconventional path to confidence: "the secret to self-confidence is to become a fountainhead of value through education and creation. You don't have to question your worth when you have public proof." Entrepreneurship, he argues, is in human nature: pursuing new challenges, never settling, expanding perspective through skill acquisition, and receiving feedback (in the form of money) on whether you are contributing to society. "Entrepreneurship is creation, and being a creator is the act of channeling the divine through your human edge." Avoid situations where challenge ceases to exist, "like a monkey in a cubicle": your psyche suffers and life feels unfulfilling even if you are a top-1% earner. "Personal growth is a gateway drug into business growth, and business growth is the unconventional path to self-confidence": improve yourself, productize yourself, earn from being yourself. **Source:** same.

"Fountainhead of value" is one of Koe's recurring terms (it is the end goal of *The Art of Focus*, and Chapter 35 treats it alongside the "value creator"). Here its function is psychological: confidence that rests on public evidence of value does not need the approval of any particular person. The argument closes the loop of this section. Autonomy requires a business that you build yourself; building it requires self-trust; and the business, once it produces public proof, becomes a source of the self-trust it required.

### 39.6 Chapters, Phases and Seasons

The waves of life (Section 39.3) said that highs and lows alternate and that you can choose your response. From 2023 onward Koe turns that observation into a model: life unfolds in chapters, and each chapter passes through recognizable phases. The model is one of the most revised ideas in the corpus. This section first presents the general claim (everything moves in cycles, and life can be read as a book with chapters), then the arc of a single chapter, then the successive versions of the phase model side by side, then the question of working hours that the model was partly built to answer, and finally Koe's own seasons as he reports them.

#### Everything goes through cycles

The most general statement comes from an August 2024 video. Asked about the debate over whether young people should work constantly, Koe answers "I agree, but I don't," because life unfolds in chapters and phases and "everything, and I mean everything, goes through cycles." There are macro cycles and micro cycles: cycles of the universe, like the seasons; cycles of culture, like holidays; cycles of your day, like going to the bathroom or satisfying sexual desire. This is "a generalized principle of reality, the principle of impermanence and entropy." **Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md. The principle is the same one Section 39.3 called "the supreme law"; here it is used to justify varying how much you work.

The image of life as a book comes from *The Art of Focus*, which Koe quotes in June 2023: "Life unfolds in chapters and phases. Books, songs, stories, architecture, bodybuilding... relationships, mental health and every other form of human creation reflect this pattern. Being able to zoom out and see what is happening beyond your thoughts can save you from a world of pain." It helps, he says, to know what part of "this everlasting book you are in, or this everlasting song," and "the harmony, the highs and lows that are playing together at this moment in your life." **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md.

He extends the image downward. "For each chapter of a book there is a purpose or goal that must be actualized," and at the start of any chapter "you have to acclimate to the environment that you chose to be in." And it is "not only... one book for your life as a whole, but subsequent sub-books within that big book, each with their own chapter, and that goes down infinitely forever into every facet of your life." He calls this "divine." **Source:** same. The structure is fractal: your life is a book, your health or your business is a sub-book with its own chapters, and a single project is a chapter within that. You can be at the climax of one sub-book and lost at the start of another.

In March 2024 he states the scale-invariance directly. "Life unfolds in chapters, phases and cycles. Your life is a story, your year is a story, your day is a story. Each of them have a series of goals, problems, highs and lows that may repeat themselves as time passes." There is "one macro cycle of life that I've found through my observation and reflection on my own experience," and once you understand it, you can identify which part of the story you are in at a large scale (your life, your year) or a small one (your day). "Once you're aware of the situation and you can make sense of it from a higher perspective, then you can start to deconstruct it, figure out what the problem is, and start solving it, and have purpose as a result." **Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md. Note how he presents the source of the model: personal observation and reflection, not research. It is a pattern he reports finding in his own life and offers as a lens.

Why does knowing your place in the story matter? The February 2023 video answers with an image: so that you can identify the point in life you are at, "break out of this thought bubble, zoom out, see where you're at in the story of your life, see the light at the end of the tunnel, and avoid treading water for too long before you're too tired to get to shore." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md. The "thought bubble" is the limited set of thoughts available to a mind inside a difficult phase (Section 39.7 calls it the "mental bubble"); treading water is effort that keeps you afloat without moving you, and that eventually exhausts you. Knowing that the phase is a phase, and what comes after it, is what lets you swim toward the shore instead.

#### The arc of a chapter

Within a chapter, Koe describes a typical arc, which he illustrates with a move to a new city. **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md.

When you move, you have not yet acclimated: "your mind hasn't ordered itself based on your conscious decisions to form actions and routines around the things you like to do." Which grocery store, which roads, how to get around the city. And yet "how good does it feel when you are navigating the city and you discover something new because you are open to discovering in the unknown." Most people, he says, do not bring this attitude into everyday life: "they are so closed off to the lack of certainty that they experience when they go into a new phase of life." The same applies to health, relationships and business: when you start something new, or achieve a big goal and drop into a low, "it's uncomfortable at first, but then it starts to become normal, just as you acclimate to that environment over the weeks."

Once you begin to acclimate, you perceive everything "from a state of decreased stress because things have become normalized," which lets your mind open to new possibilities. In the middle of the chapter, once you gain clarity and understand the purpose ("once it comes to you"), you start making immense progress: "you kind of just go all in on it, and then everything feels smooth." You reach the climax of the story in that chapter. As the chapter nears its end, "you've made significant progress, but your mind starts to decline into a new low, because you don't know what the next chapter holds": you feel off, and you know you are not making as much progress as you could. Knowing that nothing is permanent, "you kind of just have to flow with it and let it happen."

The arc, reconstructed as a sequence:

1. **Acclimation.** Discomfort while the mind has not yet ordered itself around the new environment.
2. **Opening.** Stress decreases as things normalize; the mind opens to possibilities.
3. **Clarity and commitment.** The purpose of the chapter becomes clear; you go all in.
4. **Climax.** Maximum progress; everything feels smooth.
5. **Decline.** Significant progress made, but the mind declines into a new low because the next chapter is unknown.

The transition between chapters is where people suffer most, and Koe's earliest statement about it, from January 2023, normalizes it. "Life unfolds in chapters like a book, and each chapter presents a purpose that the next chapter leads into, and you will feel lost heading into new chapters." After you complete a purpose, the next one will not show up right away; "we've all gone through this, and it's normal, you just have to sit with it." It is a time for "presence, mindfulness and other things, in preparation for that next round of intensity." "If you aren't making progress, then you are preparing for the next round of making progress," and you have to be at peace with that and self-reflect to get hints of your next purpose, "like pieces of the puzzle," because you do not recognize a problem right away; it may bother you for a while until you reflect on what went wrong and what went right. **Source:** Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md.

In a March 2024 compilation he repeats the passage and adds an example of how the puzzle pieces become a vision. If you do not know where to move next, reflect on your vacations and visits: "okay, I want to live in a place that's a big city, I want to be able to walk in some places." "This is how you create a vision for your future." **Source:** Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md. The example shows that the reflection is not abstract introspection. It is a review of concrete past experiences, sorted into what you liked and what you did not, until a pattern emerges.

Koe's most poetic image of the transition opens a July 2023 video. "Life is like a dark room. On occasion we find a candle so that we can see, but not for long. The candle provides vision and direction, but the flame will die out, sparking fear in our souls that we will never find our way, unless we move with faith that we will find the next candle." **Source:** A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md. The candle is clarity or vision, and its essential property is that it is temporary: clarity is not found once but repeatedly, and the stretch between candles is crossed by faith (in the sense of continuing to move), not by sight.

How long does the dark stretch last? In May 2025 Koe gives an estimate from his own experience, not from a study: "It takes about one to two months of confusion, feeling lost, and being on the verge of giving up for the right amount of vision to form where you have absolute clarity and launch into a new way of life." **Source:** How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md.

And the question that defines the transition: "what's next?" After a breakup, after leaving school, after changing jobs. "That question of what's next can either lead to despair or a chance to discover new potentials for your life, depending on how you perceive that question." **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md. The perception principle of Section 39.5 applies here as well: the same situation, read as an ending or as an opening, produces despair or exploration.

#### Six versions of the cycle

Koe's phase model exists in six versions. The table gives an overview; the subsections that follow develop each version after the first, which Section 39.3 has already presented.

| Date | Video | Phases | Distinctive emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022-07 | Why You Can't Change | Waves of life (unnamed highs and lows) | Separate the inevitable waves from your mindful response |
| 2023-02 | Happiness Is A Skill | Seasons of intensity; seasons of feeling lost / experimentation | The lost season as opportunity; the progress curve; systems protect gains |
| 2023-06 | If You're Lost | Clarity → intensity → consistency → lost | Four named phases; intensity threshold; bodybuilding analogy |
| 2024-03 | If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control | Lost → curious → obsessed | What to do in each phase; consistency disappears |
| 2024-07 to 2024-10 | Learn This Skill...; Minimalist Productivity; It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day | (Curiosity, intensity, consistency, feeling lost →) perplexity → curiosity → intensity → consistency | Phases as "meta states of consciousness"; no skipping; the intensity trap; higher lows |
| 2025-05 | How To Gamify Your Life | Limbo → vision → flow → resistance | Why people get stuck in limbo |

The versions differ in names, number and order, and one difference is substantive: the place of "clarity." In 2023 clarity "strikes"; it is something received after the lost phase. From 2024 onward clarity is the product of an active phase of curiosity and experimentation. (In Koe's 2022 "creation pyramid," treated earlier in the book, the vision is something you construct; the 2023 version, in which vision is partly received, sits in tension with that.) He gives a reason only for one renaming, discussed below. The versions are best read as lenses on the same cycle: the 2023 versions emphasize preserving gains; the 2024 versions, what to do in each phase; the 2025 version, why people get stuck.

#### Version of February 2023: seasons of intensity and experimentation

The "Happiness Is A Skill" video describes the cycle as alternating seasons. It begins from three hard periods in Koe's life (the details of which belong to the video's opening and are not reproduced here) and identifies what they shared: "mental turmoil, not just a slight increase in stress, but a huge increase in stress, to the point of where your attention narrows and you feel trapped. The smallest tasks turn into a mountain of impossibility. Every thought becomes an enemy that is trying to keep you trapped in this mental game, and there ceases to be a light at the end of the tunnel." These phases "aren't fun, clearly, but these moments in life are the ones that offer a lesson that can only be received with a shift in direction." **Source:** Happiness Is A Skill (How To Get Out Of A Rut).md. "Mental turmoil" is his term for this state; its signature is narrowed attention.

He then describes the common arc of his struggles in the first person. "All progress-induced happiness was stripped from me like an abrupt transition to a new movie scene after the climax. I felt lost, had little desire to sustain the progress of my previous purpose; I'd achieved it and had to move forward. I experimented both inward and outward. I tried techniques for inner peace while creating new goals with accompanying education and practice. With time the pieces of the puzzle came together, brought clarity and launched me into a new season of intensity." **Source:** same. "Progress-induced happiness" is the happiness of the Nietzschean formula (Section 39.1); the passage explains why it ends: it depends on a purpose, and when the purpose is achieved the happiness is cut off like a scene.

The season of intensity, in his description: "the universe aligns, everything clicks, and you act on an idea that makes perfect sense. You have clarity on your goals, reaching new levels of skill and enjoying the process. You feel a sense of purpose, and it seems like there isn't enough time in the day, but you wouldn't change it for a thing. The feeling of pure progress is unmatched. No distractions, absolute clarity, placing one, two, three bricks down to build your vision for the future." It makes sense to fill days with activities that sustain this state; when we can't, we resort to peace, balancing peace and progress. **Source:** same.

But seasons of intensity "aren't really the problem," because they come after a season of feeling lost. "That is exactly the problem here: those seasons of feeling lost, because people don't perceive that situation as an opportunity." In them "you don't really have a purpose to work towards, you don't have progress to be made, you're just in this grind of trying to gather pieces and preparing yourself for that next season of intensity." The mind opens to mental turmoil "when either a massive shift happens or you just gradually decline after a season of progress into this low point where you have the possibility to get stuck in a rut." And the payoff: "if you can master your mind in these periods of struggle, then the negativity ceases to be so negative," you engage your attention in the right things, gain a better feeling and more sense of control, and "it expedites the process to reaching another stage or season of intensity." **Source:** same. The practical content of "mastering your mind" in the lost season is the inward and outward experimentation of Section 39.7.

He renames the lost season as he goes. "As you progress through this season of experimentation, which is a reframe of a season of struggle, clarity begins to culminate. It isn't immediate, even though when that last puzzle piece clicks into place, it is immediate, and then that's when you launch into a season of progress." **Source:** same. The reframe matters: "season of struggle" names what the period feels like; "season of experimentation" names what it is for. And clarity has a peculiar temporal shape: it accumulates gradually, piece by piece, but arrives all at once when the last piece fits.

The video also gives a picture of progress over many cycles. In seasons of progress you hit new highs, and as you experiment inward and outward "you're going to reach new baselines of happiness in either direction, like hitting a new monthly high in business revenue, but you have to understand that all highs come down a bit." Progress is "a curve where it's like up and then down, but higher than last time, and then up higher than last time." The aim is to sustain a certain baseline, keeping in mind "the importance of systems." **Source:** same. (At one point the transcript says "down lower than last time," which appears to be a slip, since the rest of the passage describes rising lows.)

The role of systems is explained with a bodybuilding analogy from the same video. "When you bulk... at the start you're like, I'm eating more food, my lifts are going up, and then you put on a bit too much fat" (here, "mental fat") "and then you start to feel sluggish, groggy," and you want to cut back "and just start from scratch, but from a place of newfound muscle... the new baseline level of muscle that I've built." "When it comes time to cut without a proper system in place, you're going to lose a lot of that muscle. You can't just cut super quick and try to reach this other baseline, especially in business revenue," and similar connections apply to relationships. **Source:** same. Bulking is the season of intensity, which adds both muscle (real gains) and fat (bloat, excess commitments, mental clutter); cutting is the transition out of it, in which a system is what protects the gains while the excess is shed.

#### Version of June 2023: the four phases of life

Four months later, in "If You're Lost," the seasons become four named phases, described in the first person. **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md.

1. **Clarity.** "Insight strikes and a purpose reveals itself, a vision that I can start to work towards. I have the skills to make it work; all that's left is for me to accept the challenge."
2. **Intensity.** "Pure progress is being made, flow state is almost permanent, the skill set I've acquired is laying the foundation for my business, relationships, level of mind and quality of life."
3. **Consistency.** "I felt pressure to sustain the progress that I had made. This required systemization, prioritization and sacrifice that felt like a gradual decline into chaos, because massive progress was not being made."
4. **Lost**, "the most disastrous": "I actualized the purpose I had set out for in the clarity phase; my mind filled with doubts about the future."

Then the cycle returns to clarity. Each phase solves a different problem: clarity gives a purpose, intensity gives progress, consistency preserves gains, and the lost phase clears space for the next purpose.

He describes the intensity phase in more detail later in the video. As you build momentum, "you enter into what I call an intensity phase": "you have complete clarity on what you must do, you have a tangible aspect of life that you are making progress in, you don't want to do anything aside from work because work is the funnest thing you could be doing." "This blur of pure progress usually lasts one to three months, more or less." **Source:** same.

And he explains why it ends, with a concept of his own: the intensity threshold. After the upward trend of progress and momentum, "you eventually reach the intensity threshold, where you can't make any more progress: you've exhausted that vessel that you've been pulling from, or having to fill with resources from reality; you've exhausted that ore of reality." (The transcript reads "or of reality"; "ore of reality" is the likely meaning.) You have to move on to something new, "but that new something is the new purpose that you don't know of yet." "You can try sustaining it, but it's just not going to be any more fulfilling," so you come down into a consistency phase, "but you're slowly declining into another lost phase, and you have to be vigilant of that." **Source:** same. The image is of mining: a purpose is a vein of ore, intensity extracts from it, and at some point the vein is exhausted. This explains the end of intensity without blaming the person: it is not that you lost discipline but that the vein ran out.

To paint the consistency phase he returns to bodybuilding, with a different mapping from February's. There are three phases in bodybuilding: bulking, cutting and maintenance. Cutting corresponds to gaining clarity, "where you let go of the fat, physical or mental, to reveal what you've built from previous bulking cycles." Bulking corresponds to the intensity phase, "where you're in an environment that is conducive to muscle growth, physical or mental, until you reach a point of feeling groggy and demotivated." In between either is a maintenance phase that you choose "to maintain the progress that you've made," the muscle you have built. The equivalents: in business you maintain a baseline of revenue; in relationships, a baseline of fulfillment; in spirituality, a baseline of happiness. "You do all of the above by creating a system and pulling levers." **Source:** same.

| Bodybuilding phase | February 2023 mapping | June 2023 mapping |
|---|---|---|
| Bulking | Season of intensity (adds muscle and "mental fat") | Intensity phase |
| Cutting | The transition out of intensity, where a system protects gains | Clarity phase (revealing what was built) |
| Maintenance | (implicit in "the new baseline") | Consistency phase |

The two mappings differ in where cutting goes. In February, cutting is the dangerous transition after intensity; in June, it is the clarifying phase that precedes a new intensity. The analogy is used flexibly, and its stable content is the same in both: gains made in intensity must be protected by a system, or they are lost.

#### Version of March 2024: lost, curious, obsessed

In March 2024 Koe presents "the macro cycle of life" in three phases. **Source:** If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md.

1. **Lost.** "You feel lost, you don't know what to pursue next. You've solved a problem, achieved a goal, and thought it was the end."
2. **Curious.** "You become curious, you experiment with different topics, interests and ideas until you become fully aware of the problem you want to solve."
3. **Obsessed.** "You become obsessed. You dive deeper into the crevice of reality of your choice, and you can't stop learning and building toward your goal." He later calls this "the season of intensity or obsession."

What to do in each: when lost, become curious, "embrace curiosity and try a bunch of different things while being detached from the outcome of immediate results." Finding the thing "may take years or weeks or months, who knows." Then you become obsessed, find a new goal and actualize it. Once you hit it, the cycle repeats: "you feel lost because you didn't realize that that goal was not something... that would sustain itself; the high wouldn't sustain itself."

Compared with June 2023, this version drops the consistency phase and turns clarity from something that "strikes" into the outcome of a phase of active, detached experimentation. The lost phase is no longer the "most disastrous" end of the cycle but its starting point.

#### Versions of July to October 2024: perplexity, curiosity, intensity, consistency

In July 2024 Koe states the cycle as "curiosity, intensity, consistency and feeling lost." The sequence: you feel lost, but if you stay open-minded and are not "drowning in the emotions that come with feeling lost," you notice an opportunity; you get curious and dive in; "you go into this season of intensity where you build, build, build a business, your body, whatever it is; you have that intense motivation." Then the warning: "if you don't systemize along the way... when that crash inevitably comes, you aren't going to be consistent." But "if you systemize what works and you create something sustainable from the intensity and all of that energy, then the consistency kind of just becomes easy and it becomes a part of your life," so "you increase your baseline, both state of consciousness and just well-being and enjoyment in general." **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. The key instruction is to systemize during intensity, not after it, because after it the energy is gone.

In August 2024 he renames "feeling lost" as "perplexity," moves it to the front and gives each phase a role. **Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md.

1. **Perplexity:** you feel lost and do not know what to do next in life.
2. **Curiosity:** you discover an inkling of opportunity and dive down the rabbit hole.
3. **Intensity:** you gain absolute clarity and cannot pull yourself away from your work; this leads to twelve-hour workdays, "and that's when they're encouraged."
4. **Consistency:** you systemize what works, lean back, develop a sustainable routine, and make rest an essential part of your creative life; "this is where the 4-hour workday comes in."

Then you repeat. Doing intense work every day on mindless, low-leverage tasks only adds "bloat" to your mind and business. The typical error is to "try pushing through a perplexity phase of life with intensity when it is solved with curiosity." He calls the phases "meta states of consciousness," and insists they cannot be forced: you cannot jump from perplexity to consistency, because you have not built anything in the curiosity and intensity phases. And working twelve hours a day on a task you chose only because you felt you had to leads you to feel cheated by "the labor theory of value," the belief that hours put in should produce proportional results. **Source:** same.

In October 2024 a full video develops this version, which is the most detailed. **Source:** It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md. It opens with a reversal of a common belief: "I personally don't think that consistency is the route that people should take to achieve success. I think it's a balance; it's a cycle of intensity, consistency, curiosity and sometimes feeling lost." If you are only consistent, "you're not really making any progress." He names the four phases "perplexity, curiosity, intensity, consistency," which he calls "my fancy way of making the words match," and asks the viewer to think of them as chapters in a book "because humans make sense of the world in stories." The instruction: "know which phase you're in so that you can lead yourself into the next one." This is the only renaming for which he gives a reason, and the reason is stylistic and narrative.

**Perplexity.** He quotes his own tweet: "The solution to boredom is building: build a skill, build a brand, build your body, build your mind, build your social life, build anything. It doesn't matter if you don't know how; that's the entire point. You learn, act and discover your way out of boredom." Perplexity is the start of the story; the goal or purpose has not yet been found; you are in limbo. You cannot work twelve hours because you have nothing of your own choosing to work on (being told what to do at a job does not count). Most people are here. They do not let themselves be bored; they dwell in their thoughts, get stuck in constant negativity, anxiety and confusion, and numb it with cheap dopamine (video games, phones, alcohol, drugs). The way out has two steps: first, allow yourself to be bored; second, become curious. "You have to become bored enough so that you become curious": strip your life of distractions, start completely fresh, and let your mind gravitate toward something good so you can choose it.

**Curiosity.** Curiosity usually stems from identifying a problem you want to solve, "because problems and purpose exist on the same spectrum, they're just polar opposites of one another": purpose cannot exist without a problem, and vice versa. Once you discover a problem, open your mind enough to see its opposite side, where purpose lies. Problems start shallow ("I don't have enough money," "I feel sluggish and don't like how I look," "I can't get a date"), and they have to, because survival comes first: you solve shallow problems in order to transcend survival, so that your mind opens to spiritual, metaphysical and deeper problems that can carry a greater life purpose. Then you search for information, and your goals and problems, conscious or unconscious, frame what information you perceive. If you have money problems and cannot pay your bills, you will notice tools and resources in conversations, books and social media; "your life starts to take shape." People fail to progress because they focus on the wrong problems, attend to small negative and meaningless thoughts, or distract themselves because the problem is not important to them. And in this phase, against the usual advice: "in the curiosity phase this is where shiny object syndrome is kind of encouraged: you're supposed to try everything and see what sticks," trying different techniques and experiments to solve the problem until you gain clarity and see the bigger picture; then you launch into intensity. The exception is resolved by phase: the warning against shiny objects, and Koe's own advice elsewhere to focus on "one meaningful project," applies to intensity, not to curiosity.

**Intensity.** "Intensity is the climax of the story, the main battle, the most interesting and fulfilling part of your life. The 3 to 6 months go by in a blur; pure flow state; completely focused on actualizing one meaningful goal." In fitness, it is disciplined diet and training that is all you think about; in relationships, the honeymoon phase; in business, building and launching a new product or a writing spree, both of which lead to new heights of growth and revenue. "This is when you pull 12-hour workdays and it just comes naturally." The click or "aha" moment happens; you know exactly what to do; it is all you can think about; you take that energy and actualize the purpose, reaching a new baseline. Note the duration: here three to six months, where the June 2023 version said one to three.

**The intensity trap.** Intensity "can become dangerous fast": burnout and overwhelm usually stem from trying to stick out intensity too long instead of transitioning into consistency. "Don't push the bulk to the point of getting fat; don't push the cut until you're scrawny and losing muscle; don't get so obsessed with the relationship that you become needy and desperate; don't try to push revenue higher and destroy your brand reputation." Know when to "drop down to a new baseline and maintain the progress you've made." It is like trading stocks or crypto: the price will not keep going up, so be wise about when to take profits; "you can't just continue going up forever, or else everyone would be billionaires."

**Consistency.** "Consistency is not everything; consistency is a tool to maintain progress, not make progress. Stagnation isn't maintaining progress; stagnation is death." Being consistent with content creation alone does not get you anywhere: you have to become curious and experiment with new angles and strategies, and when you find the right one, double down; that is where the most growth comes from. In the consistency phase, work drops to the 4-hour workday philosophy (maintaining with about four hours a day), but with room for experimentation inside it, so you don't get trapped or fall back into the lost phase. "You don't have to go back into the lost phase": if you experiment continuously, you get out of it faster or spend less time in it. This is a notable claim: in this version the lost phase is not inevitable, because experimentation inside consistency can find the next curiosity before the old purpose is exhausted.

**Higher lows.** The overall pattern is: go up, baseline, try to maintain; go up, baseline, maintain. "It's more about higher lows than it is higher highs." The same with emotions: "you're not trying to sustain the higher highs, you're trying to sustain the higher lows," trying not to go as low as before. This is the progress curve of February 2023, now stated as a goal: the measure of a good cycle is not the height of its peak but the height of the floor it leaves behind.

**Progress is nonlinear.** He compares the cycle to building muscle over a decade. You do not build the same amount of muscle every year for ten years. You build a lot at the start ("newbie gains"), then in cycles of consistency and intensity: bulk and cut; life happens and you are thrown off for a year; the next year you regain motivation "in a hyper discipline" and gain more in year ten than in year three. Productivity works the same way: periods of high motivation in which a lot gets done, periods of mere consistency because other goals take over. You need to know how to move between them so that chaos does not build up ("I'm not productive, I'm not making as much progress"); if your life does not currently allow intensity, do not add stress on top: "lean into the phase of life that you're in so you can reap the maximum benefit from it."

#### Version of May 2025: limbo, vision, flow, resistance

The latest version opens the video "How To Gamify Your Life." "Life unfolds in chapters. And each chapter is a predictable sequence of four phases." **Source:** How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md.

1. **Limbo:** you don't know what to do or what you want.
2. **Vision:** an image of the future forms, you act on a new path, and momentum builds.
3. **Flow:** you cannot pull yourself away from the goal you are pursuing.
4. **Resistance:** "exponential progress doesn't last forever, but you don't want it to end, and that's often to your own detriment."

The 2025 version contributes a diagnosis of why people stay in the first phase. "Most people get trapped in the first phase, the limbo phase, because your entire life you've been trained to follow a script." You are used to the linear results of school and jobs; "you're used to someone else giving you your certainty." But in an unconventional life ("the only way to get unconventional results") you interpret feeling lost as a bad sign, jump ship and return to the comfortable, planned life "in a system that only cares for its own benefit." You hate the thought of ending up like everyone else, but not ending up like everyone else means you are on your own, which feels like being lost in a dense forest where you can see only trees, unaware that five yards away there is a clear path to the top of the mountain. **Source:** same. This links the phase model back to the diagnosis of Part I: the default path supplies certainty, and a person trained on it reads the absence of certainty as failure rather than as the normal first phase of a self-directed chapter.

The fourth phase also deserves a note. "Resistance" here is not the resistance whose overcoming produces happiness (Section 39.1). It is the phase in which momentum fades, and the danger is the refusal to let the phase end: the same danger the 2024 version called the intensity trap and the 2023 version the intensity threshold.

#### How many hours: productivity comes in seasons

The phase model was partly built to resolve a contradiction in Koe's own teaching. In 2023 he was known for the "4-Hour Workday," which he presented as a general rule (Chapter 12 develops it). In 2024 he published a video titled "It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day." The phase model is how he reconciles the two.

He says that his 4-hour workday philosophy received a lot of controversy, mostly because people read only the headline and latched onto the idea: it either guided them well or they said "that's impossible." But "the 4-hour workday is for a specific phase of life." He is "not a fan of sticking to something, because by sticking to something you stagnate," and "you don't actually really stay the same, you start to decline unless you change and evolve." **Source:** It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md.

At the same time he rejects the grind. Many entrepreneurs think you work twelve hours a day every day; he does not like or live that philosophy: "I think it's just stupid." "Limiting yourself and actually creating boundaries is what allows for more creative solutions and more progress than the 12-hour-workday entrepreneurs that just grind themselves into the ground." Most people do not want that lifestyle but do want to be entrepreneurs, and balance is possible and potentially more fulfilling. "12-hour workdays shouldn't be forced; they should be natural; you should want to do it": a binge in which you feel you cannot stop because you need to actualize the purpose you set. "I personally believe that no amount of work should be forced": if you have to force yourself, or are forced, either change what you do or change your mind so that you perceive the work in a better light. "Like a lion hunts and rests to be, quote unquote, productive," replicate that. **Source:** same.

And sometimes, he adds, the right move is a deliberate season of extremity. If you feel lost and tired and cannot focus on progress toward the life you promised yourself, "sometimes the only thing you can do is flip the switch, create a glitch in the Matrix, become a completely different person": change your habits all at once, remove every distraction, start the business, build the project, work twelve hours a day and forget to eat, and then look back and realize you have done more in three months than in the past three years. "You won't find rare results in an average lifestyle." "You need seasons of intensity that launch you into a new baseline of living." He attaches a condition: if your mind already pushes back on doing something different or extreme, "this video probably isn't going to benefit you very much, so open your mind or leave." **Source:** same.

The May 2025 video states the resulting position most precisely. He is not claiming four hours a day is the only way; it is one way, and you don't go one way your whole life. "Productivity comes in seasons": four hours in one, sixteen in another; sometimes six, two, eight, ten, zero. Input and output often are not linearly related. Sometimes he works twelve to sixteen hours, but he does not force himself to do so consistently. The purpose of the video is to show that a sustained sixteen-hour "grind set" will not get you as far as you think: "Consistent 12-to-16-hour workdays is not a personality trait. It is a sign of major dysfunction and a general neglect for how your mind is wired." He asks viewers to finish the video before forming a fixed opinion, because even his own approach is a system that breaks down when needed, like a forgotten Notion productivity system: "systems don't last forever." **Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md.

The principle beneath both positions appears in March 2025. The average person lacks "true balance, which lies in contrast": meaningful work and meaningful rest, both with pure focus. "Not the middle path, but the path of high highs and low lows, catastrophic failure and blissful success," which is where the most profound lessons are learned. Floating in the middle, life becomes a blur of boredom and anxiety. Many people try to escape stress and monotony with more rest or a fancy vacation; his alternative is to "go a little insane," flip the switch overnight and transform into an entirely new person. **Source:** How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md. This is Dickie Bush's polarity (Section 39.4) adopted as Koe's own position two years later. A terminological caution: "the middle" here means a life without contrast. Elsewhere Koe praises a different "middle," the zone between boredom and anxiety where challenge matches skill (Chapter 10). The two uses refer to different things and do not contradict each other.

| Phase | Hours | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Perplexity / limbo | Few hours of chosen work are possible | Nothing of your own to work on; the task is to become bored enough to become curious |
| Curiosity | Variable; exploratory | Many experiments; shiny objects encouraged |
| Intensity / flow | Twelve hours or more, naturally, for weeks or months | Absolute clarity; the work is the most enjoyable thing available |
| Consistency | About four hours, with room for experiment | Maintain the new baseline; protect gains with systems |

The resulting position, in the book's summary: the 4-hour workday has moved from a general operating rule to the consistency phase of a cycle; long days have moved from an exception to a legitimate season, provided they are neither permanent nor forced. Koe's own figures fluctuate accordingly. In 2023 and early 2024 he reports that most of his days were under four hours; while building Cortex in 2024 and 2025 he reports working much more, liking sixteen-hour days for a time but not for life. Alongside this he keeps two registers that he does not reconcile: respect for people (such as Alex Hormozi) for whom long hours are a genuine identity, and criticism of hustle culture's sixteen-hour days as distracted work worn as a badge of status.

#### The author's own seasons

Koe illustrates the model mostly with his own life, and the stories show both the phases and how his relationship to them changed.

**Periods of pure progress.** In February 2024 he recalls moments when he felt he was making progress: "that feeling of pure progress, the flow state, for like 3 to 6 months of obsession," and "all of that learning makes life good." Those periods were writing his book, writing newsletters he enjoyed, and building his products. "One newsletter outline every week brings meaning to my life," because everything he consumes and every lesson can be framed within that newsletter's topic. His book was "a massive outline" to which he could connect literally everything: on thirty-minute to one-hour walks listening to lectures, and even to fitness videos "that would have no business in my book," his mind "was lighting up with information," and he wrote what could go in the book in his phone notes. **Source:** The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md. The mechanism is the goal as a lens: a project in progress turns all input into material.

**Falling off a cliff.** In June 2023 he describes finishing *The Art of Focus*: "it felt like I fell off a cliff." He had been building toward it for three years. He had something to focus his attention on, a project to pour his energy into and base all his actions around. His nutrition was organized around "how do I operate at my peak mentally so I can pour my creativity into this book"; he designed a lifestyle "that favors idea generation and creativity"; he woke up early to start writing so he would still have time for other tasks. "My entire day was structured around this project," and that, he says, is how it goes for many people in many phases of life, where "they have one purpose." **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md. When the purpose is actualized, the structure built around it disappears with it. This is the lost phase seen from inside: not a lack of skill or discipline but the sudden absence of the organizing goal.

**The letter to himself.** In January 2024 he tells of a note he wrote two months earlier (he says he occasionally writes letters to himself "for awareness" during difficult periods). He wrote that he could not find joy in life anymore. He remembered long walks reading or listening to lectures, audiobooks or podcasts, his mind lighting up with ideas, being unable to stop writing: "that period was like a blur, a 6-month flow state," in which he built three products, gained millions of followers (the transcript is garbled at this point) and wrote a book. He asked himself whether he could do it again just as well: "no way." He concluded that there was a way back to that "higher state of mind," not necessarily to the lifestyle but to the state of mind and the enjoyment it gave him. He says he likes to fix his own problems before giving the solutions. **Source:** How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md.

**How he meets lost phases now.** In the June 2023 video he says he has "slowly learned to be at peace with them," being "a lot less reactionary and emotional than I would have been, let's say, five years ago." Now: "I observe it, I'm aware of what I'm going through, I've experienced this book before. How do I just let go and give myself the space to realize what the next purpose is in my life?" **Source:** If You're Lost - Here's How You Find Clarity For The Future.md. The phrase "I've experienced this book before" is the practical payoff of the chapter model: a lost phase recognized as a familiar chapter is much less frightening than one experienced as a permanent condition.

**The yearly "brain fried" cycle.** In March 2026 he describes a different kind of low. "Over the past few weeks, I've just felt completely brain fried... It feels like it happens in cycles for me. Like once a year I go through this period where things just pile up, the stressors, the business fires." "You feel like you're thinking about everything and nothing at the same time... when you try to have a good idea... nothing comes to mind. And it's not like a normal burnout where you're just emotionally fried, it's just cognitive. You can't think, you can't generate ideas." A month earlier, writing a newsletter or article "was a breeze... ideas were flowing... arguably one of the best feelings." The longer it lasted, the more it compounded: "Why can't I write?... I have all of these ideas... but why can't I remember them?... How do I get that back?" He says the video is "more for myself than I am for you." **Source:** If you've lost your creative genius, watch this.md. "Brain fried" is his term for cognitive, as opposed to emotional, burnout. The same video is the one in which he recommends true boredom as a route back to flow (Section 39.4).

**Overwork and the AI race.** In May 2025 he explains how overwork damaged his thinking. As a startup founder he felt he had to work all day to keep up with the AI race, until he realized there is no keeping up with it. "If I'm working so hard to keep up with something, that usually means I'm following someone else's lead," not having an outsized idea that sets him apart; narrowed into the race, he would never have the idea that lets him work less and get more results. After two months of working very hard and pumping out content, his writing suffered. Writing is his "compass" for whether he is in a good or bad state of mind, having good days or good ideas. He could not think clearly; rereading old writing (whose style he now cringes at) he wondered how he had come up with those ideas, which seemed unfathomable from his overworked state. He missed the long walks on which his brain lit up with connections, clarity, focus and a grasp of his mission. It felt like a "limiter on my pattern recognition system": he could not connect things, and he lost the dopamine that connection gives. The fix was to take more rest intentionally, physically stopping himself from working more, and letting go of the irrational fear that others would pass him (a fear he could only see as irrational once he had let it go). The result: more books, more podcasts, boredom filled with inspiration; novel information let his brain connect things and spark ideas again, and clarity returned. His viewers notice too: YouTube comments tell him when he has not rested, because his videos become "repetitive cookie cutter boringness" (though, he adds, some things are worth repeating). **Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md. The story is the intensity trap from inside: a season of intensity prolonged by fear rather than chosen by clarity, ending in the loss of the very capacity (pattern recognition) that made the intensity productive.

**The migraine cycle.** The earliest story, from November 2022, is the smallest and shows the general method. That week he had migraines every morning (he insists a migraine is not a headache). He noticed that this is a cycle that happens "every like three years" in his life. He has not pinpointed why, and it may be random, but "me noticing these cycles helps me break out of that and potentially create a solution." His fix, which he calls a very bad practice and says he will not sustain, was to put a Diet Mountain Dew by his bed and drink it on waking; caffeine helps with migraines, and he had no migraine. **Source:** The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md. The point is not the soda. It is that awareness of a cycle makes intervention possible, which is the whole argument of this section in miniature.
