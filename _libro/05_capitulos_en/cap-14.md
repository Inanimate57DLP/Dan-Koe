## Chapter 14 — Learning How to Learn: Build to Learn

Part VI begins where the self-government of Part V leaves off. Chapters 11 to 13 described how Dan Koe protects attention, organizes a few hours of focused work and restores order through rest. None of that says what the protected hours should be filled with when the person does not yet know how to do the thing they want to do. That is the question of this chapter: how a person acquires the knowledge and skill that a self-chosen goal demands.

The chapter depends on three earlier pieces of the book. Chapter 2 supplied the critique and the first definitions: Devon Eriksen's distinction between education and job training, the author's formula "education is not programming," and his 2026 definition of **self-education** as "the process of learning what's necessary to achieve self-generated goals," with "no hoarding general knowledge to feel like you're learning." Chapter 6 supplied the mechanism: a goal acts as a lens, and in its 2026 cybernetic version, "a goal creates the error signal, the error signal creates the filter, the filter creates relevance, and relevance creates retention"; from this Koe concluded that "learning is an output process." Chapter 9 supplied the unit of practice: the project, as anchor, outline and lens, which in 2024 he named the Clarity Catalyst. Chapter 11 supplied a warning that becomes central here: the difference between cheap dopamine, received without effort, and dopamine earned through struggle.

With those pieces in place, Koe's theory of learning can be stated in five steps, which are the five sections of the chapter. Section 14.1 presents learning how to learn as "the meta skill," the case for daily self-education, Devon Eriksen's seven "liberating arts" and the three "vessels" Koe proposes for practicing them, and the author's own education as the story from which the theory was drawn. Section 14.2 treats curiosity as the guide that tells a person what to learn, together with the attitude Koe calls moving "from cope to curiosity" and the three conditions he identified for retaining what he reads. Section 14.3 diagnoses the main failure: information consumed without application, which feels like progress and is not ("tutorial hell"), including the author's account of how he fell into it for years. Section 14.4 presents the remedy, the central idea of the chapter: "don't learn to build, build to learn," with the reasons Koe gives for why building works, the sequences he has proposed over five years, the guitar method and the Build Teach Earn method. Section 14.5 adds the last link: teaching is the strongest form of learning, and teaching in public turns learning into feedback, an audience and, eventually, a business.

One feature of the corpus should be kept in mind throughout. Between 2022 and 2026 Koe described the same learning method under many names ("become a builder," "start then learn," "rapid building," "start, build, learn," "build then learn," the Clarity Catalyst, "project-based learning," "start with the purpose") and with a varying number of steps. The core does not change: have a project, start with what you know, learn what the project's problems demand at the moment they appear, and teach what you learn. What changes is emphasis (how much to study before starting), vocabulary and the theoretical frame used to justify it (from novelty and dopamine in 2022 to cybernetics in 2026). The chapter presents the versions where they differ, rather than collapsing them into one.

### 14.1 The Meta Skill and Self-Education

#### "The skill of all skills"

Koe's most compact statement of why learning matters comes from a 2025 video: "The mark of a free person is that they learn how to learn; that is the meta skill, that is the skill of all skills." His reasoning has three links. First, to pick up any skill that changes your life, you need to know how to learn it effectively; every other skill passes through this one. Second, the choice of what to learn is never neutral: "If you don't choose what to learn you will be told what to learn, and if your mind is the operating system for reality the options available for your future will be drastically limited and you won't even realize it." Third, the world is changing fast (he names technology and AI), which makes learning "the single most important skill, because it allows you to learn the skills that can't be taught in schools." Schools are a large and possibly outdated system; changing a curriculum takes far longer than it takes an individual to learn a topic and make a YouTube video from which you can learn it even faster. If you keep up and adapt, "adding the latest skills to your tool belt," you "can do some incredible things" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

Two of the phrases deserve comment. **Meta skill** is Koe's label for learning how to learn: the skill whose object is the acquisition of other skills. **Operating system for reality** is one of his recurring images for the mind, which he uses in several senses across 2024 and 2025 (most people run "Windows 98"; the mind is made of goals mostly programmed by others). Here it carries a specific consequence: since the mind determines which options you can see, a mind filled with what others chose for you limits your future invisibly. The sentence is the learning version of the book's central thesis from Chapter 1, that you always pursue goals, assigned or self-generated. Applied to knowledge, it reads: you always learn something; if you do not choose it, it is chosen for you.

The phrase "the mark of a free person" echoes the conversation with the novelist Devon Eriksen that Chapter 2 reported, and the definition of education is his, not Koe's. "Education is not about how to do a task, it's about how to learn a task." Education is "learning how to teach yourself": whenever you take on a hard task, you have to learn how to do it. The consequence Eriksen draws is the one that matters here: "if you don't learn how to learn, then you can only do things that somebody already understands how to do." He adds that there is no "systematic education in this society in the modern West about how to learn and educate yourself" (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`, 2024-12-08). The point connects learning to originality: someone who can only follow instructions can only reproduce what already exists, a theme that returns in Chapter 18.

Koe attaches quantitative promises to the meta skill, and they should be read as he qualifies them. In 2024: "When you learn how to learn you can accomplish 6 years of results in 6 months." He immediately adds, "this really varies," and explains the comparison he has in mind: most people stop learning after college, and it takes them six years to learn something very basic and do something new; following his process and continuously reinventing yourself "over and over again" compresses that (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). He repeats the "6 years in 6 months" line later in 2024 (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). In 2025 the promise takes another form: "If learning allows you to be useful and get what you want out of life, imagine what you could do if you actually spent time learning how to learn." If you knew how to learn fast, "you could achieve 10 goals in the same amount of time that it would take the average person to achieve one," and the average person probably would not achieve it until graduating or until they had "learned enough." In the same video he deliberately skips the science and psychology of learning to focus only on what to do, which he calls "pretty freaking simple. But sometimes the simplest things are the most complex things in the world" to get yourself to do (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10).

These figures are rhetorical comparisons, not measurements, and the corpus itself shows that they move. The evolution record lists Koe's estimates of how long learning takes: "give yourself 20 years, not two weeks" (2023), top 10 percent in six to twelve months (early 2024), "6 years of results in 6 months" (2024), "you can learn anything in two weeks" (2025) and again "2 to 5 years" to master brand, content, marketing and sales even with AI (2026). In 2025 he clarifies that "two weeks" means substantial progress toward a goal, not mastery, which "obviously takes like 10 years." The numbers apply to different scales (a technique, a skill, a business, mastery) and are best read as claims about relative speed: the person who has learned how to learn moves several times faster than the person who has not.

A 2026 video adds an argument about why speed of learning matters at all. If the gap between where you are and where you want to be is a gap of knowledge, then "the future belongs to those who can learn the fastest." Most people stop learning after graduation and run on what Koe calls "preset knowledge." Writing is his antidote, because "80% of writing is research and learning"; it is at once teaching and understanding. He says he learned more in six months of writing when he started out than in all of college or high school. His example is history: he never applied himself to it in school, but since writing about history in some of his newsletters he understands it far better than he did from lectures (**Source:** `The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md`, 2026-07-27). The claim about writing anticipates section 14.5 (writing as a form of teaching) and Part VIII.

#### The earliest version: "figuring it out for yourself"

The meta skill has a precursor in 2022, under a plainer name. In a video whose title announces writing as "the skill" to learn, Koe first names a different one: "there is one extremely important skill, and that is figuring it out for yourself." He defines it by two contrasts. It is not doing what people tell you to do; and it is doing what you want "from a conscious mind," not from a "mediocre mind." He guards against the naive reading. "I can do whatever I want" is not what he means; the caricature of the mediocre mind is the person who replies, "you want me to jump off a bridge, okay, I'll go do that." The conscious version examines itself: what are your desires; what survival mechanism, or whatever else, is pulling you toward those things; is it good or bad; do you actually want to do it; does it align with your values; "do you actually have values." What other people tell you to do will not be conducive to what you want. Then comes a realistic nuance: much of the time you may have to do what others tell you, for survival, but at the same time you need to be pursuing a project of your own that leads to autonomy and to mastery of what you want to master, full time, "not for someone else" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28).

**Figuring it out for yourself** and the pair **conscious mind / mediocre mind** are Koe's terms. The corpus registers a small tension inside the video: the title calls writing "the skill," while the first skill named is figuring it out for yourself, with writing then presented as the skill that monetizes it. The tension resolves if the two are read as layers: figuring things out is the meta skill, writing the skill through which it becomes income. The evolution record treats the move from "figuring it out for yourself" (2022) to "learn how to learn" as the meta skill (2024–2026) as a renaming and formalization, not a change of position. The 2022 version keeps something the later one leaves implicit: the meta skill presupposes self-examination. You cannot choose what to learn if you have not examined what you want and why.

#### Daily self-education as a necessity

From the meta skill Koe derives a habit. In 2023 he presents it as the second step of a daily routine and calls it "an absolute necessity." His argument starts from a judgment about school: "Schools are necessary in many cases, but they teach you a fraction of what actually exists in reality; they train you into a compartment of reality, like chemistry, physiology or literature," without regard "for the holistic interconnectedness that breeds true intelligence." **Compartment of reality** is his term for this: an isolated slice of the world, taught without its connections. The consequence is about identity: "Without self-education you go through life with the same narrow identity and perspective as everyone else; you're not a unique individual if you never self-educate." He then lists what education does. It "expands your mind, introduces you to novel perspectives, increases dopamine in the brain as a consistent source of energy, gives you the knowledge to act with clarity," and "over time education conditions your mind to run on new systems." His example of the last point: immerse yourself only in information about building a profitable business and you would build one. Most people skip this step; their morning has no section "dedicated to reading or consuming or researching or pushing into the mental unknown" (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19).

The dopamine claim should be read in the terms Chapter 11 established: Koe uses dopamine as a name for the anticipation that accompanies novelty relevant to a goal, and the claim here is that learning toward something is a renewable source of that drive. He presents it as part of his model, not as a measured effect of studying.

Earlier in 2023 he states the same necessity in starker terms. "You own one thing: your mind; everything else can be taken away." School, he grants, got one thing right: consistent daily education and the hope of a better future. But it does not prioritize curiosity, so most students hate learning by graduation. "Learning is the foundation of the human experience, and once you stop you die." The prescription is modest in size and absolute in frequency: learn something every day, whether ten minutes or three hours. His reason is that learning is the only way to discover a potential that was not assigned to you. You cannot act on opportunities that do not exist for you, and you cannot even register them without having reached a level of skill by doing your own thing. His example: until you have started a business, you cannot look at someone online and think, "he has this business, I have this offer, he can pay me this much, I'll reach out in a way I know, then systemize it." When you stop learning, life stops progressing, "feel-good chemicals" stop flowing, and life becomes "mediocre, repetitive, mechanical, robotic" (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30).

In 2024 he gives the mechanism behind that example a name. While you learn and build, "you're increasing your awareness surface area": "when you have more skills, you have more education, you have exposure to different opportunities, you can identify more profitable problems and opportunities that you can solve and go into" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). **Awareness surface area** is a coined term: the extent of reality in which a person can recognize problems and opportunities, which grows with skill, education and exposure. It explains why the person who has started something sees opportunities that the person who is still waiting cannot see, and it connects to the reticular-activation argument of Chapter 6: what you know and pursue determines what registers.

Two further passages from 2023 tie self-education to creativity and to the unusual life. The first places it at the foundation of a creative career. The first step is self-education (not formal education, "though that can be useful"). Creativity is "using your diverse knowledge and interest to help you achieve a goal." If you have never learned anything outside what you were told to learn, you are not "an actual creative"; if you only know what schools teach, you can only create what may already exist, because "you can't create something rare or unknown without going down a rare or unknown path." This is less about becoming an expert than about "a constant influx of information" to fuel your goals and what you build. Without unique interests, knowledge and skills you lack **creative firepower**, Koe's term for the reserve of unusual inputs that creation draws on (**Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31). The second passage draws the conclusion about who can take that path: "The only people who have the choice to take a rare path that breeds rare results are the self-educators." It repeats the list of what education does and adds one more: it exposes you to potentials you were not aware of. A consistent flow of education increases the chance of encountering meaningful events, which "occur at the edge of the known, when your nervous system signals that you should pay attention": with one foot in the unknown you can "just barely metabolize new aspects of reality" and use them (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The **edge of the known** is the same border that Chapter 10 described as the place of flow, where challenge slightly exceeds skill; here it is described from the side of information rather than effort.

A 2023 video turns the necessity into a protocol for the digital age. "Lifelong self-education will be a requirement to continue adapting to the digital landscape." "Education shapes the future because it shapes human behavior"; it "expands your perspective and allows you to spot opportunities in unknown territory." "The new education system is being built online before your eyes." The steps: set a goal; find creators you resonate with; drown yourself in their information; watch new opportunities register in your awareness; and act on them "like your life depends on it" (**Source:** `The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md`, 2023-07-19). The order matters. The goal comes first, so that the "drowning" is filtered; the creators come second, chosen by resonance; and the last step is action. Without the last step the protocol degenerates into the consumption that section 14.3 criticizes.

#### Why daily learning fails

Self-education has a habitual form, and in 2024 Koe makes it the seventh of twelve rules. "You aren't where you want to be because you aren't as smart as you think you are." Every project requires a skill set and a mindset, so you need education to achieve goals and complete projects. "Daily self-education must become a cornerstone habit in your life. If you stop learning you stop evolving, opportunities stop presenting themselves and you get trapped in your current stage of development. Education is the fuel for experimentation, and without experimentation you are doing what other people tell you to do" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). A **cornerstone habit**, in his usage, is a habit that supports the rest of a lifestyle; here it is the habit that keeps a person developing. The last sentence of the rule links learning to the experimentation of Chapter 15: education feeds experiments, and without experiments you fall back on instructions.

In 2025 he explains why the habit so often fails to produce change. "Daily learning and self-education is so freaking important because all of these things overlap and intertwine. Just treat daily self-education toward your goals as a habit and your life will change." The phrase "toward your goals" carries the weight. Recounting how he transformed his health, he says he did not pick a random diet or random exercises, "because that's extremely chaotic. The mind craves order"; he refers to the psychology of the hierarchy of goals and to "turning your life into a video game" (Chapters 8 and 10). He was not forcing anything random: "I had clarity and novelty and dopamine that was leading to me doing something." Most people, he says, have two problems. First, they are not hyper-aware of a painful problem, "so their mind doesn't release dopamine at the sight of a potential solution." Second, "they learn without experimentation. So they never make the progress necessary to act as a feedback loop that keeps them motivated" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24).

The two failures are the two halves of the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6. Without a felt problem there is no error signal, and information about solutions does not register as relevant. Without experimentation there is no output, and therefore no feedback telling you whether you are progressing. The evolution record notes that the 2023 passages emphasize the necessity of self-education and its effect of expanding the mind, while the 2025 passage adds this diagnosis of why it fails. There is no change of position; the later version explains the conditions under which the earlier one works.

The durations Koe attaches to the habit vary across videos: a "general education habit" of ten to thirty minutes a day (2023), thirty to sixty minutes of self-education plus thirty to sixty of building (late 2023), "a daily 30 to 60 minute habit, this isn't optional" (2024), a book for thirty minutes a day (2025). The variation reflects different purposes (general education, acquiring a skill, reading) more than a change of view.

#### Becoming your own expert

A cluster of early passages, mostly from 2022, makes a broad claim about what self-education can reach. "Anything you want, any skill, you can learn online." If you have a problem in your life, there is something you can learn and "become directly conscious of until you can test a system for getting that desired outcome." By self-educating "you are literally creating a better life." He extends this to the inner life: self-development includes not only tangible aspects but mindset and intellectual development, which "has to be a top priority"; you must consistently learn to break through stages of development and to build creative solutions for those behind you (**Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`, 2022-06-25). The criterion embedded in the claim is practical: learning ends not when you know about a problem but when you can test a system that produces the outcome. Chapter 15 develops this as self-experimentation.

He illustrates it with his own example. Everything he knows about the gym he learned from fitness YouTubers. There are "infinite perspectives" to study, so "I can become my own personal trainer, I can become my own relationship therapist, I can become my own psychologist," "anything that I want to become," because ample education exists without paying more than $40,000. He specifies the scope: he gives advice to others on business, spirituality and personal development, but in areas like gym and health he simply uses his desire for self-education to become his own expert (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31). The claim is a statement of his practice and his opinion; he does not argue that self-taught therapy equals professional care, and the reader should not read that into it.

The same optimism appears in career terms. "We're all lucky," he says in 2022: to learn graphic design you can watch an hour-long YouTube video that teaches what you need, make a few projects and apply for a design job. If you are smart enough, and if the employer does not write off talent without a degree ("a lot of them are opening up that option"), you could get a job for watching a few hours of YouTube, or freelance and do what you want (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). The conditions are his: a few projects (not only the video), an employer willing to hire without a degree. In the same video he floats an idea about the other side of the market: in school you get an hour of after-school one-on-one mentoring, so "why isn't online mentoring a bigger thing?" If you know design well and some of your followers want to learn it to increase their own value, they can hire you for a Zoom call in which you walk them through the fundamentals; you earn an income and freelance work may come to you (same source). **Online mentoring** is presented as something he was "toying with," an opinion rather than a method; it foreshadows the education businesses of Part X.

A 2023 video gives the most concrete early recipe, framed as the second step of recreating yourself. "It's silly to think you can achieve self-generated goals without self-education." "Immerse your conscious mind in the information that will program a new identity and give you the knowledge to attack your goals." Concretely: follow social media accounts that give specific information related to your goals; then "purchase one to three books on the topic and devour them." He answers those who object to social media: they do not understand how integrated and powerful it is; people see it as bad (news, toxicity) but you can curate your experience; it is woven into society, "necessary," part of our lives "whether you like it or not" (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19).

This recipe is the starting point of one of the unresolved oscillations of the corpus, and it has to be read alongside what comes later. In December 2023 Koe says "reading books is not learning, watching tutorials is not learning" (section 14.4). In September 2025 he says books and YouTube used for learning are "entertainment" and that reading to learn "is just creating more chaos" (section 14.3). Between those, in 2024, he still recommends buying three books and "burning through them," and says reading is how you absorb years of others' experience. The evolution record classifies the question "is reading books learning?" as an unresolved contradiction. The most defensible reading, which the corpus supports without stating it in one place, distinguishes two purposes: reading to learn *how to do* something, which in his later view only works when a project gives it a use, and reading to see the world differently, which he continues to value (Chapter 16). The 2023 recipe is closer to the first purpose, but it sits inside a process whose next step is building, which is why it is less opposed to the later view than it first appears.

#### Study human nature first: the seven liberating arts

If the future is unknowable, what should one learn? Koe's 2024 answer begins with a principle: "Study human nature, then study whatever you want." It is impossible to know the future; all we know is that there will be a way of life that leads to the most satisfaction. You cannot depend on school, government, parents or anyone else to secure your future; every successful person "by any measure" has taken it into their own hands. An ideal future lifestyle will have unique aspects, but also "immutable principles of human nature that have persisted and will persist," and those you must learn. To find them, look at what schools do not teach: if schools train "modern slaves" for one task, what do "sovereign individuals" teach themselves? (**Source:** `The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md`, 2024-03-24). The argument is a bet on invariants: since content changes, learn what does not.

His answer to the question comes from Devon Eriksen, whose list he reads aloud in several videos in 2024. The attributions must be kept clear: the list and its definitions are Eriksen's; the remarks about agency, the method of learning the arts and the mapping onto vessels are Koe's. Eriksen calls his list the **Seven Liberal Arts of the modern world**, and prefers the name **liberating arts**, so as not to be confused with "the ideological monstrosity that is taught in liberal art school"; in Koe's paraphrase, "the liberal arts are liberating arts; what's taught in Liberal Arts School are not liberating" (**Source:** `The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md`, 2024-03-24; `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28). Each art is defined by what it lets you do:

| Liberating art (Eriksen) | What it lets you do (Eriksen's definition) | Koe's vessel for practicing it |
|---|---|---|
| Logic | Derive truth from known facts | Writing and thinking |
| Statistics | Understand the implications of data | Entrepreneurship |
| Rhetoric | Persuade, and spot persuasion tactics | Marketing and sales |
| Research | Gather information on an unknown subject | Writing and thinking |
| Practical psychology | Discern and understand the true motives of others | Marketing and sales |
| Investment | Manage and grow existing assets | Entrepreneurship |
| Agency | Decide what course to pursue and proactively take action to pursue it | Entrepreneurship |

(**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`, 2024-12-08; vessels from `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28.)

The list is presented as the answer to the question "what should we learn if we don't know what will be relevant?": these are meta-skills of self-education rather than task training. In the March 2024 version Koe draws the strongest conclusion: with these arts, technical or career-specific skills "won't matter," because you can adapt to and change them at any time. In the December 2024 version he adds a remark of his own: agency, "the last art," "could be the glue between it all," and asks why the liberating arts are not taught. The transcripts give only the list and one-line definitions; no order of learning and no relationship among the components is stated beyond that remark about agency. The name of the author of the list appears as "Devon Ericson" in March 2024 and "Devon Eriksen" later; the evolution record treats this as a transcription variant of the same person, with stable content and a small addition in each version.

**Complementary context:** The classical seven liberal arts were grammar, logic and rhetoric (the trivium) and arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy (the quadrivium), the curriculum considered fitting for a free person, as Chapter 2 noted. Eriksen's list keeps the classical idea (the education of a free person) and two of its arts (logic, rhetoric), and replaces the rest with capacities oriented to acting in a modern economy.

How does one learn such arts? Koe's answer in July 2024 is the most interesting part of his treatment, because it applies the theory of this chapter to them. "You must hold them in your mind as you dive into the unknown, or as you take a new path, because you don't just learn something by reading it. You hold it in your mind and you let new experiences and the chaos of the unknown bring that clarity to your mind and allow you to practice those skills in real time." Prepared in this way, "they slowly just become a part of who you are and your identity," and whatever technical skills are or are not relevant in twenty years, "you'll be able to learn them fast and get results with them fast, because you have the underlying traits that make those skills successful" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28). The method is build-to-learn applied to abstract capacities: the arts are not studied as subjects but carried as lenses into real situations, where the situations do the teaching. The goal is identity change (Chapter 4), not information.

Because abstract arts need concrete situations, Koe proposes three practical skills as **vessels** through which they are practiced. "Think of these next skills as vessels for learning the softer skills that we just talked about." The first is marketing and sales, "because if you don't know how to attract and persuade you will never get what you want, and your only option will be for an employer or the government to give it to you"; it exercises rhetoric and practical psychology. The second is writing and thinking, "the ability to communicate the value in your unique mind, the foundation of getting in front of other people"; it exercises logic and research. The third is entrepreneurship, "the process of taking my future into my own hands, hunting for my survival in building products that I want to see in the world that others care about"; it exercises statistics, agency and investment. He calls them "futureproof skills" and "evergreen." He also describes how they interact: "with writing and thinking you attract like-minded people, with marketing and sales you persuade them," and through marketing and sales "you understand the minds of your customer so that you can solve your own problems and create a solution for them" (same source). The mapping in the table above is his, not Eriksen's. **Vessel** keeps the sense Chapter 2 and Chapter 9 gave it: the vehicle through which something is pursued. Here the vessels are the vehicles through which deeper capacities are trained. The three vessels also correspond to the structure of the rest of this book: writing (Part VIII), audience and persuasion (Parts IX and XI), and the one-person business (Part X).

#### Knowledge not found in schools

Koe sums up one strand of his thinking in a tweet he quotes in two videos in 2023: "Knowledge not found in schools is the source of money not found in employment." In the first, he states it after telling the story of learning web development faster than his college course (below) and adds that "we are in the middle of a massive shift"; shifts are slow ("we don't go through an Industrial Revolution in five seconds"), and this shift is not only about money (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). In the second, the tweet sums up the video and he adds an image: with the internet, information has spread beyond measure, like "a wildfire," leading to a more advanced, global and accessible society online (**Source:** `The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md`, 2023-06-25). The tweet joins two of the book's threads: the critique of school (Chapter 2) and the economy of the individual (Part XII). Its logic is symmetrical: if the employed path is defined by the knowledge schools give, the knowledge they do not give is what opens the other path.

Why must that knowledge be turned into income at all? In 2022 Koe builds the argument as a chain. You want to do what you love full time; that requires mastery and autonomy; to have those you must be able to earn from them; so "you need to learn a skill that will allow you to monetize those things, to monetize your interests and your passions." He then presents writing as that skill, one that lets you thrive "no matter whether there's a recession" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28). In another 2022 video he states the position more modestly. He is a fan of doing what you want with your life, which usually implies pursuing and developing the skills that let you make an income doing it. "That doesn't have to be via entrepreneurship, but that's what I've done for the past three to four years" and what he teaches; you can also get a job with these skills. He speaks, he says, "from the lens of entrepreneurship and the creator economy" (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28).

That modesty does not last. From 2024 Koe calls entrepreneurship "the only path" to total control of one's work, and the evolution record classifies his stance on employment as an unresolved oscillation between "the worst outcome," "slavery," "a stepping stone" and, by 2025, admitted routes inside organizations (Chapter 2 traces it in full). For this chapter the relevant point is what the oscillation does not touch: in every version, doing what you want requires learning skills that produce income.

And learning them is hard. In 2025 he says so bluntly: "None of this is anywhere close to being easy. That's the entire [damn] point. You're going to have to learn more skills": how to attract people, write, persuade, market, sell. "You're the CEO. You need to learn every single skill that requires you to operate a business along with your multiple interests." You need to become a generalist, have taste, "package up and deliver signal to attract people," and merge art and business (**Source:** `How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md`, 2025-02-23). **CEO** here is used in his own sense: the person who operates their own business and therefore has to learn every skill it requires. The passage is a bridge to Chapter 20 (the generalist) and Chapter 18 (taste); for this chapter its importance is that it sets the scale of the learning task. The meta skill is not a luxury for curious people; it is the condition of running a one-person business.

#### The author's education: learning from the people he wanted to become

Koe's theory of learning is drawn from his own history, and he tells that history often. The story has two strands: learning online from people he admired, and learning to code faster than college could teach him. Both are told with variations, and the variations are noted where they matter.

The first strand begins in childhood. As a child he "didn't trust the education system or my parents or even my religious teachers to equip me with the knowledge necessary to secure my future." Instead he consumed content from YouTubers, bloggers and people on social media whose information aligned with his interests or sparked new ones, to pursue what schools were not teaching, usually around increasing his quality of life. He names three areas: fitness (for him, bodybuilding; for you, he says, it could be running or cycling); business (he started with freelancing); and philosophy and meaning, "the need for Spirit, something beyond yourself," because he questioned the religion he grew up in. He bought affordable courses to learn skills he probably could not have learned better through a degree (**Source:** `Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md`, 2023-04-30). Chapter 2 described that upbringing; here it matters as the origin of a habit: when the institutions around him did not supply what he wanted to know, he went to the internet for it.

He describes the internet of his youth as a refuge. Pursuing an unconventional career "has not been available for a long time," and he feels fortunate to have grown up during the internet boom; the internet was where he went to "find solace" and people to connect with. Following fitness YouTubers, he learned a great deal, which got him into the gym for ten years and "opened my mind." It expanded his awareness that "there's an economy and then there's a digital economy, and they both work similarly," and that he needed to learn how to get in and create something he could monetize (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). **Digital economy** is a common phrase used here in his sense: a parallel economy that works like the traditional one and that has to be learned in order to enter it. It should not be confused with **Digital Economics**, the name of his own program, discussed below.

The fullest version of the fitness story, from 2024, contains a scene. In eighth grade Koe (who is six foot five) and his best friend were pushed by their math teacher, Mr. Schroeder, the after-school weights coach, to start training. His first bench press was about 65 pounds on a rusty bar, arms shaking, with a strange pain and worse soreness afterward. With time and progress, "rather than being distracted and just flooding your mind with cheap dopamine," he became obsessed with weight training, and at the time of the video had spent more than ten years in the gym. His high-school routine: go home, drink pre-workout, grab his gym bag, review his training program (Matt Ogus's program), train hard with a friend, come home, drink a protein shake, and watch YouTube. He watched only fitness channels; the transcript names Matt Ogus, Elliot Holz, Chris Lavado, Chris Jones and Scott Herman (spellings as transcribed). His realization was that "these creators were doing what they loved, impacting others at scale and exposing people to their potential." That was when he began to deconstruct how to do the same. Years of failures followed (a fitness YouTube channel, a digital art Instagram page, several marketing agencies) before he saw success with freelance web design while transitioning to being a creator (**Source:** `The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md`, 2024-01-21).

A 2025 version of the same years explains the learning mechanism behind the obsession. In middle school he was "the tall and super lanky dude," disliked how he looked, felt awkward in his skin and lacked confidence. He used that pain to educate himself on fitness and nutrition: he read *Nutrient Timing for Peak Performance* on the bus ride to middle school, did push-ups in his room, went to after-school weights, and "my love for the gym just slowly picked up from there." He could not change his physical environment (he lived with his parents, had certain friends, lived in a specific place), but he could curate his digital environment, which "creates and programs your identity by immersing yourself in the right information." After school he went straight to the computer to watch "the two to three fitness vloggers at the time when YouTube was just starting out." "That changed my entire life." Each video gave him "this novel hit of dopamine, because I had something new to try": go to the gym, try the new exercise or program, stick to it, see progress, "create this feedback loop, fall in love with it." The same was true of nutrition. If you are not sticking to yours, he says, it is probably "because you're not surrounded by the people in your physical or digital space that give you these new tips and tricks and knowledge that you can implement and see direct feedback from" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24).

The passage is a compact model of the learning loop the rest of the chapter develops: a painful problem (insecurity about his body), a curated stream of goal-relevant information (fitness vloggers), immediate application (the new exercise that afternoon), visible progress, and a feedback loop that turns effort into obsession. **Curate your digital environment** is one of his terms (Chapter 11 develops it); here it appears as the one environment a teenager could control. The evolution record notes that the stories of when he started in the gym vary (after a first breakup, in eighth grade with Mr. Schroeder, from age fifteen, in middle school with *Nutrient Timing*); the variations are narrative and compatible.

He states the result of all this with unusual emphasis. Education businesses have helped him enormously, not only because he runs one but because they "changed the course of my life": "I would not have anything in my entire lifestyle if it weren't for me being able to learn online from people that I aspire to be like" since childhood. He apologizes for his language, because he gets "very passionate" about the subject (**Source:** `The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md`, 2023-04-23). The gratitude explains a feature of his business model (Part X): he sells education because education online is what made him.

Koe also names the books that resonate with him, as an example of what curiosity-driven consumption looks like. In a long 2024 guide he lists a "select few": *The Power of Now* (which he has not read in a while), *Awareness*, *The Way of the Superior Man*, *Flow* by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, *Rest* by Alex Soojung-Kim Pang, and "everything Alan Watts" (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). He uses the list to illustrate curiosity (section 14.2), not to draw ideas from the books at that point; later in the same guide he uses Alan Watts as an example of philosophical writing that may not perform well online.

**Complementary context:** *The Power of Now* is by Eckhart Tolle, *Awareness* by Anthony de Mello and *The Way of the Superior Man* by David Deida; the transcript does not name these three authors. Chapter 13 discussed Pang's *Rest*; Chapter 10 discussed Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow*.

#### Learning to code faster than college

The second strand is the story Koe uses most often as proof that self-education outpaces formal education. Its core is stable across versions; its details are not.

In the 2022 version he was living with six other young men in an old fraternity house near Arizona State University, around his junior year ("timeline fuzzy," the source notes). He took an introductory web development class, learned basic HTML and CSS in two sessions and became obsessed with coding, which promised a job without a degree, no corporate nine-to-five and creative work. He took courses after school. This, he says, solidified the value of self-education for him: always learning and building projects outside school or work, something that gave him "intrinsic energy." He learned the course's curriculum "in like three weeks just by studying on my own," did not attend class and still got an A. He knew time was running out: either make something work or "end up like everyone else" (**Source:** `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md`, 2022-06-18). **Intrinsic energy** is his phrase for the energy that learning and building on his own terms gave him, in contrast to assigned work.

The 2023 version adds the tools and the price. In his final year he took a basic web development and programming class, the first course in four years he was genuinely interested in. After school he spent three to four hours a day studying HTML, CSS and JavaScript through free platforms and paid Udemy courses at about ten dollars each. "Within two weeks I had learned the entire college course worth of curriculum on my own," stopped going to class, and still aced every test. "This is when the reality of formal education slapped me in the face" (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). The tweet about knowledge not found in schools, quoted above, follows immediately in that video.

Another 2023 version adds the motive. Koe was "a creative at heart" who wanted the dream lifestyle seen on TV and the internet ("everyone's on the beach working from their laptop, or just not working at all"). He saw artists, graphic designers and web designers and saw in their work "a way to get out of a job"; he notes that the people he followed were conditioning him to want it. He studied graphic design, film, Photoshop, Illustrator and video editing. He knew he needed a skill the market valued without giving up his **Creative Edge**, his term for the creative trait he refused to lose. In his fourth year he took a web development class and "became obsessed": he did not understand the curriculum, studied on his own, things clicked, and after school he went to the library and studied all day, buying courses, using freeCodeCamp and YouTube, coding along "for like 6 hours a day." Coding enticed him because it promised a job without a degree, and front-end development ("what you see on the internet") kept his creative edge: "I found it: this is the one thing that allows me to both be creative, earn a high income, and potentially freelance or build a software." He always wanted to do his own thing, to be an entrepreneur and creator, so "getting a job was the bane of my existence" (**Source:** `How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md`, 2023-10-08).

The 2024 versions add the lesson and the context. In one, he was in his fifth year of university with perhaps two years left, because he had switched majors many times (business, marketing, graphic design, filmmaking, a semester each) before landing on coding. He enjoyed it so much that he studied in his free time, at the library after class and through tutorials at home, and learned the curriculum in two to three weeks; he stopped attending and still aced the tests. The lesson he draws is "the power of self-education": "if you actually have a goal and curiosity you can learn so much more, so much faster than most people," and "that's how you get ahead of everyone else" (**Source:** `How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md`, 2024-10-20). The formula joins the two drivers of this section and the next: goal and curiosity.

The version in the summary of his book *The Art of Focus* places the story inside his childhood sense of isolation. As a child he felt alone, unable to relate to peers who obeyed parents and teachers and conformed to friends without questioning; they went to school, got a job, "and from that point forward you never heard anything interesting from them again." He realized a job would not give him fulfilling work. He spent five years at university with two years left; took out "$2,000 in loans even with a full-ride scholarship"; knew he wanted his own path; discovered his love of web development after an introductory course taken out of curiosity; learned the curriculum through self-education "in less than a month"; and, after multiple business failures and accepting that he needed a job to survive, used his self-study to land a high-paying tech job after his first interview. His conclusions are two questions: "If I could learn more useful skills in 6 months of self-education than I could in 5 years of formal education, why hadn't I done that earlier?" And if self-study got him a job that paid more than most, which would later transfer into "a hyper-profitable business," "why did I waste so much time in college?" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The 2022 career-path video gives the sequel. Code can be self-taught online; you can increase your value quickly and get a job without a degree in six to twelve months. "That's what I did: I got a job in a year by learning to code, I used my income from that job to fuel my side business," which at the time of recording had been his full-time business for three years. He started Digital Economics, his own school, because he wanted to train people to fuel the new digital economy (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). **Digital Economics** was the name of his program for "productizing yourself" between 2022 and 2024.

| Detail | Versions in the corpus |
|---|---|
| Time to learn the course curriculum | About three weeks (2022); two weeks (2023); two to three weeks (2024); "less than a month" (2024, book summary) |
| Year of university | Junior year, "timeline fuzzy" (2022); final or fourth year (2023); fifth year with about two left (2024) |
| Study load | Courses after school (2022); 3–4 hours a day (2023); "like 6 hours a day" (2023) |
| Loans | "$2,000 ... even with a full-ride scholarship" (2024); $20,000 in student loans in other videos |

The evolution record treats these as variations of memory; the $2,000 figure may be a transcription error for $20,000, which cannot be confirmed. None of the variations affects the lesson Koe draws, which is consistent in every version: with a goal and curiosity, a person learning on their own and applying what they learn moves several times faster than a classroom. The story also contains a detail that section 14.3 will need. Learning web development quickly did not by itself produce a business; the years that followed were years of failure. The self-taught coder had learned a skill, but not yet the skills that bring customers.

### 14.2 Curiosity as the Guide

Section 14.1 argued that a person must choose what to learn, or have it chosen for them. It did not say how to choose. Koe's answer has two parts that work together. The goal (Chapter 6) tells you what is relevant; curiosity tells you where your energy for learning actually is. The goal without curiosity produces the joyless studying he associates with school; curiosity without a goal produces endless consumption. This section presents what Koe means by curiosity, how he says to discover it, the stages it passes through, the attitude he calls moving "from cope to curiosity," and the three conditions he identified, from his own reading, for retaining what one learns.

#### Curiosity, recognized by experience

Koe admits that curiosity is hard to define. In 2022, describing the "value creator" (the person who makes a career of exploring, synthesizing and sharing ideas, Chapter 18), he says value creators dedicate themselves to exploring the unknown and their curiosity, taking as their life's work the dissection of their "crevices of reality" (self-improvement, marketing, business, spirituality, metaphysics, philosophy, email marketing), "letting one thing lead to another by curiosity." Then he concedes that curiosity confuses people, him included, because it is not tangible. He can only explain it from experience and feeling: "think of the book that you actually wanted to read, think of the videos that you actually wanted to watch," the things that were "seamless," where you thought, "man, I'm loving learning about this" (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31). He repeats the definition almost word for word in 2024 and adds two consequences. Material that resonates makes it effortless to study and makes you want to talk about it; if certain authors do not resonate with you, that is a sign to look for the people, writing and interests that do. And it yields a rule for content: "post the things that are interesting to you, because they're going to be interesting to someone else." If something in a book catches your attention, some other people will think so too; distill it for your audience and synthesize it with your interests or goals. His own resonant books, listed in section 14.1, are his example (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

**Curiosity** is a common word that Koe uses in a sense of his own: not a general disposition to be interested in things, but a recognizable experience of effortless engagement with particular material, identified after the fact by its signs (wanting to read, losing track of effort, wanting to talk about it). The definition is deliberately phenomenological. It does not tell you what you will be curious about; it tells you how to recognize it when it happens. Its practical consequence is a test you can run on your own history: look for what you consumed without being made to.

The content rule is the first link between curiosity and teaching, which section 14.5 develops. It rests on an assumption Koe states as a heuristic rather than a law: that one's own interest is a reliable predictor of someone else's. He says "some" others will share it, not everyone, which is all that a niche of one (Chapter 19) requires.

#### Choosing an obsession: you cannot outsource it

If curiosity is recognized by experience, it cannot be found by asking. In the 2022 value-creator video, Koe makes "choose an obsession" the first step of researching a field, and his first instruction is negative: "Don't try to outsource this." Asking someone "what's my obsession?" is pointless. Have you tried anything? Have you been pulled down a rabbit hole? "If you don't try, you'll never know," so try everything that potentially catches your interest, because your mind and ego will distract you. His examples of distraction are video games and going out with friends; neither is bad in itself, but they become a problem when they keep you from progress. He then gives three places to start looking. First, your browser history on YouTube and Google: what you naturally consume to learn, not to distract yourself. Second, what you go on social media to learn: whom you follow who educates others, and how you could emulate and reverse-engineer them, "deconstruct that whole into parts." Third, your own life: what have you improved the most? If you are a gym junkie, a runner, an autodidact or a polymath, how has it helped you, and how could you help others become that? (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31).

**Obsession** and **rabbit hole** are terms Koe uses in a specific sense: an interest that pulls you along by itself, discovered by exposure rather than introspection. The three starting points are evidence-based in a modest way: each looks at behavior that has already happened (what you searched, whom you followed, what you improved) rather than at what you think you should be interested in. The third connects to Chapter 19: what you have improved most in your own life is a candidate for what you can help others with, because the customer is your past self.

#### The four paths of curiosity

In the summary of *The Art of Focus*, Koe gives curiosity a direction. "Let curiosity be your guide into the icebergs of opportunity that life presents"; "curiosity is the fuel along your path." When you push into the unknown, novel information grabs your attention, and that attention can be "your best friend or worst enemy." He then lays out four paths along which curiosity can be pursued (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18):

| Path | What it involves, in Koe's words |
|---|---|
| Appreciation | Appreciate ideas, people and opportunities for what they are; no need to judge or project |
| Understanding | Seek to understand more deeply by immersing yourself in books, content and information relevant to that area |
| Mastery | Once you understand something, choose to master it; when you feel the pull to go deeper, do not suppress it |
| Monetization | Having gone all-in on a select few areas, pair those skills and interests with business principles to create a living |

The table can be read as a sequence of increasing commitment. Appreciation costs nothing and can be applied to anything; understanding requires immersion; mastery requires a choice to go deep; monetization applies only to "a select few areas" and requires business principles in addition to the interest itself. Most curiosities stop at the first or second path, and Koe does not say they should go further; the four paths describe the options, not an obligation. The **icebergs of opportunity** image (in an earlier 2022 form, the "iceberg of opportunity") says that what you consume is the tip; what lies beneath can become a career.

In the same passage he condenses his whole method into one formula: dive into the unknown by pursuing your vision-aligned hierarchy of goals; educate yourself to gain clarity; execute the smallest priority task; keep an open mind to discover new potentials. The formula places curiosity inside the structure of Part IV. Curiosity is the fuel; the vision and the hierarchy of goals are the route; self-education gives clarity; and the smallest priority task (Chapter 12) is the next step. Curiosity does not replace the goal; it powers the movement toward it.

#### The curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle

How does curiosity become a stable part of a life? In 2024 Koe describes what he calls the **curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle** "of the universe," which stories and games follow: "first you're lost, then you're curious, then you're obsessed, then it's a consistent part of your life with little extra effort." He contrasts it with what most people do: they "get lost, focus on distractions and never put effort into a goal, even if they don't see the meaning in it yet" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The clause "even if they don't see the meaning in it yet" carries a claim worth isolating: meaning may come after effort, not before it. The cycle does not begin with a passion that then justifies the work; it begins with being lost, passes through curiosity and an intense period of obsession, and ends in consistency that requires little extra effort. Later in the same video he describes routine as moving "from intensity to consistency."

The cycle has a counterpart that section 14.3 examines from the side of failure. In 2023 Koe names the phases a person passes through when they start learning: a lost phase, a clarity phase and an intensity phase. The mistake he describes is "learning and never starting," which traps people in the clarity phase until it "turns into another lost phase" and they never enter intensity. Read together, the two descriptions say that curiosity leads to clarity, and that clarity becomes obsession only through action. Curiosity alone cycles back to being lost.

#### From cope to curiosity

Curiosity is also an attitude toward other people's success, and here Koe gives it a sharper edge. In a 2022 video on a "new career path," he says that in adulthood "things need to switch from cope to curiosity": from "oh no, there's no other way" to "maybe I could do that." The method is questioning. His example: instead of saying "that guy got lucky," stop and ask. What skill did he learn to do that? What is he actually doing? What goes on behind the scenes? What is he posting about? How did he learn it? "You just ask questions horizontally and vertically until you gain the bigger picture," and then you can form any opinion you want. Without this, it is "just assumption," "a blurred lens," a story you feed yourself "that you want to hear, based on your prior conditioning" (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21).

**Cope to curiosity** and **questions horizontally and vertically** are Koe's terms. He does not define "cope" beyond the example and the contrast with "maybe I could do that."

**Complementary context:** In internet slang, "cope" (from "coping mechanism") names a comforting story someone tells themselves to avoid facing an uncomfortable fact.

Read in that sense, "that guy got lucky" is cope because it makes the other person's result unreproducible and therefore irrelevant to you. Questioning "horizontally" widens the picture (what else is he doing, what surrounds the result); questioning "vertically" deepens it (how did he learn, what is underneath). The "blurred lens" connects to Chapter 1: an assumption is the conditioned mind defending itself, and curiosity is the first crack in that defense. The same move reappears in Chapter 17 as questioning.

In the same video Koe makes curiosity the first step of a sequence. After questioning (for example, "what if I did learn that skill; that would lead to x, y, z"), "the next step after curiosity is education": buy books, look on YouTube, search Google. "The answers are out there, especially for beginner-level stuff." He distinguishes two kinds of knowledge: principles and beginner-level knowledge are available online; specific knowledge comes later, by actually doing. Later in the video he restates the sequence: "we already have the first two steps, right: it's curiosity and then it's self-education," followed by displaying your value online as a "perspective vessel" (same source). The sequence curiosity → self-education → publishing your perspective is an early outline of this chapter's argument, from 14.2 to 14.5. The distinction between principles that can be found online and specific knowledge that comes from doing anticipates the build-to-learn method of section 14.4.

A 2024 passage extends questioning across time. Discussing a deep question ("what is a state of consciousness?"), which he treats as a metaphysical problem, Koe says to be open-minded, ask questions and accept that they may take time to answer. "A lesson there: ask more questions. You won't receive the answer immediately, but you can seed your mind to receive an answer that you normally wouldn't have. It can take a day, a week or a month, and for the questions that matter, a decade" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). **Seed your mind** is his term for the effect of an open question: it primes attention so that relevant answers register when they appear. The mechanism is the same one the goal provides in Chapter 6, applied to a question instead of a target. A question is a small goal for the mind.

#### Intention, genuine curiosity and the execution gap

The most detailed account of how curiosity turns reading into retained knowledge comes from Koe's own reading of *The Power of Now*, whose circumstances Chapter 2 told (the arrest, the letter, the hour each morning on the patio with gas-station coffee). In a 2022 video titled "How I Remember Everything I Learn," he reflects on why he retained so much from that book and identifies three steps (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

The first is **intention**: something to aim toward, held consciously or not, which makes the teaching fit your situation. He had a practical problem and something concrete to try, so the teaching "stood out."

The second is **genuine curiosity**: wanting to understand rather than closing your mind and picking apart small disagreements. He had first heard of the book from Matt Ogus, one of the "OG" fitness YouTubers of about a decade earlier, who had got him into the gym; he loved Ogus's personality, aspired to be like him and trusted him. Ogus had recommended the book many times, but Koe had not yet explored spirituality. When he finally picked it up, he was genuinely curious to figure out "what this whole spirituality thing" was. Combined with intention, curiosity made him read much more closely: "I wasn't looking to close my mind off and pick apart little things that I don't agree with in the book. I wanted to understand." His mind still reacted. When the book talked about God, he thought, "God doesn't exist." But he stepped back and asked whether there was something he was missing about "the notion of God" that he would never discover if he wrote it off entirely.

The third is **reducing the execution gap**. Here he quotes his own tweet, which "popped off randomly": "the greatest skill one can develop is decreasing the time between idea and execution." Some people disagreed, saying other skills are the greatest; he concedes the point, but says "in my situation in life that was the greatest skill." Applied to reading: when he came across something he could practice, he did not keep reading; he put the book down and practiced. His example: reading outside with his coffee, he reached advice about meditation, stopped, and meditated right there. He explains why this helped. Because the ideas were aligned and at the top of his mind, and he had not forgotten any of the nuances, he could notice whether the practices were working; he did not "glance over" the outcome. It was easier to solidify the knowledge because he had "backed the theory with direct experience," which he flags as an important point.

**Execution gap** is his term for the time between an idea and its execution. **Direct experience**, a common phrase in his special sense, is experience of one's own that backs a theory and, in his 2022 and 2024 usage, constitutes "true learning." The three steps fit together as a sequence that runs from before reading to after it: intention selects what will matter, genuine curiosity keeps the mind open while reading, and execution converts what was read into something experienced. The second step is subtle. Genuine curiosity is not credulity: Koe's mind reacted against the book, and he did not suppress the reaction; he examined it. What he refused was the defensive reading that looks for reasons to dismiss. The same distinction returns in Chapter 17 as the difference between closed and open thinking.

Two qualifications from the corpus belong here. The first is Koe's own: the claim about "the greatest skill" is situational, true "in my situation in life," and he does not defend it as universal. The second concerns where the method sits in his thinking. He presents the three steps as "a great way to retain a lot of what you learn, but we can do better than that," and he places this actionable advice at the start of the video deliberately (noting that he usually drags out his intros) because the deeper material needs to be built up to. The "better" method is building and teaching, sections 14.4 and 14.5. The three steps are a starting point, not the theory.

The evolution record adds a later shift. In 2022 the aim was retention: how to remember what you read. In 2025 Koe says "it's not about what information you have... it's about changing your mind," and that "the purpose for reading is behavior change through identity change"; in 2026, "if you need to remember it, it's not important." Retention stops being the goal; integration into identity becomes the goal. The record judges the positions compatible if the 2022 retention is read as a consequence of practice rather than as an end in itself, which is how the third step already treats it: what he retained, he retained because he had done it. Chapter 16 develops the later view of reading.

### 14.3 Tutorial Hell: Information as Fake Progress

The first two sections described what to learn and what drives the learning. This section describes the characteristic way it goes wrong. Koe's diagnosis is that the most common failure of people who want to learn is not laziness but a form of activity: consuming information about the thing instead of doing the thing. It feels like progress, produces the pleasure of progress, and leaves nothing behind. He calls the condition **tutorial hell**, and he speaks of it from the inside, because he spent years in it. The section moves from the general principle (minimalism as releasing what only feels like progress), through the mechanism (the dopamine of information), to the rule that follows ("don't let knowledge outpace execution"), the author's own failures, and finally the way out, which he states in a tweet he has repeated for four years: start, then learn.

#### Minimalism: releasing what only feels like progress

The frame for the diagnosis comes from Koe's 2024 video on minimalist productivity. "Minimalism is not about having less, it's about making room for more of what matters." It is about leverage: releasing the things you think bring progress but actually slow you down, and focusing on the few tasks that bring outsized results while systemizing, outsourcing or delegating the rest. His first example of something that slows you down while feeling like progress is "watching tutorials without actively building a project." He does not just watch tutorials, he says; he finds one he wants to implement directly and learns the skill by building along with it (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). **Minimalism** is used here in his own sense: not reducing possessions, but subtracting low-leverage activity so that high-leverage activity has room.

The rest of that video's argument explains why the subtraction matters for someone learning on their own time. A minimalist workday requires "a line of work that allows you to control the work day"; if you have an eight-hour workday for someone else, you cannot do it. He restates this as the first step of finding high-leverage tasks: choose "the right vessel," the line of work that lets you control the variables, which in the modern day means a business, specifically an internet business (same source). In 2025 he lists three conditions for working less: define and abide by your ideal lifestyle; make sure "your unique strengths align with a leverage-based game"; and be aware of the tools and technology available to do more in less time as one person (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). **Right vessel** and **leverage-based game** are his terms; Chapter 12 developed the four-hour workday they serve.

Two earlier and later passages specify what "leverage" means for someone still learning. In 2023 he describes the "second pillar" of his workday: focus on the highest-leverage activities, because "the time to money ratio has to decrease as you grow or get better at business." Freelance, consulting and agency work are a good starting point for most beginners and often must be done to start making money; alternatively you can delay making money and build an audience first. But ultimately "you need a product that is able to sell and maintain itself while you sleep," if you want to do this without hiring many employees, which he does not want. He tells on himself: client work and manual outreach consumed his time, and he had been closed to being a creator, building a personal brand or building leverage along the way, instead "shooting blanks of thousands upon thousands of cold emails and having no leverage or readership built to show for it" (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). And in 2024 he defines the **one leverage task**: ask what is the one thing you can do that "both increases your effectiveness with consistent failure and brings you closer to your goal," and do only that, first thing every morning. If you have only one hour to build your own thing, do only that and be at peace with it; you neither need to nor can work twelve hours if you only have one. Successful entrepreneurs do not start by working twelve hours a day on their projects; most have a job first and work one hour a day and perhaps four to eight hours on weekends. "Working a lot of hours is a choice, and it's often a stupid choice depending on the context of your life" (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

The definition of the one leverage task contains the whole argument of this section in miniature. The task must increase effectiveness "with consistent failure": it must be something you actually attempt, where you can fail and learn. Watching a tutorial cannot fail. That is precisely what makes it feel safe and what makes it low-leverage.

#### "The most dangerous drug": dopamine from information gathering

The mechanism that keeps people in tutorial hell was named most sharply not by Koe but by a guest. In a 2025 conversation, Koe observed that the "brutal awareness" produced by ideas like those in Sahil Bloom's book "dwindles" after people finish listening. Bloom named the cause: "probably the most dangerous drug that we have in our lives, which is the drug of dopamine from information gathering, because it convinces you that the information gathering is the good thing. You get the dopamine hit from reading *Atomic Habits* and you say, oh, I'm good at habits now because I read *Atomic Habits*, but you haven't done anything." His countermeasure, applied throughout his own book, is to "hit you with the information, but then also on the back end immediately with the action" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). **Dopamine from information gathering** is Bloom's term; the attribution matters, because Koe then builds on it.

**Complementary context:** Sahil Bloom is a writer and former investor, author of *The 5 Types of Wealth*; *Atomic Habits* is James Clear's book on habit formation.

Koe's own version, from 2026, sharpens the comparison. The first reason people fail is that "they're exchanging one form of cheap dopamine for another, more insidious form." "Endless tactic collection by endlessly consuming YouTube videos around business and self-improvement is worse than just doom scrolling brain rot videos, because you're getting a sense of fake progress." With doom-scrolling "you just feel bad about yourself. But with self-improvement and business, and even this video, you feel bad about yourself, but you also get this false certainty that you're making progress just by watching the video" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19). **Tactic collection**, **fake progress** and **false certainty** are his terms. The phrase "even this video" is characteristic: Koe repeatedly includes his own content in the warning.

Why is the self-improvement version "worse"? The argument is not that it does more harm per hour, but that it is harder to stop. Doom-scrolling is recognized as wasted time and produces guilt, which is at least a signal. Tactic collection produces guilt and its antidote at the same time: the feeling of having done something useful. In the terms of Chapter 11, it is cheap dopamine (novelty received without struggle) disguised as earned dopamine (progress toward a goal). It hijacks the reward that should come from progress.

In 2025 he extends the diagnosis to the format of what people consume. "The obsession over actionable steps and bullet point summaries is just another manifestation of cheap dopamine and distraction. You think that you're focused because you want everything condensed down into a set of actionable steps, including my long videos. That's not going to get you anywhere. You have swapped brain rot scrolling on TikTok for brain rot 'I need actionable tips' on YouTube." His prescription is the opposite of condensing: "Read more books. Read longer books. Read books that have nothing to do with what you're trying to achieve. Read the books that people wouldn't even think about summarizing with AI. That's where the gold is" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). **Brain rot** is a common phrase that he uses for degrading consumption, extended here to the consumption of "actionable tips." The corpus records a tension he acknowledges himself: the same video then teaches how to read books with AI, though not to summarize them (Chapter 16).

The prescription seems to contradict this section's insistence on application, and it is worth seeing why it does not. The complaint is not that long, unrelated books are useless; it is that the demand for actionable summaries is a way of avoiding the slow work of understanding while feeling efficient. Long books that "have nothing to do with what you're trying to achieve" feed the creative firepower of section 14.1 and the worldview of Chapter 16; they are not consumed as substitutes for action. What Koe objects to is consumption that pretends to be action.

He applies the same warning to his own audience in 2024. The process he had described (realize what you don't want, launch in the opposite direction, prioritize long-term success) is part of the origin story of most successful people. If you don't know what to do, rewatch the video and truly internalize it; do not watch for "a quick dopamine hit" of learning something that "may change my life" and then never apply it. "Flip the switch inside you" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10).

#### Telling, asking and believing as substitutes for doing

Three related habits work the same way: each produces the feeling of progress without the progress.

The first is telling people about your goals. In 2023 Koe says most people stop at goal-setting: they "mentally masturbate over their goals and they tell people about it," which he says is "scientifically shown to increase dopamine levels in the brain as if you achieve that goal," and they never make meaningful progress (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30). In 2024 he develops it. People want "the dopamine hit that comes from telling people that they're doing things" instead of "the dopamine that comes from actually achieving the goal." "This is an actual scientific thing; I forget the exact study, but there's a study that shows that dopamine is released in the same manner for telling people you're pursuing a goal as it is for actually achieving the goal." People get addicted to telling others for approval, "rather than just staying quiet, building, solving the problem and then showing them." The trap to avoid: "ignore the desire to tell every single person what you're doing." The only good outcome of telling is that someone might help you, "on a case-by-case situation." Instead, "stay quiet, start building, and then tell people what you're building; not 'I'm building this, think highly of me,' but 'I'm building this, here's how you can benefit, will you come help me?'" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30).

The scientific claim should be handled with the caution Koe himself signals: he does not identify the study, and the corpus records it as an unidentified source. What he builds on it is a heuristic, not a finding: telling for approval can substitute for doing. The heuristic stands or falls on its own plausibility and on the reader's experience, not on the unnamed study.

There is an apparent tension here with section 14.5, where Koe urges people to build and teach in public. The evolution record classifies it as a refinement, and the reason Koe gives resolves it: the distinction is between telling people what you intend to do in order to be thought well of, and showing people what you are building in a way that benefits them. The first replaces work; the second is a product of work. "Stay quiet, start building, and then tell people what you're building" is a sequence, not a ban.

The second habit is asking for advice. "Most people ask for advice as a procrastination method to hide from the problems that they need to solve themselves." From his experience on social media, countless people comment under a video or post asking for advice; he answers, "just for them to go on and completely ignore it, or ask another one, or just say that I'm flat out wrong." "What was the purpose of asking the question in the first place if you're already set on keeping your mind closed?" His conclusion: "It seems like people don't want solutions; they want to feel as if they know what the solution is" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). The last sentence is the general form of everything in this section: the wish to feel that one knows, rather than to do what the knowledge would require.

The third habit is subtler: believing that the method you followed is what worked. In a 2025 video on original thinking, Koe describes the trap of blindly accepting what someone says with conviction ("the ancestral diet is the best because...") while they keep writing content that shapes your worldview. "You're just getting quick confirmational dopamine hits from those things, and then you have an ordered mind because of it; you're more clear in your actions, so you get more results. It's not because that diet or training plan is the best there is; it's because you're actually doing the thing and following the principles that gets results." You are able to be disciplined now "because you've adopted the part of your identity that would eat the healthier foods and do the certain exercises." The same happens in business. "Once you attribute those results to the special little diet, then eventually you start to push that thing as the new religion. You lose all opportunity for new discovery and original thought." So "your job is to suspend judgment and attachment, question from all angles and research all perspectives, prove yourself wrong and understand every moving part of the argument. From there you can come to your own opinion; you are no longer bound by the mind you were programmed with" (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19).

**Confirmational dopamine** is Koe's term for the reward of having one's chosen belief confirmed. The argument is a causal correction. The results came from three things the book has already named: order in consciousness (Chapter 5), the clarity that order gives to action, and the identity that makes discipline unnecessary (Chapter 3). The specific diet is the occasion, not the cause. Mistaking the occasion for the cause turns a tool into a "new religion," and the conversion closes the mind. The passage belongs in this section because it is another case of information substituting for understanding: the follower has the content of a method without the understanding of why doing anything consistently works. Chapter 17 takes up the prescription (suspend judgment, prove yourself wrong).

#### Don't let knowledge outpace execution

From the diagnosis follows a rule, which Koe states in nearly identical words in 2022 and again in a 2024 compilation. "The worst thing you can do is let knowledge outpace execution." You do not learn to write by reading about writing; reading helps as a guide, but you must practice. And practice has an order. You cannot study a senior-level English course in first grade; you need first-grade English and practice, then second, then third. Skip steps and you will not write effectively, or it will take ten times longer, because you will try to write like a college senior and have to go back to fourth grade (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31; `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). In a 2022 list of lessons from his business he makes it lesson nine, "start then learn": information is abundant; "the biggest mistake that people make is letting knowledge outpace execution"; "you can learn anything, but are you learning anything?" His favorite remedy, he says, is building projects (**Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`, 2022-11-06).

**Knowledge outpacing execution** is his phrase for the gap that opens when what you know exceeds what you can do. The grade-by-grade analogy adds something to the principle: execution has prerequisites, and knowledge acquired ahead of them cannot be used, so it is either forgotten or, worse, makes you attempt things you are not ready for. The "ten times longer" figure is an illustration of that second effect, not a measurement.

Both versions of the warning are followed immediately, in their source videos, by a qualification that the evolution record notes: not everything can be practiced tangibly, and some value requires contemplation rather than practice. The rule is about skills that are exercised in action; it does not claim that reflection is worthless. Chapter 13's defense of rest and Chapter 17's treatment of thinking are the other side of it.

Koe condenses the rule into a diagnostic that he calls one of his top tweets: "If you're lost, the answer is education; if you're educated, the answer is action; if you're acting, the answer is consistency" (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20). Read as a diagnostic, it asks the learner to identify their stage and apply the answer for that stage only. Its relevance here is the middle clause: the person who is already educated and keeps educating themselves is applying the answer to the wrong stage.

In the same video he draws a consequence for choosing teachers. "It only makes sense to match knowledge with execution." You do not want to learn from someone ten stages ahead of you, because "it won't make sense; you don't understand what's there." Learn from someone one stage ahead, match their execution so that you become aware of the "little bubble of awareness" they have at stage two; then move to stage three, "slowly expanding your awareness to encapsulate everything you're experiencing and learning" (same source). **Bubble of awareness** is his term for what is visible from a given stage. The heuristic follows from the grade analogy: a teacher ten stages ahead is teaching senior English to a first-grader. It also explains why so much expert advice does not help beginners even when it is correct.

#### The author's tutorial hell: the architect of the business

Koe's authority on tutorial hell is that he lived in it, and his account of his early failures is one of the most often repeated stories in the corpus. The story is consistent in its shape and has been told with different explanations over five years, which the evolution record traces.

The earliest version is from a 2021 conversation with Justin Welsh. Before going all in, Koe tried "five different things" as a freelancer: "Okay, yeah, I'm going to send 10, 20 cold emails. And then get discouraged, call it a day, and try something else because it's not working." He "didn't realize that the problem there was getting my offer in front of people." "I could have, like, get people the greatest results, but if I can't actually get them the results [that is, reach them], then it doesn't matter." Once he learned marketing through the book *Cashvertising*, "everything started to click": "I've acquired enough skill to do something for a specific person. And now it's like, okay, how do I make this appealing for that person? How do I actually get their attention in what seems like a saturated market?", which, he adds, is "not really the case." He tells listeners to "pay attention to the whole marketing side of things and marketing yourself" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20).

**Complementary context:** Justin Welsh is a solo creator known for his one-person business built on LinkedIn and X; *Cashvertising* is a book on advertising copy by Drew Eric Whitman (the transcript does not name the author).

In 2022 the explanation becomes more specific, in a passage he repeated literally in a 2024 compilation. He learned to code mainly to freelance, which turned out to be "more so marketing than it was code": he built websites with website builders. Everyone told him to learn a marketable skill (e-commerce, SEO, and so on), and he did. But he made no money, because he was obsessed with building the business, the website, making it look good with his design skills; when it came to acquiring customers, "that is when everything failed." "You don't have a business if you don't have customers." Things picked up once he learned writing, marketing and sales "and how they all intertwine": he understood that he needed to get his message in front of people, present an argument for his services, promote himself and display his value online to access opportunities (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28; `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

In 2023 he names what he lacked as a set of skills that sit beneath every business model. He failed at "seven-ish" business models because he did not understand the skills that lead to success with any of them: "business has a specific skill set attached to it that applies no matter what technicality you go into," whether email marketing freelancing, graphic design freelancing or anything else. The question is "what is the glue that makes those business models work." He cannot give fifty to a hundred hours of information in a thirty-minute video, but once you truly understand, practice and get results in those skills, "you can really make money whenever you want" (**Source:** `I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md`, 2023-08-16). **Glue** is a common word in his special sense: the business skills (writing, marketing, sales) that make any model work.

Two 2023 and 2024 passages fill in what he was doing instead. "When I first started out in business I had no idea what I was doing." He thought it was obvious: "if I have a skill, people need that skill, so they'll hire me." He was ignorant of the fact that anyone could learn that skill and that the online freelancing arena "was starting to become flooded with single skill commodities like web design"; that was the first problem he had to solve (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04). **Single skill commodities** is his term for freelancers offering one interchangeable skill. And in 2024: he began with freelancing because it seemed the most beginner-friendly option ("learn a skill, sell a skill"), and tried video editing, graphic design, SEO, content marketing, Facebook ads and web design. "My pain point was always choosing a niche." He downloaded free guides and "would endlessly search on Google for the top 100 niches to target": dentists, gyms, construction companies, pest control and more (**Source:** `Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md`, 2024-08-25). Chapter 19 draws the lesson about niches; here the endless search is another form of information gathering that replaces the act of getting a customer.

The 2024 version gives the trap its name. "There is one big trap in entrepreneurship, and I've fallen into it seven times; that's why I failed at seven business models when I was starting out." He was "so immersed in the skill" he was learning that it was all he wanted to do; he was "so focused on being the architect of the business," building the website, the logo, "all of the [stuff] you don't need when starting out," but when it came time to get customers he did nothing, so he did not get paid: "I didn't put product in front of people." The lesson: "you just need a product and you need to get it in front of someone and ask for payment" (**Source:** `Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md`, 2024-03-31). **The architect of the business** is his name for the trap: designing the structure of a business (site, logo, legal entity) as a substitute for its function (customers paying).

The latest versions tie the failures explicitly to tutorial hell. In January 2026: "I would just learn and learn and learn and watch tutorials, get stuck in tutorial hell." Seeing people warn against shiny object syndrome, he thought, "I'm right in the middle of that, I need to focus on one thing." "I got my dopamine from feeling smart. But when I looked back at my life, I didn't really have anything to show for it." He could not articulate what he remembered, his life did not change much, and he felt he was falling behind. In college he tried many things because he always dreamed of doing something creative, knowing that if college ended without a creative income he would have to get a job. He experimented but did not stick to one thing, and "5 years into college, one more year than I was supposed to go, I had to get a job because I just needed to survive" (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20). And in August 2026: "I got stuck in tutorial hell. I took all the courses. I tried and failed at seven different business models: Facebook ads, SEO, drop shipping, web design, digital art and a few more." He would not call them businesses but hobbies: "I would learn the high value skill and that felt good. I would start the LLC and that felt good. I would build the website and try to write the copy, and that felt good. But when it came to actually getting a customer, getting someone to pay me, I was nowhere to be found." "The missing piece was a strategy, but not just a strategy, the ability to think strategically, because how else are you going to create a strategy?" He admits that he still collects tactics "quite often right now" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

The repeated phrase "and that felt good" is the link to the dopamine argument above. Each step of the architect's work produced the reward of progress; the one step that would have tested whether there was progress (asking someone to pay) produced no reward in advance and was avoided. "I got my dopamine from feeling smart" is the same diagnosis in the first person.

The explanations are not exclusive; each names a deeper layer of the one before. Marketing is a skill; the glue is the set of skills; putting product in front of people is the act those skills serve; strategic thinking (Chapter 17) is the capacity that decides which acts matter. The evolution record also notes two tensions around the same story. First, in a 2023 video Koe recommended learning anything that gives you the dose of dopamine, including "websites, logos," while in 2024 he calls those things you do not need when starting; he gives no reason for the change of priority. Second, he reads the same failures in opposite ways in different videos: as a "big trap," and elsewhere as experiments that stacked irreplaceable skills; he defends shiny object syndrome in some years and blames it in others. The two readings can coexist if one distinguishes the businesses, which failed for lack of customers, from the skills, which accumulated. Chapter 15 develops the positive reading; this chapter needs the negative one, because it is the clearest case in the corpus of knowledge outpacing execution. The list of the seven failed businesses also changes from telling to telling (fitness channels, photography and digital art, Facebook ads and SEO agencies, dropshipping, blue-light glasses, wallets, a printing business, an animated-video agency); the number seven is stable, the composition and duration are not.

#### The way out: start, then learn

Against all of this Koe sets a short formula that he first tweeted in 2022 and has repeated in videos ever since. In the 2022 version: "If you want to learn faster, don't start learning. Outline a project, start building it out, learn along the way. Too many people get trapped in tutorial hell, stacking up useless knowledge as brain fog. Start, encounter a problem, seek specific knowledge to overcome it" (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). **Brain fog**, in his usage, is the useless knowledge accumulated in tutorial hell: information without application, soon forgotten. **Start then learn** is the name he gives the practice, with the variants "start, build, learn" and "build then learn."

The 2022 video gives the reason behind the formula. Koe calls himself a big fan of "start then learn." When you start something, even without knowing what you are doing, you encounter problems; "these problems will induce struggle and prime your brain to learn." So you do not learn blindly, without context, or from someone who is not teaching exactly what you are trying to do. The prescription: start building, then ask questions or seek specific advice and tutorials when a problem comes up (same source). He uses the phrase **specific knowledge** here in a sense of his own: knowledge sought to solve a concrete problem in your project, which cuts through what can be learned by experience. The lexicon notes that this is a different sense from Naval Ravikant's "specific knowledge" (knowledge that cannot be trained and arises from genuine curiosity), which Koe also uses elsewhere (Chapter 20); the two share a term but not a meaning.

The same video contains the story from which the formula came, a pair of contrasting experiences. When Koe first tried to learn Photoshop, he watched YouTube videos all the time, did not practice, and "didn't even have the app downloaded, and that of course led to nowhere." When he finally downloaded it and tried to create a design to test his knowledge, he could not create anything and had to go back to learning anyway. Later, learning web design, he knew he had to start "fooling around with the software" and try to build something. When he did not know how to do something (his example is a specific navigation design), he looked that up specifically, worked through the tutorial, and afterwards could replicate it on every other website he built (same source). He returns to the Photoshop failure in 2023: he tried to learn all of Photoshop before using it; when he started, he knew nothing and had to supplement with specific "create with me" tutorials (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30).

The contrast isolates the variable. In both cases he used tutorials. What differed was whether a problem of his own preceded the tutorial. Without one, the tutorial was entertainment; with one, it was a solution, and solutions stick.

The same 2022 video adds a warning about the question people ask instead of starting. "Asking vague questions like 'should I start?' is just procrastination from starting." If you do not know what the result will be after you start, you will not learn it from someone else; it comes from directly experiencing the result of starting (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). It is the asking-for-advice habit above, applied to the most basic decision.

#### The shitty rod and just-in-time learning

The most memorable illustration of start-then-learn in the corpus comes from a guest, Dickie Bush, in a 2023 conversation, and the attributions should be kept straight: the parable and the framework are Bush's; one phrase in the middle is Koe's.

**Complementary context:** Dickie Bush is a writer and co-founder of Ship 30 for 30, a writing program; he and Koe recorded several long conversations.

Bush's parable: two men start learning to fish. The first gets every fishing book, a custom fiber rod and a brand-new boat, and spends six months preparing. The second grabs a stick, ties on some line and a hot dog, and throws it in the lake. Day one: nothing. Day two: "I have to learn from this; I was probably in a bad spot, let's go try this spot." A nibble, but the rod breaks, so he gets a better rod. "Every single day he iterates." Here Koe interjects: "it's procrastination disguised as productivity." By the time the first fisherman feels ready and gets his boat to the lake, every fish is gone: the second "has sucked it dry," runs a business teaching others to fish and runs a restaurant, "all because he just got started." Bush describes his own habit: "anytime I want to start something new, in my head I go grab a shitty rod and start fishing, rather than go try to have this perfect plan, because that plan never works out and you don't understand what you need to execute that plan." It kept him from "nope, I need to read these 10 books"; "you're never going to feel ready" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). **Shitty rod** is Bush's image for starting with the minimum and improving by iteration. **Procrastination disguised as productivity** is Koe's interjection (the corpus marks the attribution as probable), and it is the best short name for everything this section describes.

The parable carries an argument about information that is easy to miss. The first fisherman's six months of preparation were not useless because preparation is bad, but because he could not know what he needed until he was at the lake. The second fisherman's broken rod told him exactly what to improve. Every iteration produced information that no book could have supplied, because it was information about his spot, his lake, his rod.

Bush gives the principle behind the parable a name: **just-in-time** versus **just-in-case** learning. Creating a product, or even an internet presence, takes many skills, and "too many people are like, oh, I need to go read these 10 books before I start doing anything." But "you have to have a lens to apply what you're learning; if you do not have that lens, you are not going to learn anything." Just-in-case learning prepares broadly before acting; just-in-time learning learns what the current action demands. He applies it to a future project of his own, a gym and real-estate venture: he will not read real estate, tax and finance books now, "because I don't have the lens yet, but the second I do, that is where I'm going to place all my attention" (same source). The idea of the lens is the same one Chapter 6 attributes to the goal; Bush's contribution is the timing. A lens exists only once you are doing the thing, so learning should be scheduled for then.

Koe gives the same principle a name of his own in 2024, by reframing a familiar idea. "Failure stacking makes you irreplaceable." He reframes skill stacking as failure stacking because "acquiring a skill puts you in the mindset that you just need to study and learn all day," whereas "acquiring a failure assumes that you are actively working toward a goal and gaining actual experience." The process: identify a goal (quit your job, find a partner, get fit); start with what you know; "you can only know what to learn when you hit a roadblock, so hit one fast"; then pursue your curiosity and let experience guide you into new paths to experiment with; everything will eventually overlap. His example is fitness: diet models to try, training programs to test, lifestyle changes to implement (**Source:** `The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md`, 2024-05-19). **Failure stacking** is his coinage. The phrase "hit one fast" turns just-in-time learning into an instruction: since only roadblocks tell you what to learn, the fastest learner is the one who reaches roadblocks soonest.

#### The learning sequences, 2023–2026

Between 2023 and 2026 Koe turned start-then-learn into a series of step-by-step procedures. They are versions of one idea, and laying them side by side shows both the stable core and the direction in which it moved.

In April 2023, describing how to actualize a goal: (1) study the fundamentals to gain clarity; (2) start a real-world project "so you don't get trapped in tutorial hell"; (3) build up the project until you discover a problem; (4) buy courses, coaching or research internet content to find the solution; (5) apply everything you learn until the project is completed. His example is health treated as a project (**Source:** `Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md`, 2023-04-30).

In January 2023 he had already given a version under the name **rapid building**, the combination of "rapid learning" and "rapid execution," the two skills that help implement everything (he credits his earlier video on learning fast). The steps: (1) choose a project to emulate that incorporates the skills you are trying to learn; (2) start building your own version, creating an outline and building "until you hit a wall"; (3) seek specific knowledge when you hit a wall, learning the skills needed for that specific real-world situation, which "cuts through fluff that can be learned through experience"; (4) as a bonus, teach along the way, because teaching identifies your knowledge gaps. This is for your "burst of building," when you feel the urge to go all in and have the clarity and education to start (**Source:** `The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md`, 2023-01-08).

In June 2023, under the name **start, build, learn**: you start the project; you build, "you begin stacking bricks with what you know," because you have already gained enough clarity to act ("or else you wouldn't have the clarity"; you have already consumed some information). When you hit a specific problem, you seek information (content, YouTube videos, mentors, coaches, courses) "that can help you solve that specific problem, to burst through that gap, reach the new level and then perceive your previous level from a state of self-reflection," and you keep correcting "until you see some form of objective success in something like business" (**Source:** `If You’re Lost - Here’s How You Find Clarity For The Future.md`, 2023-06-04). In the same video he names the failure this avoids: "People make the crucial mistake of learning and never starting, and then they get stuck in the clarity phase, and then it just turns into another lost phase; you don't enter the intensity phase." "You have to actually move; you have to use your mind to create in the physical world; you have to apply what you learn as you're building" (same source). The **lost, clarity and intensity phases** are his terms, and they connect this sequence to the curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle of section 14.2: learning that never starts cycles back to lost.

In July 2023: with your vision in mind, start building and learning; work on your project daily regardless of whether you know what you are doing. You do not know, because you have not registered the problem or hit it yet ("you're never going to find that if you don't start"). You can be a book expert, but if you don't do it, you will hit the same problem as if you knew nothing and have to research it anyway. "It's better to start then learn." The best way to learn: (1) encounter a real-world problem; (2) research specific information to solve it; (3) keep a steady flow of information from your consumption; (4) solve the problem; (5) enjoy the dopamine; (6) "let momentum carry you into a season of intensity" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). **Season of intensity** is his term for a period in which everything fits and hard work is enjoyed.

In August 2023 he restates the tweet as three steps (outline a project, start building, learn along the way) and adds the reason that tutorials work once a problem exists: with a problem in your head when watching a video, you see what the other person does, remember far more, and go back to fix your project. Hence the constant balance, "learning, building, learning, building": "you can't have output without input." He recommends a general education habit of ten to thirty minutes a day (a YouTube video, a podcast, part of a book, or a short summary such as the Shortform app) (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30). The phrase "you can't have output without input" should be read with Chapter 6's "learning is an output process, because output demands input": the two sentences are the same balance seen from opposite ends.

In 2024 the sequence adds a short initial study phase. Once you have a project that makes the goal tangible: (1) purchase a beginner-level course on the topic; (2) watch overview videos on YouTube teaching the fundamentals; (3) study them until you have clarity on what to do next; (4) do it; (5) when you encounter a problem, refer back to the relevant sections of the course or video; (6) research how to solve that problem directly; (7) watch tutorials of people building projects similar to yours and see how they overcome those problems. "Learning is problem solving, not hoarding as much knowledge as you can" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11; condensed again in `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). **Learning is problem solving** is his formula. The same February video repeats the tweet and explains the role of the outline: you outline the project so that you have "anchors" to map everyday experiences and what you consume onto (otherwise you would not notice them, nor live with meaning); as you build, once you hit a wall your mind is primed for learning "because learning comes from struggle, not memorization"; you research a specific tutorial (a Photoshop technique, for instance), go back and solve it. It takes longer to build the project, "but that's how you learn," and you get faster at building projects over time (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). In July 2024 he repeats the tweet once more, with the "6 years of results in 6 months" line (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

Two 2024 passages describe the same method from Koe's own practice. He did not study endless tutorials. He set a goal (working for himself), started a project and practiced the skills while self-educating. He studies the big picture and each skill's necessity for a specific goal such as financial freedom, gets a brief overview of how it helps and its principles, then learns the technical details by actually building, and only those he needs. This saves time compared with spending three to six months learning each skill's technical details. Most people skip building, "get trapped in tutorial hell and have nothing to show for their efforts after like 1, 2, 3 years" (**Source:** `The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md`, 2024-05-19). And in a video on deep work: because skill needs time to meet challenge, he always recommends "just starting; just start something; if you don't know how, you just start with what you know and then you learn on the go and get real-world experience; do not get trapped in tutorial hell." He calls his 2022 video "How I Remember Everything I Learn" one of his less popular but "most important" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The first passage adds a distinction the procedures leave implicit: the big picture and principles are learned first, briefly; technical details are learned only when building demands them. Chapter 15 develops the priority of principles over tactics.

In 2025 the sequence enters a template Koe calls a life plan, and two of its steps restate the method. He opens with an earlier tweet: "When I want to learn something new, I don't start learning first. I outline a project first, then I learn exactly what I need and build out the project while learning. I found that this solidifies teachings into my brain much faster," which he calls the seed of the whole method (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). Step two, outline a project: the life plan is "your aim, your frame for learning" and "optimizes pattern recognition to an extent so you can notice the right information." "The best way to learn is to build a real-world project and only search for information when you need it; how much you learn is directly correlated with how much progress you make on the project." Endless tutorials "fill your mind with noise and chaos; most of that information goes to waste; it leads to overwhelm, anxiety, and slows down how fast you learn," and when it is time to build the project, "the only reason you're learning in the first place," you feel you learned nothing and search anyway. "Skip the tutorial phase; outline the project first." Then, in the same breath, he corrects himself: you don't necessarily need to skip tutorials; "you just need to start the project and hit that struggling point, and then when you go into the tutorial phase you actually pick up the information" (same source). Step three, start with what you know: "learning comes from struggle, not memorization." If you know nothing, at least take the first step: download the software and play around, try to create anything, "get your mind in a state where it's hungry to learn, otherwise you probably won't digest the information you search for." The loop: "you start, then you don't know what to do, you try and fail, you search for the answer or ask AI, you try to implement the answer, you repeat until the project is complete, and if you can't find the answer you ask an expert" (same source).

The self-correction inside step two is the clearest statement of Koe's position on tutorials, and the **struggling point** (also "hitting a wall") is the term on which it turns: the moment in a project when you cannot progress and the mind is therefore primed to learn. Tutorials are not the enemy; tutorials before the struggling point are.

Later in 2025 he restates the rule in the language of games. "In a game you learn by doing, not by studying endless tutorials or watching gameplay." Watching before you start playing "is not learning; that's entertainment." You can watch streamers all day and think you understand, but when you sit down your fingers do not know what keys to press or how to move the mouse; what you watched does not align with the reality of doing. Once you play first, once the game is part of your identity and daily practice, tutorials and gameplay let you pick up strategies, tactics or hacks that you know how to implement, and "the more strategies you collect from other people and combine into your own, that's what makes your own gameplay unique." "Start playing the game. Don't worry if you aren't absolutely confident; you figure out what you want to do by error correcting, and you can't correct an error that doesn't exist" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08). He calls this creating a **tutorial phase**: the phase of learning from others that comes after you have started playing. Once you are playing, supplement your mind with two things: the fundamentals, "because most success is not getting distracted from the fundamentals," and **specific solutions**, "an intentional search for an answer once you can't make progress by your own knowledge or intuition." "The main priority tasks for your day should be learning and building for 1 to 2 hours at a time. These do not happen in isolation" (same source). The sentence "you can't correct an error that doesn't exist" is the 2025 form of the Photoshop lesson and anticipates the error-correction model of Chapter 15.

In September 2025 he makes project-based learning the fourth of twelve rules and states the strongest form of the critique. You know what you don't want and have an idea of what you want; now acquire the skills and knowledge that bridge the gap. You move into the unknown "by having a way to order any potential chaos": personal projects. He repeats the principle (build a real project, search only when needed; learning correlates with progress on the project). Endless tutorials fill the mind with excess noise; most information is wasted because it is not applied, like reading a book and then stuttering when you try to explain it to a friend. Books and YouTube are not bad, "but that's not learning. That's entertainment," for another domain (mental clarity, calming down, stress relief); it can be learning, but very ineffective, and reading or watching to learn means "you're filling your mind with endless options. You're just creating more chaos" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). In October 2025, as step six of a dopamine detox, he repeats the principle almost word for word and ends with the 2025 slogan: "If you want to learn faster, skip the tutorial phase, outline a project first" (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26).

Two 2026 passages bring the sequence to its latest form. The tweet is rewritten: "Learning comes from struggle, not memorization. Start the project. No, don't watch 20 tutorials beforehand. Let it expose the gaps in your knowledge. Try to figure it out. Search for the answer when your mind is most likely to remember it. Publish the project, then repeat for life" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). The new elements are the timing of the search ("when your mind is most likely to remember it") and the last step, publishing, which links the sequence to section 14.5. And in a January 2026 video on building a one-person business with AI tools, he gives the method for learning to build software: (1) start tinkering and experimenting; (2) then watch YouTube tutorials; (3) have a project you are building toward (in his example, an evolved information product), and adapt tutorials that will not be about exactly that; (4) when stuck, ask AI how to do specific things, which he does himself for Photoshop and Framer because it pulls from help centers and forums. He warns that you will still have to learn, because AI cannot solve every problem from one sentence: "how can you get so much context into one sentence?" ("create this YouTube video": what kind, structure, pacing, tone, length, ideas? Will it assume, and will you put the result under your name? "That's not how this stuff works"). His example prompts: "give me a comprehensive guide on how to vibe code correctly," then a comprehensive guide to building a specific AI chat app with specific tabs on a specific topic and knowledge base, asking the AI to ask clarifying questions and telling it "I'm one singular person with this budget," and conversing from there (**Source:** `How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md`, 2026-01-03). He names Replit, Cursor and Claude Code as tools and says he has not tried the code tab inside Claude.

The evolution record classifies the movement as a refinement: less study before starting (from "study the fundamentals" in 2023, to a beginner course in 2024, to "skip the tutorial phase" in 2025, to "don't watch 20 tutorials" in 2026), publication added at the end, and the tutorial moved to the point of being stuck, where in 2025 and 2026 AI increasingly takes its place. The reason Koe gives is constant: with a problem in your head, you retain what you see. Both ends can apply in different contexts: a short beginner course serves the fundamentals; what he rejects in every version is accumulating tutorials in place of building. The evolution record also classifies as an unresolved contradiction the oscillation, already noted in section 14.1, between recommending books ("devour one to three") and calling reading-to-learn "entertainment"; the September 2025 rule is the strongest form of the second position.

A 2024 book quote states the principle under all the sequences: "The source of learning is struggle, not memorization. Learning is the process of turning knowledge into understanding, and the lowest points of your life are those where a stackable lesson presents itself, but only if you apply it in reality." Projects frame what you learn: you pursue a goal, hit a roadblock and frame your mind from the accompanying problem; only then can you learn something useful that can be instantly applied (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). **Learning comes from struggle, not memorization** is the principle as it appears in *The Art of Focus*; section 14.4 develops what it means. The distinction between knowledge and understanding that it introduces returns in Chapter 17.

#### Starting when you don't feel like it

All the sequences begin with "start," and Koe addresses the obvious objection: what if you do not feel like starting? In the 2025 life-plan video he calls this step 3.5. "The Zeigarnik effect is a psychological phenomenon where people remember unfinished tasks more than completed tasks," so once a project has unfinished tasks, motivation to resume comes more easily. But how do you start from scratch? He cites a trick from a YouTuber who makes videos about learning, transcribed as "Justin Sun" and probably Justin Sung, called the "Zeigarnik effect squared": invoke the effect "by starting easy tasks before getting started on your project, because if you start those tasks then they'll be unfinished and you'll want to continue working on them, and they lead naturally into the task that you need to work on." His examples are setting up or cleaning the desk, making coffee and sitting down, or any ritual or routine. When he does not want to write his newsletter, he goes into the outline, promises himself to just look at it, read it, type some ideas, "and then eventually I hit something" and keeps writing. For the gym: "I'm going to go walk for 5 minutes, and if I don't feel like doing anything here I'm going to leave, and most of the time you won't leave." He adds a condition: it works "if you have clarity on what you're doing," that is, if a hierarchy of goals tells you what the next task is. If you do not know what to do, study: bring AI to the gym and ask what exercise to do next (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

**Complementary context:** The Zeigarnik effect is named after the psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik, whose experiments in the 1920s suggested that interrupted tasks are recalled better than completed ones; later replications have been mixed. Justin Sung is a learning coach who publishes videos on study methods.

**Zeigarnik effect squared** is a third-party term that Koe adapts. The trick does not make starting unnecessary; it makes the first step so small that starting is easy, and relies on the pull of an open loop to carry the person into the real task. It is the behavioral counterpart of the 2025 instruction to "at least take the first step: download the software and play around."
