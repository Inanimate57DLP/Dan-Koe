## Chapter 14 — Learning How to Learn: Build to Learn

Part VI begins where the self-government of Part V leaves off. Chapters 11 to 13 described how Dan Koe protects attention, organizes a few hours of focused work and restores order through rest. None of that says what the protected hours should be filled with when the person does not yet know how to do the thing they want to do. That is the question of this chapter: how a person acquires the knowledge and skill that a self-chosen goal demands.

The chapter depends on four earlier pieces of the book. Chapter 2 supplied the critique and the first definitions: Devon Eriksen's distinction between education and job training, the author's formula "education is not programming," and his 2026 definition of **self-education** as "the process of learning what's necessary to achieve self-generated goals," with "no hoarding general knowledge to feel like you're learning." Chapter 6 supplied the mechanism: a goal acts as a lens, and in its 2026 cybernetic version, "a goal creates the error signal, the error signal creates the filter, the filter creates relevance, and relevance creates retention"; from this Koe concluded that "learning is an output process." Chapter 9 supplied the basic vehicle of practice: the project, as anchor, outline and lens, which in 2024 he named the Clarity Catalyst. Chapter 11 supplied a warning that becomes central here: the difference between cheap dopamine, received without effort, and dopamine earned through struggle.

With those pieces in place, Koe's theory of learning can be stated in five steps, which are the five sections of the chapter. Section 14.1 presents learning how to learn as "the meta skill," the case for daily self-education, Devon Eriksen's seven "liberating arts" and the three "vessels" Koe proposes for practicing them, and the author's own education as the story from which the theory was drawn. Section 14.2 treats curiosity as the guide that tells a person what to learn, together with the attitude Koe calls moving "from cope to curiosity" and the three conditions he identified for retaining what he reads. Section 14.3 diagnoses the main failure: information consumed without application, which feels like progress and is not ("tutorial hell"), including the author's account of how he fell into it for years. Section 14.4 presents the remedy, the central idea of the chapter: "don't learn to build, build to learn," with the reasons Koe gives for why building works, the sequences he has proposed over five years, the guitar method and the Build Teach Earn method. Section 14.5 adds the last link: teaching is the strongest form of learning, and teaching in public turns learning into feedback, an audience and, eventually, a business.

One feature of the corpus should be kept in mind throughout. Between 2022 and 2026 Koe described the same learning method under many names ("become a builder," "start then learn," "rapid building," "start, build, learn," "build then learn," the Clarity Catalyst, "project-based learning," "start with the purpose") and with a varying number of steps. The core does not change: have a project, start with what you know, learn what the project's problems demand at the moment they appear, and teach what you learn. What changes is emphasis (how much to study before starting), vocabulary and the theoretical frame used to justify it (from novelty and dopamine in 2022 to cybernetics in 2026). The chapter presents the versions where they differ, rather than collapsing them into one.

### 14.1 The Meta Skill and Self-Education

#### "The skill of all skills"

Koe's most compact statement of why learning matters comes from a 2025 video: "The mark of a free person is that they learn how to learn; that is the meta skill, that is the skill of all skills." His reasoning has three links. First, to pick up any skill that changes your life, you need to know how to learn it effectively; every other skill passes through this one. Second, the choice of what to learn is never neutral: "If you don't choose what to learn you will be told what to learn, and if your mind is the operating system for reality the options available for your future will be drastically limited and you won't even realize it." Third, the world is changing fast (he names technology and AI), which makes learning "the single most important skill, because it allows you to learn the skills that can't be taught in schools." Schools are a large and possibly outdated system; changing a curriculum takes far longer than it takes an individual to learn a topic and make a YouTube video from which you can learn it even faster. If you keep up and adapt, "adding the latest skills to your tool belt," you "can do some incredible things" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

Two of the phrases deserve comment. **Meta skill** is Koe's label for learning how to learn: the skill whose object is the acquisition of other skills. **Operating system for reality** is one of his recurring images for the mind, which he uses in several senses across 2024 and 2025 (most people run "Windows 98"; the mind is made of goals mostly programmed by others). Here it carries a specific consequence: since the mind determines which options you can see, a mind filled with what others chose for you limits your future invisibly. The sentence is the learning version of the book's central thesis from Chapter 1, that you always pursue goals, assigned or self-generated. Applied to knowledge, it reads: you always learn something; if you do not choose it, it is chosen for you.

The phrase "the mark of a free person" echoes the conversation with the novelist Devon Eriksen that Chapter 2 reported, and the definition of education is his, not Koe's. "Education is not about how to do a task, it's about how to learn a task." Education is "learning how to teach yourself": whenever you take on a hard task, you have to learn how to do it. The consequence Eriksen draws is the one that matters here: "if you don't learn how to learn, then you can only do things that somebody already understands how to do." He adds that there is no "systematic education in this society in the modern West about how to learn and educate yourself" (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`, 2024-12-08). The point connects learning to originality: someone who can only follow instructions can only reproduce what already exists, a theme that returns in Chapter 18.

Koe attaches quantitative promises to the meta skill, and they should be read as he qualifies them. In 2024: "When you learn how to learn you can accomplish 6 years of results in 6 months." He immediately adds, "this really varies," and explains the comparison he has in mind: most people stop learning after college, and it takes them six years to learn something very basic and do something new; following his process and continuously reinventing yourself "over and over again" compresses that (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). He repeats the "6 years in 6 months" line later in 2024 (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). In 2025 the promise takes another form: "If learning allows you to be useful and get what you want out of life, imagine what you could do if you actually spent time learning how to learn." If you knew how to learn fast, "you could achieve 10 goals in the same amount of time that it would take the average person to achieve one," and the average person probably would not achieve it until graduating or until they had "learned enough." In the same video he deliberately skips the science and psychology of learning to focus only on what to do, which he calls "pretty freaking simple. But sometimes the simplest things are the most complex things in the world" to get yourself to do (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10).

These figures are rhetorical comparisons, not measurements, and the corpus itself shows that they move. Koe's estimates of how long learning takes vary across the years: "give yourself 20 years, not two weeks" (2023), top 10 percent in six to twelve months (early 2024), "6 years of results in 6 months" (2024), "you can learn anything in two weeks" (2025) and again "2 to 5 years" to master brand, content, marketing and sales even with AI (2026). In 2025 he clarifies that "two weeks" means substantial progress toward a goal, not mastery, which "obviously takes like 10 years." The numbers apply to different scales (a technique, a skill, a business, mastery) and are best read as claims about relative speed: the person who has learned how to learn moves several times faster than the person who has not.

A 2026 video adds an argument about why speed of learning matters at all. If the gap between where you are and where you want to be is a gap of knowledge, then "the future belongs to those who can learn the fastest." Most people stop learning after graduation and run on what Koe calls "preset knowledge." Writing is his antidote, because "80% of writing is research and learning"; it is at once teaching and understanding. He says he learned more in six months of writing when he started out than in all of college or high school. His example is history: he never applied himself to it in school, but since writing about history in some of his newsletters he understands it far better than he did from lectures (**Source:** `The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md`, 2026-07-27). The claim about writing anticipates section 14.5 (writing as a form of teaching) and Part VIII.

#### The earliest version: "figuring it out for yourself"

The meta skill has a precursor in 2022, under a plainer name. In a video whose title announces writing as "the skill" to learn, Koe first names a different one: "there is one extremely important skill, and that is figuring it out for yourself." He defines it by two contrasts. It is not doing what people tell you to do; and it is doing what you want "from a conscious mind," not from a "mediocre mind." He guards against the naive reading. "I can do whatever I want" is not what he means; the caricature of the mediocre mind is the person who replies, "you want me to jump off a bridge, okay, I'll go do that." The conscious version examines itself: what are your desires; what survival mechanism, or whatever else, is pulling you toward those things; is it good or bad; do you actually want to do it; does it align with your values; "do you actually have values." What other people tell you to do will not be conducive to what you want. Then comes a realistic nuance: much of the time you may have to do what others tell you, for survival, but at the same time you need to be pursuing a project of your own that leads to autonomy and to mastery of what you want to master, full time, "not for someone else" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28).

**Figuring it out for yourself** and the pair **conscious mind / mediocre mind** are Koe's terms. There is a small tension inside the video: the title calls writing "the skill," while the first skill named is figuring it out for yourself, with writing then presented as the skill that monetizes it. The tension resolves if the two are read as layers: figuring things out is the meta skill, writing the skill through which it becomes income. The move from "figuring it out for yourself" (2022) to "learn how to learn" as the meta skill (2024–2026) is a renaming and formalization, not a change of position. The 2022 version keeps something the later one leaves implicit: the meta skill presupposes self-examination. You cannot choose what to learn if you have not examined what you want and why.

#### Daily self-education as a necessity

From the meta skill Koe derives a habit. In 2023 he presents it as the second step of a daily routine and calls it "an absolute necessity." His argument starts from a judgment about school: "Schools are necessary in many cases, but they teach you a fraction of what actually exists in reality; they train you into a compartment of reality, like chemistry, physiology or literature," without regard "for the holistic interconnectedness that breeds true intelligence." **Compartment of reality** is his term for this: an isolated slice of the world, taught without its connections. The consequence is about identity: "Without self-education you go through life with the same narrow identity and perspective as everyone else; you're not a unique individual if you never self-educate." He then lists what education does. It "expands your mind, introduces you to novel perspectives, increases dopamine in the brain as a consistent source of energy, gives you the knowledge to act with clarity," and "over time education conditions your mind to run on new systems." His example of the last point: immerse yourself only in information about building a profitable business and you would build one. Most people skip this step; their morning has no section "dedicated to reading or consuming or researching or pushing into the mental unknown" (**Source:** `The Daily Routine That Changed My Life (4 Habits Most People Ignore).md`, 2023-11-19).

The dopamine claim should be read in the terms Chapter 11 established: Koe uses dopamine as a name for the anticipation that accompanies novelty relevant to a goal, and the claim here is that learning toward something is a renewable source of that drive. He presents it as part of his model, not as a measured effect of studying.

Earlier in 2023 he states the same necessity in starker terms. "You own one thing: your mind; everything else can be taken away." School, he grants, got one thing right: consistent daily education and the hope of a better future. But it does not prioritize curiosity, so most students hate learning by graduation. "Learning is the foundation of the human experience, and once you stop you die." The prescription is modest in size and absolute in frequency: learn something every day, whether ten minutes or three hours. His reason is that learning is the only way to discover a potential that was not assigned to you. You cannot act on opportunities that do not exist for you, and you cannot even register them without having reached a level of skill by doing your own thing. His example: until you have started a business, you cannot look at someone online and think, "he has this business, I have this offer, he can pay me this much, I'll reach out in a way I know, then systemize it." When you stop learning, life stops progressing, "feel-good chemicals" stop flowing, and life becomes "mediocre, repetitive, mechanical, robotic" (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30).

In 2024 he gives the mechanism behind that example a name. While you learn and build, "you're increasing your awareness surface area": "when you have more skills, you have more education, you have exposure to different opportunities, you can identify more profitable problems and opportunities that you can solve and go into" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). **Awareness surface area** is a coined term: the extent of reality in which a person can recognize problems and opportunities, which grows with skill, education and exposure. It explains why the person who has started something sees opportunities that the person who is still waiting cannot see, and it connects to the reticular-activation argument of Chapter 6: what you know and pursue determines what registers.

Two further passages from 2023 tie self-education to creativity and to the unusual life. The first places it at the foundation of a creative career. The first step is self-education (not formal education, "though that can be useful"). Creativity is "using your diverse knowledge and interest to help you achieve a goal." If you have never learned anything outside what you were told to learn, you are not "an actual creative"; if you only know what schools teach, you can only create what may already exist, because "you can't create something rare or unknown without going down a rare or unknown path." This is less about becoming an expert than about "a constant influx of information" to fuel your goals and what you build. Without unique interests, knowledge and skills you lack **creative firepower**, Koe's term for the reserve of unusual inputs that creation draws on (**Source:** `The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md`, 2023-12-31). The second passage draws the conclusion about who can take that path: "The only people who have the choice to take a rare path that breeds rare results are the self-educators." It repeats the list of what education does and adds one more: it exposes you to potentials you were not aware of. A consistent flow of education increases the chance of encountering meaningful events, which "occur at the edge of the known, when your nervous system signals that you should pay attention": with one foot in the unknown you can "just barely metabolize new aspects of reality" and use them (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The **edge of the known** is the same border that Chapter 10 described as the place of flow, where challenge slightly exceeds skill; here it is described from the side of information rather than effort.

A 2023 video turns the necessity into a protocol for the digital age. "Lifelong self-education will be a requirement to continue adapting to the digital landscape." "Education shapes the future because it shapes human behavior"; it "expands your perspective and allows you to spot opportunities in unknown territory." "The new education system is being built online before your eyes." The steps: set a goal; find creators you resonate with; drown yourself in their information; watch new opportunities register in your awareness; and act on them "like your life depends on it" (**Source:** `The Value Creator (A New Internet Career Path For Intelligent People).md`, 2023-07-19). The order matters. The goal comes first, so that the "drowning" is filtered; the creators come second, chosen by resonance; and the last step is action. Without the last step the protocol degenerates into the consumption that section 14.3 criticizes.

#### Why daily learning fails

Self-education has a habitual form, and in 2024 Koe makes it the seventh of twelve rules. "You aren't where you want to be because you aren't as smart as you think you are." Every project requires a skill set and a mindset, so you need education to achieve goals and complete projects. "Daily self-education must become a cornerstone habit in your life. If you stop learning you stop evolving, opportunities stop presenting themselves and you get trapped in your current stage of development. Education is the fuel for experimentation, and without experimentation you are doing what other people tell you to do" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). A **cornerstone habit**, in his usage, is a habit that supports the rest of a lifestyle; here it is the habit that keeps a person developing. The last sentence of the rule links learning to the experimentation of Chapter 15: education feeds experiments, and without experiments you fall back on instructions.

In 2025 he explains why the habit so often fails to produce change. "Daily learning and self-education is so freaking important because all of these things overlap and intertwine. Just treat daily self-education toward your goals as a habit and your life will change." The phrase "toward your goals" carries the weight. Recounting how he transformed his health, he says he did not pick a random diet or random exercises, "because that's extremely chaotic. The mind craves order"; he refers to the psychology of the hierarchy of goals and to "turning your life into a video game" (Chapters 8 and 10). He was not forcing anything random: "I had clarity and novelty and dopamine that was leading to me doing something." Most people, he says, have two problems. First, they are not hyper-aware of a painful problem, "so their mind doesn't release dopamine at the sight of a potential solution." Second, "they learn without experimentation. So they never make the progress necessary to act as a feedback loop that keeps them motivated" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24).

The two failures are the two halves of the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6. Without a felt problem there is no error signal, and information about solutions does not register as relevant. Without experimentation there is no output, and therefore no feedback telling you whether you are progressing. The 2023 passages emphasize the necessity of self-education and its effect of expanding the mind, while the 2025 passage adds this diagnosis of why it fails. There is no change of position; the later version explains the conditions under which the earlier one works.

The durations Koe attaches to the habit vary across videos: a "general education habit" of ten to thirty minutes a day (2023), thirty to sixty minutes of self-education plus thirty to sixty of building (late 2023), "a daily 30 to 60 minute habit, this isn't optional" (2024), a book for thirty minutes a day (2025). The variation reflects different purposes (general education, acquiring a skill, reading) more than a change of view.

#### Becoming your own expert

A group of early passages, mostly from 2022, makes a broad claim about what self-education can reach. "Anything you want, any skill, you can learn online." If you have a problem in your life, there is something you can learn and "become directly conscious of until you can test a system for getting that desired outcome." By self-educating "you are literally creating a better life." He extends this to the inner life: self-development includes not only tangible aspects but mindset and intellectual development, which "has to be a top priority"; you must consistently learn to break through stages of development and to build creative solutions for those behind you (**Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`, 2022-06-25). The criterion embedded in the claim is practical: learning ends not when you know about a problem but when you can test a system that produces the outcome. Chapter 15 develops this as self-experimentation.

He illustrates it with his own example. Everything he knows about the gym he learned from fitness YouTubers. There are "infinite perspectives" to study, so "I can become my own personal trainer, I can become my own relationship therapist, I can become my own psychologist," "anything that I want to become," because ample education exists without paying more than $40,000. He specifies the scope: he gives advice to others on business, spirituality and personal development, but in areas like gym and health he simply uses his desire for self-education to become his own expert (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31). The claim is a statement of his practice and his opinion; he does not argue that self-taught therapy equals professional care, and the reader should not read that into it.

The same optimism appears in career terms. "We're all lucky," he says in 2022: to learn graphic design you can watch an hour-long YouTube video that teaches what you need, make a few projects and apply for a design job. If you are smart enough, and if the employer does not write off talent without a degree ("a lot of them are opening up that option"), you could get a job for watching a few hours of YouTube, or freelance and do what you want (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). The conditions are his: a few projects (not only the video), an employer willing to hire without a degree. In the same video he floats an idea about the other side of the market: in school you get an hour of after-school one-on-one mentoring, so "why isn't online mentoring a bigger thing?" If you know design well and some of your followers want to learn it to increase their own value, they can hire you for a Zoom call in which you walk them through the fundamentals; you earn an income and freelance work may come to you (same source). **Online mentoring** is presented as something he was "toying with," an opinion rather than a method; it foreshadows the education businesses of Part X.

A 2023 video gives the most concrete early recipe, framed as the second step of recreating yourself. "It's silly to think you can achieve self-generated goals without self-education." "Immerse your conscious mind in the information that will program a new identity and give you the knowledge to attack your goals." Concretely: follow social media accounts that give specific information related to your goals; then "purchase one to three books on the topic and devour them." He answers those who object to social media: they do not understand how integrated and powerful it is; people see it as bad (news, toxicity) but you can curate your experience; it is woven into society, "necessary," part of our lives "whether you like it or not" (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19).

This recipe is the starting point of one of the unresolved oscillations of the corpus, and it has to be read alongside what comes later. In December 2023 Koe says "reading books is not learning, watching tutorials is not learning" (section 14.4). In September 2025 he says books and YouTube used for learning are "entertainment" and that reading to learn "is just creating more chaos" (section 14.3). Between those, in 2024, he still recommends buying three books and "burning through them," and says reading is how you absorb years of others' experience. On the question "is reading books learning?" the corpus contains an unresolved contradiction. The most defensible reading, which the corpus supports without stating it in one place, distinguishes two purposes: reading to learn *how to do* something, which in his later view only works when a project gives it a use, and reading to see the world differently, which he continues to value (Chapter 16). The 2023 recipe is closer to the first purpose, but it sits inside a process whose next step is building, which is why it is less opposed to the later view than it first appears.

#### Study human nature first: the seven liberating arts

If the future is unknowable, what should one learn? Koe's 2024 answer begins with a principle: "Study human nature, then study whatever you want." It is impossible to know the future; all we know is that there will be a way of life that leads to the most satisfaction. You cannot depend on school, government, parents or anyone else to secure your future; every successful person "by any measure" has taken it into their own hands. An ideal future lifestyle will have unique aspects, but also "immutable principles of human nature that have persisted and will persist," and those you must learn. To find them, look at what schools do not teach: if schools train "modern slaves" for one task, what do "sovereign individuals" teach themselves? (**Source:** `The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md`, 2024-03-24). The argument is a bet on invariants: since content changes, learn what does not.

His answer to the question comes from Devon Eriksen, whose list he reads aloud in several videos in 2024. The attributions must be kept clear: the list and its definitions are Eriksen's; the remarks about agency, the method of learning the arts and the mapping onto vessels are Koe's. Eriksen calls his list the **Seven Liberal Arts of the modern world**, and prefers the name **liberating arts**, so as not to be confused with "the ideological monstrosity that is taught in liberal art school"; in Koe's paraphrase, "the liberal arts are liberating arts; what's taught in Liberal Arts School are not liberating" (**Source:** `The Future-Proof Skill Stack (How Average People Become Millionaires).md`, 2024-03-24; `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28). Each art is defined by what it lets you do:

| Liberating art (Eriksen) | What it lets you do (Eriksen's definition) | Koe's vessel for practicing it |
|---|---|---|
| Logic | Derive truth from known facts | Writing and thinking |
| Statistics | Understand the implications of data | Entrepreneurship |
| Rhetoric | Persuade, and spot persuasion tactics | Marketing and sales |
| Research | Gather information on an unknown subject | Writing and thinking |
| Practical psychology | Discern and understand the true motives of others | Marketing and sales |
| Investment | Manage and grow existing assets | Entrepreneurship |
| Agency | Decide what course to pursue and proactively take action to pursue it | Entrepreneurship |

(**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`, 2024-12-08; vessels from `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28.)

The list is presented as the answer to the question "what should we learn if we don't know what will be relevant?": these are meta-skills of self-education rather than task training. The March 2024 version ends with the strongest conclusion: with these arts, technical or career-specific skills "won't matter," because you can adapt to and change them at any time. Whose conclusion this is remains uncertain. The sentence follows directly on the quoted list, in a passage where Koe is reading Eriksen, and nothing in the transcript marks it off as his own commentary; it may be Eriksen's claim, read aloud, or Koe's gloss on it. What is clearly Koe's comes later. In the December 2024 version he adds a remark of his own: agency, "the last art," "could be the glue between it all," and asks why the liberating arts are not taught. The transcripts give only the list and one-line definitions; no order of learning and no relationship among the components is stated beyond that remark about agency. The name of the author of the list appears as "Devon Ericson" in March 2024 and "Devon Eriksen" later; this is a transcription variant of the same person, and the content of the list is stable across the versions, with a small addition in each.

**Complementary context:** The classical seven liberal arts were grammar, logic and rhetoric (the trivium) and arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy (the quadrivium), the curriculum considered fitting for a free person, as Chapter 2 noted. Eriksen's list keeps the classical idea (the education of a free person) and two of its arts (logic, rhetoric), and replaces the rest with capacities oriented to acting in a modern economy.

How does one learn such arts? Koe's answer in July 2024 is the most interesting part of his treatment, because it applies the theory of this chapter to them. "You must hold them in your mind as you dive into the unknown, or as you take a new path, because you don't just learn something by reading it. You hold it in your mind and you let new experiences and the chaos of the unknown bring that clarity to your mind and allow you to practice those skills in real time." Prepared in this way, "they slowly just become a part of who you are and your identity," and whatever technical skills are or are not relevant in twenty years, "you'll be able to learn them fast and get results with them fast, because you have the underlying traits that make those skills successful" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28). The method is build-to-learn applied to abstract capacities: the arts are not studied as subjects but carried as lenses into real situations, where the situations do the teaching. The goal is identity change (Chapter 4), not information.

Because abstract arts need concrete situations, Koe proposes three practical skills as **vessels** through which they are practiced. "Think of these next skills as vessels for learning the softer skills that we just talked about." The first is marketing and sales, "because if you don't know how to attract and persuade you will never get what you want, and your only option will be for an employer or the government to give it to you"; it exercises rhetoric and practical psychology. The second is writing and thinking, "the ability to communicate the value in your unique mind, the foundation of getting in front of other people"; it exercises logic and research. The third is entrepreneurship, "the process of taking my future into my own hands, hunting for my survival in building products that I want to see in the world that others care about"; it exercises statistics, agency and investment. He calls them "futureproof skills" and "evergreen." He also describes how they interact: "with writing and thinking you attract like-minded people, with marketing and sales you persuade them," and through marketing and sales "you understand the minds of your customer so that you can solve your own problems and create a solution for them" (same source). The mapping in the table above is his, not Eriksen's. **Vessel** keeps the sense Chapter 2 and Chapter 9 gave it: the vehicle through which something is pursued. Here the vessels are the vehicles through which deeper capacities are trained. The three vessels also correspond to the structure of the rest of this book: writing (Part VIII), audience and persuasion (Parts IX and XI), and the one-person business (Part X).

#### Knowledge not found in schools

Koe sums up one strand of his thinking in a tweet he quotes in two videos in 2023: "Knowledge not found in schools is the source of money not found in employment." In the first, he states it after telling the story of learning web development faster than his college course (below) and adds that "we are in the middle of a massive shift"; shifts are slow ("we don't go through an Industrial Revolution in five seconds"), and this shift is not only about money (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). In the second, the tweet sums up the video and he adds an image: with the internet, information has spread beyond measure, like "a wildfire," leading to a more advanced, global and accessible society online (**Source:** `The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md`, 2023-06-25). The tweet joins two of the book's threads: the critique of school (Chapter 2) and the economy of the individual (Part XII). Its logic is symmetrical: if the employed path is defined by the knowledge schools give, the knowledge they do not give is what opens the other path.

Why must that knowledge be turned into income at all? In 2022 Koe builds the argument as a chain. You want to do what you love full time; that requires mastery and autonomy; to have those you must be able to earn from them; so "you need to learn a skill that will allow you to monetize those things, to monetize your interests and your passions." He then presents writing as that skill, one that lets you thrive "no matter whether there's a recession" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28). In another 2022 video he states the position more modestly. He is a fan of doing what you want with your life, which usually implies pursuing and developing the skills that let you make an income doing it. "That doesn't have to be via entrepreneurship, but that's what I've done for the past three to four years" and what he teaches; you can also get a job with these skills. He speaks, he says, "from the lens of entrepreneurship and the creator economy" (**Source:** `Stop Trying To Be Unique... Do This Instead.md`, 2022-05-28).

That modesty does not last. From 2024 Koe calls entrepreneurship "the only path" to total control of one's work, and across the corpus his stance on employment oscillates, without resolution, between "the worst outcome," "slavery," "a stepping stone" and, by 2025, admitted routes inside organizations (Chapter 2 traces it in full). For this chapter the relevant point is what the oscillation does not touch: in every version, doing what you want requires learning skills that produce income.

And learning them is hard. In 2025 he says so bluntly: "None of this is anywhere close to being easy. That's the entire [damn] point. You're going to have to learn more skills": how to attract people, write, persuade, market, sell. "You're the CEO. You need to learn every single skill that requires you to operate a business along with your multiple interests." You need to become a generalist, have taste, "package up and deliver signal to attract people," and merge art and business (**Source:** `How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md`, 2025-02-23). **CEO** here is used in his own sense: the person who operates their own business and therefore has to learn every skill it requires. The passage is a bridge to Chapter 20 (the generalist) and Chapter 18 (taste); for this chapter its importance is that it sets the scale of the learning task. The meta skill is not a luxury for curious people; it is the condition of running a one-person business.

#### The author's education: learning from the people he wanted to become

Koe's theory of learning is drawn from his own history, and he tells that history often. The story has two strands: learning online from people he admired, and learning to code faster than college could teach him. Both are told with variations, and the variations are noted where they matter.

The first strand begins in childhood. As a child he "didn't trust the education system or my parents or even my religious teachers to equip me with the knowledge necessary to secure my future." Instead he consumed content from YouTubers, bloggers and people on social media whose information aligned with his interests or sparked new ones, to pursue what schools were not teaching, usually around increasing his quality of life. He names three areas: fitness (for him, bodybuilding; for you, he says, it could be running or cycling); business (he started with freelancing); and philosophy and meaning, "the need for Spirit, something beyond yourself," because he questioned the religion he grew up in. He bought affordable courses to learn skills he probably could not have learned better through a degree (**Source:** `Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md`, 2023-04-30). Chapter 2 described that upbringing; here it matters as the origin of a habit: when the institutions around him did not supply what he wanted to know, he went to the internet for it.

He describes the internet of his youth as a refuge. Pursuing an unconventional career "has not been available for a long time," and he feels fortunate to have grown up during the internet boom; the internet was where he went to "find solace" and people to connect with. Following fitness YouTubers, he learned a great deal, which got him into the gym for ten years and "opened my mind." It expanded his awareness that "there's an economy and then there's a digital economy, and they both work similarly," and that he needed to learn how to get in and create something he could monetize (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). **Digital economy** is a common phrase used here in his sense: a parallel economy that works like the traditional one and that has to be learned in order to enter it. It should not be confused with **Digital Economics**, the name of his own program, discussed below.

The fullest version of the fitness story, from 2024, contains a scene. In eighth grade Koe (who is six foot five) and his best friend were pushed by their math teacher, Mr. Schroeder, the after-school weights coach, to start training. His first bench press was about 65 pounds on a rusty bar, arms shaking, with a strange pain and worse soreness afterward. With time and progress, "rather than being distracted and just flooding your mind with cheap dopamine," he became obsessed with weight training, and at the time of the video had spent more than ten years in the gym. His high-school routine: go home, drink pre-workout, grab his gym bag, review his training program (Matt Ogus's program), train hard with a friend, come home, drink a protein shake, and watch YouTube. He watched only fitness channels; the transcript names Matt Ogus, Elliott Hulse (transcribed as "Elliot Holz"), Chris Lavado (transcribed elsewhere as "Levado" and "Lovato"), Chris Jones and Scott Herman. Hulse, a strength coach known in the early 2010s for direct talking-head videos on fitness, masculinity and spirituality, is the one whose style Koe later imitated in his second YouTube channel (Chapter 37); for Lavado the transcripts do not settle a single spelling, and this book keeps the one in this passage. His realization was that "these creators were doing what they loved, impacting others at scale and exposing people to their potential." That was when he began to deconstruct how to do the same. Years of failures followed (a fitness YouTube channel, a digital art Instagram page, several marketing agencies) before he saw success with freelance web design while transitioning to being a creator (**Source:** `The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md`, 2024-01-21).

A 2025 version of the same years explains the learning mechanism behind the obsession. In middle school he was "the tall and super lanky dude," disliked how he looked, felt awkward in his skin and lacked confidence. He used that pain to educate himself on fitness and nutrition: he read *Nutrient Timing for Peak Performance* on the bus ride to middle school, did push-ups in his room, went to after-school weights, and "my love for the gym just slowly picked up from there." He could not change his physical environment (he lived with his parents, had certain friends, lived in a specific place), but he could curate his digital environment, which "creates and programs your identity by immersing yourself in the right information." After school he went straight to the computer to watch "the two to three fitness vloggers at the time when YouTube was just starting out." "That changed my entire life." Each video gave him "this novel hit of dopamine, because I had something new to try": go to the gym, try the new exercise or program, stick to it, see progress, "create this feedback loop, fall in love with it." The same was true of nutrition. If you are not sticking to yours, he says, it is probably "because you're not surrounded by the people in your physical or digital space that give you these new tips and tricks and knowledge that you can implement and see direct feedback from" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24).

The passage is a compact model of the learning loop the rest of the chapter develops: a painful problem (insecurity about his body), a curated stream of goal-relevant information (fitness vloggers), immediate application (the new exercise that afternoon), visible progress, and a feedback loop that turns effort into obsession. **Curate your digital environment** is one of his terms (Chapter 11 develops it); here it appears as the one environment a teenager could control. The stories of when he started in the gym vary (after a first breakup, in eighth grade with Mr. Schroeder, from age fifteen, in middle school with *Nutrient Timing*); the variations are narrative and compatible.

He states the result of all this with unusual emphasis. Education businesses have helped him enormously, not only because he runs one but because they "changed the course of my life": "I would not have anything in my entire lifestyle if it weren't for me being able to learn online from people that I aspire to be like" since childhood. He apologizes for his language, because he gets "very passionate" about the subject (**Source:** `The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md`, 2023-04-23). The gratitude explains a feature of his business model (Part X): he sells education because education online is what made him.

Koe also names the books that resonate with him, as an example of what curiosity-driven consumption looks like. In a long 2024 guide he lists a "select few": *The Power of Now* (which he has not read in a while), *Awareness*, *The Way of the Superior Man*, *Flow* by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, *Rest* by Alex Soojung-Kim Pang, and "everything Alan Watts" (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). He uses the list to illustrate curiosity (section 14.2), not to draw ideas from the books at that point; later in the same guide he uses Alan Watts as an example of philosophical writing that may not perform well online.

**Complementary context:** *The Power of Now* is by Eckhart Tolle, *Awareness* by Anthony de Mello and *The Way of the Superior Man* by David Deida; the transcript does not name these three authors. Chapter 13 discussed Pang's *Rest*; Chapter 10 discussed Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow*.

#### Learning to code faster than college

The second strand is the story Koe uses most often as proof that self-education outpaces formal education. Its core is stable across versions; its details are not.

In the 2022 version he was living with six other young men in an old fraternity house near Arizona State University, around his junior year (the exact timeline is unclear). He took an introductory web development class, learned basic HTML and CSS in two sessions and became obsessed with coding, which promised a job without a degree, no corporate nine-to-five and creative work. He took courses after school. This, he says, solidified the value of self-education for him: always learning and building projects outside school or work, something that gave him "intrinsic energy." He learned the course's curriculum "in like three weeks just by studying on my own," did not attend class and still got an A. He knew time was running out: either make something work or "end up like everyone else" (**Source:** `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md`, 2022-06-18). **Intrinsic energy** is his phrase for the energy that learning and building on his own terms gave him, in contrast to assigned work.

The 2023 version adds the tools and the price. In his final year he took a basic web development and programming class, the first course in four years he was genuinely interested in. After school he spent three to four hours a day studying HTML, CSS and JavaScript through free platforms and paid Udemy courses at about ten dollars each. "Within two weeks I had learned the entire college course worth of curriculum on my own," stopped going to class, and still aced every test. "This is when the reality of formal education slapped me in the face" (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). The tweet about knowledge not found in schools, quoted above, follows immediately in that video.

Another 2023 version adds the motive. Koe was "a creative at heart" who wanted the dream lifestyle seen on TV and the internet ("everyone's on the beach working from their laptop, or just not working at all"). He saw artists, graphic designers and web designers and saw in their work "a way to get out of a job"; he notes that the people he followed were conditioning him to want it. He studied graphic design, film, Photoshop, Illustrator and video editing. He knew he needed a skill the market valued without giving up his **Creative Edge**, his term for the creative trait he refused to lose. In his fourth year he took a web development class and "became obsessed": he did not understand the curriculum, studied on his own, things clicked, and after school he went to the library and studied all day, buying courses, using freeCodeCamp and YouTube, coding along "for like 6 hours a day." Coding enticed him because it promised a job without a degree, and front-end development ("what you see on the internet") kept his creative edge: "I found it: this is the one thing that allows me to both be creative, earn a high income, and potentially freelance or build a software." He always wanted to do his own thing, to be an entrepreneur and creator, so "getting a job was the bane of my existence" (**Source:** `How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md`, 2023-10-08).

The 2024 versions add the lesson and the context. In one, he was in his fifth year of university with perhaps two years left, because he had switched majors many times (business, marketing, graphic design, filmmaking, a semester each) before landing on coding. He enjoyed it so much that he studied in his free time, at the library after class and through tutorials at home, and learned the curriculum in two to three weeks; he stopped attending and still aced the tests. The lesson he draws is "the power of self-education": "if you actually have a goal and curiosity you can learn so much more, so much faster than most people," and "that's how you get ahead of everyone else" (**Source:** `How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md`, 2024-10-20). The formula joins the two drivers of this section and the next: goal and curiosity.

The version in the summary of his book *The Art of Focus* places the story inside his childhood sense of isolation. As a child he felt alone, unable to relate to peers who obeyed parents and teachers and conformed to friends without questioning; they went to school, got a job, "and from that point forward you never heard anything interesting from them again." He realized a job would not give him fulfilling work. He spent five years at university with two years left; took out "$2,000 in loans even with a full-ride scholarship"; knew he wanted his own path; discovered his love of web development after an introductory course taken out of curiosity; learned the curriculum through self-education "in less than a month"; and, after multiple business failures and accepting that he needed a job to survive, used his self-study to land a high-paying tech job after his first interview. His conclusions are two questions: "If I could learn more useful skills in 6 months of self-education than I could in 5 years of formal education, why hadn't I done that earlier?" And if self-study got him a job that paid more than most, which would later transfer into "a hyper-profitable business," "why did I waste so much time in college?" (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The 2022 career-path video gives the sequel. Code can be self-taught online; you can increase your value quickly and get a job without a degree in six to twelve months. "That's what I did: I got a job in a year by learning to code, I used my income from that job to fuel my side business," which at the time of recording had been his full-time business for three years. He started Digital Economics, his own school, because he wanted to train people to fuel the new digital economy (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). **Digital Economics** was the name of his program for "productizing yourself" between 2022 and 2024.

| Detail | Versions in the corpus |
|---|---|
| Time to learn the course curriculum | About three weeks (2022); two weeks (2023); two to three weeks (2024); "less than a month" (2024, book summary) |
| Year of university | Junior year, "timeline fuzzy" (2022); final or fourth year (2023); fifth year with about two left (2024) |
| Study load | Courses after school (2022); 3–4 hours a day (2023); "like 6 hours a day" (2023) |
| Loans | "$2,000 ... even with a full-ride scholarship" (2024); $20,000 in student loans in other videos |

These are best read as variations of memory; the $2,000 figure may be a transcription error for $20,000, which cannot be confirmed. None of the variations affects the lesson Koe draws, which is consistent in every version: with a goal and curiosity, a person learning on their own and applying what they learn moves several times faster than a classroom. The story also contains a detail that section 14.3 will need. Learning web development quickly did not by itself produce a business; the years that followed were years of failure. The self-taught coder had learned a skill, but not yet the skills that bring customers.

### 14.2 Curiosity as the Guide

Section 14.1 argued that a person must choose what to learn, or have it chosen for them. It did not say how to choose. Koe's answer has two parts that work together. The goal (Chapter 6) tells you what is relevant; curiosity tells you where your energy for learning actually is. The goal without curiosity produces the joyless studying he associates with school; curiosity without a goal produces endless consumption. This section presents what Koe means by curiosity, how he says to discover it, the stages it passes through, the attitude he calls moving "from cope to curiosity," and the three conditions he identified, from his own reading, for retaining what one learns.

#### Curiosity, recognized by experience

Koe admits that curiosity is hard to define. In 2022, describing the "value creator" (the person who makes a career of exploring, synthesizing and sharing ideas, Chapter 18), he says value creators dedicate themselves to exploring the unknown and their curiosity, taking as their life's work the dissection of their "crevices of reality" (self-improvement, marketing, business, spirituality, metaphysics, philosophy, email marketing), "letting one thing lead to another by curiosity." Then he concedes that curiosity confuses people, him included, because it is not tangible. He can only explain it from experience and feeling: "think of the book that you actually wanted to read, think of the videos that you actually wanted to watch," the things that were "seamless," where you thought, "man, I'm loving learning about this" (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31). He repeats the definition almost word for word in 2024 and adds two consequences. Material that resonates makes it effortless to study and makes you want to talk about it; if certain authors do not resonate with you, that is a sign to look for the people, writing and interests that do. And it yields a rule for content: "post the things that are interesting to you, because they're going to be interesting to someone else." If something in a book catches your attention, some other people will think so too; distill it for your audience and synthesize it with your interests or goals. His own resonant books, listed in section 14.1, are his example (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

**Curiosity** is a common word that Koe uses in a sense of his own: not a general disposition to be interested in things, but a recognizable experience of effortless engagement with particular material, identified after the fact by its signs (wanting to read, losing track of effort, wanting to talk about it). The definition is deliberately phenomenological. It does not tell you what you will be curious about; it tells you how to recognize it when it happens. Its practical consequence is a test you can run on your own history: look for what you consumed without being made to.

The content rule is the first link between curiosity and teaching, which section 14.5 develops. It rests on an assumption Koe states as a heuristic rather than a law: that one's own interest is a reliable predictor of someone else's. He says "some" others will share it, not everyone, which is all that a niche of one (Chapter 19) requires.

#### Choosing an obsession: you cannot outsource it

If curiosity is recognized by experience, it cannot be found by asking. In the 2022 value-creator video, Koe makes "choose an obsession" the first step of researching a field, and his first instruction is negative: "Don't try to outsource this." Asking someone "what's my obsession?" is pointless. Have you tried anything? Have you been pulled down a rabbit hole? "If you don't try, you'll never know," so try everything that potentially catches your interest, because your mind and ego will distract you. His examples of distraction are video games and going out with friends; neither is bad in itself, but they become a problem when they keep you from progress. He then gives three places to start looking. First, your browser history on YouTube and Google: what you naturally consume to learn, not to distract yourself. Second, what you go on social media to learn: whom you follow who educates others, and how you could emulate and reverse-engineer them, "deconstruct that whole into parts." Third, your own life: what have you improved the most? If you are a gym junkie, a runner, an autodidact or a polymath, how has it helped you, and how could you help others become that? (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31).

**Obsession** and **rabbit hole** are terms Koe uses in a specific sense: an interest that pulls you along by itself, discovered by exposure rather than introspection. The three starting points are evidence-based in a modest way: each looks at behavior that has already happened (what you searched, whom you followed, what you improved) rather than at what you think you should be interested in. The third connects to Chapter 19: what you have improved most in your own life is a candidate for what you can help others with, because the customer is your past self.

#### The four paths of curiosity

In the summary of *The Art of Focus*, Koe gives curiosity a direction. "Let curiosity be your guide into the icebergs of opportunity that life presents"; "curiosity is the fuel along your path." When you push into the unknown, novel information grabs your attention, and that attention can be "your best friend or worst enemy." He then lays out four paths along which curiosity can be pursued (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18):

| Path | What it involves, in Koe's words |
|---|---|
| Appreciation | Appreciate ideas, people and opportunities for what they are; no need to judge or project |
| Understanding | Seek to understand more deeply by immersing yourself in books, content and information relevant to that area |
| Mastery | Once you understand something, choose to master it; when you feel the pull to go deeper, do not suppress it |
| Monetization | Having gone all-in on a select few areas, pair those skills and interests with business principles to create a living |

The table can be read as a sequence of increasing commitment. Appreciation costs nothing and can be applied to anything; understanding requires immersion; mastery requires a choice to go deep; monetization applies only to "a select few areas" and requires business principles in addition to the interest itself. Most curiosities stop at the first or second path, and Koe does not say they should go further; the four paths describe the options, not an obligation. The **icebergs of opportunity** image (in an earlier 2022 form, the "iceberg of opportunity") says that what you consume is the tip; what lies beneath can become a career.

In the same passage he condenses his whole method into one formula: dive into the unknown by pursuing your vision-aligned hierarchy of goals; educate yourself to gain clarity; execute the smallest priority task; keep an open mind to discover new potentials. The formula places curiosity inside the structure of Part IV. Curiosity is the fuel; the vision and the hierarchy of goals are the route; self-education gives clarity; and the smallest priority task (Chapter 12) is the next step. Curiosity does not replace the goal; it powers the movement toward it.

#### The curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle

How does curiosity become a stable part of a life? In 2024 Koe describes what he calls the **curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle** "of the universe," which stories and games follow: "first you're lost, then you're curious, then you're obsessed, then it's a consistent part of your life with little extra effort." He contrasts it with what most people do: they "get lost, focus on distractions and never put effort into a goal, even if they don't see the meaning in it yet" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The clause "even if they don't see the meaning in it yet" carries a claim worth isolating: meaning may come after effort, not before it. The cycle does not begin with a passion that then justifies the work; it begins with being lost, passes through curiosity and an intense period of obsession, and ends in consistency that requires little extra effort. Later in the same video he describes routine as moving "from intensity to consistency."

The cycle has a counterpart that section 14.3 examines from the side of failure. In 2023 Koe names the phases a person passes through when they start learning: a lost phase, a clarity phase and an intensity phase. The mistake he describes is "learning and never starting," which traps people in the clarity phase until it "turns into another lost phase" and they never enter intensity. Read together, the two descriptions say that curiosity leads to clarity, and that clarity becomes obsession only through action. Curiosity alone cycles back to being lost.

#### From cope to curiosity

Curiosity is also an attitude toward other people's success, and here Koe gives it a sharper edge. In a 2022 video on a "new career path," he says that in adulthood "things need to switch from cope to curiosity": from "oh no, there's no other way" to "maybe I could do that." The method is questioning. His example: instead of saying "that guy got lucky," stop and ask. What skill did he learn to do that? What is he actually doing? What goes on behind the scenes? What is he posting about? How did he learn it? "You just ask questions horizontally and vertically until you gain the bigger picture," and then you can form any opinion you want. Without this, it is "just assumption," "a blurred lens," a story you feed yourself "that you want to hear, based on your prior conditioning" (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21).

**Cope to curiosity** and **questions horizontally and vertically** are Koe's terms. He does not define "cope" beyond the example and the contrast with "maybe I could do that."

**Complementary context:** In internet slang, "cope" (from "coping mechanism") names a comforting story someone tells themselves to avoid facing an uncomfortable fact.

Read in that sense, "that guy got lucky" is cope because it makes the other person's result unreproducible and therefore irrelevant to you. Questioning "horizontally" widens the picture (what else is he doing, what surrounds the result); questioning "vertically" deepens it (how did he learn, what is underneath). The "blurred lens" connects to Chapter 1: an assumption is the conditioned mind defending itself, and curiosity is the first crack in that defense. The same move reappears in Chapter 17 as questioning.

In the same video Koe makes curiosity the first step of a sequence. After questioning (for example, "what if I did learn that skill; that would lead to x, y, z"), "the next step after curiosity is education": buy books, look on YouTube, search Google. "The answers are out there, especially for beginner-level stuff." He distinguishes two kinds of knowledge: principles and beginner-level knowledge are available online; specific knowledge comes later, by actually doing. Later in the video he restates the sequence: "we already have the first two steps, right: it's curiosity and then it's self-education," followed by displaying your value online as a "perspective vessel" (same source). The sequence curiosity → self-education → publishing your perspective is an early outline of this chapter's argument, from 14.2 to 14.5. The distinction between principles that can be found online and specific knowledge that comes from doing anticipates the build-to-learn method of section 14.4.

A 2024 passage extends questioning across time. Discussing a deep question ("what is a state of consciousness?"), which he treats as a metaphysical problem, Koe says to be open-minded, ask questions and accept that they may take time to answer. "A lesson there: ask more questions. You won't receive the answer immediately, but you can seed your mind to receive an answer that you normally wouldn't have. It can take a day, a week or a month, and for the questions that matter, a decade" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). **Seed your mind** is his term for the effect of an open question: it primes attention so that relevant answers register when they appear. The mechanism is the same one the goal provides in Chapter 6, applied to a question instead of a target. A question is a small goal for the mind.

#### Intention, genuine curiosity and the execution gap

The most detailed account of how curiosity turns reading into retained knowledge comes from Koe's own reading of *The Power of Now*, whose circumstances Chapter 2 told (the arrest, the letter, the hour each morning on the patio with gas-station coffee). In a 2022 video titled "How I Remember Everything I Learn," he reflects on why he retained so much from that book and identifies three steps (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

The first is **intention**: something to aim toward, held consciously or not, which makes the teaching fit your situation. He had a practical problem and something concrete to try, so the teaching "stood out."

The second is **genuine curiosity**: wanting to understand rather than closing your mind and picking apart small disagreements. He had first heard of the book from Matt Ogus, one of the "OG" fitness YouTubers of about a decade earlier, who had got him into the gym; he loved Ogus's personality, aspired to be like him and trusted him. Ogus had recommended the book many times, but Koe had not yet explored spirituality. When he finally picked it up, he was genuinely curious to figure out "what this whole spirituality thing" was. Combined with intention, curiosity made him read much more closely: "I wasn't looking to close my mind off and pick apart little things that I don't agree with in the book. I wanted to understand." His mind still reacted. When the book talked about God, he thought, "God doesn't exist." But he stepped back and asked whether there was something he was missing about "the notion of God" that he would never discover if he wrote it off entirely.

The third is **reducing the execution gap**. Here he quotes his own tweet, which "popped off randomly": "the greatest skill one can develop is decreasing the time between idea and execution." Some people disagreed, saying other skills are the greatest; he concedes the point, but says "in my situation in life that was the greatest skill." Applied to reading: when he came across something he could practice, he did not keep reading; he put the book down and practiced. His example: reading outside with his coffee, he reached advice about meditation, stopped, and meditated right there. He explains why this helped. Because the ideas were aligned and at the top of his mind, and he had not forgotten any of the nuances, he could notice whether the practices were working; he did not "glance over" the outcome. It was easier to solidify the knowledge because he had "backed the theory with direct experience," which he flags as an important point.

**Execution gap** is his term for the time between an idea and its execution. **Direct experience**, a common phrase in his special sense, is experience of one's own that backs a theory and, in his 2022 and 2024 usage, constitutes "true learning." The three steps fit together as a sequence that runs from before reading to after it: intention selects what will matter, genuine curiosity keeps the mind open while reading, and execution converts what was read into something experienced. The second step is subtle. Genuine curiosity is not credulity: Koe's mind reacted against the book, and he did not suppress the reaction; he examined it. What he refused was the defensive reading that looks for reasons to dismiss. The same distinction returns in Chapter 17 as the difference between closed and open thinking.

Two qualifications from the corpus belong here. The first is Koe's own: the claim about "the greatest skill" is situational, true "in my situation in life," and he does not defend it as universal. The second concerns where the method sits in his thinking. He presents the three steps as "a great way to retain a lot of what you learn, but we can do better than that," and he places this actionable advice at the start of the video deliberately (noting that he usually drags out his intros) because the deeper material needs to be built up to. The "better" method is building and teaching, sections 14.4 and 14.5. The three steps are a starting point, not the theory.

A later shift should be added. In 2022 the aim was retention: how to remember what you read. In 2025 Koe says "it's not about what information you have... it's about changing your mind," and that "the purpose for reading is behavior change through identity change"; in 2026, "if you need to remember it, it's not important." Retention stops being the goal; integration into identity becomes the goal. The positions are compatible if the 2022 retention is read as a consequence of practice rather than as an end in itself, which is how the third step already treats it: what he retained, he retained because he had done it. Chapter 16 develops the later view of reading.

### 14.3 Tutorial Hell: Information as Fake Progress

The first two sections described what to learn and what drives the learning. This section describes the characteristic way it goes wrong. Koe's diagnosis is that the most common failure of people who want to learn is not laziness but a form of activity: consuming information about the thing instead of doing the thing. It feels like progress, produces the pleasure of progress, and leaves nothing behind. He calls the condition **tutorial hell**, and he speaks of it from the inside, because he spent years in it. The section moves from the general principle (minimalism as releasing what only feels like progress), through the mechanism (the dopamine of information), to the rule that follows ("don't let knowledge outpace execution"), the author's own failures, and finally the way out, which he states in a tweet he has repeated for four years: start, then learn.

#### Minimalism: releasing what only feels like progress

The frame for the diagnosis comes from Koe's 2024 video on minimalist productivity. "Minimalism is not about having less, it's about making room for more of what matters." It is about leverage: releasing the things you think bring progress but actually slow you down, and focusing on the few tasks that bring outsized results while systemizing, outsourcing or delegating the rest. His first example of something that slows you down while feeling like progress is "watching tutorials without actively building a project." He does not just watch tutorials, he says; he finds one he wants to implement directly and learns the skill by building along with it (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11). **Minimalism** is used here in his own sense: not reducing possessions, but subtracting low-leverage activity so that high-leverage activity has room.

The rest of that video's argument explains why the subtraction matters for someone learning on their own time. A minimalist workday requires "a line of work that allows you to control the work day"; if you have an eight-hour workday for someone else, you cannot do it. He restates this as the first step of finding high-leverage tasks: choose "the right vessel," the line of work that lets you control the variables, which in the modern day means a business, specifically an internet business (same source). In 2025 he lists three conditions for working less: define and abide by your ideal lifestyle; make sure "your unique strengths align with a leverage-based game"; and be aware of the tools and technology available to do more in less time as one person (**Source:** `Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md`, 2025-05-04). **Right vessel** and **leverage-based game** are his terms; Chapter 12 developed the four-hour workday they serve.

Two earlier and later passages specify what "leverage" means for someone still learning. In 2023 he describes the "second pillar" of his workday: focus on the highest-leverage activities, because "the time to money ratio has to decrease as you grow or get better at business." Freelance, consulting and agency work are a good starting point for most beginners and often must be done to start making money; alternatively you can delay making money and build an audience first. But ultimately "you need a product that is able to sell and maintain itself while you sleep," if you want to do this without hiring many employees, which he does not want. He tells on himself: client work and manual outreach consumed his time, and he had been closed to being a creator, building a personal brand or building leverage along the way, instead "shooting blanks of thousands upon thousands of cold emails and having no leverage or readership built to show for it" (**Source:** `The 4 Hour Workday (How Creatives Work Less & Earn More).md`, 2023-06-18). And in 2024 he defines the **one leverage task**: ask what is the one thing you can do that "both increases your effectiveness with consistent failure and brings you closer to your goal," and do only that, first thing every morning. If you have only one hour to build your own thing, do only that and be at peace with it; you neither need to nor can work twelve hours if you only have one. Successful entrepreneurs do not start by working twelve hours a day on their projects; most have a job first and work one hour a day and perhaps four to eight hours on weekends. "Working a lot of hours is a choice, and it's often a stupid choice depending on the context of your life" (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

The definition of the one leverage task contains the whole argument of this section in miniature. The task must increase effectiveness "with consistent failure": it must be something you actually attempt, where you can fail and learn. Watching a tutorial cannot fail. That is precisely what makes it feel safe and what makes it low-leverage.

#### "The most dangerous drug": dopamine from information gathering

The mechanism that keeps people in tutorial hell was named most sharply not by Koe but by a guest. In a 2025 conversation, Koe observed that the "brutal awareness" produced by ideas like those in Sahil Bloom's book "dwindles" after people finish listening. Bloom named the cause: "probably the most dangerous drug that we have in our lives, which is the drug of dopamine from information gathering, because it convinces you that the information gathering is the good thing. You get the dopamine hit from reading *Atomic Habits* and you say, oh, I'm good at habits now because I read *Atomic Habits*, but you haven't done anything." His countermeasure, applied throughout his own book, is to "hit you with the information, but then also on the back end immediately with the action" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). **Dopamine from information gathering** is Bloom's term; the attribution matters, because Koe then builds on it.

**Complementary context:** Sahil Bloom is a writer and former investor, author of *The 5 Types of Wealth*; *Atomic Habits* is James Clear's book on habit formation.

Koe's own version, from 2026, sharpens the comparison. The first reason people fail is that "they're exchanging one form of cheap dopamine for another, more insidious form." "Endless tactic collection by endlessly consuming YouTube videos around business and self-improvement is worse than just doom scrolling brain rot videos, because you're getting a sense of fake progress." With doom-scrolling "you just feel bad about yourself. But with self-improvement and business, and even this video, you feel bad about yourself, but you also get this false certainty that you're making progress just by watching the video" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19). **Tactic collection**, **fake progress** and **false certainty** are his terms. The phrase "even this video" is characteristic: Koe repeatedly includes his own content in the warning.

Why is the self-improvement version "worse"? The argument is not that it does more harm per hour, but that it is harder to stop. Doom-scrolling is recognized as wasted time and produces guilt, which is at least a signal. Tactic collection produces guilt and its antidote at the same time: the feeling of having done something useful. In the terms of Chapter 11, it is cheap dopamine (novelty received without struggle) disguised as earned dopamine (progress toward a goal). It hijacks the reward that should come from progress.

In 2025 he extends the diagnosis to the format of what people consume. "The obsession over actionable steps and bullet point summaries is just another manifestation of cheap dopamine and distraction. You think that you're focused because you want everything condensed down into a set of actionable steps, including my long videos. That's not going to get you anywhere. You have swapped brain rot scrolling on TikTok for brain rot 'I need actionable tips' on YouTube." His prescription is the opposite of condensing: "Read more books. Read longer books. Read books that have nothing to do with what you're trying to achieve. Read the books that people wouldn't even think about summarizing with AI. That's where the gold is" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). **Brain rot** is a common phrase that he uses for degrading consumption, extended here to the consumption of "actionable tips." The corpus records a tension he acknowledges himself: the same video then teaches how to read books with AI, though not to summarize them (Chapter 16).

The prescription seems to contradict this section's insistence on application, and it is worth seeing why it does not. The complaint is not that long, unrelated books are useless; it is that the demand for actionable summaries is a way of avoiding the slow work of understanding while feeling efficient. Long books that "have nothing to do with what you're trying to achieve" feed the creative firepower of section 14.1 and the worldview of Chapter 16; they are not consumed as substitutes for action. What Koe objects to is consumption that pretends to be action.

He applies the same warning to his own audience in 2024. The process he had described (realize what you don't want, launch in the opposite direction, prioritize long-term success) is part of the origin story of most successful people. If you don't know what to do, rewatch the video and truly internalize it; do not watch for "a quick dopamine hit" of learning something that "may change my life" and then never apply it. "Flip the switch inside you" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Change Your Life (Starting Right Now).md`, 2024-10-10).

#### Telling, asking and believing as substitutes for doing

Three related habits work the same way: each produces the feeling of progress without the progress.

The first is telling people about your goals. In 2023 Koe says most people stop at goal-setting: they "mentally masturbate over their goals and they tell people about it," which he says is "scientifically shown to increase dopamine levels in the brain as if you achieve that goal," and they never make meaningful progress (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30). In 2024 he develops it. People want "the dopamine hit that comes from telling people that they're doing things" instead of "the dopamine that comes from actually achieving the goal." "This is an actual scientific thing; I forget the exact study, but there's a study that shows that dopamine is released in the same manner for telling people you're pursuing a goal as it is for actually achieving the goal." People get addicted to telling others for approval, "rather than just staying quiet, building, solving the problem and then showing them." The trap to avoid: "ignore the desire to tell every single person what you're doing." The only good outcome of telling is that someone might help you, "on a case-by-case situation." Instead, "stay quiet, start building, and then tell people what you're building; not 'I'm building this, think highly of me,' but 'I'm building this, here's how you can benefit, will you come help me?'" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30).

The scientific claim should be handled with the caution Koe himself signals: he does not identify the study, and the corpus records it as an unidentified source. What he builds on it is a heuristic, not a finding: telling for approval can substitute for doing. The heuristic stands or falls on its own plausibility and on the reader's experience, not on the unnamed study.

There is an apparent tension here with section 14.5, where Koe urges people to build and teach in public. It is a refinement rather than a reversal, and the reason Koe gives resolves it: the distinction is between telling people what you intend to do in order to be thought well of, and showing people what you are building in a way that benefits them. The first replaces work; the second is a product of work. "Stay quiet, start building, and then tell people what you're building" is a sequence, not a ban.

The second habit is asking for advice. "Most people ask for advice as a procrastination method to hide from the problems that they need to solve themselves." From his experience on social media, countless people comment under a video or post asking for advice; he answers, "just for them to go on and completely ignore it, or ask another one, or just say that I'm flat out wrong." "What was the purpose of asking the question in the first place if you're already set on keeping your mind closed?" His conclusion: "It seems like people don't want solutions; they want to feel as if they know what the solution is" (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). The last sentence is the general form of everything in this section: the wish to feel that one knows, rather than to do what the knowledge would require.

The third habit is subtler: believing that the method you followed is what worked. In a 2025 video on original thinking, Koe describes the trap of blindly accepting what someone says with conviction ("the ancestral diet is the best because...") while they keep writing content that shapes your worldview. "You're just getting quick confirmational dopamine hits from those things, and then you have an ordered mind because of it; you're more clear in your actions, so you get more results. It's not because that diet or training plan is the best there is; it's because you're actually doing the thing and following the principles that gets results." You are able to be disciplined now "because you've adopted the part of your identity that would eat the healthier foods and do the certain exercises." The same happens in business. "Once you attribute those results to the special little diet, then eventually you start to push that thing as the new religion. You lose all opportunity for new discovery and original thought." So "your job is to suspend judgment and attachment, question from all angles and research all perspectives, prove yourself wrong and understand every moving part of the argument. From there you can come to your own opinion; you are no longer bound by the mind you were programmed with" (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19).

**Confirmational dopamine** is Koe's term for the reward of having one's chosen belief confirmed. The argument is a causal correction. The results came from three things the book has already named: order in consciousness (Chapter 5), the clarity that order gives to action, and the identity that makes discipline unnecessary (Chapter 3). The specific diet is the occasion, not the cause. Mistaking the occasion for the cause turns a tool into a "new religion," and the conversion closes the mind. The passage belongs in this section because it is another case of information substituting for understanding: the follower has the content of a method without the understanding of why doing anything consistently works. Chapter 17 takes up the prescription (suspend judgment, prove yourself wrong).

#### Don't let knowledge outpace execution

From the diagnosis follows a rule, which Koe states in nearly identical words in 2022 and again in a 2024 compilation. "The worst thing you can do is let knowledge outpace execution." You do not learn to write by reading about writing; reading helps as a guide, but you must practice. And practice has an order. You cannot study a senior-level English course in first grade; you need first-grade English and practice, then second, then third. Skip steps and you will not write effectively, or it will take ten times longer, because you will try to write like a college senior and have to go back to fourth grade (**Source:** `The Rise Of The Value Creator (A Career Path For Polymaths & Self-Improvers).md`, 2022-10-31; `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). In a 2022 list of lessons from his business he makes it lesson nine, "start then learn": information is abundant; "the biggest mistake that people make is letting knowledge outpace execution"; "you can learn anything, but are you learning anything?" His favorite remedy, he says, is building projects (**Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`, 2022-11-06).

**Knowledge outpacing execution** is his phrase for the gap that opens when what you know exceeds what you can do. The grade-by-grade analogy adds something to the principle: execution has prerequisites, and knowledge acquired ahead of them cannot be used, so it is either forgotten or, worse, makes you attempt things you are not ready for. The "ten times longer" figure is an illustration of that second effect, not a measurement.

Both versions of the warning are followed immediately, in their source videos, by a qualification: not everything can be practiced tangibly, and some value requires contemplation rather than practice. The rule is about skills that are exercised in action; it does not claim that reflection is worthless. Chapter 13's defense of rest and Chapter 17's treatment of thinking are the other side of it.

Koe condenses the rule into a diagnostic that he calls one of his top tweets: "If you're lost, the answer is education; if you're educated, the answer is action; if you're acting, the answer is consistency" (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20). Read as a diagnostic, it asks the learner to identify their stage and apply the answer for that stage only. Its relevance here is the middle clause: the person who is already educated and keeps educating themselves is applying the answer to the wrong stage.

In the same video he draws a consequence for choosing teachers. "It only makes sense to match knowledge with execution." You do not want to learn from someone ten stages ahead of you, because "it won't make sense; you don't understand what's there." Learn from someone one stage ahead, match their execution so that you become aware of the "little bubble of awareness" they have at stage two; then move to stage three, "slowly expanding your awareness to encapsulate everything you're experiencing and learning" (same source). **Bubble of awareness** is his term for what is visible from a given stage. The heuristic follows from the grade analogy: a teacher ten stages ahead is teaching senior English to a first-grader. It also explains why so much expert advice does not help beginners even when it is correct.

#### The author's tutorial hell: the architect of the business

Koe's authority on tutorial hell is that he lived in it, and his account of his early failures is one of the most often repeated stories in the corpus. The story is consistent in its shape and has been told with different explanations over five years, which the following paragraphs trace.

The earliest version is from a 2021 conversation with Justin Welsh. Before going all in, Koe tried "five different things" as a freelancer: "Okay, yeah, I'm going to send 10, 20 cold emails. And then get discouraged, call it a day, and try something else because it's not working." He "didn't realize that the problem there was getting my offer in front of people." "I could have, like, get people the greatest results, but if I can't actually get them the results [that is, reach them], then it doesn't matter." Once he learned marketing through the book *Cashvertising*, "everything started to click": "I've acquired enough skill to do something for a specific person. And now it's like, okay, how do I make this appealing for that person? How do I actually get their attention in what seems like a saturated market?", which, he adds, is "not really the case." He tells listeners to "pay attention to the whole marketing side of things and marketing yourself" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20).

**Complementary context:** Justin Welsh is a solo creator known for his one-person business built on LinkedIn and X; *Cashvertising* is a book on advertising copy by Drew Eric Whitman (the transcript does not name the author).

In 2022 the explanation becomes more specific, in a passage he repeated literally in a 2024 compilation. He learned to code mainly to freelance, which turned out to be "more so marketing than it was code": he built websites with website builders. Everyone told him to learn a marketable skill (e-commerce, SEO, and so on), and he did. But he made no money, because he was obsessed with building the business, the website, making it look good with his design skills; when it came to acquiring customers, "that is when everything failed." "You don't have a business if you don't have customers." Things picked up once he learned writing, marketing and sales "and how they all intertwine": he understood that he needed to get his message in front of people, present an argument for his services, promote himself and display his value online to access opportunities (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28; `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

In 2023 he names what he lacked as a set of skills that sit beneath every business model. He failed at "seven-ish" business models because he did not understand the skills that lead to success with any of them: "business has a specific skill set attached to it that applies no matter what technicality you go into," whether email marketing freelancing, graphic design freelancing or anything else. The question is "what is the glue that makes those business models work." He cannot give fifty to a hundred hours of information in a thirty-minute video, but once you truly understand, practice and get results in those skills, "you can really make money whenever you want" (**Source:** `I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md`, 2023-08-16). **Glue** is a common word in his special sense: the business skills (writing, marketing, sales) that make any model work.

Two 2023 and 2024 passages fill in what he was doing instead. "When I first started out in business I had no idea what I was doing." He thought it was obvious: "if I have a skill, people need that skill, so they'll hire me." He was ignorant of the fact that anyone could learn that skill and that the online freelancing arena "was starting to become flooded with single skill commodities like web design"; that was the first problem he had to solve (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04). **Single skill commodities** is his term for freelancers offering one interchangeable skill. And in 2024: he began with freelancing because it seemed the most beginner-friendly option ("learn a skill, sell a skill"), and tried video editing, graphic design, SEO, content marketing, Facebook ads and web design. "My pain point was always choosing a niche." He downloaded free guides and "would endlessly search on Google for the top 100 niches to target": dentists, gyms, construction companies, pest control and more (**Source:** `Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md`, 2024-08-25). Chapter 19 draws the lesson about niches; here the endless search is another form of information gathering that replaces the act of getting a customer.

The 2024 version gives the trap its name. "There is one big trap in entrepreneurship, and I've fallen into it seven times; that's why I failed at seven business models when I was starting out." He was "so immersed in the skill" he was learning that it was all he wanted to do; he was "so focused on being the architect of the business," building the website, the logo, "all of the [stuff] you don't need when starting out," but when it came time to get customers he did nothing, so he did not get paid: "I didn't put product in front of people." The lesson: "you just need a product and you need to get it in front of someone and ask for payment" (**Source:** `Working Hard Won't Make You Rich (Do This Instead).md`, 2024-03-31). **The architect of the business** is his name for the trap: designing the structure of a business (site, logo, legal entity) as a substitute for its function (customers paying).

The latest versions tie the failures explicitly to tutorial hell. In January 2026: "I would just learn and learn and learn and watch tutorials, get stuck in tutorial hell." Seeing people warn against shiny object syndrome, he thought, "I'm right in the middle of that, I need to focus on one thing." "I got my dopamine from feeling smart. But when I looked back at my life, I didn't really have anything to show for it." He could not articulate what he remembered, his life did not change much, and he felt he was falling behind. In college he tried many things because he always dreamed of doing something creative, knowing that if college ended without a creative income he would have to get a job. He experimented but did not stick to one thing, and "5 years into college, one more year than I was supposed to go, I had to get a job because I just needed to survive" (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20). And in August 2026: "I got stuck in tutorial hell. I took all the courses. I tried and failed at seven different business models: Facebook ads, SEO, drop shipping, web design, digital art and a few more." He would not call them businesses but hobbies: "I would learn the high value skill and that felt good. I would start the LLC and that felt good. I would build the website and try to write the copy, and that felt good. But when it came to actually getting a customer, getting someone to pay me, I was nowhere to be found." "The missing piece was a strategy, but not just a strategy, the ability to think strategically, because how else are you going to create a strategy?" He admits that he still collects tactics "quite often right now" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

The repeated phrase "and that felt good" is the link to the dopamine argument above. Each step of the architect's work produced the reward of progress; the one step that would have tested whether there was progress (asking someone to pay) produced no reward in advance and was avoided. "I got my dopamine from feeling smart" is the same diagnosis in the first person.

The corpus shows how the explanation of the same failures changed:

| Period | What Koe says was missing | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 2021–2022 | Marketing and getting the offer in front of people; writing, marketing and sales "and how they all intertwine" | Justin Welsh conversation (2021); 2022 video, repeated in 2024 |
| 2023 | The "glue" skills that make any business model work; awareness that single skills had become commodities | 2023 videos |

The explanations are not exclusive; each names a deeper layer of the one before. Marketing is a skill; the glue is the set of skills; putting product in front of people is the act those skills serve; strategic thinking (Chapter 17) is the capacity that decides which acts matter. Two tensions surround the same story. First, in a 2023 video Koe recommended learning anything that gives you the dose of dopamine, including "websites, logos," while in 2024 he calls those things you do not need when starting; he gives no reason for the change of priority. Second, he reads the same failures in opposite ways in different videos: as a "big trap," and elsewhere as experiments that stacked irreplaceable skills; he defends shiny object syndrome in some years and blames it in others. The two readings can coexist if one distinguishes the businesses, which failed for lack of customers, from the skills, which accumulated. Chapter 15 develops the positive reading; this chapter needs the negative one, because it is the clearest case in the corpus of knowledge outpacing execution. The list of the seven failed businesses also changes from telling to telling (fitness channels, photography and digital art, Facebook ads and SEO agencies, dropshipping, blue-light glasses, wallets, a printing business, an animated-video agency); the number seven is stable, the composition and duration are not.

#### The way out: start, then learn

Against all of this Koe sets a short formula that he first tweeted in 2022 and has repeated in videos ever since. In the 2022 version: "If you want to learn faster, don't start learning. Outline a project, start building it out, learn along the way. Too many people get trapped in tutorial hell, stacking up useless knowledge as brain fog. Start, encounter a problem, seek specific knowledge to overcome it" (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). **Brain fog**, in his usage, is the useless knowledge accumulated in tutorial hell: information without application, soon forgotten. **Start then learn** is the name he gives the practice, with the variants "start, build, learn" and "build then learn."

The 2022 video gives the reason behind the formula. Koe calls himself a big fan of "start then learn." When you start something, even without knowing what you are doing, you encounter problems; "these problems will induce struggle and prime your brain to learn." So you do not learn blindly, without context, or from someone who is not teaching exactly what you are trying to do. The prescription: start building, then ask questions or seek specific advice and tutorials when a problem comes up (same source). He uses the phrase **specific knowledge** here in a sense of his own: knowledge sought to solve a concrete problem in your project, which cuts through what can be learned by experience. This is a different sense from Naval Ravikant's "specific knowledge" (knowledge that cannot be trained and arises from genuine curiosity), which Koe also uses elsewhere (Chapter 20); the two share a term but not a meaning.

The same video contains the story from which the formula came, a pair of contrasting experiences. When Koe first tried to learn Photoshop, he watched YouTube videos all the time, did not practice, and "didn't even have the app downloaded, and that of course led to nowhere." When he finally downloaded it and tried to create a design to test his knowledge, he could not create anything and had to go back to learning anyway. Later, learning web design, he knew he had to start "fooling around with the software" and try to build something. When he did not know how to do something (his example is a specific navigation design), he looked that up specifically, worked through the tutorial, and afterwards could replicate it on every other website he built (same source). He returns to the Photoshop failure in 2023: he tried to learn all of Photoshop before using it; when he started, he knew nothing and had to supplement with specific "create with me" tutorials (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30).

The contrast isolates the variable. In both cases he used tutorials. What differed was whether a problem of his own preceded the tutorial. Without one, the tutorial was entertainment; with one, it was a solution, and solutions stick.

The same 2022 video adds a warning about the question people ask instead of starting. "Asking vague questions like 'should I start?' is just procrastination from starting." If you do not know what the result will be after you start, you will not learn it from someone else; it comes from directly experiencing the result of starting (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). It is the asking-for-advice habit above, applied to the most basic decision.

#### The shitty rod and just-in-time learning

The most memorable illustration of start-then-learn in the corpus comes from a guest, Dickie Bush, in a 2023 conversation, and the attributions should be kept straight: the parable and the framework are Bush's; one phrase in the middle is Koe's.

**Complementary context:** Dickie Bush is a writer and co-founder of Ship 30 for 30, a writing program; he and Koe recorded several long conversations.

Bush's parable: two men start learning to fish. The first gets every fishing book, a custom fiber rod and a brand-new boat, and spends six months preparing. The second grabs a stick, ties on some line and a hot dog, and throws it in the lake. Day one: nothing. Day two: "I have to learn from this; I was probably in a bad spot, let's go try this spot." A nibble, but the rod breaks, so he gets a better rod. "Every single day he iterates." Here Koe interjects: "it's procrastination disguised as productivity." By the time the first fisherman feels ready and gets his boat to the lake, every fish is gone: the second "has sucked it dry," runs a business teaching others to fish and runs a restaurant, "all because he just got started." Bush describes his own habit: "anytime I want to start something new, in my head I go grab a shitty rod and start fishing, rather than go try to have this perfect plan, because that plan never works out and you don't understand what you need to execute that plan." It kept him from "nope, I need to read these 10 books"; "you're never going to feel ready" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). **Shitty rod** is Bush's image for starting with the minimum and improving by iteration. **Procrastination disguised as productivity** is Koe's interjection (the corpus marks the attribution as probable), and it is the best short name for everything this section describes.

The parable carries an argument about information that is easy to miss. The first fisherman's six months of preparation were not useless because preparation is bad, but because he could not know what he needed until he was at the lake. The second fisherman's broken rod told him exactly what to improve. Every iteration produced information that no book could have supplied, because it was information about his spot, his lake, his rod.

Bush gives the principle behind the parable a name: **just-in-time** versus **just-in-case** learning. Creating a product, or even an internet presence, takes many skills, and "too many people are like, oh, I need to go read these 10 books before I start doing anything." But "you have to have a lens to apply what you're learning; if you do not have that lens, you are not going to learn anything." Just-in-case learning prepares broadly before acting; just-in-time learning learns what the current action demands. He applies it to a future project of his own, a gym and real-estate venture: he will not read real estate, tax and finance books now, "because I don't have the lens yet, but the second I do, that is where I'm going to place all my attention" (same source). The idea of the lens is the same one Chapter 6 attributes to the goal; Bush's contribution is the timing. A lens exists only once you are doing the thing, so learning should be scheduled for then.

Koe gives the same principle a name of his own in 2024, by reframing a familiar idea. "Failure stacking makes you irreplaceable." He reframes skill stacking as failure stacking because "acquiring a skill puts you in the mindset that you just need to study and learn all day," whereas "acquiring a failure assumes that you are actively working toward a goal and gaining actual experience." The process: identify a goal (quit your job, find a partner, get fit); start with what you know; "you can only know what to learn when you hit a roadblock, so hit one fast"; then pursue your curiosity and let experience guide you into new paths to experiment with; everything will eventually overlap. His example is fitness: diet models to try, training programs to test, lifestyle changes to implement (**Source:** `The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md`, 2024-05-19). **Failure stacking** is his coinage. The phrase "hit one fast" turns just-in-time learning into an instruction: since only roadblocks tell you what to learn, the fastest learner is the one who reaches roadblocks soonest.

#### The learning sequences, 2023–2026

Between 2023 and 2026 Koe turned start-then-learn into a series of step-by-step procedures. They are versions of one idea, and laying them side by side shows both the stable core and the direction in which it moved.

In April 2023, describing how to actualize a goal: (1) study the fundamentals to gain clarity; (2) start a real-world project "so you don't get trapped in tutorial hell"; (3) build up the project until you discover a problem; (4) buy courses, coaching or research internet content to find the solution; (5) apply everything you learn until the project is completed. His example is health treated as a project (**Source:** `Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md`, 2023-04-30).

In January 2023 he had already given a version under the name **rapid building**, the combination of "rapid learning" and "rapid execution," the two skills that help implement everything (he credits his earlier video on learning fast). The steps: (1) choose a project to emulate that incorporates the skills you are trying to learn; (2) start building your own version, creating an outline and building "until you hit a wall"; (3) seek specific knowledge when you hit a wall, learning the skills needed for that specific real-world situation, which "cuts through fluff that can be learned through experience"; (4) as a bonus, teach along the way, because teaching identifies your knowledge gaps. This is for your "burst of building," when you feel the urge to go all in and have the clarity and education to start (**Source:** `The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md`, 2023-01-08).

In June 2023, under the name **start, build, learn**: you start the project; you build, "you begin stacking bricks with what you know," because you have already gained enough clarity to act ("or else you wouldn't have the clarity"; you have already consumed some information). When you hit a specific problem, you seek information (content, YouTube videos, mentors, coaches, courses) "that can help you solve that specific problem, to burst through that gap, reach the new level and then perceive your previous level from a state of self-reflection," and you keep correcting "until you see some form of objective success in something like business" (**Source:** `If You’re Lost - Here’s How You Find Clarity For The Future.md`, 2023-06-04). In the same video he names the failure this avoids: "People make the crucial mistake of learning and never starting, and then they get stuck in the clarity phase, and then it just turns into another lost phase; you don't enter the intensity phase." "You have to actually move; you have to use your mind to create in the physical world; you have to apply what you learn as you're building" (same source). The **lost, clarity and intensity phases** are his terms, and they connect this sequence to the curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle of section 14.2: learning that never starts cycles back to lost.

In July 2023: with your vision in mind, start building and learning; work on your project daily regardless of whether you know what you are doing. You do not know, because you have not registered the problem or hit it yet ("you're never going to find that if you don't start"). You can be a book expert, but if you don't do it, you will hit the same problem as if you knew nothing and have to research it anyway. "It's better to start then learn." The best way to learn: (1) encounter a real-world problem; (2) research specific information to solve it; (3) keep a steady flow of information from your consumption; (4) solve the problem; (5) enjoy the dopamine; (6) "let momentum carry you into a season of intensity" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). **Season of intensity** is his term for a period in which everything fits and hard work is enjoyed.

In August 2023 he restates the tweet as three steps (outline a project, start building, learn along the way) and adds the reason that tutorials work once a problem exists: with a problem in your head when watching a video, you see what the other person does, remember far more, and go back to fix your project. Hence the constant balance, "learning, building, learning, building": "you can't have output without input." He recommends a general education habit of ten to thirty minutes a day (a YouTube video, a podcast, part of a book, or a short summary such as the Shortform app) (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30). The phrase "you can't have output without input" should be read with Chapter 6's "learning is an output process, because output demands input": the two sentences are the same balance seen from opposite ends.

In 2024 the sequence adds a short initial study phase. Once you have a project that makes the goal tangible: (1) purchase a beginner-level course on the topic; (2) watch overview videos on YouTube teaching the fundamentals; (3) study them until you have clarity on what to do next; (4) do it; (5) when you encounter a problem, refer back to the relevant sections of the course or video; (6) research how to solve that problem directly; (7) watch tutorials of people building projects similar to yours and see how they overcome those problems. "Learning is problem solving, not hoarding as much knowledge as you can" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11; condensed again in `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). **Learning is problem solving** is his formula. The same February video repeats the tweet and explains the role of the outline: you outline the project so that you have "anchors" to map everyday experiences and what you consume onto (otherwise you would not notice them, nor live with meaning); as you build, once you hit a wall your mind is primed for learning "because learning comes from struggle, not memorization"; you research a specific tutorial (a Photoshop technique, for instance), go back and solve it. It takes longer to build the project, "but that's how you learn," and you get faster at building projects over time (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). In July 2024 he repeats the tweet once more, with the "6 years of results in 6 months" line (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

Two 2024 passages describe the same method from Koe's own practice. He did not study endless tutorials. He set a goal (working for himself), started a project and practiced the skills while self-educating. He studies the big picture and each skill's necessity for a specific goal such as financial freedom, gets a brief overview of how it helps and its principles, then learns the technical details by actually building, and only those he needs. This saves time compared with spending three to six months learning each skill's technical details. Most people skip building, "get trapped in tutorial hell and have nothing to show for their efforts after like 1, 2, 3 years" (**Source:** `The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md`, 2024-05-19). And in a video on deep work: because skill needs time to meet challenge, he always recommends "just starting; just start something; if you don't know how, you just start with what you know and then you learn on the go and get real-world experience; do not get trapped in tutorial hell." He calls his 2022 video "How I Remember Everything I Learn" one of his less popular but "most important" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). The first passage adds a distinction the procedures leave implicit: the big picture and principles are learned first, briefly; technical details are learned only when building demands them. Chapter 15 develops the priority of principles over tactics.

In 2025 the sequence enters a template Koe calls a life plan, and two of its steps restate the method. He opens with an earlier tweet: "When I want to learn something new, I don't start learning first. I outline a project first, then I learn exactly what I need and build out the project while learning. I found that this solidifies teachings into my brain much faster," which he calls the seed of the whole method (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). Step two, outline a project: the life plan is "your aim, your frame for learning" and "optimizes pattern recognition to an extent so you can notice the right information." "The best way to learn is to build a real-world project and only search for information when you need it; how much you learn is directly correlated with how much progress you make on the project." Endless tutorials "fill your mind with noise and chaos; most of that information goes to waste; it leads to overwhelm, anxiety, and slows down how fast you learn," and when it is time to build the project, "the only reason you're learning in the first place," you feel you learned nothing and search anyway. "Skip the tutorial phase; outline the project first." Then, in the same breath, he corrects himself: you don't necessarily need to skip tutorials; "you just need to start the project and hit that struggling point, and then when you go into the tutorial phase you actually pick up the information" (same source). Step three, start with what you know: "learning comes from struggle, not memorization." If you know nothing, at least take the first step: download the software and play around, try to create anything, "get your mind in a state where it's hungry to learn, otherwise you probably won't digest the information you search for." The loop: "you start, then you don't know what to do, you try and fail, you search for the answer or ask AI, you try to implement the answer, you repeat until the project is complete, and if you can't find the answer you ask an expert" (same source).

The self-correction inside step two is the clearest statement of Koe's position on tutorials, and the **struggling point** (also "hitting a wall") is the term on which it turns: the moment in a project when you cannot progress and the mind is therefore primed to learn. Tutorials are not the enemy; tutorials before the struggling point are.

Later in 2025 he restates the rule in the language of games. "In a game you learn by doing, not by studying endless tutorials or watching gameplay." Watching before you start playing "is not learning; that's entertainment." You can watch streamers all day and think you understand, but when you sit down your fingers do not know what keys to press or how to move the mouse; what you watched does not align with the reality of doing. Once you play first, once the game is part of your identity and daily practice, tutorials and gameplay let you pick up strategies, tactics or hacks that you know how to implement, and "the more strategies you collect from other people and combine into your own, that's what makes your own gameplay unique." "Start playing the game. Don't worry if you aren't absolutely confident; you figure out what you want to do by error correcting, and you can't correct an error that doesn't exist" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08). He calls this creating a **tutorial phase**: the phase of learning from others that comes after you have started playing. Once you are playing, supplement your mind with two things: the fundamentals, "because most success is not getting distracted from the fundamentals," and **specific solutions**, "an intentional search for an answer once you can't make progress by your own knowledge or intuition." "The main priority tasks for your day should be learning and building for 1 to 2 hours at a time. These do not happen in isolation" (same source). The sentence "you can't correct an error that doesn't exist" is the 2025 form of the Photoshop lesson and anticipates the error-correction model of Chapter 15.

In September 2025 he makes project-based learning the fourth of twelve rules and states the strongest form of the critique. You know what you don't want and have an idea of what you want; now acquire the skills and knowledge that bridge the gap. You move into the unknown "by having a way to order any potential chaos": personal projects. He repeats the principle (build a real project, search only when needed; learning correlates with progress on the project). Endless tutorials fill the mind with excess noise; most information is wasted because it is not applied, like reading a book and then stuttering when you try to explain it to a friend. Books and YouTube are not bad, "but that's not learning. That's entertainment," for another domain (mental clarity, calming down, stress relief); it can be learning, but very ineffective, and reading or watching to learn means "you're filling your mind with endless options. You're just creating more chaos" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). In October 2025, as step six of a dopamine detox, he repeats the principle almost word for word and ends with the 2025 slogan: "If you want to learn faster, skip the tutorial phase, outline a project first" (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26).

Two 2026 passages bring the sequence to its latest form. The tweet is rewritten: "Learning comes from struggle, not memorization. Start the project. No, don't watch 20 tutorials beforehand. Let it expose the gaps in your knowledge. Try to figure it out. Search for the answer when your mind is most likely to remember it. Publish the project, then repeat for life" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). The new elements are the timing of the search ("when your mind is most likely to remember it") and the last step, publishing, which links the sequence to section 14.5. And in a January 2026 video on building a one-person business with AI tools, he gives the method for learning to build software: (1) start tinkering and experimenting; (2) then watch YouTube tutorials; (3) have a project you are building toward (in his example, an evolved information product), and adapt tutorials that will not be about exactly that; (4) when stuck, ask AI how to do specific things, which he does himself for Photoshop and Framer because it pulls from help centers and forums. He warns that you will still have to learn, because AI cannot solve every problem from one sentence: "how can you get so much context into one sentence?" ("create this YouTube video": what kind, structure, pacing, tone, length, ideas? Will it assume, and will you put the result under your name? "That's not how this stuff works"). His example prompts: "give me a comprehensive guide on how to vibe code correctly," then a comprehensive guide to building a specific AI chat app with specific tabs on a specific topic and knowledge base, asking the AI to ask clarifying questions and telling it "I'm one singular person with this budget," and conversing from there (**Source:** `How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md`, 2026-01-03). He names Replit, Cursor and Claude Code as tools and says he has not tried the code tab inside Claude.

In August 2023, in a video on advice, Koe gave a six-step learning process (a conscious, meaningful goal; the habits that allow it; managing the pain of identity change; programming the mind with relevant information; experimenting with techniques; and, only last, seeking technical advice) and, later in the same video, a differently structured list (goal, then education and effort together, then experiment) (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23); both are discussed in Chapter 17 (sections 17.2 and 17.5). Chapter 15 develops both (sections 15.1 and 15.2). Across all of them the core stays the same (a project, learning when you hit problems, teaching or publishing); what changes is the name, the number of steps and, by 2026, the theoretical frame, which moves from dopamine and novelty to the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6.

Within the sequences shown in the table, the movement is a refinement: less study before starting (from "study the fundamentals" in 2023, to a beginner course in 2024, to "skip the tutorial phase" in 2025, to "don't watch 20 tutorials" in 2026), publication added at the end, and the tutorial moved to the point of being stuck, where in 2025 and 2026 AI increasingly takes its place. The reason Koe gives is constant: with a problem in your head, you retain what you see. Both ends can apply in different contexts: a short beginner course serves the fundamentals; what he rejects in every version is accumulating tutorials in place of building. One contradiction, however, remains unresolved: the oscillation, already noted in section 14.1, between recommending books ("devour one to three") and calling reading-to-learn "entertainment"; the September 2025 rule is the strongest form of the second position.

A 2024 book quote states the principle under all the sequences: "The source of learning is struggle, not memorization. Learning is the process of turning knowledge into understanding, and the lowest points of your life are those where a stackable lesson presents itself, but only if you apply it in reality." Projects frame what you learn: you pursue a goal, hit a roadblock and frame your mind from the accompanying problem; only then can you learn something useful that can be instantly applied (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). **Learning comes from struggle, not memorization** is the principle as it appears in *The Art of Focus*; section 14.4 develops what it means. The distinction between knowledge and understanding that it introduces returns in Chapter 17.

#### Starting when you don't feel like it

All the sequences begin with "start," and Koe addresses the obvious objection: what if you do not feel like starting? In the 2025 life-plan video he calls this step 3.5. "The Zeigarnik effect is a psychological phenomenon where people remember unfinished tasks more than completed tasks," so once a project has unfinished tasks, motivation to resume comes more easily. But how do you start from scratch? He cites a trick from a YouTuber who makes videos about learning, transcribed as "Justin Sun" and probably Justin Sung, called the "Zeigarnik effect squared": invoke the effect "by starting easy tasks before getting started on your project, because if you start those tasks then they'll be unfinished and you'll want to continue working on them, and they lead naturally into the task that you need to work on." His examples are setting up or cleaning the desk, making coffee and sitting down, or any ritual or routine. When he does not want to write his newsletter, he goes into the outline, promises himself to just look at it, read it, type some ideas, "and then eventually I hit something" and keeps writing. For the gym: "I'm going to go walk for 5 minutes, and if I don't feel like doing anything here I'm going to leave, and most of the time you won't leave." He adds a condition: it works "if you have clarity on what you're doing," that is, if a hierarchy of goals tells you what the next task is. If you do not know what to do, study: bring AI to the gym and ask what exercise to do next (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

**Complementary context:** The Zeigarnik effect is named after the psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik, whose experiments in the 1920s suggested that interrupted tasks are recalled better than completed ones; later replications have been mixed. Justin Sung is a learning coach who publishes videos on study methods.

**Zeigarnik effect squared** is a third-party term that Koe adapts. The trick does not make starting unnecessary; it makes the first step so small that starting is easy, and relies on the pull of an open loop to carry the person into the real task. It is the behavioral counterpart of the 2025 instruction to "at least take the first step: download the software and play around."

### 14.4 Build to Learn

Section 14.3 described the way out of tutorial hell as a procedure: start, hit a problem, learn what it demands. This section presents the principle behind the procedure, which is the central idea of the chapter and the one Koe has formulated in the most ways: **build to learn**. It explains what he means by it, the reasons he gives for why it works, where he says it stops applying, what a project is made of, the loop that connects building to teaching, the guitar method that shows the principle at the smallest scale, and the Build Teach Earn method that shows it at the scale of a career.

#### "Don't learn to build, build to learn"

The slogan appears in 2024 as the third step of a process for people who feel lost: "learn and build for clarity," because "we have problem, solution; now we need clarity, and then we need people to round out the value equation. Don't learn to build, build to learn." You have already chosen a problem (fitness, finances, freedom, or anything preventing the life you want); "with that problem in mind, laser in on one big goal" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). **Build to learn**, **learn as you build** and **build as you learn** are variants of one coined term: build a project so that learning has something to be applied to. The reference to the "value equation" places the step inside a larger argument developed in Part XI (problem, solution, clarity, people); here it matters that clarity comes from building, not before it.

Earlier in 2024 he states the principle in its harshest form. "If you aren't building, you aren't learning. If you aren't actively applying what you learn to a problem sitting in your mind, you are simply stacking useless knowledge as soon-to-be-forgotten brain fog." When starting something new, "you only need the fundamentals; you don't need any fancy tactics," focusing on one specific thing to learn and overcome. "Learn" here covers anything: career skills, strategies for better relationships, getting fit, because for any goal you must first learn how to achieve it. And the project can be anything: "your body, a Photoshop art piece, a newsletter." What goal are you achieving, what is a big problem in your life, what goal overcomes it? (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). The phrase "a problem sitting in your mind" defines what application means: not a vague intention to use the knowledge someday, but a live problem the knowledge is applied to now. The instruction to begin with fundamentals rather than "fancy tactics" connects to Chapter 12's claim that most results come from repeating the basics.

In 2023 he gives the principle as the answer to a question about retention: you cannot learn everything that crosses your mind; you must remember "the important things that actualize your vision; that's how you learn everything." To do that, "you must learn as you build or build as you learn" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2023-07-25). The sentence quietly redefines "learning everything": not covering everything, but retaining everything that matters, where what matters is decided by the vision.

In 2025 he gives the principle the form of a correction of ordinary language. Progress comes from investing energy into a series of projects; "you aren't interested in a project until you invest energy into it." "You don't learn skills, you build projects"; "skills, like degrees, are practically useless in isolation." "If you want to receive, you must give": keeping knowledge in your head "is a surefire way to become bitter about how smart you are and how little people care." Then he switches to the vocabulary of games. "A project is a quest in your game"; building puts experience into your traits, profession, character and abilities; you learn techniques to solve a problem and achieve a result. A project is something you build that enables **interest-based education**, his term for learning that arises from your own problem and project. It must be a stepping stone toward your vision and solve the problem you identified (where you are not content); if that is your body, the gym: "your project is your body" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12). **Quest** is his term, from the life-as-a-game metaphor of Chapter 10. The claim that interest follows investment, rather than preceding it, repeats the lesson of the curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle in section 14.2: meaning can come after effort.

In 2026 he adds a metaphor of his own, also from games. Arguing against doubling down on what you already know as "the one true way," he says "learning is kind of like an expansion pack for your thinking." And he specifies what he means by learning: "I'm not talking about studying or memorizing facts; I'm talking about how to actually learn, which is by building projects, failing, getting feedback, repeating, iterating, doing it in the real world. That's how you make your life better" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22). **Expansion pack**, borrowed from video games where an expansion adds new territory to an existing game, describes learning as an extension of the thinking you already have, not a replacement of it. The list that follows (build, fail, get feedback, repeat, iterate) is the cybernetic loop of Chapter 6 in plain words.

#### Become a builder

The earliest form of the principle, from the 2022 video on remembering what you learn, is the instruction to "become a builder." It is introduced as the answer to a question about expertise. "In most domains, the true experts rarely need guidance." They used science, theory and advice and backed it with action to reach the intermediate stage. After that, the expert stage is "usually based off of intuition," because they understand the whole domain so well that they know what to do next. The question this raises is how to balance practice with theory (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). Koe's answer: "by becoming a builder," which means "always having something to build, or always having a project in alignment with the thing that you are learning." Learning then happens by first having a project (or an intention), and supplementing it with specific tutorials or specific knowledge only when you encounter a problem or do not know how to do something. That is "when it locked into my brain"; once he had solved a problem with a tutorial, he could "almost replicate that every single time after" (same source). The Photoshop and web-design stories of section 14.3 are his illustration.

**Builder** is a common word with a sense of its own in his vocabulary: in this 2022 sense, the person who always has a project aligned with what they are learning. (Elsewhere he gives it a broader meaning, "if you are not a builder, you are going to be built over, or you are going to build for someone else," which connects it to the book's title.) The path he describes has three stages: theory backed by action gets you to intermediate; intuition built on understanding of the whole domain marks the expert; and building is how the passage from the first to the second happens. Chapter 15 develops the stages of mastery.

#### Why building works: three reasons from 2022

The same 2022 video gives three reasons why building makes learning stick. The third, "start then learn" (problems induce struggle, which primes the brain to learn), was presented in section 14.3. The first two are developed here.

The first reason he calls **forced synchronicity**. "Novelty, pattern recognition and dopamine are crucial for learning": when you make a discovery, noticing a pattern or seeing how one thing can be used in another area, the dopamine and excitement make your brain ready to understand it. Knowledge is retained when it aligns with survival. A meaningful project is something you want to "survive" (to progress, develop and live), so with it in your awareness you notice things that align with it and can be applied to grow it: "when you have a project that is meaningful, you will notice things in order to survive that project" (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

He illustrates it with the image that recurs across the corpus, in a 2022 form with a specific turn. An ancestor walking past the same bush over and over is conditioned to see that it does not aid survival: no fruit, no berries, "it was just a bush." When they notice something new, like berries growing, it catches their attention, and they are far more likely to remember it next time if they need it for survival. But if the novelty were just a new leaf, with nothing to apply it to, they would not remember it. The ancestor used berries for physical survival. In the modern age, Koe says, "a lot of what we're trying to do with information and ideas is we're trying to survive the notion or the concept of self," the idea of what we are, which causes pain and suffering. A meaningful project redirects this survival-noticing mechanism toward something productive (same source).

The berry bush appears in Chapters 3, 6, 10 and 11 to explain conceptual survival, attention, curiosity and dopamine; this 2022 version contributes two elements specific to learning. The first is the new leaf: novelty alone is not enough; novelty is remembered only when it has something to be applied to. That is the whole theory of build-to-learn in one image. The second is the diagnosis of what the mechanism does when no project is present: it defends the self-concept (**survive the concept of self**, his phrase, close to the "conceptual survival" of Chapter 3). A project gives the survival machinery something better to protect. The story is a metaphor offered as a just-so explanation, not as evidence about ancestral cognition, and should be read as Koe uses it: as a way to make the mechanism of relevance intuitive. The word "synchronicity" is used loosely, for the experience of noticing relevant things everywhere once a project is in mind, and "forced" means that this experience is produced on purpose by choosing a project.

The second reason he calls the **law of inspired action**. He quotes William Faulkner: "I only write when inspiration strikes; fortunately it strikes at nine every morning." Everyone acts better when inspired: before training chest at the gym you might watch a chest-motivation or workout video so form and motivation are top of mind; before writing, he reads authors, ideas and writing that inspire him and give him ideas. With a project, "you are enticed to learn in accordance with it, because it will inspire you to keep that project going." If you consume something that helps your project just before working on it, or build that consumption into your routine (he refers to his "fill, empty, use" framework, Chapter 13), it keeps the momentum of the project's growth. If you are fully committed to a business, you will naturally gravitate toward information that helps you build it, provided you are not distracted and actively eliminate distractions, "but you have to start building the damn business in order to apply those teachings" (same source). The Faulkner quotation supports the idea that inspiration can be scheduled and engineered: by consuming inspiring material before working on a project, as part of a routine, you put yourself into the inspired state on demand.

**Complementary context:** William Faulkner (1897–1962) was an American novelist and Nobel laureate. The quip about inspiration striking at nine every morning is widely attributed to him in various forms; its exact original source is uncertain.

The law of inspired action reverses the usual relation between consumption and work. Ordinarily, consumption is what one does instead of working; here it becomes the ignition for work, and the project decides what is consumed. The condition in the last sentence is essential: without a project already under way, the same consumption is just the information gathering of section 14.3.

#### Without a project, all information is noise

Several passages from 2022 to 2025 state the corollary: information needs a destination. The bluntest is from 2022: "The first thing you do when you start learning is, one, you create a project; you don't just start learning." His example: start a Twitter account, then educate yourself on what gets engagement (copywriting, persuasive writing, how social media and the platform work); buy a Twitter course, or study and emulate others. "You don't just start learning, you create a project so you can apply the information to it" (**Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`, 2022-06-25). The Twitter account is a project chosen because it creates a reason to learn a particular skill, copywriting, and a place to apply it immediately.

In 2023 he connects the corollary to his own content. If you do not have a habit of self-education, building products or systems, and using writing to clarify your thoughts, "you are delaying the inevitable; you need to build something." His best content came from periods when he was building a product, writing his book, or "just having a lens to view everyday experiences from and extract the lesson." "If you don't have a project or something to build, or just a goal to apply what you are learning to, all of the information going into your mind is noise; you're not able to filter out the signal" without a frame of reference. And if you build for someone else who assigns projects you do not care to learn about, the two do not match: "you have to build your own thing and learn your own thing, and that's how you create this flow of information that makes life meaningful" (**Source:** `Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md`, 2023-09-17). **Signal from noise** is his phrase for what a conscious goal lets you separate in each piece of information. The last sentence adds something the other passages lack: the project must be your own, because an assigned project gives a lens you do not care to look through.

In a 2025 conversation with Sahil Bloom he gives the shortest version: "Once you identify a problem, if you don't know how to solve it, you need to learn something. When I go about learning, the way I think about learning is starting with a project. So starting with the problem and actually just trying to start solving it, because then what that does is it frames your mind with that problem. So you can start to identify what's necessary in the things that you're learning" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). "Frames your mind" is the lens of Chapter 6 again, now produced by a problem one has started to solve.

#### The limit: where your life is the project

Koe does not claim that every kind of learning requires an external project, and one 2024 passage states the limit precisely. As step two of a learning process: "if you aren't building, you aren't learning." Without actively applying what you learn (writing it down, adding it to the outline or project, doing it) you are stacking "useless, soon-to-be-forgotten brain fog." Reading without a project is not learning: "you're starting to understand things you already know; it's leisure." Then the exception: "reading a business book without having a business is kind of dumb," but "reading a philosophy book works in all situations, because it's your life that you're applying it to; your life is the project in big-picture domains like philosophy" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21).

The exception makes the principle more precise rather than weaker. Build-to-learn applies wherever the knowledge is knowledge *for doing* something specific (a business, a skill, a piece of software). In big-picture domains, the knowledge is *for living*, and the project to which it is applied is the reader's own life, which is always under way. This is also the most defensible way to reconcile Koe's oscillation about books (section 14.1): technical books without a project are entertainment; philosophical books always have a project. The definition of reading without a project as "leisure," "starting to understand things you already know," is a strong claim: it implies that reading without application mostly confirms what one already thinks, which is the confirmational dopamine of section 14.3.

#### The philosophy of the Builder

In his 2023 book *The Art of Focus*, Koe gathers these ideas under the heading "the philosophy of the Builder." In the summary video he describes the pattern in his own life: he always carved out time to build something of his own; schoolwork, client work and assigned job projects were necessary but unfulfilling. "My life was the meta project," built through personal and business projects. Projects frame attention and learning: with focus on what you build, all information is filtered through that lens. "The source of learning is struggle, not memorization": encounter a problem, discover its solution, integrate it. To identify a problem you need a goal; to solve it, a solution; to create a solution, a project to invest attention in "for the next one to six months" (**Source:** `The Cure To A Mediocre Life (Become Multidimensionally Jacked).md`, 2023-08-30). **Meta project**, in this sense, is his life as the project that contains all his other projects (elsewhere the term also names the personal brand as the project that houses all projects that become products). **Philosophy of the Builder** is the name in the book. The chain goal → problem → solution → project gives the project its place in the hierarchy of Part IV: it is the vehicle that turns a goal into something one can work on for months.

In 2024 he refers back to the principle from the book: "building a project and teaching as you build is the best way to learn something; studying and getting trapped in tutorial hell is not learning something; learning comes from struggle, not memorization; it's a principle in my book" (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). The phrase "teaching as you build" anticipates section 14.5.

What does "struggle" mean here? The corpus does not give a separate definition, but the passages consistently use it for the state of trying to do something one cannot yet do: hitting a wall, the struggling point. Learning is the movement from that state to the state of being able to do it, and "memorization" is the alternative that skips the struggle and therefore skips the learning. The same principle, in Koe's 2024 book-summary formulation quoted at the end of section 14.3, says that learning "turns knowledge into understanding," and that the lowest points of life offer "a stackable lesson," "but only if you apply it in reality."

Koe supports the philosophy with two outside sources, and the attributions matter. In 2025, opening a section on how to read, he quotes a line he attributes to Aristotle: "The purpose of knowledge is action, not knowledge." He returns to it later in the same video ("using knowledge for action, not knowledge, as Aristotle said") to frame what he calls the digestion layer of reading, connecting the dots through action (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). The attribution is the author's; later in the same video he qualifies this emphasis on action with the idea that reading is about changing one's mind (section 14.2).

**Complementary context:** A similar sentence, "the great end of life is not knowledge but action," is usually traced to the Victorian scientist Thomas Henry Huxley; the attribution of Koe's version to Aristotle is common online but has no clear source. Aristotle did distinguish theoretical from practical knowledge and held that the purpose of ethical inquiry is to act well.

In 2026 he cites Daniel Schmachtenberger, who "talks about this quite a bit." Schmachtenberger did not go to school; his parents taught him to read, write and operate in society according to their values. Beyond that, "learning is the process of life": learning is what you do when pursuing and building something meaningful; you learn what you need to learn, and become "10 times more articulate" that way (**Source:** `The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md`, 2026-07-27). The corpus classifies the use as adapted: Koe reads Schmachtenberger's unschooling as a case of learning what one needs while building something meaningful.

**Complementary context:** Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher known for his work on the "metacrisis" and civilizational risk; he has described his own homeschooled, largely self-directed education. Chapter 20 discusses his critique of hyper-specialization.

Two further principles complete the philosophy, both about the limits of what any teacher can give. In 2023: "Watching this video is not enough; watching anything or reading anything is not enough." Start a project, encounter real-world problems, and "let reality teach you what watching this video can't," because there are "thousands" of minor nuances that you pick up consciously and subconsciously as you progress, none of which can be illustrated in a course, a piece of content or a book. Practice at every moment (**Source:** `Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md`, 2023-04-09). And in the summary of *The Art of Focus*, as the third of its pillars: "You cannot take ideas, opinions or advice as law without the filter of direct experience, even if that advice is my own." Experience is gained by identifying problems in your life, solving them and making your process more efficient by evolving the systems your mind operates on. With ample experience, the feedback loop of focus and energy tightens and results compound (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18).

The clause "even if that advice is my own" turns the principle back on the teacher. It is consistent with the warnings of section 14.3 that include "even this video," and it states the epistemic status Koe claims for his own teaching: hypotheses to be tested, not law. This position later radicalizes: in 2025 he calls mistakes "the only source of truth," and everything not derived from one's own mistakes "theory, a map, a conjecture," which collides with the value he gives elsewhere to reading, studying and emulating others. It is an unresolved contradiction, possibly reconcilable by contexts (others "can only show you how they do it"; experience is the filter, not the only source). Chapter 15 takes up mistakes as a compass.

#### What a project is made of

Koe's project has a structure, and he gives it in several versions. Chapter 9 presented the Clarity Catalyst, his 2024 name for the project seen as the thing that tells pattern recognition what to look for. The versions below are the learning-side specifications of that object.

In April 2024, as the sixth of twelve rules: "Learning comes from struggle, not memorization. You need a series of tangible products to build that will actualize your vision, so turn your goals into projects": architect and outline them, determine milestones, set deadlines, map out areas of research. "Build then learn: start the project, expose your lack of knowledge and skill, and use that as a reference point for your education" (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). The last sentence defines the relation between building and education precisely: the project's exposure of what you lack is the reference point, the syllabus, for what you study.

In February 2024 he lists the components of a project (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11):

| Component | What it does, in Koe's words |
|---|---|
| Outline | A place to note ideas from everyday life that fill the project |
| Milestones | Direction and clarity on next steps; "like goals," a hierarchy: what will I hit in a week, a month, a year |
| Real-world deadline | "Forces you to act or else your survival takes a hit" |
| Experimentation | Trial and error, so that failures turn into lessons |

His example of the deadline is his own: he must publish newsletters and videos every Saturday and Sunday and launch products on the dates he chooses; if something is not done or learned by then, he is in trouble. The four components map onto the book's earlier machinery: the outline is the anchor of Chapter 9; milestones are the hierarchy of goals of Chapter 8; the deadline is the forcing pressure of Chapter 12; experimentation is the method of Chapter 15. The deadline's justification, "or else your survival takes a hit," links back to forced synchronicity: a project one must "survive" focuses attention.

In December 2023 he puts the same idea as the third step of becoming a different person in six months, with the strongest statement about reading in the corpus. "Reading books is not learning, watching tutorials is not learning; you learn by building a project and facing reality." "Projects are goals that you can measure and iterate." Turn a few one-year or monthly goals into projects: ideate a way to build something meaningful (you must do work), write milestones, create an outline in a notes app or notebook, and brain-dump ideas (he mentions Cortex, his "second brain" software). Do this for mental, physical, financial and spiritual development. It primes the mind for pattern recognition, so you get "more good dopamine out of life, because the things that you are perceiving have somewhere to go"; every book and conversation becomes useful, and you feel the need to add inspiration to your notes or project (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The corpus marks the tension in this passage: "reading books is not learning" is stronger here than in videos where Koe treats intentional reading as a key input. The second half of the passage shows the reconciliation he implies: once a project exists, books become useful because what you read "has somewhere to go."

An earlier, simpler version from 2022 shows the outline at work. Always have a project (a side project, a hobby, something meaningful) and treat everything as a project, because "a project implies that you are researching in order to add to that project." If this video were a project, he says, you would outline it somewhere you can flesh it out (Notion or notes): a potential title, introduction, body, conclusion, a bit of what to write. Then you research to build a credible argument: pull quotes from books, watch YouTube videos. Now you are intentionally learning and consuming to add to the project, and you can open it on your phone and note ideas when they come (**Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`, 2022-11-06). The definition "a project implies research" explains why the outline matters: an outline is a structure with gaps, and gaps direct search. Chapter 16 develops the note-taking systems that hold such outlines.

#### The project-based learning loop

The summary of *The Art of Focus* gives the most complete loop connecting building and teaching. "To learn information with efficiency, don't start learning first." The steps (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18):

1. Create a project for the goal you want to achieve.
2. Create a path or system to achieve that goal.
3. Start building the project in the real world with what you know.
4. Teach people about what you are building (friends, family or internet peers).
5. Identify knowledge gaps when you cannot build further or cannot articulate your thoughts.
6. Study deep knowledge to fill those gaps.
7. Return to building and teaching; repeat forever.

He says the process uses novelty, pattern recognition and self-experimentation to maximize the flow of information you experience. The book quote that accompanies it states the diagnosis it answers: "Most people learn and learn and learn just to start over when it comes time to build, because they realize that everything they've learned has no direct use in the project they began; they don't have a goal-specific lens to interpret their learnings from" (same source).

The loop is worth reading for its two sources of **knowledge gaps**, his term for what you do not know, revealed by trying to build or to teach. Building reveals gaps of capability (you cannot build further); teaching reveals gaps of understanding (you cannot articulate your thoughts). Study comes only at step six, and it is "deep knowledge," not the general overview that most learners start with. The phrase "goal-specific lens" connects the loop to Chapter 6. Step four (teaching what you are building) is the bridge to section 14.5, and its position in the sequence matters: teaching happens during building, not after mastery.

#### Start with the purpose: the guitar method

In 2025 Koe gives the principle its most concrete illustration, from music. "If I want to learn how to play the guitar, I don't start learning, I start playing. Because the entire purpose of playing the guitar is to play a song." Step one is to start with the purpose: what are you trying to achieve? Then comes the dilemma: you have a song in mind and cannot play it. "That's when you start learning in the traditional sense, but now your learning is focused": not an entire textbook, just the little technique needed for the next step. You cannot read the first note? Look up a cheat sheet for sheet music; then how to play that note on the guitar, a certain finger placement; it "sounds decent." Repeat trial (experimenting) and error (correcting, or learning the specific knowledge for your unique goal), and you will probably play the song, even if terribly, likely within two weeks (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10). **Start with the purpose** is his name for this step: begin from what the skill is for, and learn only the technique the next step requires.

The second song is easier, because you already have a group of techniques. "Let's say you learn the next song in a week, and then the next one in 3 days, and then you can learn one song a day. By that point, you have most of what it takes," "80 to 90% of the fundamentals of playing the guitar." After that, it is a matter of showing up daily and slowly increasing the challenge over one, two, three years (same source). The figures are illustrative ("let's say"), and the "two weeks" belongs to the substantial-progress scale that Koe himself distinguishes from mastery (section 14.1). What the illustration shows is an acceleration: each song adds techniques that the next song reuses, so the time per song falls. This is **technique stacking**, which Chapter 15 develops as the mechanics of skill.

A 2026 version tells the same story with different details and a blunter summary. "Do you immediately go and study music theory? Probably not. You simply choose the song that you want to play, and then you learn the first chord, and then the second, and then the third," and eventually you pick up other things: "Hmm, this doesn't sound right... I need to tune the guitar... add a capo." You learn one song, then the next, then feel confident enough to try your own simple song, "and then years later, you're a master. That is how you learn." His summary: "it's literally just do things. For all the people that want a bullet point summary, go and do things" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). The last line is aimed at the summary-seekers of section 14.3. The detail about the capo and tuning illustrates how problems reveal knowledge one did not know one needed: nobody studies tuning before noticing that something sounds wrong.

Koe applies the method to the creative software that most of his audience uses. Photoshop, After Effects and Premiere, he says in 2025, "increase your ability to take your mind and put it into reality." Most people watch an hour-long tutorial on "every single tool that lives in the Photoshop sidebar," ninety-five percent of which they will never apply. Instead, start with what you are trying to do: create an image, for example put a tree on a different background. Narrow the learning to removing a tree's background; watching a few videos shows many ways to do it; experiment, and find that a particular select-and-mask technique works better for a tree (a complex shape) than for a plain object. Once done, it solidifies: "I know this technique exists. I can do it that much faster the next time." "Selecting and masking objects gets you like 50% of the way through Photoshop." As techniques stack, you save countless hours "learning nonsense" and can create most things (**Source:** `How To Master Any Skill Fast (Give Yourself 2 Weeks).md`, 2025-08-10). The 50 percent figure is an impression, not a measurement; its function is to say that a small number of techniques covers most practical use.

In 2026 he tells the same story with After Effects, which he describes as "a bunch of buttons that mean nothing." The first thought is to watch a tutorial on using After Effects, and then "you're no better off: 'Where is everything again?'", and you get stuck thinking "I need to learn this software inside and out before I even create anything." Instead "you need something to actually build, a vision or an image of it in your head." "You need to start the project with the knowledge you have, and if you don't have knowledge, then you find a specific tutorial that is as similar to the project you want to create as it can be." His example is a minimalist line animation of the kind he used to post on Instagram: find a tutorial of that or something close, "so that I can directly apply what I'm learning in the moment." "Most of the tutorial will be useless, but you'll be filtering it out"; repeat "until you stack enough techniques" (not every tool in the sidebar) "to get the result that you want" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09).

The software examples refine the guitar method in one respect. They show that a tutorial is not avoided but chosen: the best tutorial is the one most similar to your project, and even then most of it is filtered out. The project is the filter.

#### Turning yourself into the project

The 2022 video that introduced the reasons for building ends by widening the idea. The chain: building helps you retain a lot; teaching is another modality that solidifies knowledge further; so combine the two "to create a life of novelty, synchronicity and serendipity," increasing both the knowledge you retain and your enjoyment of life, by creating a lifestyle conducive to learning what you want to learn. How? "By turning you into the project." Building a project does not only apply to external things like a Photoshop design; "if you treat your entire life as a project that you build and grow and cut down and rebuild and just have fun with along the way, then life becomes that much better," because everything you learn can be applied to your life (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). **Turning you into the project** is his phrase; it is the same move as the 2024 exception ("your life is the project in big-picture domains"), made two years earlier, and the same as the "meta project" of *The Art of Focus*. The verbs "build and grow and cut down and rebuild" describe a project that is never finished, which is how Chapter 10 describes an infinite game.

A 2023 passage adds why the self-as-project cannot stay private. "If you don't pursue a better life for yourself, you will fail. A better life demands the habits of learning, building and distributing." These are the main things to do "as a way to provide value so that you can get what you want": if you want something from someone, you must give them something; "that's just value exchange in general, that's communication, that's everything." "You can't do this alone. You have to develop yourself alone, but then you have to bring that self into the world so that you can exchange with other people." These have to become habits, part of everyday life (**Source:** `Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md`, 2023-09-17). The triad **learning, building, distributing** is the shortest summary of Parts VI to X of this book. The sentence "you have to develop yourself alone, but then you have to bring that self into the world" sets up both the Build Teach Earn method below and section 14.5.

#### The Build Teach Earn method

The fullest application of build-to-learn to a career is the **Build Teach Earn method**, which Koe presented in December 2023 for "absolute beginners." He had several skills and a freelancing business before becoming a creator; the method, he says, helped him succeed much faster and "bakes in every single thing you need to earn a living as a creator" (**Source:** `How Intelligent Creators Will Make Money In 2024 (The Build Teach Earn Method).md`, 2023-12-03). Its steps:

| Step | Purpose | What it involves |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Build | Learn high-value skills to earn with | Self-educate while building something tangible: your own creator business |
| 2. Teach | Enhance your learning and build an audience | Teach what you know, what you are learning and what you have done; teaching reveals the gaps |
| 3. Earn | Help others with what you have learned | With 5,000–10,000 followers, offer your skill set to businesses; your niche is one problem solved uniquely |
| 3.5. (alternative) | An equally viable route | Create a product or service you would buy, use and benefit from; "sell what's already selling" |
| 4. Earn more | Leverage your results and your audience | Productize your results |

The build step applies the principle of this section to the creator business itself. "The best way to learn is to self-educate so you have knowledge, as you build something tangible so you have experience." Koe's argument for why a creator business is the right thing to build rests on an observation: few creators realize that most businesses are the same nowadays: "they're all content companies, they're all media companies," acquiring customers online through brand, content, product and promotions. So the skills that make your creator business succeed can be offered to other businesses as a freelance service or a digital product. "Your creator business is a business"; running it means running an entire business as one person, and you need every skill that makes a business work. He then lists how each skill is learned by building: graphic design as you make your profile picture, banner, website images and thumbnails; copywriting and content writing as you build landing pages, website, social content and newsletters; social media ("yes, it's a skill") as you build a following, a network and authority; marketing and sales as you build your product or service; advertising and promotion as you promote to get buyers (same source). **Content companies / media companies** is his phrase for this view of modern business. The list is build-to-learn at the scale of a skill stack: each piece of the brand is a small project that teaches one high-value skill.

The teach step is section 14.5 in miniature. "The second best way to learn is to teach what you know, what you're learning and what you've done to achieve a profitable or meaningful goal." When you teach you will struggle a lot and think, "I'm not good enough, and you'd be correct, you aren't; neither is anyone." Rather than seeing that as the problem of not knowing enough, see it as an opportunity to learn and fill the holes: "that's what teaching does, it reveals the gaps in your knowledge." People write it off when "it is literally the only opportunity to learn and solidify that learning staring you in the face." What to teach: your skill set, your mindset, complex ideas made sense of from your point of view, and your polarizing beliefs (his example: "everyone should be a carnivore, and why") (same source). The reply to "I'm not good enough" is characteristic: it concedes the premise and denies the conclusion. Nobody is good enough, so inadequacy is not a reason to wait; it is the material of learning.

The earn step rests on a comparison. With a valuable following of 5,000 to 10,000 people you are "fully qualified to help people with your skill set, because most people don't have those results"; "most company accounts don't have 5 to 10,000 followers." What you offer is not only growing a following but everything that led to it: you have a wide range of knowledge you can focus on one problem a business faces, "and that's what you'd call a niche, so to say," using your skill set to solve it "in a unique, irreplaceable way." His examples of what you will have learned: content writing, thread writing, profile design, email opt-in pages, newsletter writing (same source). The definition of a niche as one problem solved uniquely with a whole skill set connects to Chapter 19's "you are the niche."

He closes with a heuristic for beginners who have nothing yet to teach. He told them to package and sell business and marketing services because those are what is building their business, and because they are very beginner-friendly: you do not need to be an expert in any topic before practicing them, and "eventually you're going to need those skills either way to build your business and sell whatever it is you actually want to sell" (a fitness product, a spirituality product, anything). If you are just starting out and have not achieved any goal, solved any problem, or do not have interests or skills, follow step three (same source). The heuristic resolves a chicken-and-egg problem: the beginner has no subject to teach, but building the brand is itself a subject, and the skills it teaches are the ones every later product will need. The numbers (5,000–10,000 followers) are thresholds he proposes from experience, not rules.

### 14.5 Teaching Is Learning; Learning in Public

The loop of section 14.4 had a step that the procedures of section 14.3 mostly lacked: teach what you are building. This last section explains why Koe considers teaching the strongest form of learning, what he takes from the psychology of the protégé effect and from the Feynman technique, and why he insists that teaching be done in public. Teaching in public turns out to do three things at once: it consolidates what you learn, it exposes your work to the market's feedback, and it gathers the people who will later be your audience and customers. The section ends with the personal brand as the practice ground for every skill and with Koe's defense of teaching what you do.

#### Learn < build < teach

In the 2022 video on remembering what you learn, the fifth step is "teach what you know," and Koe illustrates it with a simple graphic of circles whose white outline represents how much you learn from each activity. The transcript describes the graphic only partially, so its exact shape cannot be reconstructed, but its content is clear. Starting, or learning alone, teaches you some. Building teaches more, because you have something to apply the learning to, you encounter problems and you seek specific knowledge. Teaching "solidifies it that much better," because "you're forced to articulate your understanding of it," and you can identify the knowledge gaps in what you are trying to teach. It will be bad at first: his first YouTube video and all his early teaching were bad, but he identified what was bad, filled in the blanks, came back and retaught it "from a better place." This, he says, is known as the protégé effect: learning by teaching (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

The ordering **learn < build < teach** is the backbone of the chapter. Each stage contains the one before: you cannot build without learning something, and you cannot teach what you are building without building it. Each adds a source of feedback: building adds the resistance of reality (the project fails), teaching adds the resistance of articulation (you cannot explain it) and, in public, the resistance of other minds. The admission that his own early teaching "sucked" matters for the argument, because it removes the precondition most people place on teaching (being good first). Teaching badly is how one becomes able to teach well, and how one learns along the way.

#### The protégé effect: the teacher learns more than the student

Koe returns to the protégé effect in several videos between 2022 and 2026, and the attributions should be kept clear: the effect is a finding he takes from the psychology of learning, without naming any researcher; the applications are his.

In 2024 he reads a definition: the protégé effect is "a psychological phenomenon where teaching, pretending to teach or preparing to teach information to others helps a person learn that information." When you teach, "you pay closer attention when you learn and identify knowledge gaps that help you learn faster" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). In July 2024 he reads the same definition and builds on it. He recommends teaching in public as a personal brand, "your public resume or the modern resume" (your social profile), which attracts high-paying opportunities as you learn. You do not need to know something before writing about it: "you write, so you teach, and you understand it further." "I learn things 10 times faster because I teach them to other people." His example is domestic: at night he explains animation, art or a difficult concept from a book to his girlfriend; doing so shows him what he does not know, which creates a new problem that frames his perception, so he picks up the information that fills the gap much faster. Doing this online makes it part of your life, and you evolve faster; it is "not really a career, it's the collapse of work and life into one" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). **Public resume** (also "modern resume") is his term for a public profile that shows what you can do and attracts work. **The collapse of work and life into one** is his phrase for what happens when learning in public becomes a way of living rather than a job.

In 2026 he gives the most technical account of the mechanism. In learning science, "the protégé effect" shows that "students who teach material retain it better than students who merely study it." The reason, as he gives it: the cognitive load required to figure out how to explain something to someone else forces deeper understanding, better organization and the identification of gaps in knowledge. Writing, as a form of teaching, harnesses the effect (**Source:** `The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md`, 2026-07-27).

**Complementary context:** The protégé effect is a name used in educational psychology for the finding that preparing to teach, or teaching, improves the teacher's own learning. Experimental studies have found such benefits, especially when the learner actually explains the material rather than only expecting to; the size of the effect varies between studies. The corpus does not cite specific studies.

Koe's claim that he learns "10 times faster" is a statement about his own experience, not a measurement, and the reader should take it in that spirit. The mechanism he describes in 2026, however, is presented as the reason the effect works, and it is consistent with the rest of the chapter: explaining is a form of output, and output reveals gaps that input conceals.

In 2025 he connects the effect to pattern recognition and to the two roles available online. The Feynman technique "overlaps with what's called the protégé effect, which in short is the teacher learns more than the student." "Teaching what you know or what you learn encourages you to make sense of it in your own way, to finally string it together in your own mind; that's learning." It exposes even more gaps: if he talks to a camera, watches it back or stumbles over his words, "now I have something to improve, I have something to learn more." And it "increases the effect of pattern recognition: life becomes more enjoyable and serendipitous, because you notice more things in life that can help you achieve the projects or goals; you learn more just from living" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). The last point links teaching to the forced synchronicity of section 14.4: a person who intends to teach something has an additional project, and with it an additional lens.

In another 2025 video he defines the effect as "a concept in learning that suggests we learn more effectively by teaching information to others, even if those others aren't necessarily any less knowledgeable," and says he has noticed it personally. Then he draws a distinction: online there are "two options: there's consumer or contributor" (he prefers "contributor" to "content creator"). The consumer takes, gets "mentally overweight," never pieces things together and never shares a perspective. Sharing returns tenfold: you actually learn, get direct feedback on your beliefs, and are encouraged to challenge and reshape your thoughts. "The teacher learns more than the student": he learns far more by writing and making videos (synthesizing his own worldview and sharing it) than by consuming, because he is forced to solve his own problems and help others, approaching problems from a different perspective. When you solve a problem, think about how to pass it on in your own way (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12). **Consumer / contributor** and **mentally overweight** are his terms. The metaphor belongs to a family that runs from 2022 to 2026 ("information fat," "metaphorically obese," "mental bulking and cutting," "mentally bloated," "intellectual obesity"): the same idea, consumption without processing, described with escalating vocabulary. Chapter 15 develops the bodily metaphor of the mind.

Two earlier passages show the effect as an argument against the fear of teaching. In 2023: "Everyone's afraid to teach when teaching is how you learn." You do not have to lie; be honest: "hey, I learned this today, how interesting is this," or give your honest experience in a bulleted list (for example, why you should start a one-person business). "No one's stopping you but yourself from writing and producing some form of value in the world" (**Source:** `The Most Profitable Niche Is You (Create Your Niche Of One).md`, 2023-01-29). And in a 2023 video on writing: create a topic tree you can master in six to twelve months through writing, because teaching and writing go hand in hand: you have to teach what you learn to identify knowledge gaps, learn more and truly become an expert. Teaching, he believes, "is a modality that the internet has presented for individuals to become experts in a field faster" (**Source:** `How To Write Authentic Content (Social Media Growth 101).md`, 2023-02-05).

In 2024 he addresses the most common form of the fear: the sense that others are naturally articulate. "Think of your writing as teaching, because that's what it is," and leverage "the protégé effect, which is that the teacher learns more than the student, where teaching helps you learn more, faster." His analogy: after reading a book you excitedly explain it to a friend; "you don't articulate it very well the first time around, right, but then over time that's what helps it make sense to you." "People that you watch online aren't articulate because they're just magically articulate; it's because they've practiced, and you don't notice the bad iterations, because people don't care about you when you are first starting out, when you have zero followers, zero experience; people don't care until you've actually gained the experience by doing it over and over again" (**Source:** `The Future Of Work (Avoid Learning These Skills).md`, 2024-07-28). The observation about invisible bad iterations is an argument about survivorship: you see articulate creators only after their practice, so you compare your first attempt with their hundredth. The same lack of attention that makes beginners feel invisible also protects them: nobody is watching the early, bad versions.

#### The Feynman technique and the teach-learn loop

Koe formalizes teaching as a learning method through the Feynman technique, which he describes in several videos and which a guest gave him in a sharper form.

In 2025 he defines it: "a learning method popularized by physicist Richard Feynman; in short, it's about deeply understanding a concept by explaining it in simple terms as if you were teaching it to someone with no prior knowledge." The steps: (1) choose a concept; (2) teach it, explaining it in simple language "as if you were teaching it to a child"; (3) identify gaps: where you struggle to explain clearly, your understanding is weak; (4) review and simplify: go back to the source material, relearn, and explain again in even simpler terms (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

**Complementary context:** Richard Feynman (1918–1988) was an American theoretical physicist who shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on quantum electrodynamics, and was famous as a teacher for explaining complex ideas simply. The "Feynman technique" as a four-step method is a later popularization named after his teaching style; he did not publish it as a method himself.

His earliest version, from 2022, presents it as the third of a set of daily practices for becoming a deep, original and free thinker: learn, teach what you learn, identify the knowledge gaps where you struggle to teach (for example, if you teach through a YouTube video, watch it back and see where you messed up), fill in those gaps, and come back to teaching. He says "there's some study or something that shows that teaching retains the most knowledge compared to other methods," and defers fuller treatment to his next video (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20). (He announced "three or four" practices in that video and covered three.) In another 2022 video he puts numbers on the comparison: the Feynman technique "is pretty much teach what you learn," the most effective way to learn, with which you retain "90" percent of the knowledge, unlike taking notes, with which you retain "five percent of it or something like that." So write, and learn to articulate your thoughts, to make better decisions, open your mind and let new ideas register in your awareness, because you can make sense of them (**Source:** `Why You Can't Be Yourself (The Power Of Being Alone).md`, 2022-11-13).

The figures are stated loosely and should not be taken as data. The "some study" and the 90-versus-5 comparison disappear from his later treatments, where the protégé effect is defined formally instead.

**Complementary context:** Retention percentages of this kind resemble the widely circulated "learning pyramid" (for example, 5 percent from lecture, 90 percent from teaching others), whose figures have no documented empirical source and are generally regarded by researchers as unreliable.

There is also a tension with Koe's later work on notes. In 2022 notes retain "five percent" compared with teaching; later he builds note-taking systems and software of his own and teaches courses on them. The tension resolves in the role he eventually gives notes: not an archive to be retained, but raw material for creating and teaching (Chapter 16 follows that evolution).

The sharpest version of the technique comes from a guest. In a 2025 conversation, Sahil Bloom says he is "a big believer in the Feynman Technique," named after Richard Feynman ("American theoretical physicist, won the Nobel Prize in quantum electrodynamics"), known for "his ability to convey very complex topics in simple terms" to the uninitiated. His principle: "teaching is the most powerful form of learning; you cannot teach something effectively if you don't deeply understand it." His method: (1) "I default to teaching as quickly as possible": after reading something or having a conversation, "try to explain it to another friend who didn't have any context on it"; (2) observe the reaction: "where do I have gaps in my own understanding, where did I see their interest piqued, or where did I see them get confused?"; (3) "go study to fill in those gaps"; (4) "come back and try to do it again." This creates "this iterative loop of teach, learn, teach, learn that leads to this depth of knowledge very, very quickly." The contrast: if you "go study in a whole and then try to teach," you may have accumulated "knowledge that is not useful for actually creating actionable insight" (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). The **teach-learn loop** is Bloom's term.

Bloom's version adds two things to the textbook technique. Timing: teach as quickly as possible, before you feel ready, which is start-then-learn applied to teaching. And a second source of information: the listener's reactions show not only where you are confused but where they are interested, which is information about what is worth learning more about. The contrast he draws (studying everything first, then teaching) is the just-in-case learning of section 14.3, and his objection to it is the same.

Koe's response in the same conversation shows how he generalized the idea. "The way that I realized this for learning was just building an audience for me, because you can technically teach anything. Right. I don't know if you should teach everything, but you can share what you're learning to see if you can understand it, and you put it in writing or video or whatever it may be" (same source). The hedge ("I don't know if you should teach everything") marks a limit he does not develop: sharing what you are learning is always possible; presenting yourself as a teacher of everything is not always appropriate.

In a 2025 video on reading he makes the technique the third option of what he calls the digestion layer: "synthesize the ideas with writing to reflect on what you learn," based on the Feynman technique. "The best way to do this is to write, because I'm assuming you are studying something because you are, one, deeply interested in it and, two, want to integrate it as some part of your life." It can be done privately (in a chat with an AI reading partner, or in a note), "but this becomes so much more powerful when you teach in public, when you have feedback, when it's tied to your work" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). The three conditions he names (feedback, connection to your work, an audience) are the subject of the rest of this section.

He lists the practical forms teaching can take. In 2024: write in public and let people criticize you; teach your friends, which makes for interesting conversations; take notes on the subject, but "write them as if you were teaching yourself." "Teaching forces you to make sense of the information; that way you solidify it more in your mind" (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). A few months later he develops two of these. "Write in public and let people criticize you, because without the criticism you're not going to learn how to fix what you had wrong." Teach friends through interesting conversation; if you are naturally quiet, learn something and say "hey, I was learning about this new thing, it's pretty cool," and now you are conversational, because you must structure what you say, explain it, make sure you are not passing on false knowledge, and you discover what to learn next. He does not know everything when writing newsletters or videos, but four years of writing on topics that interest him have accelerated his knowledge and wisdom far more than not creating would have. "The modern way to create is content and products": life becomes a meaningful series of solving problems, learning to solve them, teaching others, and earning as you go, which is "what evolution has allowed," doing what you love without barriers thanks to the internet (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). The note "as if you were teaching yourself" shows that the technique does not strictly require an audience: the imagined learner can be oneself. Criticism, on the other hand, requires a real audience, which is why public teaching is stronger.

#### Social media as a public journal

Teaching in public, for most people, means writing on the internet, and Koe anticipates the objection that this means becoming an influencer. "I'm not talking about becoming a content creator; I'm talking about treating social media as your public journal rather than a place to just distract yourself." To merge the Feynman technique and the protégé effect with what you are learning, "you do it by writing on the internet." That gives you a chance of attracting "potential supporters, customers, employers, investors, team members." Learning, building and writing to reflect systematically in public "doubles down on the effect of learning." "It's less about building a following and more about putting your work in front of other people," like meeting new people: it will not happen if you do not leave your house, and you will grow bitter wondering why you are alone; "your chances of success are zero" (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09). **Public journal** is his term, with variants across 2022–2025 ("public note-taking software," "public school" for others): social media used as a searchable notebook and diary in which one learns in front of others.

In the reading video of the following month he lists what teaching in public opens. Writing on the internet or social media is "the new media," how you build any audience or distribution for independent creative work. Teaching in public brings three benefits: (1) "you learn much, much faster, because you have a forcing function for understanding"; (2) "you attract an audience of people with similar interests, and these people are your network and future customers"; (3) "you build leverage going into a future of job replacement. The creator economy is one area that is proving to not be replaced, even though everyone screams there will be an overabundance of content" (**Source:** `How To Read Books Fast With AI (And Remember What You Read).md`, 2025-04-20). A **forcing function** (a term Koe takes from Dickie Bush and extends) is something whose structure obliges a result; here, public teaching obliges understanding. The three benefits correspond to three parts of the book: learning (this chapter), audience (Part IX) and the economy of the individual in the age of AI (Chapter 36). The third is a forecast, and he presents it as one.

In 2026 he addresses people who love learning but dislike the idea of content. "If you don't like the idea of becoming a content creator, just think of social media as a mechanism to do independent work." "If you love learning, great. Reframe it as research, and now that's literally your main job. Most of the things I write about simply come from me learning about my interests and treating social media like I'm taking notes in public. So, you're already spending time learning. Now, just spend that time learning in public and boom, you have the foundation of a business" (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20). **Learn in public** (or "learning in public") is his term, and the reframing does real work: it converts an activity the curious person already does (learning) into the core of a business by changing only where it happens.

The objection that one has no experience to share was answered as early as 2022. "You don't need experience to post a video, an article, or anything online," especially if you understand perspective and "positioning, which is a marketing term." To make it impactful you need to understand marketing, psychology, persuasion, perhaps epistemology and philosophy, "how we make sense of things": stories, metaphors, concepts, what catches attention, what holds it, what is deemed valuable. "You can learn it in public": post your perspective on what you are learning and give credit to the people you learned it from. There are ways around having no experience, and ways of building experience while posting videos, articles and tweets (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). The condition "give credit" is the ethical boundary of learning in public: the beginner shares a perspective on what they are learning, not a claim to have originated it. In the same video he lists the benefits of posting even before one thinks like an entrepreneur: start building leverage, start posting; "eventually you will be" in the entrepreneurial mindset, "because it's part of your psyche." Posting makes you learn faster, may bring job opportunities and personal growth, "sheds light on your blind spots very quickly," and gives you a project to apply everything to (same source).

In 2023 he condenses the public journal into a definition that links learning to income. Following from documenting one's experiments: "When you treat your life as a project, it becomes profitable when you turn it into a product." "A personal brand is just discovering yourself in public and getting paid for the research that you do" (**Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`, 2023-05-08). The first sentence connects back to "turning you into the project" (section 14.4); the second redefines the personal brand as self-discovery made public. Chapter 27 develops the personal brand; Chapter 19 develops the idea that the niche is oneself.

#### Build in public: the market's data and people who care

Teaching in public consolidates learning; building in public adds information that no amount of private learning can provide. Koe's argument, from 2022, starts from the starving artist. Building must be done in public (posting, building, publishing, launching), because you need data to know whether what you make is valuable. "Starving artists, it's in the name": they create what they think is valuable, get attached to their version of value and wonder why nothing sells ("this painting is beautiful"). That is fine as a hobby, but if you want to do it full time, "you're going to have to play a back and forth game with the market." Since you are trying to improve people's lives, if people do not buy, read or stay attentive, is it really valuable? If it does not get results, iterate and pivot (**Source:** `You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md`, 2022-12-04). **Starving artist** is a common phrase that Koe uses in a sense of his own (with several variants, 2022–2026): the creator who is attached to their own version of value and does not test it against the market. **Build in public** is a common phrase that he uses for publishing work in progress in order to get the market's data.

The argument extends the learning loop beyond the individual. The project teaches you whether you can build something; the market teaches you whether what you built is valuable. Both are forms of feedback, and the starving artist is the person who accepts the first and refuses the second.

In 2025 he calls public work the missing piece of the whole method. You know what you want, you have projects that take you there, you learn and build daily; "there's one missing piece, and it turns out it's also a way to further enhance how much you learn": "people who care about what you're building." His assumption is that you want to do this full time, "replace your job or career with a calling, an obsession," which needs money, "at least enough to sustain your ideal life; you don't need millions." To make people care, "you need to show what you're doing in public, and in most cases the best way to do that is writing, teaching what you know and what you're learning as you build." His "master class" example is Pieter Levels, building a game with AI in public and adding the features his followers wanted, which attracted people who care and an audience that wants to support him (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone With AI.md`, 2025-03-09).

**Complementary context:** Pieter Levels (known online as levels.io) is an independent developer known for building and launching software products alone and documenting the process publicly; in early 2025 he built a browser-based flight game largely with AI coding tools, adding features in public.

Two passages from the 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush show what documenting in public does over years. The first is an observation by Bush that both accept: Koe developed a relationship with the fitness YouTubers he watched, whose videos were seven or eight years old by then, simply because they documented their journeys, to the point that they influenced his entire path even though he is no longer in the fitness space. "You have developed a relationship with those people just by them documenting." Both say "me too." Later in the episode this becomes the reason for planning recurring conversations among a small group (Bush names Joey, Justin and Scott): people can watch the series and see that "these guys that I look up to didn't just happen here." Following one person's or one small community's journey over time is presented as better than a podcast that branches into too many domains (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). The observation closes a circle with section 14.1: Koe learned from people who documented their journeys, and the lesson he draws is that documenting one's own journey creates the same kind of relationship with others. **Document your journey** is his term for making the process discoverable.

The second passage is Bush's own story, and it is the corpus's clearest case of learning in public turning into a business. Bush began writing in January 2020 while working at BlackRock: a weekly newsletter plus a weekly blog post for forty weeks that "basically no one was reading" except his mother. Feeling stuck, ready to give up and stay in finance, he took "one last shot": write on Twitter instead of the blog, and instead of presenting himself as an expert (on productivity, for example), take something he already did (listening to podcasts on his daily walks and taking notes that lived "in the back of a Notion folder" he never looked at) and tweet a thread distilling one podcast every day for thirty days. He gives three reasons it worked. First, "the public forcing function of putting those ideas in a concise way" made him internalize them: "I didn't have the public forcing function of putting those ideas in a concise way; I wasn't really internalizing them." Second, the social environment, unlike a blog, let people interact, which attracted people with the same interests. Third, people began to see him "as a trusted curator and source of information." On day twenty-eight (in mid-2020) he distilled Balaji Srinivasan's worldview from about ten podcasts; he woke up to find that Naval Ravikant had retweeted it, with four to five thousand likes, and his newsletter went from about a hundred subscribers in forty weeks to a thousand in twenty-four hours. People asked whether the ideas were compiled anywhere; he said no, but that he could compile them, packaged them that weekend, put them on sale on Gumroad the following Monday for twenty dollars and made five hundred. Learning how to launch and fulfill that product grew into Ship 30 for 30 (same source).

**Public forcing function** and **trusted curator** are Bush's terms. The case contains the whole chapter in sequence: an existing habit of learning (podcasts on walks), private notes that taught him nothing because they were never used, the switch to teaching in public that forced him to internalize the ideas, an audience that gathered around shared interests, a curator's reputation, and a product made from the learning itself. It also shows the role of a non-expert: Bush did not claim expertise; he distilled others' ideas and credited them, which is exactly the "learn in public, give credit" of Koe's 2022 advice.

#### The personal brand as practice ground

If teaching in public is the best way to learn, what should one build in public? Koe's recurring answer is a personal brand, and his reason is not marketing but learning: a personal brand forces you to practice every skill a business needs, on a project of your own.

He makes the connection in the 2023 conversation with Bush, linking Bush's story to his own video on learning fast: "start then learn"; you have to actually build something. It is becoming "more and more of a pattern of behavior change where you're trying to survive a project": a project is "a structured goal, in a sense, where it has other things that bring clarity to it." This is why he thinks everyone interested should have a personal brand, even for career opportunities, since "employers are hiring from the value that you provide." Building his brand introduced him to the whole skill stack: graphic design for the banner and profile picture, visual composition, what makes a compelling bio ("which kind of revolves around top-of-funnel persuasion"), writing content in short, medium and long form, the distinction between content and marketing (sales page, email, "how does that all flow"), designing an idea for Instagram, building a product, monetizing. People ask "what skills do I learn?" "You need the goal or project first, and then they all reveal themselves" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). **Survive a project** is his phrase, the forced synchronicity of section 14.4 described as behavior change. The last sentence is the principle of this chapter applied to the question of which skills to learn: you do not choose a skill stack in advance; the project reveals it.

The same argument appears in a 2023 video on niches. With a personal brand you will eventually have to learn to create a website or landing page, graphic design for a banner or profile picture; your bio, link in bio and content are all copywriting and content writing; then your email newsletter, acting as "a holistic funnel," then your products. "The more skills you have, the more successful you are in the personal branding game." You need all the high-value skills people talk about, but by building a personal brand "you give yourself a year to learn these and you have somewhere to apply them," so "you're not stuck in tutorial hell" (**Source:** `Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md`, 2023-04-30). And in a 2022 video it is the second step of a learning process: during a period of immersion, start "a personal project with potential profitability," in this case a business. "I don't want to hear that you don't want to start a business": it is in your nature; "we weren't meant to be metaphorical monkeys in cubicles that are neurobiologically deprived of what we are wired to do." He recommends (as one path, not the only one) a personal brand, a YouTube channel or a Twitter account, and prefers Twitter because it requires no editing or visual knowledge and "only requires that you know how to type on a keyboard." The purpose is to have something to tie your learning to and apply it: "if you're just learning and not doing anything with it, you're not learning." A personal brand requires every skill an online business requires (marketing, communication, writing and iterating, understanding what humans deem valuable so that they engage and spread your message), and eventually you package a solution from your experience and sell it. "A personal brand is you, just online" (**Source:** `How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md`, 2022-06-25).

The smallest form of this practice is a single skill learned on one's own profile. "Social media is the perfect vessel to build as you learn, and that's the only way to truly learn something: to build with your learning." To learn graphic design, create your profile picture, bio, banner and website featured image, learning through specific tutorials as you go; that is how you learn what you actually need to help your own business, or gain a skill to help another business (**Source:** `Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md`, 2023-11-26). And the place to post matters. Studying design, for example, "you will learn the most when you actually create something and then you have somewhere to post it": you have accountability and direct feedback. Rather than making "fancy little designs" in your room, or learning without doing anything with it, post it online. You do not have to turn it into a business (**Source:** `If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md`, 2022-05-21). In the 2022 learning video he calls this **improve yourself in public**: (1) improve yourself in public with a personal brand; (2) learn the skills necessary to build it, so that you remember those skills; (3) teach those skills or your interests, retaining that knowledge. A personal brand is "dynamic": not limited to one niche, because it is under your name, so you can explore many domains "as long as you're making your interest interesting to other people." Over time you build an audience, then create a product to monetize, "and that's how you make a living for yourself," if you stick it out long enough (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27).

Why practice on your own brand rather than on clients? Koe's answer, from 2023, compares two kinds of practice. His favorite way to integrate and practice all the skills immediately is to start a one-person business or personal brand, "because it's a business." Freelancers think, "I want to practice SEO, so I'll try to land an SEO client," but you could start your own website about what you are building and doing in life and practice SEO on your own blog; practice design with your own banner and profile picture; persuasion by writing content; distribution by getting eyes on your profile and learning those tactics; and supplement with courses or YouTube. You can write copy for your own sales page or promotions, create your own **minimum viable offer** (his term for a first, small service offer, usually priced at $500 to $1,000) and market it, or promote someone else's product as an affiliate to practice marketing. The skill stack you develop by building a personal brand for a year "can transition over into almost any online business," and with followers ("more than the average person") your direct messages are far more likely to be answered. "I wish I did this," he says, and he changed all the courses he offers because of it (**Source:** `The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md`, 2023-01-08).

A passage from 2022, which he repeated almost verbatim in a 2024 compilation, makes the contrast with portfolio work explicit. If you get results for yourself in a real-world setting, rather than building portfolio pieces and never getting paid what you deserve (because you do not know how to apply the skills to an actual business), you are practicing your skills on your own business: your own website, your own landing page, perhaps a landing page for an affiliate product to test how good you are at copywriting without having to create the product. Then you can offer landing pages for $1,000 to $2,000 to other creators. "All you have to do is just start" (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself).md`, 2022-10-23; `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). In 2023 he states the payoff more sharply. He recommends building a creator business or personal brand ("displaying your character in the virtual reality and contributing to virtual humanity") because you learn web design, graphic design, copywriting, content writing and branding: every skill a business needs, understood from the big picture. Once you get results, if you do not have a product yet, you can freelance for other businesses and make "10 times more money" than someone who just learned web design and sends cold emails without experience; they lose from the start because "you're not practicing on building your own business and getting results" (**Source:** `You Aren't Successful Because You Care Too Much (How To Stop).md`, 2023-08-08).

The comparison with the cold-emailing web designer is Koe's own early life (section 14.3) seen from the outside. A single skill, learned in isolation and offered cold, is a commodity; the same skill, learned while building one's own business and shown with results, is evidence. The "10 times more" is an impression from experience rather than a figure from data, but the mechanism is clear: the brand provides proof of results, and proof changes price.

For those who prefer practicing on others, Koe offers the **permissionless apprenticeship**, a phrase he uses in his own sense. In 2022: you learn more when you build and learn to apply to what you are building; then you teach to identify knowledge gaps and fill them in with learning. If you are learning marketing, build a brand or a portfolio piece, or do a permissionless apprenticeship: create something (for example, a website for your favorite brand), send it to them if you like, or just do it for practice. His favorite remains the personal brand, because there you can create your own website, content, email list, emails, profile picture and banner, "a plethora of the highest-value skills" applied to one project (**Source:** `You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md`, 2022-12-04; repeated in `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). In 2023 he adds the etiquette. Go to a brand and write their email sequences, or create a health program, and send it: "hey, I made this for you, take it and do what you will." If they use it and get results, good; if not, leave it. "Don't be needy, don't try and make the sale; opportunities will come to you that way." If the work is visual and you target a large creator, make a landing page for them (make sure it is good, so you are not embarrassed), post an image of it on Twitter and tag them. If they like it, they will comment, like or retweet, spreading you to their audience even if they never use it, and people interested in landing-page design will message you to hire you (**Source:** `The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md`, 2023-06-25). The apprenticeship has the same structure as the personal brand (a real project, done in public, with feedback), and the rule against neediness keeps it from turning into a sales pitch, which is the "telling for approval" of section 14.3.

How much should one study alongside the brand? Koe gives two sets of numbers. In 2023, as the first habit of a better life, "learning with intention": learn the principles of marketing, sales, writing and social media; binge-watch "30 or 50 hours of free content and paid courses"; build your personal brand along the way "so you can actually apply what you learn"; study what interests you and do not limit yourself. If your goal is building a better life for yourself, anything you learn will be seen through that lens, so "you are still focusing on one thing even if you aren't focusing on one thing" (**Source:** `Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md`, 2023-09-17). In 2024, presenting a roadmap from zero to a million dollars a year, he says it is complex ("not a 30-minute video and you'll make a million"), and that you have to spend most of your time now soaking in information and building along with it: "binge-watch like 50 to 100 hours of content and build along with it in the real world" to see results (**Source:** `Zero To $1 Million As A One-Person Business (Working 2-4 Hours A Day).md`, 2024-02-25). **Learning with intention** is his term. The numbers vary (30–50 hours in 2023, 50–100 in 2024), and Koe gives no reason for the variation; what is constant is the conjunction "and build along with it." These are the only passages on learning where Koe recommends a large volume of consumption, and in both the consumption is tied to simultaneous building. The sentence about focusing on one thing even when you are not is the goal-as-lens of Chapter 6 applied to a curious person's wide reading.

He also tells beginners that they can earn while they learn. Do not get trapped thinking you cannot make some income, or even replace your income, while learning these skills over the next decade. "You can make money instantly if you know what you're doing." Start building and executing instantly "so that you can get real-world data to iterate from," learn more to improve and be able to charge more. "You aren't going to come out of the gate charging five to ten thousand dollars for one skill"; you start small and follow "the progression that life presents," which comes in stages: $500, $1,000, higher and higher, and eventually you productize (**Source:** `The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md`, 2023-01-08). Part X develops this progression from service to product; here it shows that the learning loop and the earning loop are the same loop at different scales: act, get data, improve, charge more.

One qualification concerns the label. Koe recommended "starting a personal brand" as non-negotiable from 2022 to 2023; in October 2024 he published "The Death of the Personal Brand," arguing that personal brands had become impersonal, money-obsessed templates and telling viewers not to be a personal brand but to "be you, in a place where your work can be discovered"; by 2026 he recovers the term as "the future in many ways, especially as a value creator." What changes is the label and the criticism of a degraded version of it, not the practice this section describes: building in public, under your own name, as a way of learning every skill.

#### Teaching what you do: persuasive education

The last objection Koe answers concerns the content of teaching. If many creators already teach what you want to teach, why would anyone learn from you? In 2025 he makes "persuasive education changes behavior" the third pillar of a personal brand, "arguably the most important." Most creators build education businesses that teach their skills and interests (you could try to be a celebrity or an OnlyFans model, he says, but "we're aiming for some form of positive impact and meaningful work"). Many creators already teach what you want to teach, and any skill on YouTube has many videos about it. That is what trips up beginners: they do not believe people have a reason to follow them. His two solutions are to teach through a new lens and to persuade the people who are not yet interested (**Source:** `How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md`, 2025-06-08). **Persuasive education** is his term. The two solutions point forward: the new lens is the originality of Chapter 18 and the niche of one of Chapter 19; persuading the uninterested is the marketing of Chapter 32.

A second objection comes from critics: "people are just teaching others what they do themselves." His answer in 2025 is blunt. "No shit. That's what the market wants, and that's what people are qualified to teach. That's arguably the only thing that people are qualified to teach you. If you weren't teaching what you know, then you're a liar." He teaches writing positioned for social media because that is what people find most valuable; he could teach writing unrelated to social media, but the creator economy is so large that that is what everyone is doing (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md`, 2025-02-10). The answer turns the criticism into a criterion of honesty: teaching what you have done is not a limitation of the creator economy but its guarantee. It also closes the loop of this chapter: if you build to learn and teach what you build, what you teach is necessarily what you do.

His example of where this can lead, from 2023, is Justin Welsh, who has two $150 products based on what he has done; he "literally just created a product because he knew how to grow an audience," adding "another million-dollar-a-year revenue stream." Koe immediately scales the example down: that is at Welsh's level, but "even people that get like a 10K a month revenue stream, that's life-changing." The lesson he draws is the sequence: build an audience, learn about monetization along the way, teach what you learn, create a product or service, promote it (**Source:** `The Micro Education Business Model (How To Monetize Your Knowledge).md`, 2023-04-23). The sequence is the Build Teach Earn method of section 14.4 described from the outside, in someone else's career.

This chapter has described learning as an activity: choosing what to learn, following curiosity, refusing the false progress of information without application, building projects that turn problems into lessons, and teaching what one builds. It has said less about what a skill is and how it grows once the learning is under way: why techniques stack, why principles matter more than tactics, how self-experimentation and mistakes direct the process, and why mastery advances by plateaus and sudden jumps. That is the mechanics of skill, the subject of Chapter 15.

### Exercises

1. **Audit your last month of learning.** List everything you consumed in the past month with the intention of learning (videos, books, courses, podcasts). For each item, note whether it was applied to a problem you were actively working on at the time. Using the distinctions of section 14.3 (tactic collection, confirmational dopamine, telling and asking as substitutes for doing), classify what you find, and identify which items, by Koe's criteria, were entertainment. Then argue against at least one of your classifications: is there a case where consumption without immediate application was nonetheless valuable, and does Koe's exception for "big-picture domains" cover it?

2. **Design a learning project.** Choose a skill you want to acquire. Instead of a study plan, write a project outline using the four components of section 14.4 (outline, milestones, a real-world deadline, experimentation), and state, guitar-style, the "song" you will try to play first. Predict the first three struggling points you expect to hit. After working on the project for a week, compare your predictions with the walls you actually hit, and explain what the difference suggests about just-in-case versus just-in-time learning.

3. **Test the protégé effect on yourself.** Pick a concept you believe you understand from this book (for example, forced synchronicity or the curiosity–intensity–consistency cycle). Explain it, in writing or aloud, to someone with no context, following Sahil Bloom's version of the Feynman technique. Record where you hesitated, where the listener got confused and where they became interested. What do the gaps reveal about the difference between recognizing an idea and understanding it?

4. **Diagnose the architect of the business.** Koe explained the same early failures in four different ways between 2021 and 2026 (missing marketing, missing "glue" skills, not putting product in front of people, missing strategic thinking). Are these explanations competing or complementary? Construct an argument for which one is the most fundamental, and identify a project of your own (past or present) in which you acted as "the architect of the business." What was the equivalent of asking someone to pay, and why did you avoid it?

5. **Question the critique of reading.** Koe has said both "purchase one to three books on the topic and devour them" and "reading books is not learning" and that reading to learn is "entertainment." Write a short defense of each position in its strongest form, then propose a rule that would tell a reader, in a given situation, which position applies. Does your rule match the distinction the chapter suggests (reading to do versus reading to see the world), or does it require a different one?

6. **From cope to curiosity.** Think of a person whose success you have explained, at least privately, as luck, privilege or timing. Apply Koe's method: write at least five horizontal and five vertical questions about what that person actually did and how they learned it, and research the answers where possible. Then state what changed in your explanation, and identify which part of your original explanation was, in Koe's terms, cope.

7. **When would build-to-learn fail?** Identify at least two domains or situations in which "start, then learn" would be inadequate or dangerous (consider fields where errors are costly, irreversible or harmful to others, or where tacit knowledge cannot be found in tutorials). For each, explain which part of Koe's reasoning breaks down (the availability of feedback, the cost of a struggling point, the role of a teacher one stage ahead), and propose how the method would need to be modified rather than abandoned.

8. **Plan a public journal.** Suppose you decided to learn one of your current interests in public for ninety days. Specify what you would publish, where, how often, and how you would give credit to your sources. Then identify the line, using the distinction of section 14.3, between telling people what you intend to do for approval and showing what you are building so that others benefit. What would you refuse to post, and why?
