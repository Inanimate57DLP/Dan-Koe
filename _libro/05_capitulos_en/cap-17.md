## Chapter 17 — How to Think

The three previous chapters dealt with learning: the meta-skill of learning how to learn, the mechanics by which skills accumulate, and the inputs (books, notes, AI) from which a person builds understanding. This chapter takes the next step and asks what a person does with all of that once it is inside their head. Koe's answer is not a theory of logic or a catalogue of cognitive biases. It is a description of where thinking stops, why it stops there, and what it takes to keep it going long enough to solve a real problem, form a philosophy of one's own and design a strategy for a life.

The chapter depends on two earlier parts of the book. From Part I it takes the closed mind: the conditioned person whose ideology has become identity and who therefore experiences a contrary idea as a threat (Chapter 1, section 1.7). From Part III it takes the mechanics of the mind: attention as the scarce resource, the goal as the lens that decides what is perceived, and cybernetics, the idea that intelligence is a process of steering toward a goal and correcting the course through feedback (Chapter 6). It also leans on the notion of **level of mind** from Chapter 4: the idea, which Koe usually attributes to Einstein, that a problem cannot be solved from the level of mind that created it. Several sections of this chapter are applications of those three ideas to the specific act of thinking.

The structure follows the order in which the corpus builds the argument. Section 17.1 contrasts what Koe calls **stupid thinking** with **genius thinking**, and gathers everything he says about the closed and the open mind, the stages through which open-mindedness develops and the practice of skepticism. Section 17.2 turns that open mind on advice itself: do not take anyone's prescription as law, including the author's, and build a philosophy of your own. Section 17.3 presents questioning as the basic act of thinking. Section 17.4 explains **problem-first thinking**, the process Koe calls "the thinking process to achieve anything," and follows it into writing and selling, where the same principle becomes "start with the pain point." Section 17.5 distinguishes knowing from understanding, and horizontal from vertical growth. Section 17.6 closes with strategy: the difference between strategy and tactics, the seven pillars (2025) and seven principles (2026) of strategic thinking, the management of resources and risk, and the premortem. Chapter 18 then picks up where this chapter ends, with creativity, originality and taste.

### 17.1 Stupid Thinking and Genius Thinking

#### Studying the opposite first

Koe's most systematic treatment of thinking, a 2026 video titled "How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking)," opens with what he calls a thinking tip: "If you want to understand what something is, like thinking or genius thinking, it helps to understand the opposite, to understand what stupid thinking is." He immediately disarms the word: "I'm not calling you stupid, I'm not calling you low IQ. I'm simply observing a pattern. Everyone thinks stupidly at times. Everyone thinks stupidly every single day" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22).

Two things in that caveat matter for the rest of the section. First, "stupid thinking" in Koe's vocabulary is not a measure of intelligence. It is a pattern, a way thinking behaves at a particular moment, and the same person can think stupidly in one domain and brilliantly in another. Second, the method itself (understand a thing through its opposite) is one Koe uses throughout the corpus. He defines vision through anti-vision (Chapter 7), and here he defines good thinking through bad thinking. This section follows the same order: first the forms of bad thinking, then their positive counterpart, then the practices that move a person from one to the other.

The name for the negative pole has changed over the years, and the change is instructive. It is worth following it chronologically, because each version adds a layer to the one before.

#### Shallow thinking: mistaking the piece for the puzzle (2022)

In 2022, in a video called "The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind)," Koe calls the opposite of good thinking **shallow thinking**. He defines it as "taking what you see as law and never seeing beyond your sensory perceptions," or "thinking that what you see is all there is." The image he uses is a cosmic puzzle. You see one little piece of the cosmic image and think "that's it": there is nothing beyond it, no depth, no connection, no relationship to other things, nowhere else for the mind to go. So the mind gets stuck, closed off, "closed-minded" to the one thing it is focused on. A narrow focus means not being able to open the mind to the potential of other things, "and that is the problem" (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20).

His example is a diet fad. Someone sees a diet on social media, cuts out junk food, starts the carnivore diet, feels better, and concludes: "the carnivore diet is the best diet for everyone." But the result did not come from carnivore; it came from removing bad food. The error is not trying the diet. The error is concluding that there is no other option, that the one variable the person happened to change is a universal law (**Source:** same file).

From this example Koe derives a principle he states in compressed form: there is "infinite nuance; if it is speakable, then it is infinitely debatable." Anything that can be put into words can be argued endlessly from another angle. If you do not open your mind to see beyond your piece of the puzzle, or at least hold the nuance in your own head, you fall into shallow thinking and into argument (**Source:** same file). The principle follows from the conditioning argument of Part I: if the meaning of any statement depends on the interpretation of the person hearing it, then every spoken claim has endless angles, and treating one angle as final is a decision, not a discovery. This principle will return twice in this section, once in Koe's definition of deep thinking and once in his reading of the ancient skeptic Sextus Empiricus.

#### Stupid thinking as thinking from one quadrant (2025)

In July 2025, in "This mental model will make you think like a strategic genius," the term becomes **stupid thinking**, and its definition is anchored in Ken Wilber's quadrant model. "This is actually where stupid thinking comes from and the way that we'll define it, because most people only think from one quadrant and preach that as the one true way." Capitalists focus only on the economic system; scientific materialists only on matter and behavior; religions only on shared faith; Stoics and Buddhists only on the internal world. Wilber's point, as Koe presents it, is that "all of these hold truths and they are all overlapping." Locking yourself into one dogmatic worldview "puts you in a box." The danger is practical, not merely intellectual: "certain problems are best solved or thought about through certain perspectives," and if you cannot access the right perspective, the problem will not be solved and "it's just going to cause you unnecessary pain in life" (**Source:** `This mental model will make you think like a strategic genius.md`, 2025-07-20).

**Complementary context:** Ken Wilber's AQAL ("all quadrants, all levels") model divides any phenomenon into four perspectives: the interior of the individual (upper left: thoughts, feelings, consciousness), the exterior of the individual (upper right: body, behavior, brain), the interior of the collective (lower left: culture, shared meaning, faith) and the exterior of the collective (lower right: systems, economies, institutions). Koe's mapping (Stoics and Buddhists in the upper left, materialists in the upper right, religions in the lower left, capitalists in the lower right) follows that scheme. The quadrant model itself is developed in Chapter 38 (section 38.2); here it only serves to define what stupid thinking is.

A year earlier, in November 2024, Koe had stated the same idea without the quadrants and with one of his more vivid verbs: "If you want to become more intelligent you need to expand your mind to see the bigger picture, to tell a more holistic story, to view situations from all perspectives, to not **decapitate your thinking** by conforming to one perspective, one religion, one business model, one area of study, that is verifiably nontruth because of its one-dimensionality" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). The claim at the end is strong: a single perspective is not merely incomplete but "verifiably" untrue, because reality has more than one dimension and a one-dimensional account cannot match it. Koe gives no further argument for the word "verifiably"; it reads as an emphatic restatement of the infinite-nuance principle rather than as a demonstrated proposition.

#### Stupid thinking as stopping too early (2026)

In February 2026 the definition changes focus. Koe now lists four traits observed in people who display stupid thinking:

| Trait | Koe's description |
|---|---|
| One-dimensional | "People try to jam everything into their own perspective and have difficulty seeing outside of it." |
| Reductionistic | "Experts in one domain, like business, try to reduce everything to a strategy problem." |
| Tribal | "You only trust your group, your political party, your tribe, your religious people… and you consider everyone else wrong because they don't conform." |
| Non-questioning | "Your justification for most things is that's just how it's done." |

(**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22.)

He then sums them up in a single sentence: "stupid thinking is about closing your mind off once you've reached the limits of what you know." And he gives the definition that becomes the core of the 2026 version: "Stupid thinking is when you stop thinking too early. You can still think within your own little bubble, but you never break out of that bubble." The process has three moments. You think for a bit. You reach the limits of how far you can think. Then you "collapse in on what you know." From that point on "you just become this person who just spews out pre-programmed thoughts or what they've been told or what other people believe is right. They don't push the boundaries of their own mind" (**Source:** same file).

The shift between the versions is subtle but real, and it should not be flattened.

| Version | Name | What defines the failure |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | Shallow thinking | Taking what you perceive as law; the piece mistaken for the puzzle |
| 2025-07 | Stupid thinking | Thinking from one quadrant and preaching it as the only way |
| 2026-02 | Stupid thinking | Stopping too early and collapsing into what you already know; four traits |

The 2022 and 2025 versions describe the *content* of bad thinking: a single perspective. The 2026 version describes the *process*: the moment at which thinking stops. One-dimensionality survives in 2026 as one trait among four. The two descriptions are compatible (one names the symptom, the other the mechanism that produces it), and Koe never explains the change. What the 2026 version adds is a diagnosis that can be noticed from the inside. You may not be able to see that your perspective is one-dimensional, because from inside it looks like the whole picture; but you can learn to notice the moment when a thought reaches the edge of what you know and snaps back.

The phrase "pre-programmed thoughts" ties the definition to Part I. In Koe's model, a mind that stops thinking does not go silent. It falls back on the programming it inherited: the beliefs of parents, school, tribe and feed (Chapter 1). Stupid thinking is therefore not the absence of thought but the replacement of thought by repetition.

#### The "smart but dumb" phenomenon

The reductionistic trait gets its own name in the 2026 video: the **smart but dumb** phenomenon. "Someone very smart in a domain like business will see someone who is depressed and just attribute that to being a productivity issue": "If you're depressed, just do the work. If you're sad, just do the work." "In some cases that helps, but it doesn't help in every case." Koe's metaphor is a child's shape-sorting toy: "It's like you're a little kid… trying to mash [the circle] into the square shape." The same happens in the other direction: a spiritual person attributes a business failure to "not having the right vibration." And then the qualification that keeps the idea from becoming a simple accusation: "They're both not wrong. They're both correct in their own ways." The point is to know how to apply each way of thinking to different situations; a business person benefits from tapping the spiritual lens when a problem is best solved that way, and vice versa. "You probably do the same thing without noticing it," he adds, and simply noticing it, and not letting your thinking stop there, "unlocks this new power." Later in the video he applies the label to entire online domains: politics, religion and nutrition online are "a bunch of smart but dumb people just calling each other idiots because they can't see from each other's perspective" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22).

He illustrates the phenomenon with three portraits:

1. A businessman, very successful, makes $100 million, sells the company and is now depressed. He does not know how to solve that "because he's not in business anymore, and that's all he knows."
2. A creative with beautiful work, who has dedicated their life to their craft but cannot make an income "because they don't understand anything in the business domain or even in the psychological domain and how humans perceive value." They are not creating something others want. Koe adds that this is "not inherently a problem"; it becomes one only if the person wants to create full time while working a job, because then "you're not willing to learn what's going to allow you to do that."
3. A "meathead" who is fit but whose relationship is in shambles because he does not understand social dynamics or what Koe calls "masculine-feminine dynamics." He got buff to attract a partner but cannot keep her, since all he understands is "if I get buff, girls like me."

(**Source:** same file.)

The three cases share a structure: real competence in one domain, a problem located in another domain, and an attempt to solve the second with the tools of the first. In October 2025 Koe had stated the same principle in quadrant terms. "An internal mental problem may not be best solved by vocational means like getting a new job. A spiritual problem may not be best solved by bodily means like nutrition. A capitalist and Christian are developed in their respective domains, but will experience unnecessary pain when trying to apply their model to problems within other domains. Money often doesn't solve for meaning and vice versa, but that doesn't mean they aren't intimately connected." All the domains overlap, and developing in all of them is what lets you "become in control of your future" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19). In that video Koe gives the error a name of its own, **partial thinking**: solving a problem that lives in one domain with the model of another, so that a fully developed way of thinking is applied where it does not reach. The term appears only once in the corpus, and it is a neighbor of "smart but dumb" rather than a synonym: "smart but dumb" (2026) names the person, highly competent in one domain and blind in the others, while "partial thinking" (2025) names the operation, the act of taking one quadrant's tools to another quadrant's problem. The final clause of the money-and-meaning sentence is the important nuance: the domains are distinct enough that one cannot substitute for another, and connected enough that neglecting one damages the rest.

The idea is older than the name. In December 2023 Koe gave it as practical advice without any label. For relationship problems you can hire a therapist, watch a YouTube guru, go on a retreat, and experiment until you find the right solution; choosing and identifying with one solution that may not work is the mistake. If your business is not growing, you can buy a course, hire a coach, test new software, or zoom out and discover new goals by adopting the perspective of life, the universe or your ideal self. "There's always a way to solve your problems and you strip yourself of that power when you latch on to one solution." His prescription: "Think of each domain of life as both a project and a skill in which you collect techniques to master that domain" (**Source:** `You Won't Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The sentence links this chapter to Chapter 15 ("everything is a skill") and to Chapter 9 (the project as the practical vehicle of change): the antidote to smart-but-dumb thinking is not general humility but treating each domain of life as a field in which you have to acquire techniques, rather than a field you can colonize with techniques from elsewhere.

#### Selective skepticism and other signs of a closed mind

Stupid thinking has a particular form that Koe treats as "a critical flaw" in people's self-development: **selective skepticism**. When people are presented with opportunities to start a business, meet new people or change their life as a whole, their "unconscious, automatic and programmed response" is: "sounds like a scam," "I can't do that because…," "but what if it doesn't work." The mechanism is the one already described: "their mind can't see a potential future where they do well; their beliefs trap where their thoughts can go in this small little bubble"; their attention "can't transcend the problem." The consequence follows by a short chain: if your decisions create your future, and your mind cannot see a good future, your decisions can only lead to a mediocre life (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16).

The adjective "selective" is the key to the term. The skepticism is not a general disposition to doubt. It is applied to opportunities and never to the default path. The same person who asks for proof that an online business can work accepts without proof that a degree, a job and a pension are safe. In the vocabulary of Chapter 2, the default path is exempt from scrutiny because it is the background against which everything else is judged. Koe introduces the term right after discussing learned helplessness, and the two are related: selective skepticism is what learned helplessness sounds like when it speaks.

In a 2023 video Koe makes the cost explicit. "Schools teach outdated and less profitable ways to make money because more profitable ones emerged with the creation of the internet, just like how more profitable jobs emerged after the creation of a tractor." "By viewing everything as a scam you are delaying your development by years, if not your entire life." "This is the phase of evolution we are in, and you are missing out on what will be the new normal until the next thing emerges." "Every new idea is perceived as blasphemy or just crazy because people can't understand it yet" (**Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`, 2023-11-12). The tractor analogy places the argument in economic history: each technological shift produces new kinds of work that look illegitimate to those trained for the old kinds. Whether a particular online opportunity is sound is a separate question; Koe's claim is about the reflex that rejects the entire category before looking.

A year earlier he had sorted information consumers into three groups. The **distracted** use online information to distract themselves through argument, mainstream news or "flavor-of-the-day drama." The **curious** observe that this is a trap leading them down the wrong path and become curious about the opposite direction: finding good information to build their life with. The third group is trapped in a negative cycle of consuming content that tells them new information (courses, coaching, information products) is bad. Koe asks this third group to come with an open mind or not watch, because "it doesn't make sense that you would write off an epoch of existence, the age of information, just because you had one bad experience" with a sleazy marketer or a "snake oil salesman." As an example of the snake-oil type he mentions Liver King, the online fitness personality, while clarifying that Liver King is not a course seller and that he does not want to get into the drama around him; the name serves only to point at the kind of figure that leads people to write off the whole category (**Source:** `You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md`, 2022-12-04). The phrase "an epoch of existence" names what the selective skeptic actually rejects: not one offer but an entire historical period's way of learning and earning.

A related sign of a closed mind appears in Koe's 2023 list of the signs of close-mindedness, where the fourth sign is **vision avoidance**: repressing your potential to avoid uncertainty. A vision is not clear in an instant; it needs a constant reminder, and the path toward it is uncertain. People do not want uncertainty, so they repress their potential: they know there is something more, but they never go down "the rabbit hole of curiosity," never break out of what they are told to learn or what is handed to them, and scroll memes rather than search for something that could help. The structure is a reversal of time preference: "the instant gratification of that feels good now but hurts later, when your vision is the opposite, where it hurts now and feels good later." The task is to "flip this switch" (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22). Vision avoidance connects the closed mind to the dopamine economy of Chapter 11: the closed mind is not only a matter of beliefs but of what one does with attention when the alternative is uncomfortable.

#### The positive pole: deep thinking, critical thinking, genius thinking

The positive counterpart of stupid thinking has also changed names, while keeping a stable core: the formula of holding ideas in the **realm of possibility**.

In the 2022 "Matrix" video Koe defines **deep thinking** with three components. The first is "becoming aware of the limits your mind creates," because "that's all humans do: we create limitations, we create distinctions between two different things; that's how we learn." The second is "holding everything you perceive in the realm of possibility until filtered with direct experience." He calls this second component critical thinking: not taking what you think as law, holding it in your head without labeling, judging or attaching whatever meaning you can, until you come into direct experience that will "ground that information." The third is questioning everything, "especially thoughts, beliefs and biases that you think are absolutely true without exception." His summary: "closed-mindedness is the antagonist of deep thinking" (**Source:** `The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md`, 2022-11-20).

The first component deserves attention because it is not a criticism of distinctions. Making distinctions is how humans learn; the problem is forgetting that a distinction is something the mind made rather than something it found. Deep thinking, in this sense, begins with a kind of second-order awareness: knowing that your categories are yours.

In January 2023 the same formula reappears as "practical step 1" of a video on distraction, under the name **Critical Thinking 101**: "Hold all thoughts, ideas and opinions in the realm of possibility until filtered through direct experience." He adds a permission that explains what the formula is for: "you are allowed to let things marinate in your mind without identifying with any side of the argument," regardless of how urgent others make picking a side seem. You do not have to prove something right or wrong instantly (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22).

The **realm of possibility** is one of the author's central coined terms. It names a mental space in which an idea is held without being accepted or rejected, until direct experience filters it. Two features of the formula matter. The space is temporary: ideas are held there "until" experience decides, so the formula is not a recommendation of permanent indecision. And the filter is experience, not argument: the formula assumes that most questions worth asking about one's life cannot be settled by debate and have to be tested.

In July 2025 Koe offers a definition of thinking itself that frames the whole contrast as a conversation: "Thinking, actual thinking, not overthinking, is a conversation with yourself. Stupid thinking is a conversation that turns into an argument because you latch on to one answer and aren't able to see the bigger picture. Genius thinking is a conversation that accurately covers multiple perspectives and synthesizes the best parts of them into one." In the same passage he compares the quadrant model to the Japanese concept of ikigai ("what am I interested in, what am I passionate about, what does the world need") "but on steroids," and estimates that "like 90% of people, if not more, struggle to figure out what they want in life" (**Source:** `This mental model will make you think like a strategic genius.md`, 2025-07-20). **Complementary context:** ikigai is a Japanese term usually rendered as "reason for being"; in popular Western versions it is drawn as the intersection of what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs and what you can be paid for. The 90% figure is Koe's estimate, not a cited statistic.

The definition through conversation is useful because it locates the failure precisely. The stupid thinker and the genius thinker both start a conversation with themselves. The difference lies in what happens when one voice in the conversation finds an answer it likes: the stupid thinker turns the conversation into a defense of that answer; the genius thinker keeps listening to the other voices and synthesizes.

The fullest definition comes in February 2026. "Genius thinking, as a definition, would be the ability to hold threatening ideas in the realm of possibility paired with the intention to understand rather than just know." Its mark "is illustrated by the width, depth, and height at which you can think without being cut off from venturing further, often due to holding an idea as absolute." "It's your ability to traverse the full universal web of ideas, reality, or all potential knowledge, and pull them together into something coherent or useful, if not something entirely new." Later in the same video he restates it in its shortest form: "genius-level thinking is your ability to continue thinking" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22).

Compared with the 2022 definition, the 2026 one adds three things. The ideas to be held are specifically *threatening* ones, which acknowledges that the difficulty is emotional, not logical: holding a neutral idea open is easy; holding open an idea that contradicts your identity is what requires effort. It adds an *intention*, to understand rather than just know, which anticipates the distinction of section 17.5. And it adds *dimensions* (width, depth, height), which describe how far the thinking can travel before something cuts it off. The last restatement, "your ability to continue thinking," makes genius thinking the exact negation of the 2026 definition of stupid thinking: one stops too early; the other continues.

| Year | Name | Core formula | What is added |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | Deep thinking | Awareness of the mind's limits; realm of possibility until direct experience; question everything | — |
| 2023 | Critical Thinking 101 | Realm of possibility until direct experience | Permission to let ideas "marinate" without picking a side |
| 2025 | Genius thinking | A conversation that covers multiple perspectives and synthesizes them | Synthesis |
| 2026 | Genius thinking | Holding threatening ideas in the realm of possibility with the intention to understand | Threat, intention, width/depth/height, "continue thinking" |

#### Thinking in a nutshell, and stopping the stopping

Koe reduces the practice of genius thinking to something very small. "Reaching your highest ability to think, and thus providing the runway for your highest potential in this life, boils down to the simple practice of noticing when your mind feels threatened, being honest with yourself, and at minimum staying open to new perspectives. That's **thinking in a nutshell**… You don't need any fancy first principles thinking to do that, even though first principles thinking holds some truth" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22). The three verbs (notice, be honest, stay open) are not techniques of reasoning. They are acts of attention directed at one's own reactions. That is consistent with the model of Part III: if attention is the only root control a person has, then the control of thinking also happens through attention, specifically through noticing the moment of threat.

At the end of the same video he turns this into a request: "The next time you feel questioned, I beg of you, this is the only thing you have to do: just pause, look at it, observe how you feel, and do not collapse in on yourself. Just allow it to sit there… You don't even have to come to a solution… You just **stop the stopping of the thinking**. Just don't respond. Just let it pass by, and see where that takes you. So those who practice that will win" (**Source:** same file). The phrase "stop the stopping" is precise. Since stupid thinking was defined as stopping too early, the remedy is not to think harder but to interrupt the interruption: to notice the reflex that would close the conversation and decline to obey it. Note also the permission not to reach a solution. The practice does not require winning the argument with the challenging idea; it only requires not collapsing.

#### Opening the mind: the cup, the fly bottle and Alan Watts

Much of what Koe says about open-mindedness comes through images taken from others, and he adapts each one.

The first is Bruce Lee's "be water." "Empty your mind, be formless, shapeless, like water; you put water into a cup, it becomes the cup." Koe notes that "there's more to that quote," that everyone knows it, but asks how many have sat with it for a practical application. His interpretation: "the cup is the perspective, and your consciousness is being structured by that perspective." He explains why the image works by connecting it to peak experiences, which he says are most notable in two forms. One is the flow state, when consciousness is ordered and you have clarity: you have "the proper structure or cup to fill your consciousness with." The other is opening your mind beyond all limitation, form or belief, as in meditation, where you try to increase the gap between thoughts and open yourself to the world. Later in the same video he extends the image: you should be "like water and the cup and the water," able to flow and pour into different cups and understand them without "**freezing into one cup**" (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22). This is Koe's adaptation, not Lee's meaning: in Koe's reading the cup is a perspective, and the ideal is neither to have no cup nor to be stuck in one, but to be able to move between cups. The flow-state reference connects the image to Chapter 10, where flow is the ordered consciousness of a person absorbed in a well-designed game. **Complementary context:** the "be water" passage comes from Bruce Lee's 1971 television interview with Pierre Berton, in which Lee describes the adaptability he sought in martial arts.

The second image is Wittgenstein's. In a 2025 video on strategic thinking, Koe quotes him ("if I said that correctly"): "What is your aim in philosophy? To show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle." He uses it as the framing image for the whole video. You have a project, goal or idea for your life (making millions, marrying a person you could not see yourself without, losing weight, learning a skill, anything that leads to growth), and the mind is a bottle you need to find your way out of. He connects it to his claim that "humans have an intrinsic desire to break out of one shell, then the next, then the next" (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). **Complementary context:** the line appears in Ludwig Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (§309). For Wittgenstein the fly-bottle stood for the confusions that language creates in philosophy; Koe generalizes it to any mental limit that stands between a person and a goal. The image of successive shells anticipates section 17.5: each escape from a bottle is a vertical step, after which there is another bottle.

The third is a personal memory. Koe used to fall asleep listening to Alan Watts lectures on YouTube (one reason he likes paying for YouTube Premium: he could turn the phone off and listen). "I could literally feel my mind opening; life was better because of that; I noticed more in life." Watts made him zoom out and forget his worries, "and that's what big picture thinking or opening your mind is." Books that cause this effect should be kept and reread, he says, because they lead to a better life "simply by the fact that they open your mind, [and] that is so much deeper than we think it is" (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). The memory gives the abstract phrase "opening your mind" a concrete sense: a felt widening of attention in which more of life becomes noticeable. That links it to the Focus Matrix of Chapter 6, where open, unconscious attention is the mode in which a person notices the surroundings rather than one task.

The practical payoff, in Koe's own account, is concrete. He likes to challenge common beliefs, beyond business, and says it has helped his personal growth "a lot." It let him launch ahead of many people who are still in the same position business-wise, health-wise and in every domain "after three years," while he "developed, changed and outgrown certain methods." People who do not challenge common beliefs adopt others' beliefs (his example is cold outreach as the best and only strategy for finding clients) and get "effective but not the best results for the landscape that we are in" (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04). This is presented as his experience, not as a measured comparison. Its logic matters more than its anecdote: the methods that work in a given landscape change when the landscape changes, so a belief adopted without questioning becomes outdated without its holder noticing. The reference to "the landscape" anticipates section 17.6, where strategy is defined as positioning in a changing environment.

#### The stages of open-mindedness

In the 2023 distraction video Koe presents open-mindedness as something that develops in stages, and gives two examples.

| Stage | Religion | Business |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Belief | Religion is the only path to salvation | Freelancing is the best business model |
| 2. Rejection | Religion is mind control | Business models are scams |
| 3. Seeing truth in all | Religion has deep truths when interpreted metaphorically | "All business models hold truths that can be used to improve the one that aligns with your interests" |

(**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22.)

More open-minded stages, he says, lead to more success. He lists the benefits of open-mindedness: deeper intelligence, heightened creativity, less reactivity, fulfilling relationships, novel content ideas for creators, and the ability to navigate polarizing domains like politics artfully. (The passage also contains a self-aware plug for his course Digital Economics as a "holistic" business course.)

There is an internal ambiguity here that the reader should know about. Koe introduces the examples "in no specific order," yet he presents them sequentially, numbers them as stages and claims that the more open stages lead to more success. The passage does not resolve this. One possible reading is that the stages are not strictly linear for every topic (a person can be at stage three on religion and at stage one on business), while the third stage is still the most mature in each. What is clear is the shape of the progression: the second stage is not the opposite of the first but a reaction to it, and the third integrates both.

#### Pre-rational, rational, post-rational

The three stages of open-mindedness are an early form of a model that Koe develops over several years and eventually attributes to Ken Wilber: the sequence **pre-rational → rational → post-rational**. Because the attribution, the vocabulary and the applications change between 2022 and 2026, it is best followed chronologically.

**2023: the creator's stages and the bell curve.** In a July 2023 video against niche-down advice, Koe says that "everything goes through developmental stages, even your personal brand" (he refers to "the stages of Creator development" in his one-person business roadmap). To avoid overcomplicating it with stages of ego development or Spiral Dynamics, he simplifies with two examples. The first is beginner, intermediate and advanced. You start as a beginner, practice, get results, and move to intermediate and then advanced. "Beginners won't have validated ideas; you're going to have to test ideas," and at the beginner stage you follow a social media growth course. He adds a characteristically combative aside: "If you have problems with courses, go and sign up for a forty thousand dollar degree and see if you get anywhere in the creator economy." If you want a job, get educated for a job; if you want to work in the creator economy, get educated "at a fraction of the cost by people that are in the creator economy and that literally created it" (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04).

The second example is the pre-rational, rational and post-rational stages, "kind of created by Ken Wilber, I believe" (Koe hedges the attribution). They are "best explained by the bell curve meme." On the left, low IQ: "I do whatever I want." In the middle, mid IQ: "no, you have to niche down; how are you going to land clients if you aren't specific; all the agency owners say you have to pick the most profitable niche; being a creator is the same exact thing; you're an idiot if you try to be yourself." On the right, super high IQ: "I do whatever I want" (**Source:** same file). **Complementary context:** the "bell curve" or "midwit" meme is an internet format showing a normal distribution of IQ with a simple-minded figure on the left, an anxious, over-elaborate figure in the middle and a serene figure on the right, the two extremes saying the same thing for different reasons.

Koe then describes each stage:

- **Beginner (pre-rational).** When people start developing in any area, "they latch on to specific teachings and principles and related ideologies that they are taught from the beginning, that are conditioned into their minds; they're brainwashed." This is not only "the Matrix": "you can very well be programmed into an **anti-matrix mindset** by the people that are trying to get you out of the Matrix; you just enter a new Matrix." If you do not continue to evolve or question beliefs, you get trapped in that stage, like religious people who become "Bible thumpers" and regurgitate everything without questioning or understanding.
- **Intermediate (rational).** People question and rebel against the beginner stage. In spirituality this is where atheists come in, "very bitter; they attack and they just argue all day." In the creator domain "this is where people get bitter at the game." It comes in cycles on the timeline: callouts, drama, calling out scammers ("which isn't a bad thing"), and people who complain all day that the game is not fair, then lose followers and get sucked into "the downward spiral of their own doing": "I have to write content this way and I'm not going to do it… why am I not getting engagement; you guys don't know what's good content." His diagnosis: "you haven't accepted the game and the system and how to maneuver yourself within it, and you weren't creative enough to turn it into your own thing." Typical intermediate beliefs include "everyone is a copycat," "coaches selling to coaches is a pyramid scheme" (which Koe calls "just a lack of understanding of hierarchies"), and "high ticket sales is the best business model because everyone else is basic and not as smart as me," which is also "my way of survival, so I'm deeply tied to it, or else I feel threatened." "This can get very dangerous."
- **Advanced (post-rational).** "Lastly you evolve to the post-rational stage": you rediscover the principles and approach them from a place of experience, the way most developed spiritual people agree with and embody the principles in the Bible, "or every other pattern across all belief systems that point to truth, because truth isn't limited by cultural understanding." But they "see the futility in preaching it as the one and only ideology, like Christianity, or else you burn in hell."

(**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04.)

Several observations follow from this description. The warning about the anti-Matrix mindset is self-critical in a way that is easy to miss: Koe's own audience is made of people trying to get out of the Matrix, and he tells them that the escape can itself become a new program. The intermediate stage is defined not by questioning (which Koe values) but by questioning that has turned into bitterness, that is, by rejection that has become an identity. The high-ticket example shows the mechanism explicitly through the **law of conceptual survival** from Chapter 3: the belief is defended because it has become the person's "way of survival." And the post-rational stage is not a return to stage one. It looks similar from outside (the developed spiritual person and the Bible thumper may live by many of the same principles), but it is reached through experience and held without the need to impose it.

**2024: firm attribution and the author's own trajectory.** In November 2024 the attribution to Wilber is no longer hedged. Koe calls it "another helpful model," Ken Wilber's pre-rational, rational and post-rational, which in religious beliefs can be characterized as "bible thumper to atheist to mystic." He gives two cases. Jordan Peterson, raised in a heavily Christian household, left and became agnostic, and is now "looping back around to God," writing *We Who Wrestle With God* and approaching things from a symbolic or metaphorical point of view. Koe's own path: raised in a Mormon household, he became an atheist after challenging those beliefs and "looped back around to a deeper understanding of the nature of reality." He mostly does not call the ultimate reality "God," because the term is too reminiscent of the Bible-thumper or pre-rational stage and people misinterpret his beliefs as that; "but if God were the term, that's the term I would use" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). **Complementary context:** Jordan Peterson is a Canadian psychologist and author; *We Who Wrestle With God* (2024) is his book on the Biblical stories read as psychological and symbolic narratives.

The detail about the word "God" is a small, revealing application of the model. Koe avoids a word not because he rejects what it points to, but because, for an audience at the rational stage, the word would place him at the pre-rational stage. The pre/trans confusion, explained next, is something he manages in his own vocabulary.

**The pre/trans fallacy.** The model contains a warning that Koe states most fully in October 2025: "The pre/trans fallacy, by Ken Wilber… occurs when people confuse pre-rational, or level one, states with trans-rational, or level three, states, because both appear non-rational from a conventional rational, or level two, perspective." The confusion runs in both directions: one can elevate a level-one primitive state to level-three status, and some reduce genuine level-three development to primitive thinking. In religion, level one are "the Bible thumpers," who submit to authority and never question; level three is mysticism or a more enlightened Christianity (Koe adds that the point is religion- and ideology-agnostic). His key observation: most level-three characteristics and principles you live by "are going to also be found in the level one style of thinking. You just don't submit to the authority, and you came to that conclusion on your own." He calls the bell-curve meme, in which levels one and three think similarly and level two objects, "the most true meme that has ever came out." And he uses the model to explain a puzzling fact: why people at level three can look narrow-minded. They filter noise intentionally (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19).

That last point is an important nuance for the whole section. Open-mindedness, as Koe describes it, is not the same as taking in everything. A person at the post-rational stage may refuse to engage with some ideas, not because they cannot hold them, but because they have already held them and moved on, or because they are protecting attention for a goal. From the rational stage that refusal looks like dogmatism, which is exactly the confusion the fallacy names.

The same model explains why some books cannot be received. In 2022, before attributing the fallacy to anyone, Koe said of *The Power of Now*: "You have to be open-minded. You have to be at a specific level of development." In terms of "the pre-trans fallacy, you can't be in the pre-rational stage. Or maybe you can. You can't be in the rational stage if you're trying to absorb the teachings of this book, because it's just not going to make sense to you. It's going to sound too woo woo." You have not had those spiritual experiences, "that direct experience to kind of open your mind to this world and the difference between animal and human" (**Source:** `7 Books That Will Change Your Life In 2022.md`, 2022-04-23). The passage is visibly hesitant about which stages can absorb the book ("or maybe you can"), and should be read as a thought in progress, not a classification.

In the same year Koe gave a different explanation for the same reaction. He is "big on goal setting, whether you are aware of that goal or not." Many people he has recommended *The Power of Now* to say it is "too woo-woo," "too spiritual," or that they did not understand it. His explanation this time: "they're not in the situation in life where the teachings will register in their awareness" in a way that would make them change their behavior to get the outcome they want. Intention is what makes a teaching stand out and become usable (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). The same content can be life-changing or meaningless depending on the reader's situation and aim.

The two explanations do not contradict each other, but they locate the barrier in different places. In the first, the barrier is developmental: the reader is at a stage from which the book looks irrational. In the second, it is situational: the reader has no goal that would make the teaching relevant. The second is an application of the goal-as-lens principle of Chapter 6: information registers only when it serves a goal the person is pursuing. Taken together they suggest that a teaching needs both a mind capable of holding it and a life that needs it.

**2026: the social scale.** In the 2026 genius-thinking video, the same sequence is extended from individuals to societies. "They're both deeply intertwined, because the tools allow for the worldview or the value system to emerge." Physically, societies move from tribes to villages to empires to nation-states. Technologically, from hunting and gathering with simple tools, to digging sticks and hoes (some food surplus), to the horse-drawn plow (more surplus, allowing people to "discover and explore and conquer," which he says is "close to the Enlightenment and why that happened"), to machines and computers that abstract us further from human and animal labor. Mentally, collectives move from premodern to modern to postmodern, or from pre-rational to rational to post-rational to trans-rational. Early societies conformed to authority ("rain gods in the sky"); then came science, reason and valuing progress; now a postmodern world that "deconstruct[s] everything, and we think everything should be equal, when it doesn't work that way." His conclusion: "We need to transcend and include the good parts" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22). The attribution of this social version to Wilber is inferred rather than stated in the source. "Transcend and include," Wilber's formula for how each stage surpasses and integrates the previous one, is treated in Chapter 38.

The evolution of the model across these years can be summarized as follows:

| Date | Vocabulary | Attribution | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | Pre-trans fallacy; pre-rational, rational stage | None | Why *The Power of Now* sounds "too woo woo" |
| 2023 | Beginner/intermediate/advanced; pre-rational/rational/post-rational; bell curve meme | "Kind of created by Ken Wilber, I believe" | Stages of creator development; bitterness at the game |
| 2024 | "Bible thumper → atheist → mystic" | Wilber, firmly | Peterson's and the author's own religious paths |
| 2025 | Levels 1/2/3; pre/trans fallacy | Wilber | "The most true meme"; why level 3 looks narrow |
| 2026 | Premodern/modern/postmodern; adds "trans-rational" | Inferred | Social evolution of tools and worldviews |

The model is the same throughout; what changes is the confidence of the attribution and the vocabulary. It also refines the three stages of open-mindedness discussed above, which are the same pattern stated without a source: belief, rejection, integration.

#### Practicing open-mindedness

Koe does not leave open-mindedness as a description of stages. He gives practices, and they come from different years and videos, so they are best presented as a set of complementary exercises rather than as a single protocol.

**Call yourself out.** In a 2024 video he warns that when you start your own path, "this will be a difficult transition; your mind will close off and react [to] everything that threatens your worldview." The typical reactions are recognizable: "social media, that's toxic, it's all memes and negativity"; "starting a business, that's for people who already have money, not people who want to start making more"; "writing, that's only for English majors, academics and authors." He adds an ironic one: "I definitely don't text and persuade my friends every day; I definitely couldn't provide some form of value and find more like-minded people on the internet." The practice: "when you notice a negative reaction you must call yourself out on your own [bullshit]; you must see beyond the ideas and beliefs that older generations programmed into your head, because they're blinding you from your potential." "Rather than reacting and distracting yourself from problems, open your mind and think through all perspectives, not just the one that discourages action and keeps you a slave" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md`, 2024-07-13). The last clause contains the criterion that makes the practice usable: among all the perspectives on a choice, the one that discourages action deserves the most suspicion, because it is the one that serves the old identity.

**Study general principles.** In the same video, the step he calls "open-mindedness for awareness" begins with a quote from Daniel Schmachtenberger: "traditional education and hyperspecialization is a way to make people subservient to the dominant paradigm or system; study the generalized principles of nature and be a deep generalist." He then recommends *The Kybalion*, "a book about hermetic philosophy," acknowledging that online "you're going to find a bunch of people trashing it." He found it helpful as someone "spiritually, metaphysically and ancient-knowledgy inclined" and enjoyed its esoteric side; it "goes over the universal principles or laws," and he wants the reader to "try to notice those things in reality, so you can start to understand what I mean by saying big picture thinking, or what Daniel Schmachtenberger here says as studying deep generalized principles of reality or nature" (**Source:** same file). The transcript does not list the principles. **Complementary context:** Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher known for his work on the "metacrisis," the interlocking global risks he attributes to rivalrous dynamics and technology outpacing wisdom. *The Kybalion* (1908), published under the pseudonym "Three Initiates," presents seven "Hermetic principles" (mentalism, correspondence, vibration, polarity, rhythm, cause and effect, and gender); historians generally regard it as a modern New Thought text rather than an ancient one. Koe returns to *The Kybalion* in section 17.6, as the seventh pillar of strategic thinking.

**Collect consciousness by transferring it.** The third practice is one of Koe's coined terms: **collecting consciousness** through **transferring consciousness**. "To collect consciousness you need to be able to transfer consciousness and seek to understand, not judge." He insists this is not unique to humans: a predator can transfer its consciousness into its prey and assume its next moves, not by planning but by knowing what mistakes it will make and positioning itself. If a predator can, a marketer can, and so can you. Marketers and content creators are good at it because they understand human psychology, so he recommends studying psychology, marketing and sales to understand other minds and help them through your content, product, services and life's work. He gives a writer's hack he teaches in *The 2 Hour Writer*: transfer your consciousness into the reader or viewer and ask whether they will engage and share; if not, how can you make it better? And an everyday example: the driver who cut you off. Assume something positive: what if their mother is in the hospital and they are trying to reach the exit, while you worry about your comfort ("oh my God, I'm gonna die")? (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22.)

The practice operates on the same mechanism as "smart but dumb," in reverse. The smart-but-dumb thinker pushes every problem into their own lens; the person who transfers consciousness borrows someone else's lens for a moment. The predator example also shows that Koe does not mean sentimental empathy: transferring consciousness is a form of accurate modeling, useful to a hunter, a marketer and a writer alike. Section 17.5 returns to the practice with a clarification about what it does and does not transfer.

#### The true skeptic

The last strand of this section comes from a 2025 video titled "You're Not Boring: How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast)." It presents skepticism as the first step toward original thinking, and it is the most demanding version of the open mind in the corpus.

**The mental gym.** "The first step in a solution to this is becoming a skeptic, and to become a skeptic you need to step into the **mental gym**. Original thinking is difficult work; that's why many people don't do it." "You need to step out of the nice cozy home your mind has adapted to and step into the mental gym. But when you first step into the gym it's daunting; you don't want to go; you're afraid of embarrassing yourself; you're threatening the current structure of your mind that allows you to survive," unless the group you belong to encourages you to step into the gym (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19). The metaphor brings two earlier ideas together. The "cozy home" is the identity as a structure from Chapter 4, which defends itself because it keeps the person conceptually alive. And the gym is the mental bodybuilding of Chapter 15: thinking, like strength, grows only under a load that is uncomfortable. The exception (unless the group encourages you) is an honest admission that environment decides much of this: in some groups, questioning is what earns belonging.

**That moment.** Koe then gives a heuristic that functions as a self-test: "If you as a person right now do not have a moment in your life where you have stepped back, thought, deconstructed your beliefs, realized how wrong you were, no matter how right you think you were, and started to fundamentally change the beliefs that your mind operates on, then you have work to do… If you can't identify that moment in your life, you need to hit that moment if you want to do anything great" (**Source:** same file).

**Reject everything.** The method he proposes is radical, and he means it to be. "The first step to becoming an original thinker is to reject any prior belief, ideology, value or idea, because when you absorbed them you didn't question them. I don't care how right you think they are; if you didn't think them through from scratch, you can only assume they are wrong." "When I say reject everything, I mean everything. You need to hold all ideas in the realm of possibility before you accept them," because "you need a radically open mind if you want to think outside of the box that you didn't know you were in in the first place." He gives an extreme illustration: "for all I care, you weren't born from your mother, because you weren't there to see it." He clarifies that he does not mean the physical body; he means that "your identity wasn't even formed then… you didn't have a distinction between self and other; you didn't exist." And then he explains the purpose of the exercise: "Whether or not what I just said is valid, my mind is actually able to go down that path and discover new things, because I rejected and questioned something that is probably true; but by holding that belief loosely I'm able to explore original thought" (**Source:** same file).

Two features of this passage keep it from being nihilism. The rejection is methodological, not final: beliefs go back into the realm of possibility to be re-examined, not into the trash. And the criterion for rejection is not whether a belief is true but whether you thought it through yourself. A true belief absorbed without thought is, for the purposes of original thinking, as much a cage as a false one, because it is a box "you didn't know you were in."

**Skeptical of your own skepticism.** Koe then distinguishes his skeptic from the familiar online type. "Not the type of skeptic you see on Reddit or in YouTube debates: a **true skeptic** who is skeptical of their own skepticism. Someone who is skeptical about one belief but not their own is not a skeptic; they're simply blind and dumb. And once you label yourself or identify as a skeptic, you're not a skeptic" (**Source:** same file). The definition closes a loophole. Selective skepticism, earlier in this section, doubted opportunities but not the default path; the online skeptic doubts other people's beliefs but not their own. Both are skepticism turned into identity, and identity, by the argument of Part II, is what a person defends rather than examines. That is why the label disqualifies: the moment skepticism becomes who you are, it stops being something you do.

**Firm beliefs held loosely.** For the philosophical root, Koe goes to Sextus Empiricus, "the OG skeptic," and quotes the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*: "For to every account an equal account is opposed, and from this we think we come to hold no beliefs." In other words, "for every argument there's an equal opposing argument." "You can argue in any direction infinitely and you will never come to a conclusion"; arguments usually end when someone closes their mind and gets strict. Once you understand this, "you suspend judgment and cease to hold firm beliefs." Then comes Koe's adaptation, which is a real modification of the source: "that's not to say that you shouldn't act with conviction toward your goals; it simply means that you should pivot or change direction when you're exposed to better information. So rather than not holding firm beliefs, think of the contradiction: **firm beliefs held loosely**." He adds: "There isn't one set thing that I can tell you for how to act… that's up to your own discretion, making mistakes and just learning" (**Source:** same file).

**Complementary context:** Sextus Empiricus (2nd–3rd century CE) was a physician and the main surviving source for Pyrrhonian skepticism. For the Pyrrhonists, the equal weight of opposing arguments (*isostheneia*) leads to suspension of judgment (*epoché*), and suspension leads to tranquility (*ataraxia*). Koe keeps the first step and modifies the second: instead of suspending belief, he keeps beliefs firm enough to act on and loose enough to drop. The formula answers an objection his own method invites: if everything is held in the realm of possibility, how does one ever act? His answer is that action does not require certainty, only conviction that remains open to revision. The phrase "firm beliefs held loosely" appears elsewhere in the corpus in other contexts (identifying with one's goals but loosely, writing with conviction while staying open); this is its most philosophical formulation. It also restates, from the other side, the 2022 principle of infinite nuance: if everything speakable is infinitely debatable, then debate cannot settle what to do, and only action and its consequences can.

**Prove yourself wrong.** The practice Koe recommends for skepticism is to try to prove yourself wrong. He says he fell into it naturally through his interests (health, nutrition, the gym, business). Online takes swing between "this business model or diet, for example veganism, is wrong" and "this is the best new model, for example the ancestral diet," which gets popular and then fades. Navigating social media, you will be convinced things are good or bad and form beliefs and decisions based on what you accept. The challenge: "whenever you notice these things you need to research both sides": what is wrong with the ancestral diet, what are its benefits; "let both ends go down these certain rabbit holes, because you can't just search it on the surface… you're not going to be able to form your own opinion, because you don't have enough other opinions to pull from" (**Source:** same file). The last sentence is a compact theory of opinion formation: an opinion of your own is a synthesis, and a synthesis needs materials. A person who has only heard one side has nothing to synthesize, so whatever they believe is someone else's opinion.

**Study the exact opposite.** The same exercise had appeared two years earlier in a more detailed form, as practical step 4 of the 2023 distraction video, under the name "pause and question." The challenge is to study the exact opposite of your beliefs: if you are vegan, study carnivore; if you are a freelancer, study a creator or personal brand and how they get clients; if you are Christian, study atheists, trying to prove yourself wrong. Koe warns that it is very difficult: you will have an ego reaction and even "a physical threat response" while reading, and you should "become aware of that too," and of how absurd it is. The exercise is designed to force a reaction so that you can become aware of it, seek to understand it, improve and overcome it. "Questions are how you deepen understanding." The protocol: pick a business, religion or political ideology you disagree with, study it, wait for the reaction, and then ask six questions:

1. Is there something I can learn in this situation?
2. What goal are they working to achieve?
3. Does that align with my goals, and does it matter if it doesn't?
4. What is the context they're saying this from?
5. What is the essence or lesson of what they're saying?
6. Can I incorporate an aspect of their beliefs into my own?

Over time, he says, these questions birth "incredible insights," and those who work through them get results and more peace (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22).

The six questions are worth reading as a sequence, because they reproduce the logic of the whole section. The first sets the intention (learn, not win). The second and third apply the goal-as-lens principle: every belief serves a goal, and a belief that serves someone else's goal may be correct for them and irrelevant for you. The fourth restores context, which is what shallow thinking removes. The fifth looks for the essence beneath the words, which section 17.5 will call understanding as opposed to knowledge. The sixth is synthesis, the act that defines genius thinking. The exercise is also a deliberate version of "thinking in a nutshell": it manufactures the threat so that the practice of noticing it, being honest and staying open can be trained.

There is a tension in the corpus that this exercise brings into view. In 2023 Koe also recommended building a "tribe of mentors" and exclusively consuming their content until the subconscious begins to produce original ideas. That advice and the advice to study opposites are never explicitly reconciled. A plausible reading, consistent with the contexts in which each appears, is that the tribe of mentors serves to learn a style of expression (voice, tone, structure), while the study of opposites serves to think and form opinions. The first narrows inputs to learn a craft; the second widens them to avoid decapitating one's thinking.

The section has moved from the forms of closed thinking (shallow, one-quadrant, stopped too early, smart but dumb, selectively skeptical) to the open mind (realm of possibility, genius thinking, thinking in a nutshell) and the practices that build it (calling yourself out, studying principles, transferring consciousness, true skepticism). The next section applies the same open mind to a specific kind of input that Koe considers especially dangerous: advice.

### 17.2 Creating Your Own Philosophy

Section 17.1 described a mind that stops thinking when it reaches the edge of what it knows. This section deals with the most common way people avoid reaching that edge at all: they borrow someone else's conclusions. Koe's position, repeated with remarkable consistency from 2023 to 2025, is that advice (from courses, books, gurus and, explicitly, from himself) has to be filtered through one's own goals, context and experience, and that the end point of that filtering is a personal philosophy built and tested by the person who lives by it. The section follows his argument in the order he builds it: first the critique of advice-seeking, then the image of advice as a map, then the source of the problem in attachment to ideology, then the positive program of creating one's own philosophy, and finally a worked case, the development of a personal writing system.

#### Advice is not starting

A 2023 video titled "Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why)" opens with a question Koe is constantly asked: how do I start a business, start talking to people, start pursuing goals? His answer is blunt: "by starting. **Advice is not starting**." And then, more provocatively: "Asking for advice is making you stupid." Most people ask for advice, he argues, because they fear the embarrassment and failure that everyone else went through to reach their goals. He does not claim that advice is always bad, but he points to a pattern: if "the 99% of people that are unsuccessful ask for 99% of the advice," there is a lesson there. There are books upon books on starting a business or getting six-pack abs, and 99% of people still do not have them (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23). The percentages are rhetorical, not measured; the argument is that the availability of advice is evidently not the bottleneck.

He adds an observation about the questions themselves. Ninety-nine percent of them are so vague that they are impossible to answer. Asking "how do I start a business?" is like asking someone to write four books and make a hundred videos so that you can avoid all the trial and error they went through. "Write your own book, live your own life." Stop doing things that do not result in what he calls **experience boosts**, the gains that only one's own experience provides. "You'll never get a truly actionable answer by outsourcing your entire learning journey out of fear of it" (**Source:** same file).

The diagnosis connects to the closed mind of section 17.1 in an unexpected way. Asking for advice looks like openness (the person is seeking input), but in Koe's reading it is often a defense: a way to postpone the threatening experience of acting and failing. The advice-seeker wants to receive a finished conclusion so that their own thinking, and their own risk, can stop early.

#### The learning process: where advice belongs

The underlying problem, Koe argues in the same video, is that people do not understand how to learn. They never contemplated the learning process, so they ask the wrong questions from the start. He lays out the process in six steps:

1. Have a conscious, meaningful goal.
2. Form the habits that allow the goal to be achieved.
3. Manage the pain and emotions that come with identity change.
4. Program your mind with information relevant to that goal.
5. Experiment with different techniques.
6. Seek technical advice when you encounter problems along the way.

(**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23.)

The position of advice in this list is the point. It comes last, and it is technical: it answers a specific problem that arose while experimenting toward a specific goal. Advice "is only as good as it is specific to your situation." Books, podcasts, articles and social content contain great advice, but only "0.001%" of it applies to your exact situation, because the millions of bits you perceive now differ from the next second and from anyone else's experience and interpretation. "Only once you are in a state of pain, struggle or challenge can you receive the insight necessary to transcend to the next level." Following the process, he says, prevents getting "**mentally overweight** with useless knowledge" (**Source:** same file).

Each step depends on the model built in earlier chapters. The goal comes first because, as Chapter 6 argued, the goal is the lens that decides which information is relevant. Habits and the pain of identity change come before information because, as Chapters 3 and 4 argued, behavior follows identity and identity resists change. Information is programmed in only after that, and filtered by the goal. Experiment comes before advice because only experiment produces the specific problem that makes advice usable. The sentence about pain anticipates section 17.5: insight arrives at the moment of struggle, which is when horizontal knowledge can turn into vertical understanding.

A note on consistency. Later in the same video Koe gives a second, differently structured list (goal → education and effort → experiment → systemize), whose second step is discussed in section 17.5. The two lists do not coincide exactly, and Koe does not reconcile them. Their shared core is clear, though: a goal first, learning and effort in contact with reality, experimentation, and advice or systems only as refinements of what experience has already produced. Across the corpus Koe offers many named learning sequences, with different numbers of steps and different vocabulary; the core (a project, learning when you hit problems, and correcting course) remains stable while the names change, as Chapter 14 showed.

#### Advice junkies and the value of fluff

Near the end of the same video Koe turns on a specific type of advice consumer. For every actionable piece of advice people "drool over," he says, there are a hundred thousand people seeing no results. Stop "masturbating mentally" to actionable, no-fluff advice: you do not understand what the fluff is and why it is important; what one person perceives as fluff another perceives as pure value; often it is the "fluff" that changes lives. "**Don't be an advice junkie**; do what you want and get better at doing it through education and effort." The video itself, he says, is meant to frame decision-making, not to be maximally actionable (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23).

"Fluff" is a common word that Koe uses with a specific sense. For the advice junkie, fluff is everything that is not a step: the why, the nuance, the context, the story, the big idea. For Koe, that is often the part that changes behavior, because it changes how a person sees the situation, and a changed perception produces actions that no list of steps could specify. The critique ties back to the knowledge-without-understanding problem of section 17.5 and to the "mental masturbation" of Chapter 14: the advice junkie consumes actionable tips the way others consume entertainment, getting the feeling of progress without the experience that would make the tips mean anything.

#### Maps, not territory

In 2025 Koe gives the critique its most memorable image. "Most people try and try and try just to fail and fail and fail, because they try to match someone else's map to their territory." Courses, books, podcasts, teachings and advice "are maps; they are guides, not truth." They are useful only if you understand that they are "nothing but a candle in the distance that guides you through a dark room; they do not determine the direction of your steps or if you stumble into a pit of snakes." He adds a further reason why another person's map cannot be yours: you do not have access to their state of mind, to how they perceive the world, or to their identity (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16).

**Complementary context:** "the map is not the territory" is a formula associated with Alfred Korzybski, the founder of general semantics, for the gap between a representation and what it represents. Koe uses the distinction without attributing it. His candle image modifies it in a useful way. A map, in the ordinary sense, tells you where to step. A candle in the distance only tells you which way to face. It orients without guiding the feet, and it does not warn you about the pit of snakes, which you will discover by walking. That is a precise statement of what advice can and cannot do in Koe's view: give direction, not steps.

The positive counterpart appears in a 2024 passage about self-reliance. Figuring things out by himself, Koe says, taught him more than any course or college. "True learning is by directly experiencing something, by not taking people's advice as law, testing it for yourself." This applies beyond business, to health and to any ideology or belief system. His example of the trap is himself: he once got stuck believing that "raw liver is the best thing you can eat." Life, he says, is "a conglomeration of perspectives and goals," with problems and benefits that form a singular perspective that makes someone believe something; and "accepting a specific ideology as law is just closing your mind off to any opportunity you can have" (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). The phrase "a conglomeration of perspectives and goals" explains why advice is so often confidently wrong: each piece of advice is a true report of what worked for a particular person pursuing a particular goal from a particular perspective, and becomes false only when it is generalized into law. **Direct experience**, in this sense, is a common phrase Koe uses with a specific weight: it is the only filter that can decide which part of a map applies to your territory.

#### Pockets of the internet and the source of suffering

Why do people take maps for territory? Koe's answer, given in a 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, is that they attach to an ideology, and that the attachment then defends itself.

A potentially dangerous thing for beginners on social media, he says, is "finding those little pockets where people are giving out… confident advice, which is a good thing, but you have to approach everything with a critical mind, and you have to take into account the context and goals that everyone is working towards." He speaks from experience. He found himself in "a radical pocket of the internet" during the COVID period (information about the virus, masks) and started intentionally exposing himself to the other side, for example researching whether seed oils were actually bad for him. That exposure showed him that "any side of any argument, regardless of health, politics, whatever… can be argued on either side to the end of the world." Picking one side without regard for that "seems to just be the source of suffering": "when you attach to some form of idea or ideology, that is the source of your suffering." When people challenge it, or get results with something else, it threatens you, you feel the need to defend it, and it digs you deeper into the internet pocket (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

The mechanism is the one Part II described for identity in general: an idea one identifies with becomes part of the "mental body," and a challenge to it registers as a threat to survival. The term **pockets of the internet** names the environment that accelerates this: algorithmic niches where confident voices reinforce each other and the other side is never heard. (Elsewhere, in 2024, Koe uses the same phrase in a positive sense, for the corners of the internet where one can build the society one wants to see; the two uses are of opposite sign, and here the negative one is meant.)

The consequence for Koe's own teaching is explicit. He tries to give holistic, non-dogmatic advice: he likes the one-person business model and Twitter, but prefaces everything with "make sure this is aligned with your goals… there may be a better option for you," and avoids "petty little arguments online about the best outreach strategy or the best way to write a tweet." Joy, he says, comes "through experimenting and figuring out a solution, creating some form of value, and then passing it off to others in a non-dogmatic way" (**Source:** same file).

A guest on his podcast put the same idea in nearly the same words. Discussing the extremes of the debate about alcohol (abstinence versus moderate drinking), Sahil Bloom concludes that "it just depends on… where you are, your personality, your own thing. And I think what this fundamentally comes down to is not just blindly accepting dogmas one way or another, like, frankly, don't listen to what I say, what you say, what anyone tells you to do. Find what works for you, take advice from people, but then put it through your own lens, your own set of filters." He repeats "do what works for you" later in the conversation, about a ritual he calls the life dinner and about Bryan Johnson's routines (**Source:** `The Time Billionaire (A Concept That Will Change Your Life) With Sahil Bloom.md`, 2025-01-30). The formulation is Bloom's, not Koe's, but Koe includes it in his own channel without disagreement, and it matches his position. **Complementary context:** Sahil Bloom is a writer and investor, author of *The 5 Types of Wealth*; Bryan Johnson is an entrepreneur known for an extreme, publicly documented anti-aging regimen, which makes him a natural example of a routine that should not be copied wholesale.

#### "Don't take what I say as law": the rule applied to the author

What distinguishes Koe's position from a generic warning against gurus is that he applies it to himself, repeatedly and in the same terms.

In 2024 he advises his audience not to listen only to him or be dogmatic about what he says: "do what makes you the most curious, do what you want, so that you can make mistakes and correct those mistakes." That, he argues, is a faster route to success than following him, failing, blaming him, and quitting altogether (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md`, 2024-06-02). In another video of the same month he recommends thinking for yourself, not taking his advice as law, trying things and failing. He is presenting an argument; if it does not make sense to you, "go do what you were going to do." What he does not recommend is "accepting someone's outdated business ideology as law" (**Source:** `Ideas Are The New Oil (How More People Get Rich In The Digital Age).md`, 2024-06-09). The phrase "outdated business ideology" echoes the selective-skepticism argument of section 17.1: the advice most in need of questioning is not the new, strange-looking advice but the old advice that looks like common sense.

The most explicit version opens the official summary of his book *The Art of Focus*, where he makes three requests of the reader:

1. **Read with a radically open mind.** Do not label or judge immediately; seek to understand through direct experience, which may take days, years or decades.
2. **Question, challenge and build with the ideas.** They will shape your life like any idea does; use, test, iterate and improve them to contribute to humanity.
3. **Do not take what he says as law.** "I am one perspective out of infinite; none of what I say is absolute truth." His ideas served his life in certain situations and may or may not resonate with the reader's.

(**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18.)

The three requests are a compressed version of the whole chapter: the realm of possibility (section 17.1), questioning (section 17.3), and the creation of one's own philosophy (this section).

This position sits in an acknowledged tension with the rest of Koe's work, and the tension should be stated rather than smoothed over. He teaches step-by-step methods, sells courses with frameworks, and in other places recommends paying creators to learn from those who have done it. In 2022 he says that using other people's systems is "great… and this is what I want you to do," and in the same breath that absolute advice strips you of the ability to think for yourself (see the writing-system case below). In 2023 he says he is "kind of against prescriptions, but as a beginner prescriptions are very good." In 2024 he says there is "absolutely no set path that someone can give you… or else that's an assignment," while continuing to teach his own paths. His partial resolution, stated in several places, is that prescriptions are valid in the beginner stage and serve as a temporary scaffold (Chapter 23 calls them "training wheels"), after which experimentation must make them one's own. The map-and-candle image of 2025 is the clearest version of that resolution: the course gives direction, and the steps are yours. Read this way, his self-applied warning is not a disclaimer but an instruction about how to use everything else he teaches.

#### Theory must meet practice

The positive program begins with a criticism of philosophy itself. In a 2023 video Koe says he loves philosophy but quickly saw its flaw: theory has to meet practice; philosophy has to be grounded in reality. A philosopher can intellectualize all day, but can he run a business? What happens when the business has a fire, employees quit, the market shifts and he must pivot? What about "philosophers that don't call themselves philosophers," those who teach how to attract a partner or "text game": do they have results? What about the philosopher who preaches action and not being a slave to mental expectations, but hesitates to post content, or reacts badly to losing a follower or an email subscriber? And in practical life, what if your dog dies, or your brother is kidnapped? His conclusion: "**Actions must align with words**" (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05).

The examples escalate deliberately, from business crises to personal tragedy, and they share a test: a philosophy is measured by what it does when reality pushes back. A philosophy that only works in conversation is, in the vocabulary of section 17.5, knowledge without understanding.

From this he draws the principle that "the best philosophy is that which is grounded in reality and that is individual." One can blame others for their lack of congruence, he says, but what he found works is creating your own philosophy and living by it, not taking prescriptions or hard-set advice until you test how, and which parts of them, fit your life. Business advice he gives may not apply to your situation or resonate with your **level of mind**; then it will not be conducive to action and will only cause overwhelm. So: understand, reframe, act on what helps, and internalize the knowledge and philosophy picked up along the way (**Source:** same file). The reference to level of mind links the principle to Chapter 4. Advice is pitched at a level; if the reader is not at that level, it does not register as usable, exactly as *The Power of Now* sounded "too woo woo" to the reader at the rational stage in section 17.1.

#### The medium and the message

A practical consequence of not taking anyone as law is that one can learn from people one does not wholly approve of. In a 2023 video Koe discusses Ken Wilber, who had made questionable remarks, and Alan Watts, who "was a victim of alcoholism." There is still much to learn from both. "The medium and the message are separate," and "even spiritual people are human too," with vices they battle. In his opinion, Watts's alcohol problem "still aligned with his philosophy," though he does not explain how. He encourages researching these things yourself, following curiosity, questioning, and not forming hard-set beliefs about things most people would label bad, "because we live in a world of relativity and there are no absolutes except for the absolute" (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15).

The principle is the mirror image of the theory-meets-practice test, and the two have to be held together. The test says that a philosophy should be judged by the life it produces. The separation of medium and message says that an idea can be true even if its messenger fails to live by it. They are compatible if one distinguishes two questions: whether an idea is worth taking (the message, judged on its own merits and tested in one's own experience) and whether a person is worth following as a model (the medium, judged by congruence). Koe's position amounts to taking ideas from anyone and taking no one wholesale. The closing formula ("no absolutes except for the absolute") is a characteristic paradox, pointing to a spiritual ultimate reality while relativizing every particular moral label; Koe does not elaborate on it in this passage.

#### Creating your own philosophy: truth seekers and going deep

The positive program is stated most fully in a 2023 video on the future of one-person businesses, as step 2 of a process: **create your own philosophy**. There is enough shallow advice, he says, on making money, getting laid, and band-aids for mental health. The world needs more **truth seekers**: people who understand that step-by-step shallow advice looks good outside but is hollow inside, and who can attack the root of problems, which is often metaphysical, spiritual or existential, rarely the stuff of shallow self-help, because self-help itself came from the philosophical material. "The world is desperate for depth, and you can only create depth by forging your own path" (**Source:** `The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md`, 2023-03-12).

He then explains how, and the method has five moves:

1. **Ground it in experience.** Philosophy is based on experience; self-reflect to notice the patterns in your own life.
2. **Study the greats.** Find patterns in their lives that align with yours.
3. **Set your mind on an ideal future.**
4. **View your problems and daily actions from the lens of your ideal self,** so that you have a compass for decision-making.
5. **Take the best from multiple greats and make it your own,** while staying open to changing your philosophy when you encounter new or better ways.

(**Source:** same file.)

The method combines three ideas from earlier chapters: the ideal self as a lens (Chapter 4), the goal as a filter (Chapter 6) and intelligent imitation (Chapter 1), the conscious choice of whom to imitate. The decisive step is the fifth: a philosophy of one's own is not invented from nothing but synthesized from several sources, and it remains revisable. That makes it the practical form of the genius thinking of section 17.1, "a conversation that accurately covers multiple perspectives and synthesizes the best parts of them into one."

A 2024 passage adds the method for going deep in a specific domain. "Go deep and learn everything about that domain: study everything, read every book you can, read every course, watch every video you can. It's going to be hard not to do this; you're going to be drawn to it." "Dissect all perspectives and avoid becoming dogmatic about one." In nutrition: "collect truth from veganism, keto, carnivore, [a word unclear in the transcript], flexible dieting and other ideologies until you are confident enough to create your own that is closer to truth. **Truth is a process, not a commandment**." In business: "collect truth from e-commerce, freelancing, software and other models until you note the principles that bring clarity to your own endeavors" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10).

The phrase "truth is a process, not a commandment" is the section's thesis in five words. A commandment is received and obeyed; a process is carried out and revised. Note also the condition Koe attaches to the end of the process: you create your own view when you are "confident enough," and the result is "closer to truth," not truth. The method is the third stage of open-mindedness from section 17.1 ("all business models hold truths that can be used to improve the one that aligns with your interests") turned into a research program.

#### Conscious experiments and building your own solution

Where does the experience that grounds a philosophy come from? Part of Koe's answer is that it must sometimes be produced deliberately, even at a cost.

In the conversation with Dickie Bush he describes what happened after he stopped consuming dogmatic fitness content. He "went in the complete opposite direction just to see what would happen": eating more fried food, going out a lot more. It was "a **conscious experiment** where I knew that I may damage my health." He came "back full circle to just experimenting and finding what works best for you." The condition that makes this work, he insists, is self-reflection: you must be conscious of the mistakes you are making. If a weekend of drinking too much impacts your work and goals and prevents progress, and you do not internalize that and let your goals go unachieved, "then yeah, that's a major problem." His conclusion: rather than adopting one specific diet, business model, relationship or ideology, experiment with multiple things and figure out how each can be implemented "in a sustainable way that will allow me to reach my goals but not… take away from other things in life." Do not write anything off for good. "You can't find what you enjoy if you don't find what you don't enjoy" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

The anecdote should be read as an illustration of a method, not as a recommendation to damage one's health. What makes it a method is the pairing of two elements: deliberate exposure to the opposite of one's habits, and reflection on its effects measured against one's goals. Without the second element, the experiment is just a lapse. The closing sentence connects to Chapter 7's subtractive route to vision: you often discover what you want by experiencing what you do not want. It is also the behavioral counterpart of the "study the exact opposite" exercise of section 17.1: there one reads the opposing position; here one lives it for a while.

The last piece of the program turns a philosophy into a solution. In a 2024 guide to the one-person business, Koe connects back to the motto that opens the video: "if you want to accomplish something, build a solution to accomplish it." If you want to start a business without showing your body or face, "then build a [ __ ] business without having to show your body." Stop letting limiting beliefs convince you that you cannot do something because you are not doing it like everyone else. He adds an observation from his experience: the highest-paid individuals are not the ones with looks and glamour, who bank on sponsorships and are not making much money (**Source:** `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06).

This is where creating your own philosophy stops being an intellectual exercise. Advice describes how others did something; the limiting belief says that unless you can do it their way, it cannot be done. Building your own solution is what replaces both. The faceless-business example is apt: the standard advice says a personal brand requires showing yourself; the person who cannot or will not do that either accepts the advice as law and gives up, or treats the goal as fixed and the method as something to be built.

#### A worked case: developing your own writing system

Koe's clearest demonstration of the whole program is not philosophical but practical: how to build a personal writing system. The step appears as "step 8" of his writing-business process in 2022, again in a 2023 video and in a 2024 compilation, and the versions can be merged.

**The principle.** You can use other people's writing systems, he says, and in 2022 he even calls that "great… this is what I want you to do." But "things like systems and **absolute advice**… strip you of your ability to figure things out for yourself, self-experiment and come to your own conclusions, which is what makes a real expert" (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md`, 2022-08-28; repeated in `The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md`, 2024-02-06). The apparent contradiction (use them; they strip you of your ability to think) dissolves once the order is clear: other people's systems are inputs to research, not the end product. The definition of expertise in the quote is the important part. A "real expert" is not someone who knows the best system; it is someone who has come to their own conclusions through self-experiment.

**The universal procedure.** Koe presents the following as a universal principle, applicable well beyond writing: research what you want to do; find different perspectives and frameworks; create your own by literally writing down your steps; test and experiment to see what works for you; and eventually package the system and tell people why it works for you. The steps can concern content structure (for example: problem, context, step-by-step) or work habits (writing in 30-minute or one-hour blocks, in the morning or at night). He takes pride, he says, in the fact that his course *The 2 Hour Writer* helps people create their own system rather than adopt his (**Source:** same files).

**The 2023 version, with the mechanism.** In "I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day," the same step is spelled out as a procedure with four moves:

1. **Study frameworks.** Take out a notebook and study writing and social media frameworks: PAS (problem, amplify, solution), AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action), PASTOR (problem, amplify, story, testimony, offer, resolution), and social frameworks such as DMing people to network or commenting on larger accounts ("just take a basic social media course and you're fine").
2. **Write down a process** you can test for writing newsletters, tweets and so on.
3. **Test it daily.**
4. **When you hit a roadblock, change the steps.** If you need better ideas, for example, look it up, find a technique, implement it, and modify the process.

(**Source:** `I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md`, 2023-05-05.)

He then explains why this works. "You're just creating systems… step-by-step things that you are going to do on a daily basis to do things better." When a problem comes up, "you actually have something you can change and test." A written process turns vague dissatisfaction into a specific variable. The outcome is a system "so impactful and gets you so much results that you can sell it if you want to, or you can just milk it." "That's how you get results with anything… create steps, refine them over time, boom, you win." Refinement also decreases what he calls **mental energy expenditure**: what took four hours of writing now takes one or two, because it has become second nature (**Source:** same file).

**Complementary context:** PAS, AIDA and PASTOR are standard copywriting formulas; AIDA goes back to the advertising theory of around 1900 (it is usually credited to E. St. Elmo Lewis), and PASTOR is usually credited to the copywriter Ray Edwards. Koe does not claim them as his; he uses them as raw material to study. Chapter 23 treats them as "training wheels," and section 17.4 of this chapter returns to PAS as a problem-first framework.

The writing-system case is a small model of the whole section. It starts by refusing to take any one system as law (advice is not starting); it uses other systems as maps; it builds a personal process through experiment; it treats roadblocks as the moments where technical advice becomes useful (the sixth step of the learning process); and it ends with something the person owns, can explain and could even sell. The same structure, Koe insists, applies to any domain. It also prepares the next two sections. A personal system is built by asking questions of one's own experience (section 17.3), and every refinement begins with a problem (section 17.4).

### 17.3 Questioning

The two previous sections kept arriving at the same act. Deep thinking, in 2022, included "questioning everything." The non-questioning mind ("that's just how it's done") was one of the four traits of stupid thinking. The "study the opposite" exercise ended in six questions, with the remark that "questions are how you deepen understanding." And creating one's own philosophy required questioning the advice one receives. This section gathers what Koe says about questioning as such: why he considers it the central skill, how it works as a method for finding causes, how questions can be held over time as anchors for attention, and how AI can be used to do the questioning for you, but only in a specific way.

#### Question everything: a diluted message

In a 2022 video listing eleven lessons from growing a seven-figure one-person business, Koe's third lesson is "question everything." He begins by acknowledging the problem with the phrase: it has been said so often that its message is diluted. It is not novel, so people do not take it seriously. Yet he calls it probably the best skill in life, especially in business, because people take advice as law, do not see results and then call it a scam. Questioning, he says, "is the driver of my success in business." He lists three pieces of advice he questioned:

- "You have to niche down."
- "Wait to monetize until 10,000 followers."
- "You can't make a million dollars working two to four hours a day." To this one he answers: "who said?" Just because someone else cannot, it does not mean he cannot build something that allows it.

The instruction that follows: get limiting beliefs out of your head; stay mindful and question everything you have been told, especially whatever prevents progress (**Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`, 2022-11-06).

The three examples are not random. Each became a pillar of Koe's later teaching: the critique of niching down became "you are the niche" (Chapter 19); monetizing early became his advice to sell a micro offer before having an audience (Chapter 29); and the million dollars on a short workday became the 4-Hour Workday (Chapter 12). In each case a widespread rule was treated as a hypothesis, held in the realm of possibility, and tested. The pattern also clarifies what Koe means by "everything." He does not mean doubting every fact indiscriminately; he means paying special attention to the beliefs that set limits on action, which are the ones selective skepticism protects. The criterion, "especially what prevents progress," is the same one he gave in section 17.1 for calling yourself out: distrust most the perspective that discourages action.

#### Questioning is thinking

In December 2025, introducing a one-day protocol for resetting one's life, Koe states the principle in its most general form. How do you dig into your mind, become aware of your conditioning, and reach profound insights? "Through the simple, but often painful, act of questioning." He adds a criterion for recognizing those who do not do it: you can tell by how they speak. "It's like you're talking to a TV, or the news, or the most popular social media posts." And then: "**Questioning is thinking**, and very few people do it." That is why the protocol he is about to present is built entirely out of questions (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28).

The equation "questioning is thinking" explains why the non-questioning trait belongs on the list of stupid thinking in section 17.1, and why the person who does not question sounds like the news. In the model of Part I, a mind that has not questioned its programming does not produce thoughts; it replays received ones. A question is the smallest act of resistance to that replay: it opens a gap in which something other than the program can appear. The adjective "painful" matters too. Questions that reach conditioning threaten the identity built on it, which is why they trigger the same reactions as the threatening ideas of section 17.1.

In August 2025 Koe makes a related claim with a slightly different emphasis: "Thinking comes from questioning; thinking comes from conversation with yourself" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). This links questioning to the definition of thinking as a conversation from section 17.1. A conversation with oneself is a sequence of questions and answers; stupid thinking is a conversation in which one answer is defended instead of questioned; genius thinking keeps asking.

#### Cause and effect: peeling back the layers

The oldest passage in this section, from 2022, gives questioning a method. The way you become aware of things in general, Koe says, "is to question." If something happens and you do not like it, ask why. Do not just let it remain unconscious and keep wondering "why is this all happening to me": it is happening because you are not aware of what is causing it. Cause and effect, he says, is "a law of the universe." You become aware of the effect (which may be negative in your perception) and then ask what the cause is. The cause "is much deeper than you will be aware of at first," so you slowly peel back the layers until you reach it, starting by solving surface-level problems (**Source:** `Why You Can't Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02).

The method has three parts. Start from an effect you dislike, because that is what you can observe directly. Ask for its cause, knowing that the first answer will be shallow. Solve the surface problem, and use what you learn to ask again at the next layer. This is the questioning counterpart of the "digging to the root problem" chain that section 17.4 presents ("I don't like how I look" leading, layer by layer, to "I don't want to end up alone"). The phrase "a law of the universe" is Koe's framing; cause and effect is also one of the seven principles of *The Kybalion*, which he recommends elsewhere in this chapter, although this 2022 passage does not cite it.

#### Questions as attention anchors

A 2024 passage on finding purpose adds a temporal dimension to questioning. If you are absolutely lost about your purpose, Koe says, "you need to ask better questions to yourself, because questions dictate the quality of answers, [and the] quality of answers dictate the quality of your life." He proposes using questions as **attention anchors**: questions held in the back of awareness rather than answered on the spot. Meaningful questions are not answered immediately; "you have to make mistakes and let struggle and pain lead to answers." You experience life, self-reflect, and notice which answers are piecing together. The questions he gives:

- What do I really want out of life?
- What is important to me?
- What is the highest version of myself?
- What does my ideal day look like?
- What do I have to accomplish in order to get there?
- Does that solve a problem in the world?
- What product or service aligns with both?
- What is the next phase of my purpose?

"Write these down" (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21).

The term "attention anchor" (which Koe had already used in early 2023 for questions held in the background of consciousness) depends on the attention model of Chapter 6. Conscious attention is scarce, but the mind keeps open loops running in the background; a question that is written down and held becomes a deliberate open loop, a filter that makes relevant experiences stand out when they occur. The question works the way a goal works: as a lens. That is why the answers come through experience rather than through thought alone, and why they arrive in pieces. The list itself moves from the personal (what I want, what matters, my ideal day) to the world (does it solve a problem?) to the economic (what product or service fits both) and finally to time (the next phase), which is the progression of purpose described in Chapter 9: from the self to contribution, through a vehicle, in phases.

#### Letting AI question you

Koe's recent work adds a new instrument for questioning: the AI model. His position is precise, and it is easy to misread as generic enthusiasm. The key sentence comes from the 2025 discipline video already quoted: "a good way to use AI is just this: not having AI tell you what to do with your life, but having it question you in a thoughtful way, so that it can uncover what you don't want out of life" (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). The direction of the questioning is the whole point. An AI that gives answers is one more source of advice, subject to every warning of section 17.2. An AI that asks questions is an instrument for the person's own thinking. The phrase "what you don't want" connects to Chapter 7: the anti-vision is often easier to articulate than the vision, and a good interrogation surfaces it.

**Personal context.** In an October 2025 video Koe explains why the questioning has to come before any conclusion. For the exercise he calls the pain and gain story (two stories, one of where a person's life ends up if nothing changes and one of who they become if they change; it is developed in Chapter 4, section 4.2, as a trigger of change, and in Chapter 11 as the first step of the dopamine detox protocol, and it is the anti-vision and vision of Chapter 7 in narrative form), he "loves using AI," but he does not like "having AI tell me what my pain story is or my gain story is, because it needs my personal context." When he gives it ample personal context, it "can string things together and show me things that I didn't know before." Normally, he says, the story comes "through a few months of just going about life and having conversations and noticing little things" ("Oh, yeah, I don't want that. I do want that. Now I have a piece of this story"), but "you can get that faster by conversing with AI in the correct way." He built "an extremely extensive AI prompt" that asks questions to determine your pain and gain story, programmed "to be pretty intense," "not to hold back," and to dig "to the root of your motivation." He gives it away free and includes four more in a paid program (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26).

Two claims are combined here. One is a limit: without personal context, the AI cannot know your story, so anything it says about it is a generic map. The other is a capacity: with enough context, the AI can connect pieces of your own material that you had not connected, compressing months of slow noticing. What the AI provides is not the content of the answer but the questioning and the connecting. This is the same structure as the reading method of Chapter 16, where the AI first gathers the reader's context and only then connects a book to the reader's life.

Koe has built several prompts on this principle. They are best presented as a set, each with its own function:

| Prompt | What it does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Life reset prompt | "It questions you. It converses with you to come up with" an anti-vision, a vision and projects, "and it spits out a document with kind of your entire life plan that isn't static, that you can iterate on and improve." | `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14 |
| Pain-and-gain prompt | Intense questioning to reach the root of your motivation (described above). | `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26 |
| Metatype prompt and Human 3.0 knowledge base | Paste the knowledge base of his Human 3.0 model into an AI project and ask it questions; send the metatype prompt to Claude or ChatGPT and it asks you questions and breaks your type down. | `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19 |
| Coach prompt | An interview phase that gathers context, followed by day-by-day coaching and accountability; can be scheduled through an agent. | `How To Build A $1M One-Person Business Faster With AI.md`, 2026-03-15 |

A few details from these sources deserve mention because they qualify the general principle.

The life reset prompt is offered to people who struggle to think through the anti-vision, vision and plan by themselves. Its output is explicitly a document that "isn't static": the AI's role ends in a draft that the person iterates on, consistent with the plan as an evolving blueprint in Chapter 8.

On the metatype prompt, Koe tells an anecdote: a business partner and close friend plugged it in, "and it made him cry because it went that deep." The anecdote illustrates the "intense" questioning described above; it is a single case, not evidence of a general effect. (The Human 3.0 model and its metatypes are treated in Chapter 38.)

The coach prompt is described through an example instruction he gives to an AI after reading a guide on building a personal brand: "take this guide and let's turn it into a prompt that first asks for information from me, so that it knows what kind of personal brand I actually want to create, and then after that context-gathering phase and interview phase, just coach me day by day into doing this. Make sure I write content. Make sure I have my bio set up. Make sure I report back to you." "You can just turn it into a coach, or another person that holds you accountable." In an agent (he mentions a custom agent, called Eden in the transcript, which he describes as forthcoming), "you can set a specific schedule": it "reaches out to you to give you a source of inspiration and tell you to turn that into a post that you then refine with it, and then it can post directly to Twitter." He calls that "a more tasteful way of using AI rather than just saying 'hey, go and write all this content for me.'" The nuance is worth noting: Koe accepts a degree of automation (the agent may even publish), but the thinking step, turning an inspiration into a post and refining it, stays with the person. The two phases of the prompt (context gathering, then coaching) are the same two-phase design as the reading companion of Chapter 16.

The deep work accelerator is a promotional mention, but it contains a method that operationalizes the first steps of his deep work routine (choosing the most important tasks and quantifying them, Chapter 12) with AI support. Its questioning function is narrower: it clarifies tasks rather than identity.

Finally, Koe mentions a method by Max Bernstein, presented in one of the monthly live streams of his paid newsletter, called the **cognitive fingerprint**. It is meant for people who feel they have nothing to say or no ideas worth putting out; with the help of AI, it uncovers what you do have to say that is stuck in your head. The transcript does not give the details of the method. Koe frames it in terms of his broader argument: "if you're pursuing your life's work, you need to attract people to your life's work" (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). The method is Bernstein's, not Koe's; what Koe takes from it is the same principle as in his own prompts: the AI draws out what is already in the person rather than supplying content from outside.

Across all these tools the principle is constant, and it follows from everything earlier in this chapter. Thinking is questioning; advice taken as law stops thinking; therefore the right use of a machine that can produce endless advice is to make it ask instead. The tools differ in scope (a life plan, a motivation, a type, a brand, a day's tasks), but in each one the person supplies the context and the answers, and the AI supplies the pressure of the questions and the connections between the answers.

The next section takes questioning in a particular direction. Of all the questions one can ask, Koe treats one as the starting point of all useful thinking: what is the problem?

### 17.4 Thinking from the Problem

Chapter 6 ended with a principle that this section develops in full: without a goal there is no problem, and a problem only exists in relation to a goal. Chapter 8 then redefined purpose as the most urgent problem in a person's life. This section shows what that principle means for thinking itself. Koe's claim is that useful thinking starts with a problem, not with a topic, a goal or an idea, and that training the mind to go to the problem first is the most valuable mental habit a person can build. The section follows the principle from life to writing to selling, because Koe uses the same move in all three: identify the problem, understand it deeply, and let the solution follow from it.

#### The thinking process to achieve anything

In a July 2025 video Koe states what he calls the whole of the thinking process in four steps. "If you want to solve a problem or achieve a goal, you need to identify a problem. Then you need to understand it deeply. Then you need to hypothesize a reasonable goal or solution to the problem. And then you need to create and execute a reasonable strategy to achieve the goal" (**Source:** `This mental model will make you think like a strategic genius.md`, 2025-07-20).

| Step | What it requires |
|---|---|
| 1. Identify the problem | Notice that something is wrong; name it |
| 2. Understand it deeply | See its causes, its context, how it looks from other perspectives |
| 3. Hypothesize a goal or solution | Propose a reasonable end state, held as a hypothesis |
| 4. Create and execute a strategy | Design and carry out a plan to reach it (section 17.6) |

It sounds simple, he concedes, but it is "incredibly complex." Most people fail "because they don't understand the situation they're in. They don't identify the problem. They don't see a goal. They don't strategize. That's the entire thinking process to achieve what you want in life." And he specifies the kind of thinking he means: "We're not talking about thinking to sound book smart here. We're thinking so that we can achieve a good life." Over a ten-year timeline, he claims, improving your thinking lets you "achieve 10 times more than the average person just by learning how to think" (**Source:** same file). The multiplier is rhetorical; the claim behind it is that thinking compounds, because each solved problem improves the next decision.

He illustrates the failure with two domains. In business, he cites "a common statistic that goes around where it's like 99% of businesses fail within the first 1 to 2 years" (he presents it as a circulating figure, not as data he vouches for). The reason, he says, "is almost always that they didn't have a product that solved the right problem. They didn't understand the problem deep enough in order to create a solution." In relationships, most fail because the partners do not realize there is a problem, or realize it but do not attempt to solve it, or do not attempt to understand it from the other person's perspective (**Source:** same file). **Complementary context:** commonly cited figures for small-business failure are considerably lower than 99% in one to two years; in the United States, official data suggest that roughly a fifth of new businesses close within the first year and about half within five. The point Koe draws from the figure (that failure usually comes from solving the wrong problem) does not depend on its exact value.

The relationship example is worth a second look because it shows that steps 1 and 2 can fail independently. One can fail to identify the problem at all, or identify it and fail to understand it, and the second failure is often a failure of perspective: not transferring consciousness into the other person (section 17.1). Understanding a problem deeply means, among other things, seeing it from more than one quadrant.

#### Why thinking starts with a problem

In February 2026 Koe gives the reason behind the process. "When you are thinking, you're usually thinking to solve a problem. That's what humans do. That's how we grow. That's what we find enjoyment in… That's progress. And the minute you stop growing, and thus stop thinking, problems start to multiply and life just becomes considerably worse." Identifying a problem "gives us a starting point to think… and it also gives us an aim for our thinking" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22).

The passage closes a loop with section 17.1. Stupid thinking was defined as stopping too early; here, stopping thinking is tied to stopping growth, and both to the multiplication of problems. That is the entropy argument of Chapter 5 applied to the mind: there is no standing still, and a mind that stops working on problems does not stay where it is but accumulates disorder. The problem gives thinking two things it otherwise lacks: a starting point and an aim. Without them, thinking drifts into what Koe elsewhere calls overthinking, a conversation with no direction.

A year earlier, in the original-thinker video, he had turned the principle into a mental habit. "If you want to think originally, just start with a problem… you have a topic, you have an idea; next your mind should immediately go to the problem, and then you can start questioning from there. That is the easiest way to think originally or just think impactfully." He applies it on the spot to one of his own ideas from the same video: take "being skeptical of your own skepticism" and ask, "what's the problem with that?" Then the general instruction: "Train your mind to think problem, problem, problem; that's the most valuable mindset you can create in any situation: business, startups, relationships. If you can just become a **problem solver**, all of your goals are implied. You don't need to be a goal setter… a goal doesn't exist without a problem, and then once you start solving it… you go deeper; that's what allows for creativity." The next step is to "think of an example or a story that illustrates the problem." He points to a template in his note-taking app for "core notes" that structures "a creative and persuasive argument," and promises that "once you have 10, 20 [of them], I guarantee you won't be able to stop writing" (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19).

The claim that goals are "implied" by problems reverses the usual self-help order, in which one sets a goal and then meets obstacles. In Koe's version, the problem comes first and the goal is its "polar end" (a phrase discussed below). This fits his model of the goal as a lens: the goal is what makes a problem visible, but in practice a person usually notices the problem first and only then articulates the goal it implies. The remark that solving a problem lets you "go deeper" and "allows for creativity" prepares Chapter 18: originality, in this account, does not come from looking for new ideas but from following a problem further than others do.

There is an apparent tension with an earlier position that the reader should know about. In 2023 Koe had written that "problems are only problems if you interpret them as such from your narrow perspective," as part of an argument for zooming out. In 2025–2026 he tells the reader to think "problem, problem, problem." He does not reconcile the two. They can be read as applying to different situations: the zoom-out relativizes discomfort that is merely a matter of interpretation (not wanting to get out of bed), while problem-first thinking turns real problems, those that stand between a person and a goal, into a direction for thought. The shift is one of emphasis rather than a reversal, and both are compatible with Chapter 6's principle that problems exist only in relation to goals: change the goal and the problem dissolves; keep the goal and the problem becomes the starting point.

#### The order of operations

A January 2025 video gives problem-first thinking a mathematical metaphor. There is an **order of operations** to achieving anything within reality's bounds. Consider 4 + 6 × (5 + 3 − 2). The answer is the goal; the equation is the problem; you have to add, subtract and multiply in a specific order or you get the wrong answer. Simple equations like 1 + 1 = 2 are like tying your shoes. Achieving goals in the unknown (a lot of money, a thriving relationship, being more social) are much longer and more complex equations. And then the crucial difference between mathematics and life: in life, "you don't have the equation; you don't even know the answer; all you have is a guess for what you want the answer to be," plus the ability to create the equation (knowledge) through experimentation and error correction. That equation is valuable to you and to others. It is "specific knowledge," which can take the form of a product, a service, a book, or advice to your children, contributing to humanity's progress (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Ruin Your Life (Avoid This While You're Young).md`, 2025-01-12).

The metaphor reorganizes the four-step process. In a textbook, the problem is given and the answer is unknown. In life, neither is given: the goal is a guess, and the problem (the equation that leads to it) has to be constructed by trial and error. That is why steps 1 and 2 (identify, understand) are so hard, and why Koe insists on experimentation. It also explains why the equation, once built, has value for others: it is a solution to a problem that others share but have not solved. **Complementary context:** "specific knowledge" is Naval Ravikant's term for knowledge that cannot be trained for in school and is found through pursuing one's own curiosity; Koe uses it here in a compatible sense, as knowledge produced by one's own experiments. The link between solved problems and products is developed in Parts X and XI.

#### Mistakes, levels of mind and holding a problem in mind

How do problems become visible? Koe's answer, in a 2024 video, is: through mistakes. "You can't skip making mistakes. Mistakes are your light in the dark. Mistakes reveal problems to be solved. Problems are the limits on your mind. And as Einstein once said, you can't solve a problem with the same mind that created it." He adds: "Most people skip that part" (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10).

The passage is a chain of four links, and each one matters:

1. A mistake happens (you act and the result differs from what you expected).
2. The mistake reveals a problem (something you did not know or could not do).
3. The problem is a limit on your mind (the reason you could not see the solution is in how you think, not only in the world).
4. Solving it requires a changed mind (you have to think from a level above the one that produced the problem).

The phrase **light in the dark** connects to the candle image of section 17.2. Other people's advice is a candle in the distance that shows a direction; your own mistakes are the light that shows where you actually are. And the claim that problems are limits on the mind ties problem-first thinking to vertical development (section 17.5): solving a real problem is not only a horizontal acquisition of a technique but often a change in the level from which one sees the situation.

In a March 2025 video Koe uses the same quotation, hedging its attribution ("as Einstein said, or supposedly said"), to explain vertical growth: "you have to transcend, and if you don't, you're stuck with that problem, and you won't achieve the polar end of a problem, which is a goal" (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). The **polar end of a problem** is one of Koe's recurring formulas: the goal is defined as the opposite pole of the problem. If the problem is exhaustion, the goal is energy; if the problem is a job you hate, the goal is work you choose. The definition makes the goal derivable from the problem, which is the logic of "all of your goals are implied." **Complementary context:** the saying is widely attributed to Einstein in various forms ("We cannot solve our problems with the same thinking we used when we created them"), but no reliable source for it in his writings has been found. Koe's own hedge reflects that uncertainty; the idea he takes from it (level of mind) is developed in Chapter 4.

A third passage, from June 2025, adds what happens after the problem is identified: you hold it. In a video about disappearing for six months to focus on a few habits once one reaches a tipping point, Koe paraphrases Ken Wilber: new knowledge comes when you keep in mind what you need to know; "keep holding the problem in mind and it will yield"; human history testifies that individuals obsess over a problem until it is solved, whether it takes a week or a millennium; the cosmos seems to be such that solutions are forthcoming. Koe's summary: "**all problems are soluble**." That, he says, is what the six-month disappearance is for: obsessing over a problem (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29). The paraphrase is Wilber's idea in Koe's words; the claim that all problems are soluble is a philosophical conviction, not a demonstrated fact, and Koe presents it as such through the quote. In practice it functions as an instruction for attention: a problem held in mind becomes an open loop, an anchor like the questions of section 17.3, and the mind keeps working on it in the background until something yields.

#### Every lack of results is a problem

Problem-first thinking has an uncomfortable corollary, which Koe states in a 2024 video on minimalist productivity. People do not see progress, he says, because when a piece of content does poorly they say "that's expected, I'm a newbie." There is some truth in that, but it is also a problem: why are you not getting engagement? You have to learn more and solve it. The same goes for a product launch that sells nothing: what is the problem? You have to educate yourself on copywriting, landing pages, funnels and so on, in order to have the knowledge to even identify the problem. It is a constant process of learning and experimentation. "That's the power of minimalism backed by trial and error, the essence of any form of improvement" (**Source:** `Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md`, 2024-08-11).

Two points stand out. The first is the refusal of the excuse: an explanation ("I'm a beginner") can be true and still function as a way to stop thinking. The second is subtler: identifying a problem requires knowledge. You cannot see that your landing page is the problem if you do not know what a landing page should do. Step 1 of the thinking process ("identify the problem") therefore depends on horizontal learning, which is why the learning chapters (14–16) come before this one.

#### Other people's problems look simple

A heuristic for step 2, understanding the problem, comes from a 2021 interview with Justin Welsh, and the idea is Welsh's. Asked whether he has a conscious practice on his walks, Welsh says no, but the walks follow a pattern: they start with his wife's business challenges. When she describes hers, "the answers become really obvious to me… 'Oh, that's really simple. I would fix it this way. One, two, and three.'" Then he realizes that "her problem isn't too dissimilar from one that I'm facing, but I overcomplicate my own solutions rather than being simplistic in nature like I am when thinking through her solutions." Once he helps her solve hers, "that applies to my problem as well": two birds, one stone. Koe connects it to a piece of content he once read: "I am great at marketing other people's business, but when it comes to marketing my own business, I suck," because "you overthink it too much… But then if someone comes to you with advice, it's like, 'Oh, yeah, easy'" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20).

The heuristic works by borrowing distance. One's own problems are entangled with identity, fear and history, which complicate them; someone else's similar problem arrives stripped of all that, and its structure is visible. It is a practical form of the "transferring consciousness" of section 17.1, run in the opposite direction: instead of entering another mind to understand it, you let another person's problem take you out of your own mind long enough to see your problem as an outsider would.

#### Digging to the root problem

Understanding a problem deeply often means finding the problem beneath the problem. In the original-thinker video Koe describes how this is done, starting from how a person gains the experience that thinking needs. "You gain experience by noticing your own problems. You notice your own problems by snapping out of your mindless routine of trivial tasks. You look at yourself in the mirror and be honest about your discontentment. You dig deeper to get to the root of that problem." His example is a chain:

> "I don't like how I look" → "I'm uncomfortable taking my shirt off" → "my wife or husband deserves better" → "I don't have the energy to build the life I want" → "people won't respect me if I keep doing this" → "I don't want to end up alone."

"Now you're problem aware; start researching and experimenting with solutions" (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19).

The chain is the cause-and-effect questioning of section 17.3 applied to discontent: each link answers "and why does that matter?" about the previous one. The surface problem is aesthetic; the root is relational and existential. The phrase "problem aware" (borrowed from the vocabulary of copywriting, where awareness levels describe how much a prospect knows about their problem) marks the point at which research and experiment become possible, because one now knows what one is solving. And the method starts with an act of attention: snapping out of routine and being honest in front of the mirror, which is the brutal awareness of Chapter 7. In the same video, Koe contrasts this with people who study what he calls "esoteric crap" instead of fixing what is in front of them; the point is not that general principles are useless (section 17.6 recommends studying them) but that they cannot replace the concrete problem one is avoiding.

The same chain reappears, almost word for word, in Koe's advice on building an offer later in this section (the weightlifting example), which shows how directly his thinking method feeds his marketing.

#### Problem first in writing: "problem is the inception of gold"

Koe applies problem-first thinking to his own writing more consistently than to anything else, and the corpus contains a series of statements, from 2024 and 2025, that build on each other.

**Start everything with a pain point.** In a September 2024 video on writing frameworks, he says he likes to start every piece with a pain point or problem; if he has one to target, the writing flows easily (blocks still happen). Starting with the problem makes it easy to come up with the point and to back it up with further pain points and illustrations. He explains the mechanism in terms of the reader's mind. A pain point frames the situation and **qualifies the reader**: when someone becomes aware of a pain point, their mind relates it to a goal in their life, sparking a desire to learn more and helping them know whether the writing is for them. Those who stay want a solution; if it is good enough, they are more likely to stick around and invest in further value. And humans make sense of the world in stories: "stories usually start with a problem that foreshadows a goal or outcome," which makes the mind start filling in the blanks and want to keep reading to see if it was right (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08).

The mechanism is the goal-as-lens principle seen from the writer's side. A pain point works because it activates a goal the reader already has; the reader's own mind supplies the motivation. And because a problem implies its polar end, the opening of a piece already foreshadows its conclusion, which is what makes the reader want to continue.

**Problem is the inception of gold.** A month later he states it as a personal habit: he "almost always" starts anything he thinks about with a problem. With an idea, he immediately asks what problem is related, and that leads to many good content ideas. "**Problem is the inception of gold**": that is how you frame someone's mind, capture attention, and show from the start whether the idea is valuable. When people are receptive to an idea, it is because they understand the problem. That is why his videos usually start with the problem: it qualifies whether the video is for the viewer, so they stay or leave (**Source:** `The Death Of The Personal Brand (& The Future Of Creative Work).md`, 2024-10-06). Note that the habit is described as a way of *thinking* before it is a way of writing: he asks "what problem is related?" when an idea arrives, not only when he sits down to write.

**80% of a beginner's content.** In February 2025 he turns it into a rule of thumb for beginners. To build authority, "just solve more problems": "80% of your content as a beginner should all be focused around a pain point." He calls himself out as well: he writes an idea, it looks and sounds good, and then it underperforms. The fix is "a process of programming your mind to think articulately": when you have an idea, immediately think of the pain point (how it helps people, what the value is), whether you are writing, recording a reel or making a YouTube video (**Source:** `Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md`, 2025-02-02). The phrase "think articulately" defines articulation in an unusual way: not as eloquence but as the ability to connect an idea to the problem it solves. An idea that looks good but has no pain point attached is, in this sense, inarticulate, however well written.

**When you fall off track.** In January 2025 he describes the habit as a recovery technique: "whenever I fall off track with writing, it's always: okay, just train my mind to think in terms of pain points, where it's like, I have an idea; okay, what's the pain point?" This makes the idea "a lot more possible to make tangible," especially if it is "a pain point that's happened to you in the past, so you can write to that person more effectively" (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md`, 2025-01-28). The last clause is the link to Chapter 22: the best pain points are the ones the writer has lived, because then the writer is writing to a past version of themselves.

**Openers and two mini-frameworks.** In the February 2025 video he gives concrete openers: "Most people…" (most people suck at writing); "If you struggle with…" (to focus for more than five minutes); "The worst…" (the worst skill you can learn is web design). His best content, he says, hits a pain point: "problems capture attention, problems open a curiosity loop, problems make it 100 times easier to make the rest of the post impactful." If you do not know what to write, take any idea, think of a pain point, and finishing becomes simple. He then distinguishes two mini-frameworks:

| Framework | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Pain point → process | The pain, then a list of steps to overcome it (bullet-list content) | "Most people suck at writing. Do this, this and this." |
| Pain point → why / insight | The pain, then a perspective or novel insight that explains it | "Most people suck at writing because…" |

(**Source:** `Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md`, 2025-02-02.)

The **curiosity loop** (Koe uses "curiosity loop" and "curiosity gap" interchangeably) is the open question a problem creates; once opened, the mind wants to close it, which is why the reader keeps going.

**"If you want…" and "If you don't want…".** In June 2025 he adds a variant built on desire as well as pain. Practice by thinking in pain points and desired outcomes. Start with some permutation of "if you want" (desire) or "if you don't want" (pain point), so that most people "at least have a chance at becoming interested." His example: "If you hate the thought of building someone else's dreams for the rest of your life, start a personal brand." Then list the benefits, how low-risk it can be, and how you can sell almost anything through it (digital products, software, physical products): a compelling argument that presents the option (**Source:** `How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md`, 2025-06-08). The pairing of pain and desire is the problem and its polar end, now used as two entrances into the same argument.

**At scale.** Finally, a February 2025 video on multiple interests shows the principle applied to a whole body of work. Koe focuses on attracting people with similar interests "because I know I can help them." To do the same, take the list of pain points you wrote down, "write one article or post for every pain point you listed out, sprinkle in your personal story throughout your writing, bake in connected ideas to synthesize unique ideas, and of course be persistent." He adds a reflection on time: "You have taste, but it doesn't reflect in your work until you practice for years." "A few years is not a lot of time, considering you spent 18-plus years being drilled into something you don't want to do." People do not commit because they think they have something better to do, "when all that involves is numbing the pain with cheap pleasures to escape your current situation" (**Source:** `How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md`, 2025-02-23). The recipe combines three ingredients that the book develops elsewhere: the pain point as structure (this section), personal story as raw material (Chapter 22) and synthesis of connected ideas as the source of originality (Chapter 18). The reflection on taste anticipates section 18.5.

#### Pain and process

The writing heuristics converge in one framework that Koe eventually describes as the origin of nearly everything he produces. In the January 2025 original-thinker video: "Almost everything I write or speak stems from one specific framework that I call **pain and process**… or problem and process; arguably the easiest one. You start with a pain point or a problem, and then you go over the process to overcome that thing." He pairs it with the pyramid principle, "which helps with persuasive writing or speaking": "if you were to just follow those two frameworks, you would have great writing and speaking, if you practiced." And he gives four reasons for starting with a problem: it captures attention; it qualifies the reader or listener, answering "what's in it for me?"; it is the start of any good story, since "all stories start with a problem or pain point," or open a curiosity loop "because something's wrong"; and it frames the ideas that come next, such as a story or a solution (**Source:** `You're Not Boring How To Become An Original Thinker (Fast).md`, 2025-01-19).

The framework had appeared under that name a few months earlier, in the September 2024 video on writing frameworks, as the first and simplest of four frameworks of increasing difficulty, abbreviated **PP** or **P&P**. It can be used for a social post, for the start of a long piece, or for an entire newsletter or video. State the pain point or problem; then show, step by step, how to overcome it. His example tweet:

> "If you're tired all the time [pain]: stop scrolling at midnight, fix your shitty nutrition, go on walks, move your body, become obsessed with a new interest [process]. You're tired because you're trapped in a routine that has the outcome of being tired [conclusion]."

It is usually wise to wrap a tweet with a conclusion, he says, which many people skip, because the last sentence "locks in the engagement," making people like, share or comment. (He uses "tweets" for any short social post; his Instagram, he notes, is all his tweets.) (**Source:** `4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md`, 2024-09-08.)

The conclusion in the example is worth noticing because it is not a summary. It reframes the pain: "You're tired because you're trapped in a routine that has the outcome of being tired." That sentence is a small "pain point → why" insight attached to a "pain point → process" post, and it applies the goal-as-lens idea of Chapter 6: a routine is a system, and every system produces the outcome it is designed for, whether or not anyone designed it.

In December 2024, in a guide to building a one-person business from zero, Koe gives the newsletter version step by step. Choose the topic (research YouTube if unsure, or choose a pain point). **Section 1**, the first two paragraphs or first five lines: state the pain point; to make it easier, use personal experience, describing when you faced that pain point, walking the reader through the story and what changed. **Section 2**: a list, steps one to five (or however many), or tips, quotes or lessons that resolve the pain point. For each item, write one to two paragraphs covering "the what, the why and the how," so that each is a full point. "That's how you write your first newsletter" (**Source:** `Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md`, 2024-12-01).

The framework has a history in the corpus. Koe's earliest structures for a 1,000-word piece (2022) went problem or lead → context or benefits (optional) → steps; in 2023, problem → personal experience → steps; in early 2024, hook → lead → body → conclusion → call to action. By late 2024 the skeleton had been renamed "pain and process," with the personal story placed inside the pain and each step expanded into what, why and how; in 2025 he calls it the source of "almost everything." These are versions of the same problem-then-solution skeleton, progressively refined and finally named.

A related change concerns the pyramid principle mentioned alongside it. In 2024–2025 Koe presents the two frameworks as complementary. In December 2025 he distinguishes them more sharply: the pyramid principle "takes an answer-first approach… you're not starting with a problem, you're starting with the idea," and he ranks a problem-first framework like PAS as a beginner's tool and the pyramid as an intermediate one; in 2026 he writes that "articles start with the conclusion; essays figure it out." This is not an abandonment of problem-first writing but a hierarchy: starting with the problem remains the default, especially for beginners, while answer-first structures suit persuasive pieces whose idea is already formed. **Complementary context:** the pyramid principle is Barbara Minto's method, developed at McKinsey, of stating the main conclusion first and then supporting it with grouped arguments and evidence. Koe does not name Minto. The essay-versus-article distinction is developed in Chapter 21.

#### Problem-first persuasion

The same principle governs Koe's frameworks for selling. Here the problem is not only the start of a piece of writing but the start of a decision in someone else's mind.

**Persuasion 101.** In an August 2024 video Koe compresses persuasion into four sentences: "Make your audience conscious of their goals and problems. Argue the importance of the goals and illustrate the impact of their problems. Show the benefits of achieving the goal and the pains of not solving the problem. This is persuasion 101" (**Source:** `Don't Find A Niche. Become The Niche.md`, 2024-08-25). The formula has a symmetrical structure: goals and problems are each made conscious, and then each is given weight (the importance of the goal, the impact of the problem) and consequences (the benefits of reaching, the pains of failing). It is the thinking process of this section turned outward: the persuader helps another person identify their problem and understand it deeply.

**PAS for promotions.** In October 2023, Koe offers "a simple framework for promotions… you can just practice with now": PAS. **Problem**: "capture their attention by talking about the problem they are facing that your product solves"; "'if you struggle with' is a great place to start." **Amplify**: "talking about how the problem impacts their life and why it needs to be solved." **Solution**: "positioning your offer as the solution to the problem so they click and read more." Placement: plug products, services or your newsletter in the comments of your post, in your story, or randomly in your writing, as he did with his book at the start of that very video. He recommends studying other copywriting frameworks to learn more (**Source:** `How To Make $1 Million A Year As A Digital Writer.md`, 2023-10-08). PAS is a standard copywriting formula, not Koe's invention; what he adds is its placement in the problem-first logic and the practical opener.

**The selling framework.** The fullest version comes from a June 2023 video, as a practice framework to apply to everything in a personal brand (landing pages, promotions, DMs), "taking the essence of each part":

1. **Identify their goal or desired outcome** and make it top of mind by talking about it in content, promotions or DMs ("what are you building right now? what's your goal?").
2. **Identify what stands in the way.** Why are people not achieving it? Why did you not, at the start? Write it into promotions or ask it in outreach ("what's preventing you from achieving the goal?"). Dig into the pain point: if the goal is desirable, they become more aware of the problem.
3. **Present your solution** to the burning problem, once the goal is top of mind and they realize they cannot reach it themselves.

Knowing the goal and the obstacle tells you whether the person is a fit. If not, leave it alone; if so, "hey man, I have this service or product and I think it really helped; do you want to hear more about it?" He adds a fourth element: ask how their life will be impacted if they do not reach the goal, because "people move when there is a problem in their life that they are aware of, like really aware of." You can resonate with your own experience ("when I didn't have this business it impacted me this way; does that resonate?") and then say, "this wasn't initially meant to be a pitch, but… do you want to hear more?" His example: goal, more money; obstacle, no time, not knowing how to start, or no business model that fits; solution, Koe teaching the one-person business model for $500 over two calls (**Source:** `The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md`, 2023-06-25).

The selling framework is the four-step thinking process run on another person's behalf: identify their goal, identify the problem that blocks it, make them understand its impact, and offer a solution. The fit check is an important ethical and practical detail: the framework includes the instruction to stop when the solution does not match the problem. (The $500 two-call offer is an early form of what Koe later calls the micro offer, discussed in Chapter 29; the price range he recommends for it stays fairly stable across the years while the name changes.)

**Self-qualification, even at home.** In a July 2026 video Koe restates the principle with an everyday example. He always starts videos by naming the problem; that very video opened with "Nobody knows what skill to learn right now." It lets people self-qualify ("do I know what skill to learn?") and it is the start of the story: a novel usually starts in tension, which is slowly resolved through adventure until the conclusion. You can learn it by noticing it on social media, where most posts imply or state a problem. "This is the most powerful thing you can do. It's so simple": start your writing, speaking, post or product with the problem; "frame the situation." His example: convincing your wife that you want a night out with the boys. Start with a problem ("you haven't had a night to yourself in a long time; you want to watch Grey's Anatomy and have a chill night alone"), then continue the argument (**Source:** `Learn This Skill If You Want To Win In The Next 2-3 Years.md`, 2026-07-05). The example is light, but its logic is the selling framework in miniature: the problem chosen is the other person's (her lack of time alone), not the speaker's, so the request arrives as a solution to it.

**Justin Welsh's promotion sequence.** A more elaborate sequence comes from Justin Welsh's 2021 interview, and it is Welsh's method, partly adapted from the copywriter Eddie Shlainer's idea of a "peek behind the curtain." In order:

1. **Prove expertise.** "Every single thing that I've written gets 100,000 impressions or more. Like, I can prove that I know how to build an audience there."
2. **Prove you have turned it into money,** with screenshots of what the business and products make from LinkedIn. Steps 1 and 2 are "all just setting the table… all table stakes… being an expert, having some authority, showing people that you know what you're doing."
3. **Signal complexity.** "Let my audience know that the way that I do those things is really complex," and that the complex systems "exist inside my product."
4. **Paint the outcome.** "What happens when you have 130,000 LinkedIn followers? Well, you get great job opportunities… asked to speak on podcasts… on stage at events like SaaStr. You make money. You can sell products. You can be a thought leader. You can win awards." "Imagine being able to attain all of these things."
5. **Name the gap.** "What's the gap between not having them and having them? Growing an audience, becoming an expert, and having some authority."
6. **Offer a peek behind the curtain.** "Want to do that? Here's a peek behind the curtain. Want to peek behind the curtain fully? Here's how much it costs."
7. **Make it an impulse buy.** His product cost $150 ("not necessarily as much of an impulse buy as my first product at $50"), but "with a nice 25 or 30% discount, it becomes something that people can easily pull the trigger on."

(**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20.)

Welsh's sequence differs from Koe's frameworks in where it puts the problem. It begins with credibility rather than pain, and the problem appears as "the gap" (step 5) between the painted outcome and the reader's present. Structurally, though, it is the same polarity: a desired outcome and the distance from it, with the product as the bridge. The differences are those of an audience that already knows the author (setting the table matters more when the reader must trust you) versus a cold audience that must first recognize its problem.

#### The big problem and the desired outcome

The deepest application of problem-first thinking in the commercial domain is the design of the offer itself. In a February 2025 guide to making a first profitable product, Koe names the first two elements of an offer, and both depend on thinking from the problem.

**Element 1: the big problem.** The first and "most important thing that needs to be nailed down" is the big, **burning problem** related to the topic. Where are people now, and if they do not change, where will that lead? That is the big problem and its **negative outcome**; "the problem is the starting point of their transformation." It must be relatable (they can feel and experience it), relevant, important and worth solving, and research-backed: "make sure that you aren't just making up the problem." Most burning problems fall within health, wealth and relationships (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md`, 2025-02-10).

His example is weightlifting, analyzed across the three domains. **Health**: without resistance training you are more likely to develop chronic disease and have difficulty moving as you age. **Wealth**: if you do not look good, people will not respect you, "because your body reflects your discipline and commitment," so you get fewer opportunities, promotions and trust from high-value people. **Relationships**: you lack the confidence to talk to a stranger, or feel insecure taking your shirt off around your spouse, leading to a poor sex life and problems elsewhere. Then the surprising conclusion: "Surprisingly, with weightlifting, the most burning problem here isn't the health aspect. The burning problem is being perceived as high value, so that you can get what you want out of life and stop feeling so sorry for yourself." To amplify the negative outcome, he follows its consequences: lack of social opportunities leads to loneliness, which affects mental health, which makes discipline harder elsewhere; you get stuck in a job and an environment with no idea why (**Source:** same file).

The example shows what "understand it deeply" means in practice. The obvious problem (health) is not the burning one. Finding the burning one required following the problem through the three domains and asking which one people feel most acutely. It is the same root-digging chain as before ("I don't like how I look" → … → "I don't want to end up alone"), now used to design a product rather than to change oneself. The phrase "high value" is one Koe elsewhere criticizes as popular and undefined; here he uses it as the audience's own language for what they want.

**Element 2: the desired outcome.** "People want a transformation… That's the only thing that sells, is a transformation. I'm here. I want to be here. What's going to help me get there? This product." If your product cannot help them get there, or does not tell them it will, it will not be perceived as valuable. And the dependence on element 1 is explicit: "If you don't nail the big problem, then the desired outcome isn't going to be something desirable, because it's based on a problem that isn't that valuable." So take time with marketing: write about the problem a lot on social media, test health, wealth and relationship angles, and see what gets picked up (**Source:** same file). The advice to test angles in public is problem-first thinking turned into market research: the audience's response tells you which formulation of the problem is the burning one.

For weightlifting, if the problem is lack of confidence, insecurity, not wanting to take your shirt off around your spouse, the general desired outcome is "a body that commands respect." It has to be made relevant, relatable and research-backed: what do people actually want, and why? For men, he suggests: visible abs, a decent jawline, broad shoulders, feeling confident in their skin when talking to others or getting naked. For women ("I can't really speak to this, but I'd assume"): an hourglass figure, a nice butt, visible abs, feeling hot and sexy, "they want their man to fawn over them." To make it more potent, add a **big benefit**: why do men want chiseled abs? Why do women want a big butt? The answer stems from the target persona; for himself, abs are "mostly to look sick when I take off my shirt" (**Source:** same file). Koe flags his own uncertainty about the women's list; these are his assumptions, offered as an example of the exercise, not as research.

**Surface before depth.** The same video adds what Koe calls an "esoteric tip." Usually start at a fairly surface level, because beginners are not looking for depth. Spiritual people in his audience (himself included) go deep and get annoyed when people do not appreciate depth; "it's because they haven't even started." "You have to start on the surface before you go into the depth." With fitness, start with superficial things and then introduce depth. "People start in the gym for vanity and stay for the therapy. They don't start for therapy and stay for the vanity. That's most cases." So talk about the abs and the big butt, then ease people into depth: how rewarding tiny gains are, the parallels between life and weightlifting. Depth will not attract many people on the front end (**Source:** same file).

The heuristic connects problem-first thinking with the developmental stages of section 17.1. A person's burning problem depends on where they are, and most people, at the start, feel surface problems. Elsewhere Koe says that 90% (in some passages 95%) of the market are beginners, and in 2026 he restates the same heuristic in terms of developmental stages, saying that most people are at the survival and identity stages and need to be taken "up the ladder." Meeting people at the surface is not a betrayal of depth but the condition for reaching it, which matches Chapter 9's principle that superficial goals are portals to deeper ones.

**Point A to point B.** The offer elements are a specific case of a definition of marketing that Koe gave in 2023: "The essence of marketing is getting people from point A to point B via a transformation." What lies in the middle, he adds, is not as compartmentalized as in a discipline like biology. In his program Modern Mastery he helps people "work less, earn more, enjoy life"; point B is living a good life. That is a niche goal: most people do not want it, some do, and even fewer want it from him as a teacher, so "people resonate with me; that's niche enough." His process from A to B is not limited to one thing, like building a business; it covers health, wealth, relationships, everything it takes to create a good life. Limiting yourself to one specific thing limits the value you can give your audience. Later in the same video he restates it: marketing is getting people from point A to point B "with a unique solution" (**Source:** `The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md`, 2023-03-12).

Two practical consequences follow in the same video. First, **sell the lifestyle, not the goal**. The key is not to focus on the goal itself ("I learned how to create a website") but on the lifestyle it creates. Koe sells a writing course, he says, not mainly because people want to be writers, but because, as his landing page explains, writing is cathartic, improved his thinking, helped him think clearly, and built an audience that built a business in which he can do most of what he wants, or at least know with 100% certainty that he can create that life (**Source:** same file). In the vocabulary of this section, the goal ("learn to write") is an intermediate step; the problem the buyer actually feels is about their life, and the outcome that sells is the polar end of that deeper problem.

Second, the **synthesis** that makes the solution unique. If you create a self-improvement product like "the warrior program," where point B is living the life of a warrior, what is necessary? Only fitness? No. Only mindset? No. Only business? No. The synthesis could be a culmination of business principles, mindset advice and a fitness program. "What is your way of getting them to point B?" "What is the synthesis that you are going to use to create that unique solution?" Have fun; get creative (**Source:** same file).

The warrior program closes the section by returning to its beginning. A problem that spans several domains (as most real problems do) cannot be solved from one quadrant; the smart-but-dumb thinker of section 17.1 would sell only the fitness program. The unique solution is a synthesis across domains, which is genius thinking applied to a product. And the uniqueness comes from the creator's own combination of experiences, which is why section 17.2's personal philosophy and this section's problem-first thinking are two halves of the same skill. The next section asks what kind of mental development makes such synthesis possible, distinguishing the knowledge one accumulates from the understanding one grows into.

### 17.5 Knowing Is Not Understanding

Several earlier passages in this chapter relied on a distinction that now needs to be made explicit. Genius thinking was defined as holding threatening ideas "with the intention to understand rather than just know." The advice junkie consumes knowledge that does not change anything. The philosopher who cannot run a business has theory that has not met practice. And problems were described as limits on the mind that can only be solved from a higher level. Behind all of these lies one distinction that Koe formulates in several ways between 2022 and 2026: **knowing** is not **understanding**. This section reconstructs the distinction in its successive formulations and then connects it to the model of development it belongs to, the difference between horizontal and vertical growth.

#### Reading the rules versus playing the game

The simplest version comes from a July 2023 video. "I can learn all day and mentally masturbate," Koe says, but executing means "bridging that gap, so turning knowledge into understanding through experience and actually playing the game." "I can read the rules of the game all day long and feel like I have a grasp on it, but unless I play that game for years, then I'm not going to get good at it," and that includes the skill of making money (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04).

The image is precise about what knowledge gives and what it does not. Reading the rules produces a real "grasp," a feeling of understanding that is not entirely false: you do know the rules. What it does not produce is the ability to play, which only years of playing can give. The verb "mentally masturbate" (one of Koe's recurring terms, also used in section 17.2 for the advice junkie) names the problem: an activity that produces the sensation of the real thing without its result. The game image also connects to Chapter 10, where life is framed as a game; in that frame, knowledge is the rulebook and understanding is skill at play.

In a 2022 video Koe had placed the problem in its historical context. We love to hoard "theory, orthodoxy and conceptual understanding of things without the direct experience to back them," but "life doesn't work that way." His examples come from social media: commenters who write "a book's worth of information" on fitness but whose profile shows they have never been "within 10 feet of a barbell," and people giving business advice whose own business looks like it has never been "within a 10-foot radius of a customer" (**Source:** `Learn New Skills FAST (How I Remember Everything I Learn).md`, 2022-11-27). The modern problem, in this reading, is an abundance of information: never has it been easier to accumulate knowledge without the experience that would turn it into understanding, and never has it been easier to speak with apparent authority from knowledge alone.

#### Knowledge without skill, and awareness until execution

Two passages from 2023 sharpen the claim into principles.

The first, from a February 2023 video: if you do not make an actual effort to create or practice in the real world with your skills, "you are not acquiring skills, you're acquiring knowledge." Both are useful, "but **knowledge without skill is absolutely worthless**, because you have to be able to take what's in your mind and then use your hands to actually build something tangible; that's what humans do." His closing advice: "learn, act, repeat, and don't let your mind get trapped in a thought loop of things that don't matter" (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). The concession ("both are useful") and the verdict ("absolutely worthless") seem to conflict, but the qualifier "without skill" resolves it: knowledge is useful as material for skill and worthless as a substitute for it. "Learn, act, repeat" is the minimal cycle that turns one into the other, the same cycle Chapter 14 called build to learn.

The second comes at the end of Koe's three-hour conversation with Dickie Bush in March 2023. If anything stuck, Koe says, "think about it, distill it and create with it." He dislikes plagiarism, but likes people "building on the ideas… using them in your life and… plugging them into your own perspective and then building from there." In his book, he says, he invites readers to write a chapter, or even a book, on one of his chapters, just not to take it without "your own lessons or perspective." Dickie Bush adds a warning: "the worst thing someone could do is watch this full three hours and feel productive because of it and do nothing." Koe then states the principle: "everything is awareness… **all information is just awareness until you execute** and… make it real; even the most actionable advice in the world, it's still awareness." As a minimum exercise he proposes writing ten takeaways, or starting with one: "that makes your effort stored… your attention stored in some way." Bush adds his own rule: from any podcast he must leave with three takeaways, otherwise "I might as well have just been listening to music or junk food" (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16).

"Awareness" here is a word Koe uses with a specific sense. In Chapter 2 awareness was the first step out of the Matrix; here it names the status of all information before action: it makes you aware of something, and that is all it does. The claim that "even the most actionable advice in the world" is still only awareness is the strongest form of the critique of advice in section 17.2: actionability is a property of what you do with information, not of the information. The ten takeaways are a bridge between pure awareness and execution: writing them converts attention into something that persists (**stored attention**), and the instruction to build on ideas rather than copy them is the creative counterpart, the beginning of the personal synthesis that section 17.2 called creating your own philosophy.

#### Education and effort go together

In the August 2023 video on advice, the second of Koe's two lists (mentioned in section 17.2) makes the same point as a process step. Approaching a goal, **education and effort** create understanding together. Buy general books, listen to podcasts, follow accounts that offer various perspectives; pursue your goal with the time you have, and implement what you learn. "**Action solidifies true knowledge**" and adjusts your decision-making. You will suck at first; that is the reality of any new endeavor (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23).

Three details refine the earlier passages. The education he recommends is deliberately varied ("various perspectives"), which is the antidote to the one-dimensional thinking of section 17.1. The verb "solidifies" suggests that knowledge without action is not false but fluid, unfixed, and that action is what gives it form. And the admission that you will "suck at first" is a necessary part of the process, not a flaw in it: the gap between what you know and what you can do is where understanding is built, and it can only be crossed by doing badly for a while.

#### Concepts let you know; experience lets you understand

In March 2023 Koe places the distinction inside a larger contrast between concept and experience: "concepts allow you to know; experience allows you to understand." Knowledge is a layer on top of reality, a symbol representative of an experience; understanding is an inherent aspect of human consciousness, internal rather than external. Along with depth of focus, the ability to build and the ability to plan into the future, he counts the ability to understand among the characteristics that set humans apart. And he explains why people prefer knowing: they love to conceptualize without moving forward or experiencing life (the classic **armchair philosopher**), because it is uncomfortable to solve problems and achieve goals, which would lead to experiencing more of what he calls "the human experience," something he argues we are here to do. Instead they build layers on reality into a "**mental house** or intellectual structure," a "little bubble of mental comfort" (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05).

This version adds a motive to the description. Knowledge without understanding is not only a mistake but a refuge. The armchair philosopher builds a mental house in order not to step outside, which is the exact opposite of the mental gym of section 17.1 ("step out of the nice cozy home your mind has adapted to"). The two images, from 2023 and 2025, describe the same choice from the two sides: stay in the comfortable structure of concepts, or leave it for the uncomfortable work of experience. The passage also connects to the theory-meets-practice argument of section 17.2, which comes from the same video: the philosopher who cannot run a business is the armchair philosopher whose house has not been tested by weather.

#### Upward and inward; all understanding is metaphorical

The official summary of *The Art of Focus* (2024) states the distinction as three principles, numbered 13 to 15 in the book's list:

- (13) "One can know a lot but understand very little; **knowledge is constructed upward, while understanding is discovered inward**."
- (14) "The only way to make novel discoveries is to explore the unknown; reality exploration must become a daily practice; you must puncture the default path you've been conditioned to live."
- (15) "The world needs more holistic synthesizers: those who explore reality and connect the dots between multiple domains, rather than getting stuck in one pocket and basing their entire identity around that."

(**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18.)

The three principles form a sequence. Principle 13 gives the distinction a direction: knowledge is stacked up, layer on layer, like a building; understanding is found by going in, like a discovery. Principle 14 says how understanding is found: by exploring the unknown daily, which requires breaking the default path, because the default path, by definition, only exposes a person to the known. Principle 15 names the kind of person this produces: the **holistic synthesizer**, who connects several domains rather than building an identity in one "pocket" (the same word as the pockets of the internet in section 17.2). This is the positive figure that the smart-but-dumb thinker of section 17.1 fails to become, and it anticipates the synthesizer of Chapter 18.

The deepest version of the distinction comes from the January 2023 distraction video, where it clarifies what the practice of **transferring consciousness** (section 17.1) actually transfers. Focus on the big picture, Koe says; people get wrapped up in technical details. We do not transfer consciousness into another person to steal their knowledge, their words or their articulation. We want to understand the essence or experience of the situation, note patterns, understand their actions and observe the outcomes. Collected over time, these improve decision-making "tenfold." He then clarifies with an example: transferring your consciousness into someone who speaks another language will not teach you the language, because "we're talking about understanding here, not knowledge." Knowledge is built up, "a mental construction"; understanding is "an inherent part of human consciousness"; we seek the essence beyond mere human existence. His example of what understanding grasps is strength. Strength shows not only in a bodybuilder but in the universe holding up the world through a gravitational constant, in the strength of a bear, in the strength of a mother: a pattern understood through a metaphorical lens. "**All understanding is metaphorical**" (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22).

The passage makes several claims at once, and they should be separated.

1. **What is transferred.** Transferring consciousness does not copy content (words, facts, a language). It extracts essence, patterns and the links between actions and outcomes. That is why the predator can do it without language: it does not know what the prey knows; it understands how the prey behaves.
2. **Knowledge as construction.** Knowledge is assembled from symbols, which is why it can be stored, transmitted and accumulated (upward).
3. **Understanding as inherent.** Understanding is a capacity of consciousness that recognizes the same pattern across very different contexts (inward).
4. **Understanding as metaphor.** Because understanding recognizes patterns across contexts, it works by metaphor: to understand strength is to see what the bodybuilder, gravity, the bear and the mother have in common.

The last claim explains something about Koe's own way of teaching that this book has repeatedly noted: his explanations proceed by metaphor (the cup, the fly-bottle, the mental gym, the candle, the operating system), and in his view this is not decoration. If understanding is metaphorical, then a metaphor is the vehicle by which understanding passes from one mind to another, while facts pass only knowledge. The claim is a philosophical position, stated without argument beyond the example; it is consistent with the rest of his thought but should be read as his view, not as an established result. (Elsewhere in the corpus he phrases a related idea by saying that knowledge consists of symbols that represent, but are not, what they represent.)

#### Knowing is horizontal, understanding is vertical: the operating system

In February 2026 the distinction is mapped onto Koe's developmental vocabulary. He prefaces it with a disclaimer ("not an explicit definition") and then states it. Knowing is horizontal development, domain expertise: "accumulating facts, memorizing insights, studying the textbook." Understanding is vertical: "the sophistication of your **cognitive operating system**." "You can know a lot and understand very little. You can also understand deeply and know relatively little. But if you understand more than you know, you can still act more effectively than the person who thinks they know everything." This explains why smart people get stuck: they read books and collect knowledge, "but they can't see anything above that. They can't see into other domains" (**Source:** `How To Think Like A Strategic Genius (5-Dimensional Thinking).md`, 2026-02-22).

His metaphor is technological. Smart people are "installing new apps onto their operating system without upgrading the operating system itself." "When you have an old iPhone and you download a new app, that app is probably going to be very slow… A lot of the features are developed for the newer operating system. So when it comes to your own mind, you have to do the same" (**Source:** same file).

The metaphor adds something the earlier versions lacked: an explanation of why more knowledge can fail to help. Apps (pieces of knowledge, techniques, frameworks) are designed for a certain level of system; installed on an older one, they run slowly or not at all. A sophisticated framework learned by someone whose understanding has not grown to meet it produces confusion rather than results, which is the experience of the beginner who reads advanced advice and feels overwhelmed (the "level of mind" problem of section 17.2). It also explains the claim that someone who understands more than they know can outperform someone who knows more: a newer operating system with few apps can do more than an old one crowded with them. The phrase "see into other domains" ties the metaphor back to the smart-but-dumb phenomenon: knowledge stays within a domain; understanding is what lets a person recognize when a problem belongs to another one.

The operating-system image also returns to Part I. There, conditioning was described as an inherited operating system installed in childhood. Here, vertical development is the upgrade of that system. Knowing more does not upgrade it; understanding does.

| Formulation | Year | Knowing / knowledge | Understanding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rules vs playing | 2023 | Reading the rules of the game | Playing it for years |
| Concept vs experience | 2023 | Concepts; a layer on reality; the armchair philosopher's mental house | Experience; inherent to consciousness |
| Upward vs inward | 2023–2024 | Constructed upward; a mental construction | Discovered inward; essence, patterns; metaphorical |
| Horizontal vs vertical | 2026 | Horizontal: facts, memorization, the textbook (apps) | Vertical: the sophistication of the cognitive operating system |

The four formulations describe the same distinction with different images; Koe never signals a change of position between them.

#### Horizontal and vertical growth

The 2026 mapping depends on a broader distinction Koe had been using since 2024: horizontal versus vertical growth (or development). It is the frame in which knowing and understanding take their place.

In March 2025 he states it as his own formulation. "There are two types of growth: horizontal and vertical." **Horizontal growth** is "expanding within the level of development you are in": learning, acquiring skills, acquiring knowledge. **Vertical growth** is "transcending to a new level after a period of struggle, pain or wrestling with your own beliefs, to the point of needing to adopt a new perspective to view the struggle from a new light, so that you can overcome it." The key to growth, he says, is continuous self-education, skill acquisition, and "not numbing yourself from the pain or problems that spring up." You increase your level of development by making mistakes and realizing them (not ignoring or suppressing them), "leaning into them and self-correcting toward the evolving string of goals you are trying to achieve" (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16).

This definition joins several threads of the chapter. Vertical growth begins in struggle, which is why section 17.2 said that insight arrives only "once you are in a state of pain, struggle or challenge." It requires adopting a new perspective, which is what genius thinking practices. It proceeds through mistakes that are faced rather than suppressed, which is the "light in the dark" of section 17.4. And it is driven by "an evolving string of goals," which is the cybernetic model of Chapter 6: a goal, an attempt, an error, a correction, a new goal. The warning against "numbing yourself" ties it to Chapter 11: cheap dopamine is, among other things, a way of avoiding the pain that vertical growth requires.

A few months earlier, in November 2024, Koe had presented the same distinction as it appears in ego development theory, with three directions rather than two:

1. **Horizontal**: "lateral expansion at the same stage," developing new skills and adding information and knowledge.
2. **Vertical up**: "transformation, so growth to a new stage or perspective."
3. **Vertical down**: "temporary or permanent regression due to life circumstances, environment, stress and disease."

(**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03.)

His metaphor is a cup, which recalls Bruce Lee's cup from section 17.1. Horizontal development is "filling one's cup with skill, knowledge, etc."; vertical development, "the thing that we're aiming for, a new level of mind," is "expanding the cup, the capacity of the cup." The water is still there, but now there is more to fill, so you need more horizontal development as the vertical goes up. That is "increasing and expanding your complexity of self; that's becoming a more unique individual" (**Source:** same file). **Complementary context:** the three-direction model comes from the developmental psychologist Susanne Cook-Greuter, whose theory of ego development describes a sequence of stages of meaning-making and distinguishes lateral growth within a stage from transformation to a new one. Koe adapts it; the stages themselves are treated in Chapter 38.

The cup metaphor clarifies a point the two-type version leaves implicit: the two kinds of growth depend on each other. Vertical growth without horizontal growth leaves a large, empty cup: a broader perspective without the skills to act on it. Horizontal growth without vertical growth eventually overflows a small cup: more knowledge than the current level of mind can organize, which is the old iPhone crowded with new apps. **Complexity of self** (a term Koe adapts from Csikszentmihalyi, for whom complexity combines differentiation and integration) grows only when both move. The inclusion of regression is also a useful nuance: development is not guaranteed to be permanent, and stress, illness or environment can push a person down a level.

In October 2025, in the context of his Human 3.0 model, Koe gives the same relation its most compact form. The phases of the model "represent vertical development, which is moving up a level or regressing back down"; its traits are "horizontal development." "You kind of need to fill the space of the level vertically and horizontally so that you can move to the next one" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19). The 2025 formulation is a refinement rather than a change: the general distinction of March 2025 is mapped onto the specific components of his own model (phases and traits), and the requirement to fill a level in both directions before moving on restates the interdependence of the cup metaphor.

#### What it takes to reach a new stage

If understanding is vertical, the practical question is how a person grows vertically. Koe answers it in two places.

In November 2024 he lists five requirements. "Reaching new stages of development requires an evolution of your perspective." You need:

1. **Time, understanding and horizontal development in your current stage**: new skills, challenges and goals.
2. **"Awareness of and open-mindedness to explore a perspective different from your own."**
3. **"A fundamental change in who you are through education and conscious conditioning."**
4. **"A commitment to deeper understanding and seeking truth."**
5. **"Cybernetic trial and error, to have a goal that resides in a new stage of development, and solve problems in the way of that goal, and self-correct when you realize you didn't solve the right problem."**

(**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03.)

Each requirement corresponds to a section of this chapter or of earlier ones, which shows how tightly the argument is built. The first is horizontal growth (filling the cup) as a precondition for vertical growth. The second is the open-mindedness of section 17.1. The third is identity change through chosen inputs (**conscious conditioning**, Koe's term for deliberately choosing what one is conditioned by, introduced in Chapter 1, where the main character "creates themselves through years of conscious conditioning"). The fourth is the truth-seeking of section 17.2. The fifth is the most specific, and it ties the whole sequence to problem-first thinking. **Cybernetic trial and error** means choosing a goal that belongs to the next stage (one that cannot be reached from the current level of mind), working on the problems that block it, and correcting course "when you realize you didn't solve the right problem." The last clause is the important one: vertical growth often consists in discovering that the problem one was solving was the wrong problem, which is precisely the experience of seeing a situation "from a new light."

The second answer is simpler and comes with an attribution that Koe hedges. In March 2025 he says: "Ken Wilber actually found, I believe it was through research, either way go research Ken Wilber, read his books, it'll change your life, but he found that the simple act of meditation, 15 minutes of meditation a day or more, drastically increases how fast you can improve through these stages." He calls this "step one to going up": meditate more, practice mindfulness (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). Koe himself flags his uncertainty about the source, and the claim should be read accordingly. **Complementary context:** Wilber has written that meditation practice can accelerate development through stages, and has referred to studies of meditators measured on developmental scales; the specific figure of fifteen minutes a day is Koe's recollection and is not documented in the video. Within Koe's model the recommendation makes sense independently of the research: meditation trains the noticing of one's own reactions, which section 17.1 called "thinking in a nutshell," and that noticing is the precondition for the second requirement, open-mindedness.

The distinction between knowing and understanding thus turns out to be the cognitive face of the distinction between horizontal and vertical growth. Knowledge fills the cup; understanding enlarges it; problems that cannot be solved from the current level are the pressure that forces the enlargement; and the open, questioning, problem-first thinking of the earlier sections is the way of meeting that pressure without collapsing into what one already knows. The last section of the chapter turns from development to direction: once a person can think in this way, how do they decide where to put their limited resources?

### 17.6 Strategy

The thinking process of section 17.4 ended with a step this chapter has not yet developed: "create and execute a reasonable strategy to achieve the goal." Strategy is where everything else in the chapter converges. It requires an open mind that can see the situation from several perspectives (17.1), a philosophy of one's own rather than borrowed tactics (17.2), questions that gather intelligence (17.3), a clearly identified problem (17.4), and enough understanding to recognize what matters (17.5). Koe's two main treatments of strategy are a March 2025 video, "How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking," and an August 2026 video, "The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do)." The first offers seven *pillars* in a practical, identity-centered version; the second, seven *principles* in a more classical version drawn from military, sporting and business history. This section presents the definitions first, then both frameworks with the changes between them, then the management of resources and risk, and finally the premortem.

#### Strategy is positioning, tactics is execution

The 2026 video begins by diagnosing why people who collect tactics still fail. The second reason he gives is that "they don't have a strategy that allows them to use the tactics they're collecting to their highest degree." Then the definition: "**Strategy is positioning, tactics is execution.**" A strategy, he says, "exposes you to the right tactics that you didn't even know about in the first place, or [that] didn't register as an opportunity." Without one, "you're just going to be thrown around like a puppet by internet marketers, because they know how to capture your attention and persuade you that something is valuable, even if it may not fit your specific situation and your specific mind" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

The definition explains the failure of the advice junkie from section 17.2 in a new way. Tactics are not useless; they are useless without a position from which to use them. And the claim that strategy "exposes you to the right tactics" is the goal-as-lens principle of Chapter 6 applied to opportunities: a person with a strategy notices tactics that fit it, while a person without one is exposed to whichever tactics are marketed most persuasively. The puppet image is the opposite of the "create or be created" thesis of the book: without strategy, the person's choices are made by whoever captures their attention.

Koe adds a claim about great achievers: strategic thinking is "the secret behind Steve Jobs, Alexander the Great, military strategists in general. Most great and successful people that we look up to have the ability to think strategically. They may not say that that is their superpower or their secret, because they may be **unconsciously competent**, but that is exactly it." The video promises a life strategy and principles applicable to "business, sports, relationships, anything," and argues that "a lack of strategy means a lack of control over the outcome of your life." He asks viewers to take notes and implement (**Source:** same file). The claim about Jobs and Alexander is Koe's opinion, offered without analysis of either case; its function is to suggest that strategy is often invisible in those who practice it best. **Complementary context:** "unconscious competence" is the last stage of the four-stage model of competence (unconscious incompetence, conscious incompetence, conscious competence, unconscious competence) used in training literature; at that stage a skill is exercised without the person being able to articulate it.

#### Tactics flow from a superior position

The first section of the 2026 video is built around a quote from the chess grandmaster Bobby Fischer: "Tactics flow from a superior position." From it Koe derives a second definition: "strategy is getting yourself into a superior position while at the same time making sure your opponent is in a worse one" (**Source:** same file).

He illustrates it with a chess game, narrated in detail because the details are the lesson.

- Player one spends the early game doing things "that look boring": not attacking, not capturing, just moving pieces to certain squares. "Not all of the squares on that grid are created equal. It's the same with life. A piece in the center of a board can reach many more places than a piece stuck behind a corner."
- Player one slides a rook onto an open lane, places a bishop on a path pointing at the enemy king, and places a knight near the middle.
- Meanwhile, player two's pieces get in each other's way; their most powerful pieces are trapped behind their own pawns; their king is stuck in the middle "because they never found a safe moment to tuck it away." "Player two was only reacting to player one's moves."
- Then player one moves the knight to attack the king and the queen at once: a **fork**. Player two must move the king; player one takes the queen; "essentially game over."

The lesson: "any player who got to that exact arrangement of pieces could have played that game-winning move." Player one got there by building up to it; remove any boring step and "the fork… wouldn't exist. The knight would have gotten blocked, and the king would have escaped." Koe's conclusion is his fullest definition of strategy: "Strategy is the work of making your position better and your opponent's position worse, move by move, until the gap between the two is so large that tactics start appearing on their own" (**Source:** same file).

Three points in the example carry over to life. First, the winning tactic is not clever in itself; anyone in that position could have found it. What made it possible was the position, which was built through moves that looked like nothing was happening. This is the logic of the "boring fundamentals" (Chapter 12) and of the compounding of a body of work over years: most of the decisive work looks unspectacular while it is being done. Second, squares are not equal: some positions reach many more possibilities than others, which is why Koe elsewhere argues for positions such as an audience or a skill with leverage. Third, player two loses not through one error but through reacting; a person without strategy plays the game their opponent sets, and in life, as the 2026 principles will say, the opponent is "often yourself."

**Complementary context:** Bobby Fischer (1943–2008) was an American world chess champion. A fork is a tactic in which one piece attacks two of the opponent's pieces at once, so that only one can be saved.

#### Three definitions of strategy

Koe uses two further definitions, both taken from others, and adapts each.

The first comes from the military historian Lawrence Freedman, quoted in the 2025 video: "Strategy is not a lengthy action plan. It is the evolution of a central idea through continually changing circumstances." Koe uses it to argue that most lives are mediocre "due to a lack of strategic thinking," because most people are not in "the strategist stage" (a stage of ego development, in the developmental vocabulary of Chapter 38), "but you don't have to be there to create a strategy"; you can create a strategy to get to any of those stages. Successful people are highly strategic: they do not let life happen to them, they reject the default path, they choose the game they want to play (both the ideal life and the games that allow them to reach it), and they become the person who can win it through skill acquisition and learning (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). **Complementary context:** Lawrence Freedman is a British scholar of war studies; the definition comes from his book *Strategy: A History* (2013).

Freedman's definition separates strategy from planning, which matters for Koe's whole approach. A plan is a fixed sequence of steps; a strategy is a central idea that adapts as circumstances change. That is why the adaptability pillar below says the vision does not change while the tactics do. It also connects to Chapter 8, where the plan is a blueprint that evolves rather than a script.

The second comes from Alex Hormozi, quoted in the 2026 video as part of the execution principle: "strategy is how you choose to allocate your limited resources against unlimited options." Koe adds his own definition of execution: "Proper execution of strategy is the continuous act of jumping between macro vision and micro daily action, ensuring that the decisions you make have aim and intention. This is the core of what strategy is, doing the opposite of what 99% of people do, because most people do not think outside of their conditioning, assigned goals, or repetitive routines" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

The three definitions describe the same thing from three angles, and together they are richer than any one of them:

| Definition | Source | What it emphasizes |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning (vs execution); getting into a superior position | Koe, after Bobby Fischer | Where you stand determines which moves are available |
| The evolution of a central idea through changing circumstances | Lawrence Freedman | Continuity of intent, flexibility of means |
| How you allocate limited resources against unlimited options | Alex Hormozi | Scarcity and choice |

Koe's addition to Hormozi, the jump between macro vision and micro daily action, is the operational link between them. Vision supplies the central idea; daily action spends the limited resources; the jumping back and forth keeps each decision aimed. It is the zoom-in, zoom-out movement of attention from Chapter 6, applied to decisions. And the reason it is rare, according to Koe, is the diagnosis of Part I: most people's decisions come from conditioning, assigned goals and routine, not from a central idea of their own.

A remark from a 2025 video on his deep work routine shows what strategy changes in daily practice. "For the creators and solopreneurs and freelancers and everyone else in my audience that gets it, most of my work depends on the quality of my ideas and my strategy. And with that, most of my work is done before I even start working. I have absolute clarity on what I need to do when I sit down first thing," because of how he spends the second half of his day (thinking, reading, planning). He acknowledges that "this may not be true for you" (**Source:** `The Deep Work Routine That Changed My Life.md`, 2025-05-18). For idea-driven work, in other words, strategy is not a document but a state of the mind at the start of the working day: the position is built during the hours that do not look like work, and the tactics then "flow" at the desk. The caveat limits the claim to work whose output depends on ideas.

#### The seven pillars of strategic thinking (2025)

The March 2025 video presents a framework of seven components for designing a life strategy, filled out in a template Koe calls a "strategy worksheet." He calls them pillars or principles, and he notes that the last two are "mental" steps rather than practical ones.

**Pillar 1, strategic intent.** "You need direction; you need a new goal from which your mind perceives the world." The steps: (1) think of everything you hate about your current life (the job, the body, the routine, how you feel): "start somewhere"; (2) think of a few potential possibilities for your future life (starting a business, being fit and energetic, living out your ideal day): "don't dwell on this; write a few things down and continue"; (3) choose the **macro game** you want to play. The purpose is to "create an aim, an overarching filter… this starts to frame your mind to notice the right information in alignment with that intent." You are not learning skills blindly: "**your intent frames what you learn**" (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). The pillar is a compressed version of Chapter 7 (anti-vision first, then a vision) and Chapter 10 (the macro game), and its rationale is the goal-as-lens principle.

**Pillar 2, self-analysis.** You need to understand yourself to work with your strengths and overcome your weaknesses, "because your identity clearly isn't serving you here." The tool is a SWOT analysis:

- **Strengths**: what comes easily to you; what are your strengths and talents? If you answer "nothing," Koe says, "you are lying and already can't see past the reactions your lower self is feeding your mind to spit out like a robot; think harder."
- **Weaknesses**: what are you notoriously bad at, and how is it impacting your progress?
- **Opportunities**: what can help you overcome weaknesses and leverage strengths (learning skills, courses, meeting new people)?
- **Threats**: what threatens your progress (friends inviting you to a party, lack of time, fear of failure)?

You can come back and add to it over time; the answers will not all come at once (**Source:** same file). **Complementary context:** SWOT analysis is a standard business-strategy tool, usually traced to work at the Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s; Koe adapts it from the company to the person. His remark about the "nothing" answer applies the stupid-thinking diagnosis of section 17.1 to self-knowledge: the answer comes from the programmed reaction, not from thought, and "think harder" is the instruction to stop the stopping.

**An AI strategic advisor.** Between the second and third pillars, Koe recommends a "strategic advisor prompt," which he says he improved from a previous video. You paste it into an AI tool, ask for feedback on your entire strategy, and return to that chat to share progress; it "will help you overcome blocks and give you clarity on what to do next." He shows an example output from when he said he wanted to build an audience: "it gets pretty granular and very clear on what you need to do." He also suggests it for brainstorming solutions in the premortem (**Source:** same file). The advisor is a questioning tool in the sense of section 17.3: it critiques a strategy the person has already drafted, rather than supplying one.

**Pillar 3, strategic preparation.** The question: how do you change your identity so that you act on your strategy with ease? "You start by immersing yourself in a completely new environment": change the books you read, the information you consume, the people you follow, everything else, to reflect the goal. Open your mind to new perspectives and let them challenge your beliefs about what is possible. Concrete actions: delete most apps on your phone; unfollow most people; buy three new books; choose a podcast to listen to on walks; invest in resources to learn the skills you need; have pre-written messages that decline friends' invitations to go out. The principle: "**anything that does not align with your goals is by definition a distraction**." You need an environment "solely conducive to you achieving your goal" (**Source:** same file). This is the reprogramming through immersion of Chapter 4 placed inside a strategy. The definition of distraction is relational: nothing is a distraction in itself; it is a distraction relative to a goal.

**Pillar 4, concentration of force.** With self-understanding, direction, no distractions and readiness to learn, "now we need to focus our energy on the goal." "The only way to actualize a goal is to build a real-world project; that's the only way to learn as well." "**A project is a vessel for making mistakes and overcoming those mistakes in a structured way.**" The steps: write down three to five projects you could build to achieve the goal (for example, if the goal is to quit a job you hate: build a personal brand, start a weekly newsletter, or start a business in general); choose the highest-leverage project, the one with the most advantageous outcomes; and map out three to five priority tasks you can do daily to make progress on it. He argues that social media is "the highest leverage thing right now; that's where you attract people to your work" (**Source:** same file). The definition of a project as a "vessel for making mistakes" links strategy to the learning chapters: the project is where the mistakes that reveal problems (section 17.4) are produced in a structured way, and where knowledge is turned into understanding (section 17.5).

**Pillar 5, disciplined execution.** "You need to replace your old routine with a new one, because you probably fell into your current routine without knowing it, and if your actions create your future, you are falling into a future without knowing it." Execute your three to five priority, "**lever-moving**" tasks for one to two hours in the morning, or one to two hours at night. Then "everything you do throughout the day should fuel your work": what you read, what you scroll, your conversations, going on walks instead of distracting yourself, eating well. "The only thing on your mind should be strategizing how to overcome obstacles of the game"; this, he says, becomes seamless after the premortem (**Source:** same file). The pillar condenses the 4-Hour Workday of Chapter 12 (a short block of deep work on the tasks that move the needle, with the rest of the day as fuel) and gives its strategic rationale: an unchosen routine is an unchosen future.

**Pillar 6, adaptability.** "A mental step." "A strategy is like everything else in life: it is not static; it evolves with feedback." You need to identify when things are not working, go back to your strategy and experiment with something new. Koe states the logic as a syllogism: mistakes are the only source of truth; experimentation is how you make mistakes; "thus experimentation is the only way to achieve what you want in life." "It's going to be uncertain, because anything that is certain isn't a mistake… if it's certain, it's the map or theory of someone else" (**Source:** same file). The last sentence connects the pillar to section 17.2: certainty belongs to borrowed maps; one's own territory can only be discovered through the uncertainty of experiment. The first premise ("mistakes are the only source of truth") is a strong formulation, and it should be read within its context: Koe means truth about what works for you in your situation, the kind of truth that advice cannot supply.

**Pillar 7, study general principles.** Another "mental step": study the general principles both of the strategy you are executing and of reality in general. For reality: study "the philosophy of mind, or Hermetic philosophy… read the book *The Kybalion* for just the seven laws of the universe… those are like the fundamentals of reality in general and what's allowed." For mastery of your craft: "you repeat the boring fundamentals." The fundamentals are "usually the basics, just done with taste and style"; study, refine and practice them every day. For writing a newsletter or a social post, the fundamental is the hook (**Source:** same file).

The pillar combines two scales of principle, the universal and the craft-specific, and both serve the same function: they hold across situations, which tactics do not. The recommendation of *The Kybalion* repeats the "open-mindedness for awareness" step of section 17.1. There is an unreconciled tension in the corpus here worth noting: in January 2025, two months before this video, Koe criticized people who study "esoteric crap" instead of fixing the problems in front of them (section 17.4). The two positions can be read as addressed to different people: the criticism targets those who use metaphysics to avoid concrete problems, while the recommendation is addressed to someone who is already executing a strategy and wants general principles. The craft half of the pillar, the "boring fundamentals… done with taste and style," anticipates the treatment of taste in Chapter 18.

The 2025 framework at a glance:

| Pillar | Type | Core instruction | Key line |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strategic intent | Practical | List what you hate, list possibilities, choose the macro game | "Your intent frames what you learn." |
| 2. Self-analysis | Practical | SWOT on yourself | "If you respond… nothing… you are lying." |
| 3. Strategic preparation | Practical | Immerse yourself in a new environment | "Anything that does not align with your goals is by definition a distraction." |
| 4. Concentration of force | Practical | Choose the highest-leverage project and 3–5 daily priority tasks | "A project is a vessel for making mistakes." |
| 5. Disciplined execution | Practical | New routine; 1–2 hours of lever-moving tasks; the rest fuels the work | "You are falling into a future without knowing it." |
| 6. Adaptability | Mental | Let the strategy evolve with feedback through experiment | "If it's certain, it's the map or theory of someone else." |
| 7. Study general principles | Mental | Principles of reality and the boring fundamentals of the craft | "The basics, just done with taste and style." |

#### The seven principles of strategic thinking (2026)

In August 2026 Koe returns to the subject with a new list. "How to think so strategically you finally take back control of your life," he says, introducing principles reverse-engineered from "centuries of military and war and sports and business… a massive amount of historical wins and losses." "I've personally compiled seven": **vision, research, preparation, focus or concentration of force, execution, adaptability, and the study of principles**. He adds an important caveat: "That doesn't mean that your strategy is going to be a winning strategy. You can still lose, but it sure helps you in not being trampled over by your opponent, which is often yourself" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19).

The caveat sets the realistic expectation for everything that follows. Strategy does not guarantee victory; it reduces the chance of being overrun. And the phrase "your opponent, which is often yourself" relocates the chess game of the earlier example: in a life strategy, the player who merely reacts, whose pieces block each other and whose king is never tucked away, is usually the person's own conditioned self. The premortem, below, is the tool for facing that opponent.

Of the seven, the passages gathered for this chapter develop six in detail; the first, vision, is named here, and its content is the vision of Chapter 7.

**Principle 2, research.** "In a war, a general sends out spies, studies the terrain, and learns the enemy's habits and tendencies, because you need intel before you act." Koe applies this first to the self: "When was the last time you studied yourself? When was the last time you audited your habits and what kind of life they were compounding into? When was the last time you paid attention to how your mind reacts to silly little things that end up clogging your head space for hours?" Then to the domain: "Most people have zero intel on how to start a business, how to build a career, what healthy relationships look like, how to stay lean and healthy, or what the mastery process looks like." They get a spurt of motivation (a New Year's resolution), go about it blindly, and fail. For a spirituality practice, for example: study multiple traditions, read the research on techniques, talk to people who have done it for years, follow a range of accounts, understand your own psychological patterns and triggers; do not "grab the first meditation app that shows up on an Instagram ad" (**Source:** same file). The principle fuses the self-analysis of 2025 with the "research both sides" practice of section 17.1, and its final example is selective skepticism in reverse: the person who would never buy a course without research will adopt the first app an ad shows them.

**Principle 3, preparation.** "You wouldn't just drop into a battlefield without food or logistics or weapons or support." Identify your resources and your gaps, then build reserves "so eventually you can execute on your strategy." Resources include money, time, knowledge, skills, location, connections and relationships. Lacking money does not block strategy: you can still develop skill and knowledge, and the young have time. People past 20, 25 or 30 have ingrained habits, bills and responsibilities, and therefore little time or money. "If you lack those resources and they're very difficult to get, what other resources can you go after? Where can you leverage yourself?" Before a major change (quitting a job, ending a relationship, starting a venture), build financial runway, decrease expenses, develop the emotional regulation skills you will need, and line up support systems. "You don't just rush into battle… you need to hedge against risk, because every goal comes with risk. And as you go deeper and deeper into the hierarchy of goals… the risk only gets greater." His example: after a one-person business (an information product, a simple AI product), do you build a team, expand, move into e-commerce, or keep a lifestyle business, which is "perfectly valid"? Bigger visions require resources "so that the risk of doing so doesn't absolutely destroy you" (**Source:** same file).

**Principle 4, focus or concentration of force.** Koe introduces it with a question from Greg McKeown's *Essentialism*: "Is there a point at which doing more does not produce more? Is there a point at which doing less, but thinking more, will actually produce better outcomes?" Then the definition: "Concentration of force is the military principle of focusing all of your resources at the decisive point, rather than spreading thin." His historical example is France in 1940. The French had more tanks and troops and "far more raw resources," but "distributed their armor evenly across the entire front, so every sector had a few tanks. The Germans took every panzer they had and drove them through a single point, which the French thought was impassable, so they barely defended it." "**Focus is about where you put what you have**, and in life, you only have so much of it at any given time" (**Source:** same file). **Complementary context:** the "impassable" point was the Ardennes forest, through which German armored divisions broke through in May 1940; the episode is a classic illustration of the principle of concentration in military history. Greg McKeown's *Essentialism* (2014) argues for doing fewer things better. The principle restates the 2025 pillar with a stronger image: the advantage lies not in having more resources but in where they are put.

**Principle 5, execution.** This is the principle introduced with Hormozi's definition, discussed above: strategy as the allocation of limited resources, and execution as "the continuous act of jumping between macro vision and micro daily action."

**Principle 6, adaptability.** Koe's example: you committed to a morning routine, and then your job schedule changed. "You don't just abandon the routine. That's what most people do." "The vision and intent of your strategy doesn't change, which is becoming, let's say, more disciplined and grounded. That's why you created a morning routine. But the tactics do." If a meditation practice or a business strategy stops working after six months, change it: "be in the constant state of experimentation." "Systems fall apart over time. You should expect that." His examples are contemporary: "the Notion system that you built out 6 months ago and don't use now. Or the Claude skill you downloaded and don't use anymore." "You have to be skilled enough to know when something isn't working." On social media people say, "I posted this and it was supposed to work… What do I do?" His answer: "You adapt. You experiment. You keep trying. This isn't a job. You don't just study and train a repeatable set of tasks. You have to have the entrepreneurial mind, the creative mind… taking in what's going on and interpreting it, and then outputting it, and then slowly getting better at that entire process" (**Source:** same file). The distinction between fixed intent and changing tactics is Freedman's definition made practical. The remark that "this isn't a job" connects to Chapter 2's critique of employment as training in a repeatable set of tasks: adaptability is the capacity that employment does not train.

**Principle 7, the study of principles.** Koe calls it "**the principle of all principles**." "Adopt the belief that everything is a skill. Everything is a field of study that you can stack experience in and get better at. Money, mindset, beliefs, relationships, business, social media. Everything that you think is luck is not luck. It's skill. It's only luck because you're gambling and you don't have strategy. And strategy itself is a field of study, with principles that hold across domains." It is "a lifelong, ongoing practice." He offers a reading list: Sun Tzu, cognitive biases, how habits form, principles of negotiation, why content goes viral, how attention economics works. And he ends with a definition of originality: "You don't just copy what other creators do. You study the principles so you can come up with your own moves. That is essentially what originality is" (**Source:** same file).

The seventh principle joins this chapter to the next and to Chapter 15. "Everything is a skill" was the thesis of section 15.1; here it is extended to luck, which becomes the name for outcomes produced without strategy. And the definition of originality as studying principles in order to make one's own moves is the strategic version of the personal philosophy of section 17.2: tactics are copied; principles are understood and then applied in ways no one has prescribed. **Complementary context:** Sun Tzu is the traditional author of *The Art of War*, the classical Chinese treatise on strategy, whose title Koe's 2026 video echoes.

The two frameworks are versions of the same structure, and their differences are instructive:

| 2025 pillar | 2026 principle | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic intent (hate list, possibilities, macro game) | Vision | Renamed; same function of giving direction |
| Self-analysis (SWOT) | Research | From a self-audit grid to gathering "intel" on oneself *and* on the domain |
| Strategic preparation (environment immersion, identity change) | Preparation (resources, gaps, reserves, hedging risk) | From changing the environment to managing resources and risk |
| Concentration of force (highest-leverage project, 3–5 tasks) | Focus / concentration of force (France 1940; McKeown) | Same principle, with a historical illustration |
| Disciplined execution (new routine, 1–2 hours) | Execution (macro vision ↔ micro action; Hormozi) | From a routine to a definition of how decisions stay aimed |
| Adaptability (feedback, experimentation) | Adaptability (fixed intent, changing tactics; systems decay) | Same principle, with the intent/tactics distinction made explicit |
| Study general principles (*The Kybalion*; boring fundamentals) | Study of principles ("the principle of all principles") | Elevated to the master principle; everything is a skill; originality |

Koe does not explain the changes beyond saying, in 2026, that he compiled the principles from centuries of military, sporting and business history. The two versions can both be useful in different contexts. The 2025 version is more practical and more concerned with identity: it tells a person stuck in a life they dislike how to start changing who they are. The 2026 version is more classical and more concerned with resources: it tells a person who is already moving how to avoid being overrun as the stakes rise.

#### Resources, risk and the stepping-stone strategy

The preparation principle raises a practical question that the 2026 video answers with two heuristics, and that the corpus answers in tension with a third.

**Acquire resources first.** "If you don't have many resources, then it probably wouldn't make sense to start crypto trading or start stock trading or day trading, because you're just going to lose all your money. You're gambling at that point, and that's not a strategy. You are much better off starting smaller so that you can acquire resources. That is the beginning of your strategy: how do I do something that allows me to get the resources I need to do what I eventually want to do." And the corollary: "Just because you can't start the business you want to start now doesn't mean you can't start another form of a business that allows you to start that business" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19). The heuristic applies Hormozi's definition to the beginning of a strategy: when resources are scarce, the first move is to acquire them, through a stepping-stone project chosen for what it makes possible rather than for what it is. The distinction between gambling and strategy repeats the seventh principle: luck is what outcomes look like when there is no strategy.

**The depression apartment.** Speaking specifically to young people, Koe says: "you have so much time on your hands. You are in one of the greatest positions you can possibly be." For those with "high and mighty dreams," "the best thing you can do is just hedge against risk as much as you can, so that you can take risk and have nothing to lose. What that means is selling everything you own. Living in a **depression apartment**, as Devon calls it, aka an apartment that costs nothing. You just have no standard of living for your young years, and you cut all your expenses, so that everything you have can go into the thing that you're building, because you have the resource of time, but you don't have the resource of money. So you need to pull all of your reserves in and put it toward generating the resource of money" (**Source:** same file). The term is credited to "Devon"; the transcript gives only the first name. (Koe has interviewed the novelist Devon Eriksen elsewhere in the corpus, and he is probably the person meant, but the source does not confirm it.)

The logic of the depression apartment is an inversion worth spelling out. Hedging against risk usually means keeping a safety net. Here it means removing the things that make failure costly: if your expenses are near zero, a failed venture cannot ruin you, so you can take the risk of building with everything you have. The resource analysis of the preparation principle drives it: the young have time and lack money, so the strategy is to convert time into money as fast as possible, with nothing else competing for either.

**A tension within the same video.** Earlier in the same 2026 video, in a passage treated in Chapter 10 (section 10.6, on tactical stress), Koe recommends creating pressure deliberately, for example by moving into an apartment you cannot afford, to force yourself to grow. A few minutes later, in the preparation principle, he warns: "you don't just rush into battle"; build runway, decrease expenses, hedge against risk. The video does not reconcile the two. They can be read as addressed to different situations: pressure for a person stuck in comfort who needs a forcing function; preparation for a person facing a major change (quitting a job, ending a relationship) where failure would be costly. The depression apartment sits interestingly between them: it creates pressure (no comfort) by reducing risk (no expenses).

**Small bets.** A different approach to risk comes from Justin Welsh's 2021 interview, and it is his, not Koe's. Welsh is "a big fan of small risk." Much advice says "don't have a plan B," "go all in on one thing," "go hard or go home, go deep… if you put all your energy towards one thing, you have the most chances for success. I disagree with all of that." He invests in businesses the way he invests money, "diversified. And therefore… if something happens to one of them, I'm not out of luck." "I'd rather build 10 $50,000 businesses than one $500,000 business." He credits the idea to "people like Daniel Vassallo, who talk a lot about that on Twitter… the way that he thinks about small bets," and calls reading Vassallo's early Twitter, as Vassallo was discovering the idea, "the most profound piece of advice that I've received" (**Source:** `Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md`, 2021-12-20). **Complementary context:** Daniel Vassallo, a former Amazon engineer, popularized the idea of a "portfolio of small bets": many small, independent projects instead of one large one, so that no single failure is catastrophic and occasional successes can be pursued.

Small bets stand in an open tension with the concentration of force. In the same 2021 interview Koe partly reintroduces the case for going "all in." In 2025 he says that "concentration of force is the only way to get rich" and advises giving yourself "no other option"; in 2026 he recommends building your own thing and committing to it with no other option, and, for the young, the depression apartment. He never explicitly reconciles these positions with Welsh's. The two can apply to different people and goals: diversification suits an operator who already runs several small businesses and wants stability; concentration suits someone at the start who needs one breakthrough, or who is trying to get rich rather than to stay comfortable. It is also possible to read them as operating at different scales: concentration of force within a project or a period (one platform, one offer, one decisive point), and diversification across a portfolio once resources allow it. Neither reading is stated in the corpus; the tension remains unresolved, and the reader is better served by seeing both arguments than by an artificial reconciliation.

#### The premortem: choosing what not to do

The 2025 video ends its seven pillars with a technique, introduced by a quote from the business strategist Michael Porter: "The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do." "We need to figure that out," Koe says, because most people do not fail because they cannot complete their project; "they fail because of internal psychological thought processes that take control of their focus and lead to the belief that success isn't possible." Most people's problem "is they just can't start" (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). **Complementary context:** Michael Porter is a Harvard Business School professor whose work on competitive strategy argues that strategy requires trade-offs; the quoted line comes from his 1996 essay "What Is Strategy?"

Koe lists typical internal failure modes:

- **Paralysis by analysis**: over-researching and not taking action.
- **Losing sight of the big vision** because of others' opinions.
- **Lacking complementary skills**, such as writing and audience building, needed to find customers.
- **Financial insecurity**, to the point of accepting that you need to focus on the job you hate.
- **Burning out** without a coping mechanism that refreshes you.
- **Getting bored** with the mundane work that gets results.
- **The desire to do something fancy and high-status** instead of something you enjoy or that gets you out of your situation.

(**Source:** same file.)

The list is a catalogue of ways the "opponent, which is often yourself" wins the game. Several items correspond to earlier chapters: boredom with mundane work is the problem of the boring fundamentals (Chapter 12); burnout without recovery is the rest problem (Chapter 13); the desire for something high-status is the status game (Chapter 2); losing the vision to others' opinions is the closed mind of the group (Chapter 1). Paralysis by analysis is the advice junkie of section 17.2 in strategic form: research used to postpone action.

The tool for confronting these is the **premortem**: "a strategic thinking tool that identifies failure points in any project before it even begins." Its four steps:

1. **Imagine spectacular failure.** Visualize your strategy failing horribly: what does it look like, and where does your life end up? Koe links this to his anti-vision: strategic intent is like the vision, and the anti-vision "helps you frame that so much more."
2. **List the causes of failure.** Brainstorm every possible reason, including psychological ones (personal weaknesses, self-sabotage, lack of knowledge or skill) and external factors (not having enough money).
3. **Prioritize.** Reorganize the list with the most threatening causes at the top.
4. **Brainstorm solutions for each cause.** You can use the AI strategic advisor for this.

The purpose, in his words: "how do you fail before you even start… so that you're much more likely to follow through." The result is awareness of how your mind works plus a clear action plan, and with that, "now it's literally on you" (**Source:** same file).

The premortem is problem-first thinking applied to the future. Instead of waiting for mistakes to reveal problems (section 17.4), it imagines the mistakes in advance and works backward to their causes, so that the problems become visible before they cost anything. The link to the anti-vision is natural: the anti-vision describes the life one refuses to live, and the premortem describes the specific route by which a strategy would lead back to it. Porter's quote explains why this is strategy rather than pessimism: knowing how you would fail tells you what not to do, which is half of deciding what to do. The phrase "now it's literally on you" marks the end of the excuses that the closed mind supplies: once the failure modes are named and their solutions planned, failure can no longer be attributed to circumstances one did not foresee.

**Complementary context:** the premortem is a technique developed by the psychologist Gary Klein, who described it in a 2007 *Harvard Business Review* article; teams imagine that a project has already failed and generate the reasons. Koe presents it without attribution. In the 2026 strategic-thinking video he describes the same exercise (imagining failure and working backward from the anti-vision) under the name "post-mortem." The logic is identical, and the change of name appears to be a slip rather than a new technique, since a post-mortem ordinarily examines a failure after it happens.

The chapter ends where Koe's thinking about thinking ends: with a person who can hold threatening ideas open, who does not take anyone's map as their territory, who questions, who starts from the problem, who knows the difference between what they know and what they understand, and who can turn all of that into a strategy, a central idea that adapts as circumstances change. Chapter 18 takes the next step. A strategy built from studied principles produces, in Koe's own definition, "your own moves," and that, he says, "is essentially what originality is." The next chapter asks how original ideas are produced: how they are collected, connected and articulated, and why taste matters more as ideas become cheap.

### Exercises

1. **Locating where your thinking stops.** Choose a belief you hold strongly in one domain (diet, politics, business, religion, work). Write out the strongest argument against it that you can, then keep writing until you notice the moment your mind wants to "collapse in on what you know." Describe that moment: what did you feel, what thought did you reach for, and which of Koe's four traits of stupid thinking (one-dimensional, reductionistic, tribal, non-questioning) best describes it? What would "stopping the stopping" have required at that point?

2. **Diagnosing a smart-but-dumb pattern.** Identify a domain in which you are competent and a problem in your life that belongs to a different domain (for example, a relationship problem you have been treating as a productivity problem). Explain, using Koe's quadrant language or his "circle into the square hole" image, why your usual tools may not fit. Then list at least three solutions from the other domain that you have not tried, in the spirit of treating each domain "as both a project and a skill."

3. **Running the six questions.** Pick a business model, ideology or lifestyle you disagree with and study it for at least an hour from sources that support it. Record any "ego reaction" or physical threat response. Then answer Koe's six questions in writing. Finally, evaluate the exercise itself: did it lead you toward synthesis, toward a firmer rejection, or toward a "firm belief held loosely"? Which of these outcomes would Koe consider a success, and why?

4. **Testing a piece of advice as a map.** Take one piece of advice you have followed (from a course, a book, a creator or this book). Using the six-step learning process (goal, habits, identity-change pain, information, experiment, technical advice), identify where in your own process that advice actually belonged and whether you received it too early. Rewrite the advice as it would read if it were "specific to your situation." What would Koe's candle image say about what the advice could and could not do for you?

5. **From pain point to process.** Take an idea you want to write about. First write it as a bare idea. Then apply Koe's habit: ask "what's the problem?" and dig at least four layers down, as in the chain that ends in "I don't want to end up alone." Write two short posts from the deepest problem you reach: one using "pain point → process" and one using "pain point → why/insight," each ending with a conclusion that reframes the pain. Which version is stronger, and what does that tell you about the difference between the two frameworks?

6. **Knowing or understanding?** Choose a skill you "know about" but have not practiced (or have practiced little). Using Koe's distinctions (rules versus playing, concepts versus experience, apps versus operating system), describe precisely what you know and what you do not yet understand. Design the smallest project that would act as "a vessel for making mistakes" in that skill, and predict the first problem it will reveal.

7. **Drafting a strategy and its premortem.** Using either the 2025 pillars or the 2026 principles (justify your choice), draft a one-page strategy for a goal you care about: intent or vision, an honest SWOT or research summary, your resources and gaps, the one decisive point where you will concentrate force, and your daily lever-moving tasks. Then run a premortem: imagine spectacular failure, list and rank its causes, and write a solution for the top three. Which cause on your list is "your opponent, which is often yourself"?

8. **Weighing concentration against small bets.** Koe argues for concentration of force and, for the young, the "depression apartment"; Justin Welsh, following Daniel Vassallo, argues for many small bets. Describe a concrete situation (your own or a realistic case) in which concentration is clearly the better strategy and one in which small bets are. What features of the situation (resources, age, stage of the project, goal) decide between them? Is there a way to combine the two without contradiction, and where would Koe's own words support or undermine your answer?
