## Chapter 4 — How Identity Changes

Chapter 3 built a model of the person: identity as a mental body made of ideas, beliefs, standards and goals, which filters what you perceive and defends itself with the same survival machinery that protects the physical body. That model explains why people stay the same. This chapter uses it to explain how they stop staying the same. Koe's answer has several layers, and the chapter follows them in order. First, a thesis: every lasting change of behavior is a change of identity, and the self is meant to grow in complexity goal after goal (4.1). Second, the triggers that start the process: getting fed up, pain, rock bottom, or the slow accumulation of small wins (4.2). Third, the phases the process goes through and the metaphors Koe uses to describe the identity being rebuilt: a shell, a city, a house, a block of marble (4.3). Fourth, the main technique: reprogramming the mind by immersing it in the information and environment of the person you want to become (4.4). Fifth, the deliberate use of the survival instinct described in Chapter 3, through the future self, the alter ego and the highest self (4.5). Sixth, the step-by-step protocols he has offered over the years, and the idea that failed attempts stack rather than reset (4.6). Finally, the concept that ties the chapter together and points forward to the rest of the book: the level of mind, and the process for raising it (4.7).

A warning about tone before starting. Much of what this chapter draws on comes from videos with titles that promise speed ("How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal", "How to fix your entire life in 1 day", "Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months"). Inside those videos, the author is usually more careful than the titles: he speaks of relapses, withdrawals, seasons of struggle and years of skill acquisition. Where his statements about speed, intensity or pain differ across the corpus, the chapter shows both versions and the change.

### 4.1 All Change Is Identity Change

#### You are not the person who would have reached the goal

The foundational claim of the chapter is stated in several videos with almost the same words. "The reason you haven't achieved a goal is that you aren't the person that would have achieved the goal" (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). In that video the sentence works as a bridge: it follows the account of identity as a web of goals (Chapter 3) and opens the account of the mind as a system that helps you achieve goals. Goal achievement, in other words, is a function of identity. A goal that does not fit who you are will be abandoned, sabotaged or forgotten, not because you lack information or willpower but because nothing in the current self is organized to pursue it.

Koe's most vivid version uses two images (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23). The first is an anchor: "every new goal you set is like throwing an anchor into the unknown." The anchor lands somewhere you have not been, and reaching it demands a new worldview, specific skills and beliefs, all of which form who you are. The second is a video game: you are not level 100 because you lack the **character development** that makes a level-100 character. The difference between the game and life is that in games the risks are not real, while in life they are, although they are "often over conflated with thought": the risk you imagine is usually larger than the risk you face. The conclusion he draws is the sentence that gives this section its title: "all change requires a change in identity."

The context of that video adds a practical corollary about advice. You cannot ask for advice and expect it to give you the experience that would bring you to the goal's level. Koe asks two questions to make the point: when was the last time you seriously took advice you asked for? And, since everything you read is technically advice with an underlying lesson, how many lessons did you integrate "into your being" today? Advice is information addressed to the person who would reach the goal; if you are not yet that person, the information has nowhere to land. This is the same mechanism Chapter 3 described as the identity filter: information that does not serve the goals of the current identity is not registered as relevant.

#### First-order and second-order change

In late 2025 Koe sharpened the claim with a distinction between two orders of change (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). "You aren't where you want to be because you aren't the person who would be there." Two things are required for change. The first is changing your actions so they progress toward the goal: "the least important thing and what most people do. It is **second-order**." The second is "changing who you are so that your behavior naturally follows. This is the most important thing. This is **first-order**."

The terms are Koe's own, and they should not be confused with the "first-order being / second-order knowing" distinction that appears elsewhere in his work in an ontological sense; here "first-order" simply means "the change from which the others follow." The distinction explains a pattern he describes immediately afterward. Most people set surface-level goals, hype themselves up to be disciplined for a few weeks, and then go back to how they were "without much struggle", because "they were trying to build a great [thing] on a **rotting foundation**." The image of the foundation will return several times in this chapter (the city, the house, the marble), always with the same meaning: behavior rests on identity, and changes made only at the level of behavior collapse when the structure underneath has not changed.

In an earlier formulation, the same thesis is linked to the concept that closes this chapter (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). "All real change is identity change." Your **level of mind** dictates what values, beliefs and standards are available to your identity. He gives an example meant to provoke: "You don't care about global problems because you haven't solved the personal problems that restrict your mind from seeing them as important." The point is not moral. It is that values are not chosen from a menu; they become available, or remain invisible, depending on the level from which you perceive. He ends the passage with one of his broadest statements: "The purpose of humanity is to expand your mind to that of the universe." That sentence belongs to the philosophy of Part XIV; here it signals that identity change, in Koe's framework, is not only a technique for reaching goals but a direction of development. Section 4.7 returns to the level of mind in detail.

#### The chain: behavior, identity, and what you repeatedly do

Koe often argues by chains of implication, and the identity thesis is stated as one (**Source:** `It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12): "all change is behavior change, all behavior change is identity change, so all change is identity change, because you are what you repeatedly do." The last clause ("you are what you repeatedly do") is a common saying that he uses without attribution as if it were his own principle; it is the premise that makes the chain work. If identity is constituted by repeated behavior, then changing behavior durably is the same thing as changing identity.

An earlier chain starts from the same first link and goes in a different direction: toward systems (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05). "All change is behavior change." To change your life you must change your actions. To change your actions in the right direction you need a plan, "there isn't any other way, and if you don't create your own someone will create a plan for you." To stick to the plan you need a system, and "systems are organized behavior changes." To create a system you must attack your goals, embrace trial and error, double down on your successes, and persist "until success becomes the default state in each area of your life: health, wealth, relationships and happiness."

Read together, the two chains describe one process from two ends. The 2023 chain moves outward, from the decision to change to a plan and a system. The 2024 chain moves inward, from behavior to identity. They meet in the idea of a default state: a system that runs until success is the default is the same thing, seen from outside, as an identity whose repeated behavior produces success without effort. The phrase "if you don't create your own someone will create a plan for you" repeats, in practical form, the thesis of Chapter 1 that if you do not choose your goals they are assigned to you.

#### A map of the process: seven ideas

The 2025 video that introduced first- and second-order change also offers the most complete outline Koe has given of how he thinks about behavior change (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). He presents "seven of probably the most impactful ideas" on behavior change, psychology and productivity, asks the viewer for full attention, for saving the video and taking notes, and for setting aside time to think. The ideas, in the order delivered, are:

| # | Idea as delivered | Where this book treats it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | You aren't the person who would be there (identity) | This section |
| 2 | You don't actually want to be there (goals) | Chapter 3 (unconscious goals), Chapter 6 |
| 3 | You're afraid to be there (anatomy of identity, fear) | Chapter 3, sections 4.2–4.3 |
| 4 | The life you want lies in a specific level of mind (stages) | Section 4.7, Chapter 38 |
| 5 | Intelligence is getting what you want (cybernetics) | Chapter 6 |
| 6 | A one-day protocol | Sections 4.2 and 4.6 |
| 7 | Turn your life into a video game | Chapter 10 |

There is an ambiguity in the numbering that the transcript does not resolve. He announces "six ideas, and then in the seventh idea, it's actually a protocol", but in the delivery the protocol is the sixth point and the seventh is "turn your life into a video game." He warns that the protocol takes about a full day, digs into your psyche, and that "if you take this seriously, you're going to get quite emotional." The outline is useful less for its numbering than for the sequence it implies: identity first, then goals, then the fear that protects the current identity, then the level of mind at which the desired life becomes possible, and only after that the mechanics of intelligence, a protocol and a game frame. The present chapter follows a similar order.

#### Identity death and the good pain

If change is identity change, and identity is defended like a body (Chapter 3), then changing must feel like a kind of death. Koe accepts this conclusion and draws a practical lesson from it (**Source:** `It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12). "You are disciplined towards the goals in your head right now": it is not hard to watch Netflix all day. For many high performers that life would be "the worst thing in the world", and "the pain of not achieving the goals that make them who they are would eat them alive because their identity is dying." Then the key distinction: "There's physical death and then there's **mental death**." When your identity changes or is threatened, or when your attachment to something is threatened (he gives the example of video games being taken away), it hurts. When your lifestyle and goals are taken away or become unattainable, "that should feel painful and that's a good thing." Your journey will be painful because "you are **letting your old self die off**", shedding old beliefs and habits.

The claim that this pain is good is developed through a source Koe credits explicitly (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23). He recalls an older "roof talk" video by Frank Yang, which Yang himself framed as theorizing, not absolute truth. Yang, citing Freud, says we have two drives: the sex drive, which stands for creation, survival and extending our lives in "the Matrix or the video game of life", and the death drive, which stands for destruction, "absolute Oneness or returning to Infinity." We enjoy pain, but many hide from it and inflict worse pain on themselves through surface-level comfortable pleasures, while some seek "**the good pain**": the pain that brings meaning and fulfillment because it lets a part of yourself die so another can grow. The gym is the model. People enjoy the pain of training because they are tearing muscle cells so new ones grow. By analogy, "**mental bodybuilding**" lets part of your identity die so a new one emerges, toward a higher and more holistic self. The lesson Koe extracts is an instruction: "curate an environment like the gym but in multiple areas of your life to generate suffering that leads to New Life." He adds that monks and mystics who seek ego death shed layers of thought to become one with "what is or nothingness", and that many people endure greater pain pursuing pleasure, while those who pursue pain end up enjoying it.

Three things should be kept distinct here. The two drives are Freud's concepts as relayed by Frank Yang, and Koe adopts them through Yang's explicitly speculative framing. The gym analogy and "mental bodybuilding" are Koe's adaptation. And the advice to curate gym-like environments across life is his practical conclusion.

**Complementary context:** Freud introduced the death drive (often called *Thanatos*) in *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920), opposing it to the life drives (*Eros*), which include but are broader than sexuality. Koe's use is loose and metaphorical, and he relies on Yang's paraphrase rather than on Freud directly.

There is a tension here that the corpus does not resolve explicitly. In 2023 Koe values the "death" of parts of the self and cites the monks' ego death approvingly; from 2024 he says "you don't get rid of the ego, you develop it" (see section 3.5). He gives no reason for the shift. The two positions can coexist if the "death" refers to particular contents of the identity (beliefs, habits, attachments: the good pain) while the ego as a whole, the function that interprets and organizes experience, is developed rather than destroyed. That reading fits the rest of this chapter, where the old identity dies piece by piece while the capacity to construct identities grows.

#### The self should evolve toward complexity

Why change at all, rather than simply find a comfortable identity and keep it? Koe's deepest answer is cosmological, and he offers it as a metaphor (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04). "You are complex, at least that should be your aim. The universe evolves toward complexity", and the information age is the most complex age ever. Thousands of years ago "the universe was a Little Acorn even though it looked physically the same" (he says this is why he likes mentalism, the view that reality is fundamentally mental); now it is "like an oak tree with a thick stump and branches and branches on that and leaves ... with interconnections ... in the mental realm." If the universe evolves toward complexity, "so do you as you learn, implement and understand as you age." The acorn-to-oak image is a metaphor for the growth of interconnection in the mental realm, not a claim about physics, and it should be read as Koe's interpretive frame.

The same video makes the idea operational with the concept of **complexity of self** (same source). "The complexity of your character is determined by the goals that you've pushed through, and each goal you achieve requires a specific perspective and skill level to match. The higher your skill level across multiple domains the higher your complexity of self." As complexity increases, you can solve deeper and more meaningful problems, in your own life and in others'. He applies this to personal brands: "If you are the niche then your brand should grow in complexity as your skills and interests evolve in the pursuit of your goals, because your brand, what you're leading people towards, is goal after goal after goal." (The idea of being the niche is the subject of Chapter 19; here it shows that the identity thesis extends naturally into business.)

The 2024 summary of his book *The Art of Focus* gives the shortest chain from goal to complexity (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). It starts with a childhood observation: "if everyone does the same thing they will get the same results", and most people are not happy with those results. Few people set their own goals, and fewer stick to them, "yet that is the only way to evolve." Then the chain: a goal implies a problem; "that problem is what allows you to develop your skill set and mindset"; that development leads to a more **complex identity** that can find meaning and progress in life. This sequence (goal → problem → skills and mindset → more complex identity) is the micro version of the personal evolution loop described in section 4.3 and of the process for raising the level of mind in section 4.7.

The earliest version of the idea is explicitly borrowed and is expressed in the language of games (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11; repeated without changes in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). "**Leveling up increases the complexity of the self**." The phrase adapts Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, whose work on flow describes how each flow experience leaves the self more complex. Koe's argument runs in reverse from the effect to its cause: improving your skill set by learning, executing and taking on challenges allows you to take on higher-level challenges, which opens many more opportunities, "because that knowledge and experience expands your awareness of what is actually possible." In a game such as World of Warcraft, a max-level character can navigate the world freely and stress-free, fly across the map and teleport ("all metaphorical", he adds). Climbing "the **ladder of challenges**" lets you do much more and "pick your battles." And "as you develop yourself you gain more ability to create order from chaos; you have the power to create a game out of more situations in life." The phrase "create order from chaos" anticipates Part III, where order in consciousness becomes the central skill, and the phrase "create a game" anticipates Chapter 10.

**Complementary context:** In *Flow* (1990) and *The Evolving Self* (1993), Csikszentmihalyi argues that the self grows in complexity through two processes, differentiation (becoming more unique) and integration (connecting with others and with ideas beyond oneself), and that flow experiences push both. Koe takes the phrase and the direction (complexity as growth) but not the two-process analysis.

A 2023 passage turns the idea into a time scale (**Source:** `Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md`, 2023-07-04). "Everyone starts at level one." You will not build everything in a blink. It took Koe "quarter after quarter after quarter", three-month periods in which he would "build, launch, iterate, get feedback and build the next thing." The instruction is to set your mind on building the first level of the pyramid (in context, the pyramid of products and projects he describes for a one-person business), and once that project emerges, to transcend to the next level by building another product or project. Then a diagnosis: "most people never evolve because they stop building and they get stagnant." He voices the stagnant attitude: "I'm only going to write content for the next three years ... not create a new product ... not contribute anything more to this new society." Complexity of self is not a state reached once but the cumulative result of not stopping.

#### Why most people never change

The same 2022 passage that introduced "leveling up increases the complexity of the self" also states the opposite case, in one of Koe's strongest formulations: "in the real world the main spiritual problem is that people never change." They identify with their beliefs, their jobs, "or just another finite aspect of the external constructed world", become static, stop learning, stop stacking skills, and never level up to where more opportunities are available (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11). The word "spiritual" is used loosely: he does not mean a religious failing but a failure of development. And the cause he names is identification: the static self is the self that has fused with something finite. This is the negative side of the conceptual survival described in Chapter 3. Whatever you identify with, you defend; if what you identify with is fixed, your development stops there.

A list Koe delivers rapid-fire in a 2023 video shows what not changing looks like in practice (**Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`, 2023-11-12). He warns that "this may trigger you", and then: you say you want to help others but will not learn the skills that allow you to help others; you focus too much on art and then blame the market when it does not see the value in what you offer, because you could not articulate it with marketing and sales; you focus on your craft rather than on getting your craft in front of potential customers; you have an incomplete perspective on what business is, which for him is "how you participate in the advancement of humanity by creating a product that raises the collective consciousness"; you took these points as personal attacks, closed your mind to your potential and allowed your conditioning to rule you; you do not create something valuable that the market wants to pay for; and "you find comfort in the beliefs that you believe are high and mighty; you lock yourself into a paradigm that prevents you from growing as an individual; you begin **coping rather than creating**." The fifth item is self-referential and is the key to the list: the reaction of taking the list as an attack is itself an instance of the defended identity. "Coping rather than creating" is his name for the result: an identity that protects itself with flattering beliefs instead of growing.

The 2025 video opens with a figure about New Year's resolutions (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). "Studies actually show", he says without naming any study, that about 80–90% of people quit their resolutions. The explanation he offers is psychological and fits his model: most people do not actually want to change on a deep internal level. They set a resolution because everyone else does, and "humans want to impress others more than they want to impress themselves." We create superficial meaning out of status games (Chapter 2), and status games do not meet the requirements for true change, which goes deeper than convincing yourself you will be more disciplined or productive. The gym is crowded in January and empty by February: "it's a meme." The figure should be read as an illustrative statistic cited without a source; the interpretation (resolutions as status games) is Koe's.

#### From identity to daily behavior: the mechanics

If identity is first-order, what is the role of habits, routines and systems? Koe does not dismiss them. He treats them as the means by which a new identity is installed and stabilized, provided they are anchored to the identity rather than substituted for it. Several passages show the mechanics.

*Resolutions need three supports.* "I'm not a huge fan of New Year's resolutions", Koe says, though now is as good a time as any to start (**Source:** `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21; the same passage appears in `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05). A decision to change "isn't going to be sustainable unless you have absolute Clarity on how to reach that goal, unless you have a system that you can repeat daily and make it a part of your life and base your identity around it in alignment with the higher version of yourself." The mechanism is conceptual survival: humans survive on a conceptual level and try to survive what they identify with, so if you identify with your highest version or your purpose and build a system to act in alignment with it, "eventually ... you become that." He closes with a line that summarizes the stakes of the default path: "if you can spend 8 hours building someone else's dreams you can spend 1 hour building your own." Clarity, system and identity are the three legs; section 4.5 develops the third.

*Repetition programs the mechanic.* The 2022 video-game talk describes how mechanics improve (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11). In World of Warcraft people log in daily to perform repetitive tasks: farm gold, create armor, mine ore, farm experience points by running dungeons over and over. "You have to program the specific mechanic in your brain to the point of where you're getting results with less effort, in other words habit formation." And: "if you can automate to an extent the decisions that you make on a daily basis that are conducive with your hierarchy of goals and achieving your end vision, then success kind of becomes inevitable." The phrase "hierarchy of goals" ties the habit to the vision (Chapter 8): a habit is valuable when it automates a decision that serves the hierarchy.

*Stacking whys makes an action automatic.* Koe's account of how writing became his first action of the day is a small case study (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). When he discovered writing, he understood that "it is the foundation of all media and it is how I get my products and services in front of people and build a name for myself", and also that "it helps me clear my mind, it helps me harness my creative ability." So "I started **stacking whys** behind this one action so that when I woke up it was almost automatic that I just got straight into writing." The mechanism is the reverse of willpower: rather than forcing an action, he multiplied the reasons attached to it until the action carried itself. Section 4.4 presents the general method ("hunt and stack whys").

*Routines condition the mind for a new identity.* In a 2024 guideline on routines, Koe states the link to identity explicitly (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). "You have a routine even if your routine is no routine." Then: "Routines are how you condition your mind to run on new systems. If you want to solidify a new identity you must start, stick to and refine a specific routine that encourages the growth of that identity. For the first month building your project will feel uncomfortable. You're supposed to feel overwhelmed with this. It means your mind is expanding into a new identity. Slowly then all at once everything flows with ease, you begin making exponential progress, you move from intensity to consistency and let the results compound." Two ideas from this passage recur throughout the chapter: that overwhelm is a signal of expansion rather than of failure (developed in 4.3), and that progress is nonlinear ("slowly then all at once"), with a difficult first phase and a breakthrough (the perception threshold of 4.7).

*Four reasons his habits stuck.* In 2025 Koe gives the most explicit account of why his own habits (the gym, writing, walking) eventually became permanent (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). "All of those habits became a part of my life for four specific reasons":

| # | Reason, in his words | What it corresponds to in this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "I was deeply aware of a painful problem in my life." | The trigger (4.2) |
| 2 | "I searched for evidence to support a desirable future version of myself." | The future self (4.5) |
| 3 | "I changed my physical and digital environments to solidify my new identity." | Immersion and environment (4.4) |
| 4 | "I blocked out time during the week to do those things as if I were going to be doing them for the rest of my life." | Routine and system (this section) |

The fourth reason deserves attention. Blocking time "as if I were going to be doing them for the rest of my life" is a way of treating the habit as a feature of identity rather than as a temporary project. The question changes from "how long do I have to keep this up?" to "this is what I do."

*Triggers and the smallest step.* Two passages from a 2024 video on walking address the moment of starting (**Source:** `The Power Of Walking – How One Habit Can Change Your Brain, Health, & Life.md`, 2024-06-16). "The walk is the easiest part, but getting yourself to go on the walk is the hardest part." The first solution is a trigger. Just as a smoker trying to quit can use a nicotine craving as the cue to get up and walk, you can use the moment you feel bored or uninspired during a focus session. Koe's own use: if, an hour and a half into a planned four-hour focus session, his work starts to suffer, he walks to realign and set the intention for the next block, so he can get straight back into it; otherwise he would sit for two hours getting less done than if he had walked, realigned and come back with pure focus. The second solution applies when there is no trigger: reduce the action to the smallest, most frictionless step. The gym tip is to just put on your gym shoes; having invested that energy, you might as well get in the car and go. If even that is too hard, ask why you cannot control your body enough to walk a few feet and put on a shoe, and break it down further: can you walk to your room? Stand up? Move your foot? If the answer is still no, "nobody can save you except for yourself."

This advice (shrink the action until it is frictionless) belongs to the gradual register of Koe's thinking, and it contrasts with other passages, especially from 2025, in which he recommends the opposite: "rip the band-aid off" and change everything at once. The contrast is discussed at the end of section 4.3, because it is one of the main unresolved tensions in his account of change.

*What breaks routines.* Koe is candid that his own routines get interrupted (**Source:** `How To Reset Your Life In 7 Days.md`, 2024-01-07). New Year's and holidays knock him out: when he stops for a week to see family and do other things, he cannot work day after day; he does not binge, but nobody is online or doing business, so it is almost impossible to stay on routine and he has to get back into it. "Sometimes you're just ripped of your routines and that's fine." The observation he draws from it is general: in the parts of life when your mind is not structured and focused, you pick up bad habits and are more likely to go through **negative thought cycles**, and those habits are difficult to break. The routine is not only a way of producing output; it is a structure that keeps the mind ordered, and its absence lets disorder in. This anticipates the theory of entropy in Chapter 5.

*Do what you want to do in the future now.* The last mechanic reframes consistency itself (**Source:** `Become A Digital Renaissance Man (And Join The New Rich).md`, 2023-02-19). In the context of becoming what he calls a "digital renaissance man" (Chapter 20), Koe argues that if you do not have an **educational habit** (learning daily or weekly, even just watching YouTube videos on fitness or personal development, which "primes your mind to receive opportunities in those domains") and do not set aside time for both theory and practice every day, "you're not going to win in this economy." Acquiring enough skills to differentiate yourself takes years, so start now: if you will do it for the rest of your life, paid or not, why not now? "You do what you want to do in your future now and you slowly increase the time you're allowed to spend on doing those things and the money ... those are what increase with consistency." Consistency, in this formulation, is not repetition for its own sake but the practice of the future identity in the present, at whatever scale is possible today. Chapter 8 develops the same idea as "bringing the ideal future into the present."

These mechanics complete the picture of section 4.1. Identity is first-order; behavior is second-order. But second-order change is how first-order change is practiced, stabilized and protected, as long as each habit is tied to the identity it serves. The next question is what sets the process in motion.

### 4.2 The Triggers: Getting Fed Up, Pain, Rock Bottom, or Small Wins

If the identity defends itself, something has to break that defense before change can begin. This section collects what Koe says about the triggers of change. His statements span 2022 to 2026, and his account changes in an important way over those years: from rock bottom as an external event, to a psychological state you can provoke on purpose, to a precondition that is hard to fake. The section follows the logic of the trigger first (fed up, contrast, the flip, the crossroads, the role of pain), then the two routes he distinguished in 2022, then the 2026 refinement, and finally the question of how to use the emotional energy a trigger releases.

#### Getting fed up

The simplest statement of the trigger is autobiographical. Koe wrote it as a tweet that, he says, "popped off" more than he expected: "the best periods of my life always came after a period of getting absolutely fed up with the lack of progress I was making" (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). The same observation opens a 2023 video almost word for word: "the best periods of my life always came after a phase of getting absolutely fed up with the lack of progress that I was making" (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11). The two contexts add different things. In 2023 the observation frames the thesis that a low point, used consciously, is the trigger for change: being fed up is not just something that happens to you but something you can turn into fuel. In 2025 it introduces the one-day protocol meant to launch a new life (section 4.6). In both cases Koe uses **fed up** in a specific sense: not general unhappiness, but exhaustion with *your own lack of progress*. The object of the frustration is the self, and that is what makes it useful: it points at the thing that has to change.

#### Contrast and polarity

Before being fed up, there is usually a more neutral form of the same trigger: seeing clearly what you do not want by looking at people. Koe describes how his business journey started out of curiosity and observation (**Source:** `11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md`, 2022-11-06). He observed people he did not want to be like and people he did, which created **contrast**, "a polarity": "I don't want this, I want this, and this is what I'm going to work towards." On one side were people who were unfit, unhappy, living paycheck to paycheck and seemingly unwilling to change; he insists on framing this "not out of judgment but out of **discernment**": "it became very clear not out of judgment but out of discernment that that isn't how I want to live my life." On the other side were people living the dream, spreading a great message, educating, providing value and inspiring others, people he followed on YouTube and Instagram. He was 26 at the time.

He adds an observation about age. Many people in his network experienced this contrast young. It is possible to overcome the limiting belief when older, but when you are young you have more "rebellious Essence": you see something, think it is stupid, question it and move away from it, for good or bad. The observation is offered as a pattern among people he knows, not as a developmental law. The importance of the contrast story is structural: it is the earliest form of the anti-vision (Koe's name for a deliberately vivid picture of the life you refuse to end up living, used as a reference point to move away from; Chapter 7 develops it). The anti-role models of his youth (Chapter 2) gave him the negative pole; the creators online gave him the positive one; the polarity between them gave direction.

A small example from 2022 shows how the same mechanism operates on a minor scale (**Source:** `Why You Can’t Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02). You are walking down the street, make eye contact with someone, "something happens in you", and you think "no, I didn't like that." So you ask how to start learning about it. What most people do is watch a YouTube video on how to be confident, which gives good information and helps them begin solving the problem. Then, as they become more aware and realize what confidence is *not*, they start working towards what it is. The example illustrates developmental stages that start from what you do not want: a moment of discomfort, a first piece of information, a growing awareness of the negative, and only then a clearer picture of the positive.

#### Relief versus cure, and the full circle

The 2023 video that opens with "fed up" develops the idea with two concepts of his own (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11). The first is the distinction between **relief** and **cure**. Relief feels good. It comes as cheap pleasures, mindless entertainment and "prescriptions": confident how-to advice, step-by-step game plans like "go to school, get a job, retire at 65." Koe's point about prescriptions is not that they are false but that their status is misunderstood: such plans are subjective, they work for the individual who made them, they must be created by the individual, and they should be perceived as guidelines, not law. Humans fear change, so they cling to the "**illusion of security**" offered by the external world: advice, what he calls "the porn beyond sex" (consumable promises of a better life), persuasive marketing angles. All of these give security, but none is "of your own understanding." A cure, by contrast, is painful. It comes as "getting sick of being sick", to the point of coming "full circle."

The second concept explains that last phrase. Koe asks the viewer to imagine a line with a gradient from black to white, with shades of gray in the middle. Now bend the line so that the ends meet, where black and white touch. That is what he means by **full circle**: not the ordinary meaning of returning to where you started, but "a complete and radical flip in perception." He lists everyday instances of the pattern: a person so stupid they become insightful; an outfit so unstylish it becomes stylish; a joke so unfunny it becomes funny; a nonbeliever so trapped they become a believer; and, the case that matters here, "a person so fed up with who they are they become an entirely new person." The metaphor gives a shape to the trigger. Change does not always come from gradually moving along the gradient toward the opposite pole; sometimes it comes from going so far into one extreme that it flips. Getting sick of being sick is the self-directed version of this flip. (The expression "full circle" has other senses elsewhere in the corpus; this is the one that concerns the trigger of change.)

#### The crossroads of dissonance and insight

In 2025 Koe named the inner experience that precedes the flip (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29). Many people never reach the tipping point because they are not "brutally aware" of how their current trajectory affects their thoughts, their emotions and their progress toward goals that do not exist. What you need to reach is "the **crossroads of dissonance and insight**." Once you have been where you are long enough, "you will start to feel like you are being ripped in half": a lower part wants to stay the same, a higher part wants to change. Pay attention to that split. As you intentionally lean toward the higher part, you are met by insight (he says you are "given the answer to everything"), which leads to a burst of clarity, which leads to obsession for a meaningful goal. That is the tipping point that pushes you into "being extreme", the subject of a later subsection.

The sequence is worth spelling out because it reverses a common assumption. Clarity is not the starting point; dissonance is. The split between lower and higher selves (a pair of terms that reappears in 4.5) is uncomfortable, and the instruction is not to resolve the discomfort quickly but to lean into the higher side of it. Insight is presented as the result of that leaning, clarity as the result of insight, and obsession as the result of clarity. "Given the answer to everything" is hyperbole; the substance is that insight arrives when attention stops defending the lower self.

#### Why people fail to change: two fears

Why do so many people feel the split and still not change? Koe's answer, in a 2025 video, is a comparison of fears (**Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`, 2025-10-05). Many attempt to change and fail "for one reason. The fear of ending up like everyone else doesn't outweigh the fear of discomfort." And he connects it to the theory of unconscious goals from Chapter 3: "You can tell yourself that you deeply care about your goals all day long, but the reason you don't change is because another goal is taking priority and that's unconscious to you." The unconscious goal is usually the goal of staying the same, the goal of the current identity.

His prescription follows from the diagnosis. To generate a goal so meaningful that you have no option but to pursue it, you need to: gain complete awareness of where your life is heading if you keep doing the same thing; question your life and sit with the discomfort without searching for someone else to give you the answer; "become so disgusted that the pain of staying the same makes the pain of discomfort feel like a little pinch"; and remind yourself of your **anti-vision**, "the life you don't want to live, as a reference point from which you can aim in a different direction." The anti-vision (the term Koe says he coined) is developed in Chapter 7; here it appears as an instrument for changing the balance between two fears.

A 2025 principle from another video states the same balance in reverse, as a comparison of fears where the fear of the unknown is the heavier one: "The fear of the unknown, the fear of doing something different with your life is greater than the fear of ending up like everyone else. So we need to decrease that perceived fear of stepping into the unknown. That way ending up like everyone else takes priority and then you are more likely to act" (**Source:** `12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md`, 2025-09-14). The two statements are complementary: you can increase the weight of one fear (by awareness and anti-vision) or decrease the weight of the other (by making the unknown less frightening, the subject of section 4.3).

A third version adds a social dimension (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). "There is one pattern that I've noticed in everyone that is trying to improve their life: the pain of where you are must outweigh the pain of what other people think." People will think you are stupid for "stupid decisions" and will talk about you when you make big changes, but "most of the time they just want to entertain themselves, they don't want the information behind your decisions, and you will learn to ignore them with time." The alternative is to "let your dreams die on the Shelf because people say something you don't like", which is "a great way to end up like everyone else." Social judgment is one of the forms the identity's defense takes: the old identity is partly stored in the people around you (section 4.4), and their reaction to your change is felt as a threat.

The most general statement of this logic is from 2023 (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17): "Becoming the person you want to be is the most painful and rewarding process you can dedicate your life to." You begin this path when you realize that "the pain and pleasure of where you are now are of lesser magnitude than the pain of not receiving the rewards that come from seeing what you are capable of." The formulation includes pleasure, not only pain: what has to be outweighed is not just the suffering of the present but its comforts too.

#### Pain is the signal: limbo, strategic dissonance and the pain-and-gain story

Three passages from 2025 turn the role of pain into method.

The first concerns the state Koe calls **limbo** (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). Limbo is feeling lost, "floating off in space", not working toward the life you want and tired of the life you are living. Koe reinterprets the desire for discipline that usually accompanies this state: "The reason you want to be disciplined is that you've exhausted your current stage of life." You have stalled, every day is the same, and doing the same things will not get you the life you want. That is painful, "but you misinterpret that pain and think of the first solution that comes to mind. Be more disciplined." That interpretation traps people in a loop of forcing themselves, "just to quit 2 weeks later and go back to the life they swore against." His alternative reading is the coined phrase **limbo is the laboratory**: "Pain is the signal that change is happening. So, the way out of this is to lean in." Become hyper-aware of how painful your situation is; "make the pain of where you are outweigh any other pain in your life." Then "discipline as you define it is no longer painful ... It's the thing you want to do. When you see no other option ... and you have complete clarity that it will take you away from the life that you are now disgusted with. That's how you change your identity." The passage connects directly to section 3.6, where discipline is a feature of identity: here we see the moment at which an action stops requiring discipline because the identity has shifted.

The second passage, from the same video, gives the deliberate version (same source). Koe calls it **strategic dissonance**: "To avoid getting trapped in a state of feeling lost or limbo, you let the divide between who you are and who you want to be grow wider. You cultivate dissatisfaction with your current lifestyle by noticing where you will end up if you don't change." The surprising instruction is to widen the gap rather than close it. The mechanism he gives is cognitive: by becoming aware of problems "you prime your mind for pattern recognition. That's what your mind is, a pattern recognition machine. That's what leads to good dopamine." Your brain confirms that the change is right and helps you notice information, conversations, books and social media content that help. "In order to learn something new, you don't need to take a course ... by simply having this goal that you deeply care about, you'll achieve it because that's the thing on your mind", which frames what you pull out of every situation. The claim about "good dopamine" belongs to the neuroscience vocabulary Koe uses throughout (Chapter 11 treats it); in this context, it is his explanation for why a vivid gap makes useful information salient. It is the same filter mechanism as in Chapter 3, now pointed at the desired future rather than at the current identity.

The third passage is the practical tool (**Source:** `A Full Dopamine Detox Protocol To Reset Your Life In 30 Days.md`, 2025-10-26). The problem Koe addresses is that the viewer is motivated now but "you will give up in two weeks like always", because "**all lasting behavior change is identity change**." So "you need to create a visceral, emotional, and specific story. Two stories to be exact. One for where your life will end up if you keep living in pain, and one for who you will become if you decide to change." This is the **pain and gain story**, and Koe calls it "the foundation for having the motivation to actually change", which is why it is the first step of his 30-day dopamine detox protocol (a month of cutting back the effortless, high-stimulation habits that crowd out harder goals; section 11.4 presents the protocol in full). The two stories are the anti-vision and the vision in narrative form: the pain story strengthens the fear of staying the same; the gain story gives the identity something to move toward.

The role of pain in Koe's account shifts over time, though without contradiction. In 2023 pain is mainly a requirement: "man cannot remake himself without suffering" (section 4.3), "a season of internal struggle is the requirement" (4.7), "the most painful and rewarding process." In late 2024, in a passage on stages of development, it is "sometimes a necessary requirement for reaching a new stage of development and even if it's not a prerequisite to it it's usually the byproduct of transformation" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). In 2025 it becomes a signal to be used deliberately ("pain is the signal that change is happening"), and in a 2025 description of the phases of change, an intentional vision makes the chaos "tolerable" (4.3). The movement is from pain as something to be endured to pain as information to be read and, if possible, made bearable by direction.

#### Two routes to reinvention: rock bottom or stacking small wins

The most structured treatment of triggers is from 2022, in a video that describes two routes to reinventing yourself (**Source:** `Why You Can’t Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02). It uses the metaphor of identity as a city, which section 4.3 develops in full; for now it is enough to know that the identity is pictured as a constructed city with central buildings and foundations.

The first route is **hitting rock bottom**. It means changing one of the foundational pillars of the city you have built, "getting kind of pissed at yourself and changing it." Koe grants that this works: many people who hit rock bottom turned their lives around immediately; it is "more than a possibility." But it comes with a very low period that you must bounce out of, and afterward you have to be mindful while "the harmonic oscillator returns to equilibrium", or "as your psyche is trying to maintain homeostasis." The image is of a system that, pushed out of balance, swings back and forth before settling. The mechanism of rock bottom, he says, is "becoming directly conscious of all of the things that are causing ... surface level pain." His example is the breakup (same source): being broken up with by a girlfriend is usually what makes young men start going to the gym and working on themselves. The relationship they were attached to "was a part of their identity", so losing it caused a low point; it made them directly aware of what they had to do, which was to improve themselves "so they can survive in the dating market in the future and not end up alone, which is a biological need."

The second route is the one he calls "the better option, a more sustainable option": **stacking small wins** (same source). Rather than attacking the foundation of the city, think of ancient Rome and new Rome: ancient Rome was not as sturdy, and you slowly take down ancient Rome while slowly building up new Rome ("you are in essence slowly taking down ancient Rome while slowly building up new Rome"); the new city can resemble the old or be different. You attack buildings in your identity that will not cause a ton of pain, and you do not attack the foundation at first, because you have to "slowly peel back the layers." The identity is layers upon layers, "systems in your head" hardwired over time; once a thought pattern is conditioned enough it becomes a habit and can become unconscious. So you peel back and solve the problems you are directly aware of right now, and deeper layers become visible as the outer ones are removed.

Koe explicitly equates this route with three other formulations: "building a portfolio of small bets" (attributed to "some people", unnamed), Alan Watts' "gambling on yourself", and James Clear's "one percent better every day" (same source). He uses them as alternative names for the same gradual, sustainable route, as opposed to rock bottom. The equivalence is his; the three phrases come from different contexts.

**Complementary context:** "Portfolio of small bets" is the name Daniel Vassallo gave to his approach of running several small, independent income projects instead of one large one. James Clear's "1% better every day" comes from *Atomic Habits* (2018) and expresses the compounding of small improvements. Koe's equivalence focuses on what they share (small, cumulative, low-risk steps) and sets aside their differences.

| | Route 1: rock bottom | Route 2: stacking small wins |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | A foundational pillar of the identity, at once | Peripheral "buildings", layer by layer |
| How it starts | Getting angry at yourself; direct awareness of surface pain | Solving the problems you are directly aware of now |
| Typical example | The breakup that leads to the gym | Ancient Rome taken down while new Rome is built |
| Cost | A very low period; oscillation before equilibrium | Slowness; deeper layers appear only later |
| Koe's 2022 assessment | Works, "more than a possibility" | "The better option, a more sustainable option" |
| Equivalent names he gives | — | Portfolio of small bets; gambling on yourself; 1% better every day |

#### The worst place, and the rock bottom you do not need to hit

Two later statements refine the first route. In 2024 Koe says (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04): "Sometimes you need to hit rock bottom before you can receive the lesson life is trying to teach you. The worst place you can be stuck is right before Rock Bottom: not painful enough to change, just comfortable enough to stay the same." He says he is glad he got arrested in the summer of his freshman year of college (the episode is told in Chapter 2) and that "if I hadn't hit rock bottom the lessons wouldn't have hit the same", but adds immediately: "the cool thing here is that you don't have to hit rock bottom for it to have the same effect on you." The "worst place" is a precise diagnosis: the danger is not pain but moderate discomfort, enough to complain about and not enough to act on.

In 2025 he names the alternative (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). "When you look at any successful person, you realize that they went through this. They hit this rock bottom." Not necessarily poverty or a struggle to survive, "but a **psychological rock bottom**": "They just hated the thought of ending up like everyone else. And then they changed in an instant and they loved doing the thing that people consider to be self-disciplined." The claim that every successful person went through it is a generalization from his observation, not a documented finding.

A 2023 observation shows what the emotional state looks like in practice (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11). Most successful people Koe knows began from "raw hatred" for their situation: hating the commute, "wearing a monkey suit five days a week", letting a relationship drag on, or not being good enough for the person they wanted to keep. That, he says, is why "like 80" percent of young men get into the gym: "I wasn't good enough in this relationship ... I'm gonna go get jacked", a superficial problem but a real phase of life. (The figure is a loose estimate offered in conversation, not a statistic.) The mechanism is what matters: when these people felt into their low point, let go of their attachment to it, realized there was a way out and found "a light" to work toward, "a new potential revealed itself and action started to become frictionless" because of pent-up emotion. "Frictionless" here means the opposite of what it meant in the walking advice of section 4.1: not an action made small enough to be easy, but an action made easy by the emotional charge behind it.

#### 2026: the crack and the high-gravity reason

The 2026 video on reinvention adds a refinement that limits the earlier optimism about provoking change (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). The refinement has three parts.

First, Koe explains why the standard visualization exercise (imagining where your current life is heading) usually fails. Most people are dishonest with themselves, and the exercise does not hit them. The reason is structural: "the self doing the exercise is the self being evaluated. You are asking the current identity to honestly assess where the current identity is heading, but the current identity has a survival interest in not seeing this clearly, because seeing it clearly would mean death." So you soften the blow, rationalize, and imagine future variables that may rescue you, so that changing now does not seem to matter. "For this exercise to work, you have to be able to receive the honest answer." This is a direct application of conceptual survival (Chapter 3) to the tools of change themselves: the defense mechanism can neutralize the anti-vision.

Second, the real requirement is "hard to fake": "you need something that has already created a **crack**", so that the visualization "just splits you open." For most people "something catastrophic has to happen before they actually make the real decision to change." His examples: the car breaks down, and they realize how broke they are and that they must make more money to fix it or prevent it, because they hated the experience; a health scare like a heart attack ("I'm not saying go and do that"); a parent or loved one dies; or, commonly, a breakup as a kid, emotional turmoil, and the decision "I'm going to go to the gym, get jacked, make sure this never happens again." That last decision "obviously doesn't solve the problem", but it shows that behavior can change very fast after a catastrophic event. The breakup example is the same one he used for rock bottom in 2022, which shows the continuity between the two accounts.

Third, he describes the first of four patterns for recreating yourself (the full list is in section 4.6): "you need a reason with extremely high gravitational pull, and when that fades, you must find another." "The most intense periods of my life came after I stumbled across a very intense reason to change", a reason with such pull that he could not focus on anything but actualizing the positive trajectory of his life. Such a reason "doesn't last forever": months later you can be back in a rut "because you let autopilot take over." And it is very difficult to force. When he publishes a video, he assumes it will not hit most viewers, because "they're not at the right phase in life in which this information will cause that spark to change." People try to force action or think through everything and still cannot change. "You have to find the reason in a book, a conversation, or experience that happens at such a perfect moment that everything clicks for you." You discover it by breaking out of "the familiar past and predictable future" (a phrase that connects to the psychological time discussed in Chapter 2 and to "living yesterday today" in 4.3).

The table summarizes how Koe's account of the trigger evolves.

| Period | What triggers change | Can it be provoked? |
|---|---|---|
| 2022-07 | Rock bottom (route 1) or stacking small wins (route 2) | Route 2 is deliberate; rock bottom happens |
| 2023-06 | Getting fed up, used consciously; relief vs cure; full circle | Yes: "get mad at where you are" |
| 2024-08 | "You don't have to hit rock bottom for it to have the same effect" | Yes |
| 2024-12 | Manufacturing a "mental rock bottom" and flipping the switch overnight (Chapter 7, section 7.1) | Yes |
| 2025-08 | Psychological rock bottom: hating the thought of ending up like everyone else; strategic dissonance | Yes, by widening the gap on purpose |
| 2025-12 | A one-day protocol can launch a new life | Yes |
| 2026-05 | A pre-existing crack; a high-gravity reason that arrives "at a perfect moment" | Hard to fake; difficult to force |

The direction is clear: rock bottom moves from an external event, to an internal state that can be provoked, to a precondition that cannot simply be manufactured. The reason Koe gives for the 2026 position is the one quoted above: the current identity has an interest in surviving and softens the blow. There is also an unresolved tension with a 2024 passage discussed in section 3.6, in which a catastrophe (the car breaking down) produces no change at all in a person with low standards, because they do not read it as a problem. The two can be reconciled: a catastrophe opens a crack only if it is perceived as a problem, and that depends on standards. A protocol works on someone who already has the crack; for someone who does not, it may slide off.

#### Be extreme

Once the trigger has fired, how intensely should you respond? Koe's 2025 answer is that you should be extreme, and he gives a neurological reason (**Source:** `You Need To Be Extreme If You Want Your Life To Change.md`, 2025-06-29). He cites Hebb's law (he names it only as "Heb's law"), a neuropsychological principle related to neuroplasticity, summarized by the saying "neurons that fire together wire together." Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to rewire itself by forming new neural connections throughout life; the brain is a dynamic network that adapts, learns and changes based on experiences, thoughts and actions. Being extreme about changing your life, he argues, quickens neuroplasticity. People shout "consistency is key", and that is true because repetition reinforces neural pathways, but you can go further: novelty and challenge stimulate neuroplasticity even more. "When you flip the switch and pursue a goal with all your might, you put your brain in an environment that quickly adapts and makes it your new standard."

**Complementary context:** Donald Hebb proposed in *The Organization of Behavior* (1949) that when one neuron repeatedly takes part in firing another, the connection between them strengthens; "neurons that fire together wire together" is a later summary attributed to Carla Shatz. Research does associate novelty, attention and challenge with plasticity, but the specific claim that being "extreme" in a life change accelerates rewiring is Koe's extrapolation from these principles, not a finding he cites.

In 2026 he restates the idea and attaches a personal preference (**Source:** `How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md`, 2026-08-09). "Being extreme toward a goal changes your brain and enhances neuroplasticity." People say "don't quit your job, have a backup plan first", and he grants: "that's great ... that can work, absolutely. But personally, I thrive when I'm under pressure. I want to quit my job. I want to feel the weight of the world on my back and I want to carry it." He acknowledges "that's a very masculine thing", and that people with a very feminine orientation may not resonate with it. "To me, that is a much better option, to just **burn the boats**. I like to romanticize my life a little bit. I like to feel like I'm going to war because that's what my brain responds to, and it gives me a huge advantage over other people who don't have that same energy and drive." The passage should be read for what it is: a preference stated as personal ("personally", "to me", "what my brain responds to"), with the alternative explicitly validated. Elsewhere he warns that his strategy of "tactical stress" is "not for the faint-hearted" (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). **Tactical stress** is Koe's name for deliberately putting yourself in a high-pressure situation that you know you have the skill to get through, so that the pressure forces the growth; his own example in that passage is moving to a place he could not afford, which forced his business to grow. Chapter 10 (section 10.6) develops the concept and its variants. Burning the boats, an old expression for removing the possibility of retreat, is used here in the specific sense of eliminating the backup plan in order to operate under pressure.

#### Using the fuel: emotional alchemy

A trigger releases emotional energy: anger, disgust, grief, frustration. Koe insists that this energy must be directed, and he describes how.

The warning comes first (**Source:** `Get Mad At Where You Are In Life (A Deep Explanation).md`, 2023-06-11). Channeling negative emotion "can become dangerous very fast." The aim is to use our emotions, not to be unconscious slaves to our state of mind, and to "catch, feel and direct our negative emotions like a mental Tai Chi Master." The image of the Tai Chi master suggests redirection rather than suppression: the force is received and guided, not blocked. The principle he states is one of his more memorable formulas: "**emotional energy is the fuel and intentional effort is the vessel**." And intention, he specifies, means goal-directed action: what are you stretching toward that is greater than you? Without the vessel, the fuel burns uselessly or destructively; without the fuel, the vessel does not move.

The method is described in a 2023 list of tactical stress strategies (**Source:** `A Dangerous (But Effective) Strategy To Get Out Of A Rut.md`, 2023-10-02). The sixth strategy is "emotional transmutation, or allowing yourself to be angry, allowing yourself to have those negative emotions and feel into them and bounce back out the other side to use stress in a good way: eustress, that's a thing, a good stress where you are channeling it into a better future." The key moves are permission ("allowing yourself"), feeling into the emotion rather than avoiding it, and coming out "the other side" with the energy redirected. **Eustress**, a term from stress research for beneficial stress, is used here in its ordinary sense.

A 2024 principle removes the excuse that emotions must be right before action is possible (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). "You can act regardless of how you feel. I don't care what thoughts you have on your mind right now: if you have legs you can stand up and walk no matter how you feel ... if you are the most depressed individual in the world there's a way to get up and get to the other side of the room; if you feel depressed or you're crying all the time you can still get up and wash the dishes." And "by doing them, that's how you break out of the conditioned closed mind that you've dug yourself into or trapped yourself in." The statement is blunt, and it should be read in context: it is motivational advice about the possibility of small actions, not a clinical claim about depression. Its function in the argument is to separate action from feeling, so that the emotional energy of a trigger does not have to wait for a good mood.

A 2023 principle explains why small actions matter so much (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22). Your life is the result of thousands of tiny choices over the past year. You did not make the choices that would have led to a purposeful career, fulfilling relationships, a healthy and aesthetic body. Genetics, birthplace, a job that leaves no time: these play a role, "but you just made another choice to close your mind off" to the potential choices that would slowly get you out, such as freeing time or moving environments. "Choices come after choices": you do not quit your job without prior choices that enable it. You are not a special case; thousands or millions of people in similar situations chose their way out. If your business is not growing, "that's your fault": you have not zoomed out or developed the mental capacity or skill to register the problem; the same applies to relationships and your life situation. The way forward is to push into the unknown to discover the skill or mindset that lets you return "from a higher level of mind" to solve the problem. The language of fault is harsh, but the logic connects to the core of this chapter: excuses are themselves choices, and the inability to see a problem is a matter of level of mind (4.7), which can be raised.

Finally, a 2024 video summarizes the whole emotional process as a "comeback" in three steps (**Source:** `How To Make The Greatest Comeback Of Your Life.md`, 2024-12-15). (1) Feel into your situation; become brutally aware of the life you do not want; "let your mind simmer with negativity." (2) Launch into the unknown: make a stupid decision, force yourself to pursue the dream you have postponed, sink or swim, knowing that you can learn to swim. (3) "Learn and build like a mad scientist": perform "**emotional alchemy**" with the stress, pain and overwhelm, channel everything into one meaningful goal, and study while your mind is primed to store relevant information. He adds a practical warning: whatever course, book or tool you acquire, build the project and supplement it with education; do not go through the course and forget it. The three steps compress the section: trigger (brutal awareness), leap (the unknown, which section 4.3 examines), and transmutation of emotional energy into learning and building (which sections 4.4 and 4.6 develop). The coined phrase "emotional alchemy" names the same operation as "emotional transmutation": turning a low emotional state into fuel for a goal.

### 4.3 The Phases of Change and Identity as a Structure

Once change has been triggered, what does it feel like from inside, and what exactly is being changed? This section brings together two kinds of ideas. The first is a sequence of phases (dissonance, uncertainty, discovery) and a loop of personal evolution, which Koe presented in 2025. The second is a set of metaphors for the identity being changed: a shell, a city, a house, a block of marble. The metaphors are not decoration. In Koe's teaching they carry the argument: each one encodes a claim about how much of the identity can be changed at once and what happens when too much is changed too fast. The section closes with the question of how to enter the unknown that change requires, and with Koe's idea of "true comfort."

#### Three phases: dissonance, uncertainty, discovery

Koe describes the same three-phase pattern in two 2025 videos, once under the name **identity flips** and once as the phases of profound change.

The shorter version comes from observing people who successfully flip their identity (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). It happens rather quickly, after a build-up of tension or "dis-ease" (he plays on "disease", splitting the word to mean the absence of ease). Phase 1, **dissonance**: they feel they do not belong and become sufficiently fed up with their lack of progress. Phase 2, **uncertainty**: they do not know what comes next, so they either experiment or get lost and feel worse. Phase 3, **discovery**: they discover what to pursue and "make six years of progress in six months." The phases are "feedback that you're on track." They are uncomfortable, and you will feel the opposite of hyped, "and that's the good sign"; it is a sign *not* to mask the discomfort with phone scrolling or quick dopamine.

The longer version is from a video about reinventing your life (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19). Koe presents three general patterns he has observed when an individual goes through profound change, which in that video means moving between levels of development. In the dissonance phase, once an individual has gotten "their taste of their current stage of life", and if they are not numbed by narrow-mindedness and comfort, they feel tired of where they are but unsure what life comes next: "this isn't the life I want to live, but I don't know what life I do want to live." You feel lost, in a rut. In the uncertainty phase, if they are aware enough of their distaste, they take "an uncertain step into the unknown" and open themselves to new knowledge and skill; you become okay with not knowing and understand that the only thing to do is take an uncertain step and do something new. In the discovery phase, "like navigating a map", they discover the education, tools, resources and insights that allow them to reach the next level: new avenues to a higher potential.

| Phase | Identity flips (2025-12) | Phases of profound change (2025-10) | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dissonance | Not belonging; fed up with lack of progress | "This isn't the life I want … but I don't know what life I do want"; lost, in a rut | Do not numb it; read it as a signal |
| Uncertainty | Experiment, or get lost and feel worse | An uncertain step into the unknown; openness to new knowledge and skill | Take the step; accept not knowing |
| Discovery | Find what to pursue; "six years of progress in six months" | Discover the education, tools and insights of the next level | Pursue; build the next level |

The two versions are the same framework with different emphases; Koe uses "identity flips" and "dissonance / uncertainty / discovery" as equivalent names for it. Koe reuses the same three phases inside each level of his developmental model, Human 3.0 (Chapter 38), so the framework applies at two scales: the change of a particular identity and the passage between stages of development.

The same 2025 video adds a principle about how to live through the phases (same source). "An enjoyable life, as shown in flow psychology and the musings of many philosophers such as Nietzsche, resides in the progression through these phases by overcoming resistance. **Profound change rarely happens by accident**." When you are intentional about your vision, goals and priorities, without regressing through distractions and comfort, the chaos that would consume most people during these phases becomes tolerable, "and often the most fulfilling parts of life that you hold close to your heart." He then adds a striking observation: at "level three and beyond", individuals can pursue more complex challenges and "simulate these phases for the love of the game", like a CEO who sells their company, becomes depressed, and starts another from a more enlightened view. Because they create the game, "it becomes infinite." The reference to flow psychology and Nietzsche is a loose appeal to authority (no text is cited); the claim that enjoyment comes from overcoming resistance is Koe's synthesis of both. The idea of simulating the phases deliberately anticipates Chapter 10, where life is designed as a game.

**Complementary context:** The phrase "infinite game" echoes James Carse's *Finite and Infinite Games* (1986), which distinguishes games played to win from games played to keep playing. Koe does not cite Carse here; the resonance is noted only to situate the term.

#### The personal evolution loop

In the same 2025 video Koe gives a process that shows the phases as a repeating cycle (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19). Applied to personal growth: (1) you have the desire to reach your potential; (2) you take a step into the unknown and are introduced to complexity or chaos; (3) you acquire knowledge and skill to solve the problems preventing forward movement, or you stagnate and "let chaos consume you"; (4) "your identity expands and ascends to a new level"; (5) "the process repeats unless you get stuck."

The **personal evolution loop** is the dynamic counterpart of the three phases. Step 2 corresponds to dissonance turning into uncertainty; step 3 is the fork between discovery and stagnation; step 4 is the identity change itself; step 5 is the return of dissonance at the next level. It is also the same structure as the 2024 chain from section 4.1 (goal → problem → skills and mindset → more complex identity), seen as a cycle rather than a line. The phrase "unless you get stuck" is important: the loop is not automatic. Stagnation is always one of the two outcomes of step 3.

#### Living yesterday today

What keeps people from stepping into the unknown in the first place? A 2026 concept describes the default alternative (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). For most people, right when they wake up "they are met with a thought that triggers a behavior, and instantly they are now operating as the past version of themselves." You think about the commute, which was stressful in the past; you bring that feeling into the present, and you are stressed and narrow-minded. The cascade continues: you experience past feelings and behaviors, "which create a predictable future that you don't want. You get stuck in this loop of **living yesterday today**." The way out is the unknown: "Only in the unknown, only in the things that you haven't experienced so far, or at least internalized or made repetitive, is where you will find the novel insight that will launch you into the next phase of life." This concept links the phases of change to psychological time (Chapter 2): the familiar past and the predictable future are what the identity uses to reproduce itself each morning.

#### Identity as a shell: the crab and the lobster

The first metaphor for the identity in transition is a shell. In the 2025 video on identity flips, Koe says that to change you create a new frame, a new lens of perception, a new identity "to put on as this shell" (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). Like a crab or a lobster, you shed the old shell, get into a new one, and grow into it until it is too tight. Then you reach dissonance again: "this doesn't feel right ... I need to get out of my shell and find a new one or create a new one." The shell metaphor thus maps directly onto the phases: dissonance is the shell becoming too tight; uncertainty is the period without a shell; discovery is the new shell.

Earlier in 2025 Koe had stated a principle that the shell metaphor illustrates (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16): "Humans have an intrinsic desire to break out of one shell, then the next, then the next." He presents it as the reason people pursue growth goals at all. In the same video he develops the image (same source). "Growth is like being a lobster." It is painful to grow too big for your shell. If you do not remove it, the pain only intensifies, "until death." If you do decide to remove it ("which most don't"), you will be vulnerable and go through a period of building a new shell. "The shell here is your identity." The application is sweeping: "Almost all of your pain, insecurity and lack of progress boils down to you being trapped in the shell of your lower self that is doing everything it can to stay the same." You refuse to open your mind or remove your shell, so you cannot see that a better life is possible. Later in the same video the image returns to explain why moving up stages of ego development is hard: "removing the shell is painful, it makes you vulnerable, you don't want to do it, your mind fights back, your ego fights back."

The lobster image adds two things to the phase model. First, it explains the pain of staying the same: a shell that is too small hurts, and the hurt increases. This is the biological version of "the worst place you can be stuck is right before rock bottom" (4.2). Second, it explains the pain of changing: the period without a shell is a period of real vulnerability. Both options hurt; only one leads to growth. The pair "lower self / higher self", which appears here, is developed in section 4.5.

#### Identity as a city: city hall, Seattle and the survival response

The oldest of Koe's structural metaphors is the city, from 2022 (**Source:** `Why You Can’t Change (How To Reinvent Yourself).md`, 2022-07-02). Cities are constructed; they are "a system that is constructed and conditioned over time", whether or not they are built on a solid foundation. Your mind, or identity, is such a construction: a city. If you take down city hall, the entire city would not know how to operate. Trying to change that aspect of your life without being able to navigate the low that comes with it is dangerous: you remove city hall while at a high, hit a new low, then the "harmonic oscillator" brings you back to a high, then a low, and eventually to equilibrium. Navigating that oscillation requires awareness and the ability to navigate. So, he says, "the first step is a mindful approach to all of this", and the second is understanding the model of identity as a city.

He extends the metaphor with a historical story (same source). Think of your identity as Seattle, Washington, in 1889, when "a glue pot tipped over and nearly burned down a majority of the city." Besides the person who poured water on the flaming glue and made it spread (Koe says "glue spreads with water"), the city was built on a terrible foundation: wooden sewer pipes made of blocks of wood, poor foundations. "It was a poorly built city and it led to its inevitable demise of being burned to the ground." Modern Seattle was rebuilt on a better foundation, "in a way that functions as a cohesive whole", with better streets and better ways to get from point A to point B, which he compares to making connections in your mind. The lesson of the story is twofold: an identity built on poor foundations is fragile and can collapse; and a collapse can be the occasion for rebuilding as a more coherent whole.

**Complementary context:** The Great Seattle Fire of June 6, 1889, began when a glue pot boiled over in a cabinet shop and destroyed about 25 blocks of the business district. Accounts say that water thrown on the burning glue helped spread the fire. After the fire, the city was rebuilt with brick and stone and the streets were regraded, raising them above the old ground level. Koe's account is broadly consistent with the standard history; his use of it is metaphorical.

The third piece of the city metaphor is the mechanism that makes changing the city painful (same source). When something burns down, everyone freaks out; he grants that you cannot expect calm in such a situation. The point he draws is that "our psyche is wired for survival; we have the brains of our ancestors but we're living in this modern world." What threatens us now is not what threatened our ancestors: it is threats to our identity, our beliefs and other things we are exposed to. These throw us into a low period, macro or micro ("whether it's a week long or it's five seconds long"), triggering fight or flight and stress hormones, "even though there is no real threat." And "when you try to change your identity by pulling a piece of your city out, it's going to cause that survival response": fight or flight, a low period, a struggle. You must understand this before you start changing yourself. This is the 2022 version of the law of conceptual survival from Chapter 3, which Koe would name the following year.

The practical heuristic follows (same source). When pulling a piece out of the city, ask which piece you are pulling first. "Are you pulling out the heart, which is going to send you into a very low period of life that you may not be able to navigate?" Alternatively, go step by step: take out small pieces, replace them, take out "what you're directly conscious of right now", and work to repair it over time. "There is a dynamic and balance to be had with that." This heuristic is the structural basis of the "stacking small wins" route described in 4.2: removing the heart is rock bottom; replacing small pieces is the gradual route.

#### A house, not a home

In 2023 Koe replaced the city with a house (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). Think of your concept of self as "an intellectual structure that you have built yourself into over time, **a house not a home**." The phrase plays on the difference between a building and the place you belong: the self is a structure you live in, not your essence, and therefore it can be remodeled. When you remodel a house, you do not go straight for the foundation or demolish the whole house at once. When you start chiseling, making small changes, chipping away at the wood or the exterior, you do not rip it off; you make "small calculated taps so that it's seamless", and you can piece something back on perfectly.

The book version of the same metaphor, quoted in the 2024 summary of *The Art of Focus*, adds a criterion for which changes to make (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). Creating an identity that "works less, earns more, enjoys life and has its ideas ripple through the universe to create heaven" will be painful. Think of your self as an intellectual structure, "a house but not a home." When you remodel a house "you don't rip the foundation out and watch it fall"; you make small, strategic changes *as you become aware of problems that ruin its aesthetic and function*. And then the criterion: to change who you are, solve problems aligned with goals you create, not with goals assigned to you; "that's how you live with purpose." The house metaphor thus connects the gradual route to the theory of goals: the problems worth remodeling for are those defined by self-generated goals.

#### The marble and the sculptor

The third image is sculpture, and it appears twice in 2023 with slightly different uses.

In the first, Koe uses a line he does not attribute: "man cannot remake himself without suffering, for he is both the marble and the sculptor" (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). He uses it to support the claim that reinventing yourself involves suffering because you are both the material being chiseled and the one doing the chiseling. There is no external sculptor whose hands are spared; every cut is felt.

**Complementary context:** The sentence is usually attributed to Alexis Carrel, the French surgeon and Nobel laureate, from *Man, the Unknown* (1935). Koe does not name a source.

In the second, the image becomes a step in a process for changing who you are (**Source:** `Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md`, 2023-08-23). The step is understanding the pain of identity change. By 18 you are like "a giant square slab of marble that looks, feels and acts ugly." The first decision is the most painful: a commitment to change, "to pop an arm out of the marble against its will" so that you can start sculpting. Then be careful: strike too hard and you may destroy the slab. You must make "small precise taps to sculpt your **ideal mental physique**." Every situation presents a small tap. Approach situations consciously; when anxiety spikes as you enter the unknown, pause and push through, because "the pain is temporary." The marble version reconciles the two routes of 4.2 in one image: a single painful first decision (the arm popped out, a small rock bottom), followed by many precise taps (small wins). It also warns against the risk of the first route taken too far: a slab struck too hard breaks.

#### The art of self-architecture and the paradox of personal development

The 2023 video that introduced the house names the whole subject "**the art of self-architecture**": deliberately reinventing yourself (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). The name extends the structural metaphors: the self is designed and built. Koe adds that "the art of self-architecture demands holistic development": pursuing outward goals without inner work leaves you superficial. (In the same period, Koe announced a video called "The Art of Self-Architecture" when discussing mental programming; see section 4.4.)

In the same video he states what he calls **the paradox of personal development** (same source): "The higher you push, or the further you push into the world, the further you have to dig into yourself to get to your core." Pushing outward while "mending or chiseling inwards" increases your level of mind. At each level of the game, reaching a new goal means you increased your level of mind enough to solve the problem standing in the way of that goal; "that's how you unlock the lesson that life has to offer you" as you move forward and gain experience. The lesson of the paradox is that it "doesn't come in an instant": you have to fight with reality, and you cannot let expectations make it seem as if the pain is not supposed to be there.

A heuristic completes the idea (same source). As you pursue external goals you have to go through inner turmoil and "**chip away at who you are** so that you can see things for what they are", because who you are "is just a culmination of ideas and beliefs", so you have to shed those beliefs. Koe clarifies that you do not think of it this way while doing it: "this is more of a heuristic." You chip away so that you can see reality and what you are trying to accomplish. The heuristic links the structural metaphors to the theory of perception from Chapter 3: the beliefs you remove are filters, so removing them is not only a change of self but a change of what you can see.

#### Gradual or extreme? An unresolved tension

The metaphors of this section (city, house, marble) all recommend gradual change: replace small pieces, make calculated taps, do not strike too hard. Section 4.1 added the advice to reduce an action to its most frictionless step. Section 4.2, however, presented the case for being extreme, burning the boats and flipping the switch, and section 4.4 will present the advice to rip yourself out of your environment overnight and "do nothing the same for at least a day." It would be easy to read this as a drift, from a gradual early Koe to an extreme late one. The dates do not support that reading. Koe never explicitly corrects one position in favor of the other; he alternates between the two registers across the corpus, and both run in parallel from 2022 to 2026.

| Period | Gradual register | Extreme register |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | Identity as a city: remove small pieces, not the heart (2022-07); stacking small wins as "the more sustainable option" (2022-07) | "Disappear for 6 months", "rip the band-aid off" (2022) |
| 2023 | House remodeled with "small calculated taps" (2023-05); progressive overload: raise income by $5,000 at a time, not by $100,000 in a month (2023-07); marble sculpted with "small precise taps" (2023-08) | — |
| 2024 | Shrink the action to the most frictionless step (2024-06); "higher lows, not higher highs" and the intensity trap (2024-10) | Successful people "disappear from society for months at a time" (2024-07); "rip the band-aid off" again (2024); manufacture a "mental rock bottom" and flip the switch overnight (2024-12) |
| 2025 | "Take it gradually" while the new identity solidifies (2025-01); add "the smallest 2.5 lb plate", no "ego lifting" (2025-05) | "Go a little insane"; true balance in "high highs and low lows" (2025-03); "you need to be extreme", easier to change everything at once (2025-06); "six years of progress in six months" (2025-12) |
| 2026 | — | Rip yourself out of your environment overnight; do nothing the same for a day (2026-06); burn the boats rather than keep a backup plan (2026-08) |

The gradual entries after 2023 deserve to be spelled out, because they show that the gradual register does not fade. In 2023, arguing that persistence comes from the momentum of small wins, Koe borrows the gym's principle of progressive overload: "you don't try to increase income by $100,000 a month in one month"; you increase it by $5,000, then it picks up, "and before you know it you're bench pressing a financial 315 pounds for 10 reps" (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11). In October 2024 he names the **intensity trap**: burnout and overwhelm "usually stem from trying to stick out intensity too long instead of transitioning into consistency", so you must "know when to drop down to a new baseline and maintain the progress you've made"; "it's more about higher lows than it is higher highs" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13). In January 2025, describing the fight between who you are and who you want to become, he advises taking it gradually until the new identity solidifies (Chapter 3, section 3.6). And in May 2025: "You don't jump up in your bench press by 50 lbs at a time. The most developed lifters know that adding the smallest 2.5 lb plate every 1 to 2 weeks is how they see the most progress and stay addicted. And they also understand that ego lifting isn't going to get you anywhere" (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08).

The extreme register is just as continuous. In 2024 he describes a pattern he has noticed "in all successful individuals: they live in their own little world. They disappear from society for months at a time to force pure focus on themselves and their vision" (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). In March 2025 he redefines balance itself: the average person lacks "true balance which lies in contrast", meaningful work and meaningful rest, "not the middle path but the path of high highs and low lows, catastrophic failure and blissful success"; floating in the middle, life becomes "a blur of boredom and anxiety", and the alternative to a fancy vacation is to "go a little insane", flip the switch overnight and become an entirely new person (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). In other words, in 2025 the two registers appear within months of each other.

The reasons Koe gives for his positions are partial. The extreme route, he says, is a personal preference ("very masculine"), and the backup plan "can work, absolutely"; tactical stress is "not for the faint-hearted." In a 2025 discussion of developmental levels he adds a warning about what he calls **glitches**: tactics or technologies that force you into a channel or bust through a plateau, such as psychedelics, performance-enhancing drugs, moving into an apartment you cannot afford in order to create a real deadline ("tactical stress, a tool to kick you into an intense season of progress"), and, most recently, AI, "the most recent and widely available glitch that crosses into all domains." Even if they accelerate progress, glitches "increase in risk at lower levels of consciousness", so his advice is to "max out your natural potential first, so you have ample experience and don't get one-shotted" (**Source:** `A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2025-10-19). (The term belongs to his developmental model, Human 3.0, treated in Chapter 38; it is distinct from his older phrase "glitch in the Matrix", which means breaking a robotic pattern by becoming aware of it.) In one 2025 video he even says that changing your life "doesn't mean packing up, moving across the country", in tension with his own war-mode advice. A coherent reading, which the corpus supports but does not state, is that the two registers apply to different objects. The gradual register fits the central pieces of an identity (the "heart" of the city), concrete habits, and people whose situation cannot absorb a large shock. The extreme register fits the moment of the identity flip itself, the environment (which can be changed abruptly without demolishing the self), and brief, deliberate periods of intensity. The marble metaphor already contains this synthesis: one forceful decision, then precise taps.

#### Stepping into the unknown

Every version of the process includes a step into the unknown. How should that step be taken?

Koe's general principle is from 2025 (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). Even with a plan and a vision, you must be okay making guesses and stepping into the unknown without being told what to do; you must get used to making your own decisions and accept that all of life is uncertain. "The more you can bear that uncertainty the more potential you have." It is okay not to know what to do and to feel overwhelmed: you are doing something new. Changing your life will be painful, but the pain "is actually a very good sign."

A 2022 metaphor offers reassurance (**Source:** `You Have A $100,000 Product In Your Head (One Person Business Series).md`, 2022-12-04). In the context of starting to write, build and promote, Koe says that if you are anxious or fearful you should remember that "the unknown becomes known with time." If you were thrown into the middle of the ocean without knowing how to swim, and you could still breathe and live, you would eventually learn. He admits the example is imperfect. You will have to lean into the unknown at some point, "and it might as well be now."

A 2024 metaphor qualifies the reassurance and argues against jumping in unprepared (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (Here's How You Win).md`, 2024-07-21). To change your life you must remove yourself from the repetitive string of tasks and dive into the unknown, but how? Imagine you cannot swim and are offered a scenic helicopter ride over the ocean; you may fear or reject it, but "you'd be silly not to accept." Now imagine the pilot flies low, pushes you out, and lifts just enough so that you are forced to swim. There are three outcomes: you succumb to fear, overthink and drown; you flail like a madman, lose energy and drown; or you remain calm, attempt to float, and perhaps stay up long enough to devise a plan. "None of those are optimal." He contrasts this with the map of a video game: you cannot teleport into unknown areas, or you would be swarmed by monsters fifty levels higher and killed in one hit. "Is that fun?" The helicopter push is the extreme register taken to its limit, and Koe rejects it: entry into the unknown should be progressive, at the edge of your current level. (The same video develops the game-map model, which Chapter 10 treats.)

Placed side by side, the two ocean images are not contradictory. The 2022 image says that you can adapt to the unknown; the 2024 image says that being thrown into it with no preparation is not the best way to do it. Both are compatible with the marble: a committed first step, then progression.

#### True comfort

The last piece of this section reframes what discomfort means. Koe's 2023 video on mental mastery opens (after an anecdote about finding a huge earwig in his new place) with a principle (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05). "Comfort is an impermanent state that humans love to call home." The longer we box ourselves into this "**mental house**" we call home, the more uncomfortable it gets. The only path to **true comfort** is reaching "the level of mind that is comfortable with the inevitable discomfort life presents." The problem is that when faced with discomfort, most people's automatic reaction is to flee rather than fight: not necessarily physically, but the mind flees toward the mental comfort of a past experience. "The mind wants to move even if the body is still." The image of the mental house links back to "a house, not a home": the comfort we cling to is the structure of the current identity, and it becomes a trap the longer we stay inside. True comfort is not the absence of discomfort but a level of mind at which discomfort no longer drives you to flee.

Three reframes help to lean into discomfort.

First (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12): "you have to come at this from a perspective of: hey, this isn't actually threatening my survival, this isn't going to kill me." What it may kill is "a few of my beliefs, which is going to cause that stress response, but at the same time it's just a belief", and you are able to create new beliefs that lead to a beneficial future. The reframe applies conceptual survival against itself: the stress response is real, but what is at stake is a belief, not your life.

Second (same source): "90 plus percent of the population are a **walking contradiction**." They call a bold move a risk even when it brings massive reward. Koe's question: what is riskier, "diving into the discomfort that life holds, where a positive potential future lies for yourself, or repeating the same familiar thoughts, beliefs and experiences that you have had for the past 10 years of your life?" The implied answer is that familiarity is the real risk. The percentage is rhetorical.

Third (**Source:** `The creator economy is dying thanks to AI.md`, 2025-02-16): "people become so accustomed to pain that it feels like home, and leaving it seems like the real suffering." This explains why people do not change even when they say they hate their jobs. Koe repeats it later in the video in connection with social media: most people want things fed to them, and social media companies give them exactly that. Staying in familiar pain feels safer than the suffering of leaving. This is the emotional side of "living yesterday today": familiar pain is predictable, and predictability is what the identity defends.

Finally, the 2024 summary of *The Art of Focus* explains why masters seek out difficulty (**Source:** `The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md`, 2024-01-18). It quotes the book: "The closed-minded quick fix attitude toward life is the greatest trap of our times and it runs much deeper than the average person believes." Ironically, thinking you are not a victim of closed-mindedness is closed-mindedness itself. Most people close their minds at the first hint of difficulty; masters embrace difficulty because they have experienced its pattern, "the **perception threshold** or runner's high": when you stick through increasing difficulty, you eventually break through into enjoyment, at both macro and micro scales. You did not need a quick fix to learn to walk, to speak or to brew your favorite coffee; those skills came from necessity and interest, even though they were as difficult as any other skill. Because you do not treat your ideal future as a necessity or an interest, situations become more difficult than they are. "Don't fight with it, flow with it." The perception threshold is one of Koe's central coined terms; section 4.7 reconstructs it in detail as the third step of the process for raising the level of mind.

### 4.4 Reprogramming: Immersion in the Environment of the Future Self

Sections 4.2 and 4.3 described what starts change and what it feels like. This section describes Koe's main technique for carrying it out. The premise comes from Chapter 1 and Chapter 3: identity is made of information, installed by repetition and conditioned by environment. If so, the most direct way to change identity is to change the information and the environment that feed it. Koe calls this **reprogramming your mind**. The section starts with his framing of the problem (who wrote the program?), then the model of the mind as an operating system, then the four "certainties" he lists before reprogramming, then the method itself (new goals, new stimuli, immersion), and finally the environment, which he treats both as what conditions you and as something you create.

#### Who wrote the software running in your head?

A 2025 video opens with a question from a tweet by Elon Musk: "Who wrote the software running in your head? Are you sure you actually want it there?" (**Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`, 2025-10-05). Koe's elaboration is his own. Most people have not practiced thinking; when you talk to them "you aren't talking to them, you're talking to the TV, social media, their parents, their teachers, their insecurities and trauma, or even patterns they picked up from AI. It's very robotic." He notes the irony that everyone cries about being more authentic while parroting that very statement from someone else. And he does not present this as a moral failing: "You can't escape this, this is just how the mind works." We remember information that is important to survival, and the more it is repeated, the more efficient it becomes. The program is the normal product of a mind that learns by repetition.

The same video states what follows (same source). The conscious mind processes about 50 bits of information per second; the unconscious, about 11 million. "If you want to change your life, your most important life task is using your limited conscious attention to become aware of and reprogram what's happening in the depths of your unconscious mind." He cites Carl Jung (the transcript reads "Carl Young"): "until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate." If you reprogram the unconscious toward success, "you achieve success automatically. You don't have to think about what to do because you just do it." Most people have not done this and "are heading off a cliff by default": set on a path at birth, unquestioned, blind to their own actions, with a destination of "pure mediocrity." The figures of 50 bits and 11 million bits are the ones Koe uses throughout his work (Chapter 6 examines them); here they serve to establish an asymmetry: the conscious mind is small, but it is the only instrument that can rewrite the large unconscious one.

**Complementary context:** The Jung line is a widely circulated paraphrase; a close version appears in *Aion* (1951), where Jung writes that when an inner situation is not made conscious, it happens outside as fate. The 11-million-bits estimate for sensory input and the much smaller figure for conscious processing come from information-theory approximations popularized in the 1970s–1990s; they are orders of magnitude, not measurements.

An earlier statement assigns the task to the individual (**Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`, 2023-05-08). "It is your job to become aware of your mental programming and work to reverse it; this is how you become the **main character** of your own story." And: "Changing who you are is difficult, it takes intention and effort, and nobody can do it for you." In that video he announced a follow-up called "The Art of Self-Architecture" (the name of section 4.3's subject). "Main character" is Koe's term for the opposite of the NPC (Chapter 1): the person who has a clear vision, self-generated goals and daily actions, and who imitates consciously rather than by default.

#### The mind as an operating system

Koe's favorite metaphor for the reprogrammable mind is software (**Source:** `This Simple Reading Habit Will Change Your Life (I Promise).md`, 2024-08-04). "The mind is the operating system for reality, and most people are running on Windows 98; many others have devolved into a pager that beeps when they need to have a negative reaction to something that challenged the lines of government rhetoric in their software." The joke has a structure: Windows 98 is outdated but still a general system; the pager is a system that can do only one thing, react. You change your life by reprogramming your mind: read books that challenge your beliefs, that expose how much you do not know, and that encourage behavior change that benefits you and others. He adds two pieces of practical advice: "Don't expect to do a mental 315lb bench press on your first try", and pass over ideas you do not understand, because "your mind will slowly connect the dots, because that's just what the subconscious mind does." The bench-press image connects reprogramming to the gym analogies of section 4.1: mental capacity is built progressively.

The possibility of reprogramming gets theoretical backing from a source Koe calls "a certified genius thinker", Daniel Schmachtenberger (**Source:** `This mental model will make you think like a strategic genius.md`, 2025-07-20). Koe quotes him: "You could say that what humans' genetics selected for [is] memetics. Our genetics selected for radical neuroplasticity and the capacity to have much more significant software upgrades that could change our capacity without needing hardware upgrades." The quote is used in the context of explaining "why smart people are incredibly dumb", and the implication Koe draws is that your level of mind (the software) can be upgraded without any change to the biology (the hardware). The idea is Schmachtenberger's; the application to individual reprogramming is Koe's.

**Complementary context:** Daniel Schmachtenberger is a social philosopher known for his work on the "metacrisis" and on civilizational risk. "Memetics" refers to the study of how ideas (memes, in Richard Dawkins' original sense) replicate and spread. The claim that human evolution favored cultural learning capacity over fixed instinct is common in cultural evolution research; Schmachtenberger's phrasing is his own.

#### Four certainties before reprogramming

Before presenting his method in the 2025 video, Koe lists "a few things we know for sure" (**Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`, 2025-10-05). They function as premises.

| # | What Koe says "we know for sure" | Where it is developed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The mind evolves and expands through stages of increasing complexity, which makes life more interesting and enjoyable, but you can get stuck at a low level for life (his model Human 3.0) | Section 4.7; Chapter 38 |
| 2 | Flow, or optimal experience, stems from "a self-generated goal, complete clarity on how to achieve it and a level of challenge that is just above your skill level" (he refers to Csikszentmihalyi and Steven Kotler) | Chapter 10 |
| 3 | Your mind interprets reality through a lens of psychological survival: identity forms around your culture's values and beliefs, and you feel threatened when identity is threatened | Chapter 3 |
| 4 | "Being extreme changes your brain because it enhances neuroplasticity"; "novelty, challenge and big goals shape how you process and store information" | Section 4.2 |

The phrase "we know for sure" overstates the status of these premises. The first is a developmental framework (Koe's own synthesis, built on models by others); the second summarizes flow research; the third is his law of conceptual survival, which he himself has called "just a concept, a metaphor" (Chapter 3); the fourth is his extrapolation from neuroplasticity. Taken together, they explain why reprogramming should work: the mind can grow (1), it grows best toward a self-generated goal at the edge of skill (2), it resists because it treats identity as survival (3), and intensity and novelty accelerate the rewiring (4).

#### Create your own matrix

The first step of the 2025 method is to replace the goal at the root of the program (**Source:** `You're Stuck In The Matrix, Here's How You Escape.md`, 2025-10-05). "The mind craves order and certainty, that's why we are so quick to adopt the **psychological infrastructure** that others assign to us." The chain of conditioning runs from goals to habits: "we pursue society's goals, which influence what we deem important enough to learn, which influences the choices we make, which condition those behaviors as habits that we unconsciously repeat." If the chain begins with a goal, so must the reprogramming: start with a goal, a clear vision, and replace "go to school, get a job and retire" with something self-generated and more meaningful. Koe calls this **creating your own matrix**: not escaping all programming (which is impossible, since "this is just how the mind works") but writing your own.

The same logic appears in a 2024 formulation (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). "If you were to condition yourself with new stimuli, constant self-education, to the point of having an identity that couldn't survive without achieving new goals, you would inevitably achieve whatever they are with ease." For a successful business, relationship or anything out of the norm, "you must fundamentally change the goals your mind operates on by changing who you are. To change who you are you must educate, practice and experience new information to reprogram your mind's **faulty wiring** that was installed by Society." The phrase "an identity that couldn't survive without achieving new goals" is the bridge to section 4.5: the aim of reprogramming is to make the survival instinct work for the new goals.

A 2023 formulation puts the whole sequence in two conditional sentences (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). "With or without knowing it we are all reinforcing our potentially mediocre identity that determines the outcome of our lives", and for most people the outcome is negative. "If you want to change the outcome of your life, change who you are. If you want to change who you are, change the direction of your life: expose yourself to new experiences, environments and information to allow your mind a chance to discover new goals you can adopt that will carry you toward a better future." Goals "are everything; they create your entire life." The interesting feature of this version is its modesty about goals: you do not need to know the new goal in advance. Exposure gives the mind "a chance to discover" goals it can adopt. This is consistent with Chapter 7's thesis that you will never know exactly what you want and must discover it by experiment.

#### Hunt and stack whys

A method from 2023 explains how reasons accumulate around a new behavior (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). "Hunt and stack whys, or reasons, to cultivate a personal philosophy." "If you struggle to act it's because you don't have a compelling personal and intrinsic reason to act." The first step is difficult, but you must take it; then, as you experience life, "keep an eye out for reasons you can collect." Reasons accumulate around an action until it becomes self-motivating. This is the general form of the "stacking whys" behind writing described in section 4.1, and it is the third step of the four-step reinvention process of 2023 (section 4.6). The verb "hunt" matters: reasons are not invented by reflection alone; they are found in experience and collected. And they add up to a "personal philosophy", which is to say, an identity.

A practical companion asks you to study two things (**Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`, 2023-09-24). First, study what generates attention: psychology, media, design, writing, marketing, sales, persuasion, influence and the mind. Second, study yourself: "your thoughts, emotions, actions, and follow them to their root: what goals, problems and beliefs have others projected on you that led to those thoughts, emotions and actions." It may sound like a lot, he says, but you have your entire lifetime; when you are not doing this you are distracted, mind-numbing, doing nothing. "There's nothing wrong with rest, but there is something wrong with not balancing it out with ... meaningful work, because the only pain greater than seeing what you are capable of is not seeing what you were capable of. Your education should and must reflect that." The second instruction is the excavation method of Chapter 3 (working backward from behavior to the unconscious goals that produce it), now used as a step of reprogramming: you cannot rewrite a program you have not read.

#### Immersion in the environment of the future self

The core of the method is immersion. Koe's 2026 formulation is the most explicit (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). "If you want to change your life fast, the most useful thing you can do is immerse your mind in the **environment of your future self**. Bathe your psyche in the opinions, beliefs, and education that person would have without judgment." A course is useful for learning structured knowledge, "but the ins and outs and the opinions, all of the different angles, are best learned by just diving into a new environment where that's all there is." He sees an opportunity in modern life: in the past you were confined to your little tribe; today "you can pick and choose. You can become anything ... plop yourself in the environment, deal with the discomfort, and you'll want to become that person because **memes replicate**." The phrase "memes replicate" turns the mechanism of conditioning (Chapter 1) into a tool. The same process that installed the old program (ideas spread to whoever is exposed to them) will install the new one, if you choose the exposure.

The same video adds a condition (same source). "Identity is created through learning. That's like the lesson." But you are not learning if you are just reacting all the time or think you know everything, because then you do not absorb information. "So, you must willingly expose yourself to perspectives that you disagree with and try to understand why they are right." The instruction to bathe the psyche "without judgment" and the instruction to seek out disagreement are the same rule: immersion only works if the defense mechanism is suspended long enough for new information to enter.

A 2026 video adds a concrete example and the reason immersion is necessary (**Source:** `How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md`, 2026-06-28). The first action is to "hurl yourself into an environment that forces growth"; the fastest way to change your life is to rip yourself out of your physical and digital environment overnight: the places you go, the accounts you follow, the information you consume. "**Behavior change equals identity change**." You can diet and lose 30 pounds, but if you are not a person who values health and enjoys the process, you will always feel as if you are running uphill and, like most people, gain it all back "unless you fundamentally change who you are." If you do not adopt now the values of the person you want to become, by hurling yourself into a new environment and reconditioning your mind, you will regress to who you were. The diet example is the clearest illustration in the corpus of the difference between first- and second-order change (4.1): the weight is lost by behavior; it is kept off only by identity.

The same 2026 video gives an exercise (same source): "**flip the switch**." Become incredibly conscious of all stimuli, since all of it feeds into who you are. "You wake up tomorrow and you do nothing the same for at least a day." Set your alarm for a different time; plan exactly what you will do when you wake up, different from usual; eat different foods; talk to different people; consume different content: everything. Koe presents the video itself as a change of environment and suggests filling yourself with more such content (books, other YouTubers) instead of "entertaining yourself to death on a dopamine feed", a feed of what Koe calls **cheap dopamine**: novelty received without effort (scrolling, streaming, games, fast food), as opposed to the earned dopamine of pursuing a hard goal (section 11.2 develops the distinction). The exercise is deliberately total and deliberately short: one day of breaking every pattern makes visible how much of the day was automatic.

The idea predates 2026. A 2024 process describes reprogramming in two steps (**Source:** `It’s actually pretty easy to be self-disciplined (if you stop getting distracted).md`, 2024-05-12). First, "just awareness that it's going to be difficult to change." Second, "reprogramming your mind to operate towards becoming a new identity": throw yourself into a new environment that aligns with who you want to become; "drown your mind in ideas, information and education that exposes you to new goals and ways to achieve those goals"; and "view every situation through the perspective of your ideal lifestyle and make decisions accordingly." "Throw yourself into the deep end and teach yourself to swim." But most people will not take the leap out of fear, "and that's why we need to talk about self-confidence rather than self-discipline." The last sentence is a pivot that the rest of the video develops: what blocks immersion is not lack of discipline but lack of confidence, the fear of the unknown discussed in 4.3.

A 2024 video on focus gives the same method under the name "**throw your mind off the deep end**" (**Source:** `It's actually pretty easy to focus 12 hours a day (if you do this).md`, 2024-10-13). It is the second way out of what he calls the lost phase. "Everything starts with identity": the web of ideas that shapes how you view the world, the perspective shaped by decades of social conditioning that makes you desire what you desire. To love working toward your dreams, your mind must reflect that: "it can't be some idea you like the sound of; it has to be who you are." How do you change who you are? Reprogram your mind: rip yourself out of your current lifestyle and immerse yourself in information that slowly shapes how you see the world. Follow people with the goals you want; listen to podcasts; read books; "put your money where your mouth is" by purchasing courses that give you ideas that compound into clarity. "Wake up tomorrow and do nothing the same": plan a new week, set new priority tasks, have new conversations, overload yourself with new information. The phrase "it can't be some idea you like the sound of; it has to be who you are" is a compact restatement of first-order change.

A 2023 version names the possible scale of the change and the emotional cost (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). "With a vision, goals and projects you are primed for an entirely new life; all you have to do is take the leap." Launch yourself into a new physical or digital environment: move across the country, unfollow everyone online, follow people aligned with your future, change what you wear, where you shop and who you see, "drown your mind in the books you've been putting off reading." He singles out clothing as "a very big one": he sees people lock themselves into specific identities through what they wear. The goal is to "align reality with who you want to become." He is explicit about the cost. You will feel threatened at the start, because you are expanding your identity and it wants to survive; it will be painful; "**your old identity will die because it is starved of the information that reinforces it**." Hold strong during this "extremely difficult yet extremely meaningful turning point." The phrase about starvation is the key mechanism of the whole section. An identity is maintained by the information that confirms it. Cut off that information and the identity weakens; supply different information and a different identity grows.

A 2025 version explains when learning actually happens (**Source:** `How To Gamify Your Life (And Reinvent Yourself... Fast).md`, 2025-05-08). "To change your mind completely you need to immerse yourself in new sources of information to discover the puzzle pieces that lead to clarity": read new books, talk to new people, follow new accounts, visit a place you have wanted to go, take a long walk with a podcast, take a course on a new skill for your career or a business. (In the video he mentions his own mini course on systemizing life with AI, which he describes as "a focus coach or a tribe of mentors", as one way to do this.) "It doesn't really matter what information you consume as long as it has the potential to spark change. Because when your mind is in a state of wanting to avoid the current trajectory of your life, the new goal, this is when true learning occurs. You will feel the dopamine spurt into your brain when you find a potential opportunity to pursue." The claim links this section to 4.2: the dissonance that triggers change is also what makes immersion effective. A mind that wants to escape its trajectory is primed to notice anything that might help.

The last statement of the immersion method comes from the 2025 video on the alter ego and draws the conclusion about motivation (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). "You don't need motivation or discipline when you are the person who would take specific actions." You must educate, practice and experience new information to reprogram "your mind's faulty wiring that was installed by society"; rip yourself out of the environment that keeps you the same; "**force yourself into places you don't belong until you do**"; and channel what you learn into a project toward your vision. Then "take your alter ego and go war mode" (both developed in sections 4.5 and 4.6). "Force yourself into places you don't belong until you do" is the immersion principle in its most compressed form: belonging is not a precondition for being in an environment; it is the result of staying there.

Koe's statements on immersion span 2023 to 2026, and their content is stable. What varies is emphasis: in 2023 the stress is on starving the old identity; in 2024 on throwing the mind into the deep end and on confidence; in 2025 on the state of mind that makes learning possible; in 2026 on bathing the psyche without judgment, seeking disagreement, and changing everything overnight.

#### Your environment conditions you, but you create your environment

Immersion assumes that environment shapes identity. Koe makes the assumption explicit through a theory he borrows (**Source:** `The Cheap Dopamine Epidemic Stop Ruining Your Life.md`, 2024-11-24). He summarizes the "broken windows theory" "in a nutshell": an experiment studied two environments, one with intentionally broken windows and one clean and tidy. Where new windows were installed and the area was cleaned up, crime decreased by about 50 percent; where windows were broken and a mess was created, crime increased. The reason: "your environment heavily conditions your behavior." On the internet "we're constantly exposing our minds to an area with thousands of broken windows a day", and "digital environments condition your mind much faster than physical ones because there is more information available." Then the turn that makes the theory useful: "few people realize that you chose to put yourself in that environment and you as an intentional individual can create a new one. **Your environment conditions who you are, but most people forget that you create your environment**."

**Complementary context:** The broken windows theory was proposed by James Q. Wilson and George Kelling in a 1982 article in *The Atlantic*. It drew on earlier experiments by Philip Zimbardo on abandoned cars. The theory influenced policing in the 1990s and remains contested; the specific "about 50 percent" figure Koe gives is not traceable to a named study in the transcript. His use is an analogy for digital environments, not a claim about criminology.

The 2026 reinvention video explains why the environment must be changed quickly (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). This is the third of the four patterns of that video (section 4.6): "You need to change your environment faster than the identity can recalibrate, because your old self is partly stored in your surroundings": in the people you follow online, your bed, your routine, the people you hang out with ("a big one"). "Your identity is often programmed socially. The same person in two different friend groups will behave like two different people." When you log on to social media, you also become a different person. The principle that the old self is "stored" in the environment explains the urgency of the immersion practices described above. If you change slowly, the environment keeps loading the old self back into you; you must change the surroundings faster than the identity can rebuild itself from them.

A concrete method follows in the same video (same source). "A simple move here is to just wipe your phone, or throw your phone in a lake and get a new one, and start from scratch, and you'll really be able to notice the threat response then": "What about all my contacts, apps, accounts?" Koe admits he would feel the same, but the last time he had to switch phones he started from a clean slate: if people needed him, they would contact him; if he needed someone, he would figure it out; if he needed something, he would get it. The method: wipe your phone and download only what is necessary; if you need to do something, do it, "go through the friction of doing it. Don't make it this easily accessible thing." The exercise has a diagnostic function that he points out: the panic you feel at losing contacts and apps is the threat response of the identity stored in the device.

A 2025 process calls the redesign of surroundings **environment engineering** (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). The actions: unfollow people who do not put out information that helps you achieve a better life; "throw away the food that you allow yourself to eat at 3:00 a.m."; "pull out a paper and map out exactly what you're going to do that week down to the hour"; "review and iterate every week. You need that feedback loop"; immerse yourself in people, places and things that give you "the little novel tactics, things to experiment with"; follow new accounts "even if you disagree with them or don't like them as a person, but their information helps you"; scroll Amazon until a book sounds interesting; scroll YouTube "until you break out of your normal algorithm and can start curating another one"; or skip algorithmic social media and "only go based off of search." The list mixes physical changes (food), temporal structure (the week mapped to the hour, with a feedback loop) and informational curation (accounts, algorithm, search). All three are environment in Koe's sense.

The physical environment matters at a larger scale too (**Source:** `Focus Is A Superpower (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-07-11). Koe plays on a word: in the past people formed settlements; today people "settle" for where they are and do not pursue more, because society said it was enough to operate within and feed the machine. They group into environments that justify each other's mediocre behavior and program themselves to think it is normal or good. "If that's what you want, do it." But "the unknown is where your potential lies." And then a strong claim: "the single most important decision you make is where you live", because it drives business opportunities, relationships, food and water supply, politics, activities and day-to-day quality of life. The claim is Koe's opinion, offered without comparison to other decisions, but it is consistent with the principle that the old self is stored in the surroundings: where you live determines most of the surroundings.

At the smallest scale, the environment is the half-hour before work. Koe's seventh "commandment" for focused work, from his 2023 video on the 4-hour workday, is "manipulate your environment" (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). Your environment and everything leading up to your focus sessions matter, or at least you must be conscious of them. If you do focus work in the morning (which he recommends), are you setting yourself up to be undistracted by the time you reach your desk? Have a routine: brush your teeth, take out the dog, make coffee, straight into work. This makes getting out of bed easier because you are not wondering what to do with thirty spare minutes, wasting time and running over into work time; "that helps reduce **decision fatigue** going into the work itself." During work, prepare for distractions: the phone on the other side of the room; a hat "to narrow your attention, which actually does help"; noise-canceling headphones. "None of this is set in stone ... life is a process of iteration and growth and evolution." As long as you become aware of the problems preventing an efficient four hours and work to improve them, "you're doing fine." The guiding question: "how can you manipulate your environment to conserve mental energy for your priority tasks?"

The evolution of Koe's thinking on environment is visible across these passages. In 2023 the emphasis is on optimizing the work environment to conserve mental energy (the pre-work routine, the phone across the room) and on where you live. From 2024 to 2026 the environment becomes a lever of identity change: broken windows, environment engineering, wiping the phone, changing the environment faster than the identity can recalibrate. The two are compatible. The first treats environment as a support for a given identity's work; the second treats it as the storage medium of identity itself.

### 4.5 Using Survival in Your Favor: Future Self, Alter Ego and Highest Self

Chapter 3 ended with three possible responses to the law of conceptual survival: release identities, detach from them, or direct the survival instinct deliberately. This section develops the third. If the mind defends whatever it identifies with, then identifying on purpose with the person you want to become should make the defense work for the change instead of against it. Koe has several names and tools for this: identifying with a positive identity, weaponizing your survival, the alter ego, the highest version of yourself, the perspective of your ideal self. The section begins with the problem these tools are meant to solve (the cycle of motivation and relapse), then presents the mechanism, then the tools one by one, and ends with the perspective ladder that frames them.

#### The problem: a vicious cycle and learned helplessness

The 2026 reinvention video opens with a description that many readers will recognize (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). People between 20 and 35 get trapped in a **vicious cycle**. They are motivated by an external source (a video, a book, a conversation), feel "this is your moment", and go all in: start the business, start working out, start learning a skill. Then, "like clockwork, 2 days, a week, 2 weeks later, you fall off." You easily go back to the life you swore you despised, as if you were addicted to it, as if you preferred it, "as if the desire to change your life was just another form of cheap dopamine that you couldn't resist" (cheap dopamine in the sense given in section 4.4: effortless novelty). You get another hit of motivation, start from zero and repeat; you are frustrated, "but not enough to solidify the change." Then you wake up one, two, ten years later. Another video on productivity, self-help, motivation, discipline or dopamine "isn't even going to solve half the problem." What is needed is to understand "the composition of the mind": how its intangible parts act "like a puppet master over your actions." The age range is Koe's observation of his audience; the diagnosis is that motivation, being external, does not touch the identity, which therefore reasserts itself.

The 2025 video gives the emotional consequence of the cycle (**Source:** `How to fix your entire life in 1 day.md`, 2025-12-28). Koe thinks reflecting and changing direction is wise, and that the new year is a good moment for it. But "the worst feeling is when you make a promise to yourself and then you end up breaking it", especially repeatedly, because you start to feel helpless. Without knowing what you are doing, you may continue the cycle for years.

Earlier in 2025 Koe had given that helplessness its technical name (**Source:** `How To Take Back Control Of Your Life With Strategic Thinking.md`, 2025-03-16). He opens the video by defining **learned helplessness** as the reason most people never achieve their goals: "a psychological condition where a person or animal learns to behave helplessly in a particular situation even when the opportunity to avoid or escape it is available." It occurs after an individual has experienced repeated aversive stimuli (pain or failure) that they cannot control. As a result, they come to believe their actions have no effect on their environment, and they give up trying to change their circumstances. His example: "you probably have that friend who believes they just can't do anything", and you look at them and think, "can't you see that this is possible if you look at it from a different angle?" Later in the video he gives the reframe that makes the concept hopeful: "You aren't helpless, you've just learned to be so." What has been learned can be unlearned.

**Complementary context:** Learned helplessness was described by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier in experiments with dogs in the late 1960s. Later work by Maier and Seligman (2016) revised the original interpretation: passivity appears to be the default response to prolonged aversive events, and what is learned is the expectation of control. Koe uses the standard textbook definition without naming its authors.

The same video frames the whole problem as a game (same source). "Success is mostly about winning the internal battle between your lower and higher self. It's a **mind game**." You lose that game when you accept that negative outcomes for your life are the only outcomes; you win it when you "cultivate a mind that can thrive in any situation." He adds: "Everything is a mind game within one big mind game." To play it you must understand how the mind works, and from there create a strategy to win, which is to say, become a strategic thinker. He justifies starting "from ground zero" with beliefs before tactics: "you need the belief before you can actually act." The first third or half of that video is explicitly meant "to reprogram your mind", installing beliefs so that when you create a strategy your mind does not immediately think "I can't do that." And he argues the order of operations directly (same source): "If you don't understand the root cause of why you fail to achieve big things, then the practical steps aren't going to help you at all." The evidence he offers is rhetorical: "You've already gotten all of the practical steps, you've watched all the YouTube videos; has it gotten you anywhere?"

In 2026 he adds a sharp line about the game (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). "Just think of this as the mind game ... the mind game of life. And most people don't play it because they are a slave to their mind. And the characteristic of being a slave to your mind is that you don't even know it." You need to become the master instead. The phrase "the characteristic of being a slave to your mind is that you don't even know it" is the reason the rest of this section starts with awareness: the tools below only work once you can see the game.

#### The mechanism: survive the identity you choose

The foundational argument is from Koe's 2023 video on the 4-hour workday (**Source:** `The 4-Hour Workday (Focused Work Changed My Life).md`, 2023-01-01; the same passage appears in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). One quality separates humans from other beings: "humans survive more so on a conceptual level, while animals survive on a physical level"; "you wouldn't see a giraffe getting offended because it doesn't have the same beliefs as you." Therefore, "if you are trying to survive the idea or belief of the 4-hour workday, that is going to be much more powerful for actually making it happen"; "you kind of have to identify with it in a good way." He acknowledges the objection: spiritual teachers warn against attaching your identity to things, because it becomes part of yourself, you work to survive it, and "if that is static then it is going to cause some form of unnecessary suffering." But "this can be spun for good": you can create a **positive identity** that leads to a better future, and the struggles and pain you face become learning experiences for building a system more conducive to your desired lifestyle. Later in the same video he restates the mechanism in terms of threat: you will feel threatened when something threatens that belief or system, and you will therefore make better decisions to productize, systemize or maintain your four hours of focused work.

This argument is Koe's own inversion of a spiritual teaching. The teachers say attachment causes suffering; Koe agrees that static attachment does, and proposes a dynamic, chosen attachment as a lever. As section 3.4 showed, his position on attachment shifts over the following years, and this passage is the starting point of that evolution.

The 2023 newsletter-style argument about New Year's resolutions (already cited in 4.1) states the same mechanism as a rule (**Source:** `How To Hard Reset Your Life (In 30 Minutes).md`, 2023-01-05). Any resolution or goal will not be sustainable unless you have absolute clarity on how to reach it, a system you can repeat daily and make part of your life, and an identity based around it "in alignment with the higher version of yourself." The mechanism: "humans survive on a conceptual level and we try to survive the things that we identify with." So if you identify with the highest version of yourself or your purpose, and create a system to act in alignment with it, "eventually you become that."

A metaphor Koe says he uses "all the time" explains why the chosen identity changes what you notice (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). If you want to quit your job and start a business, dopamine narrows your focus on the information (from conversations, social media, books) that achieves that goal, and you store it "because it aids in the survival of the identity that you're trying to create, because you survive on a conceptual level." The ancestor example: a bare bush goes unnoticed; when it bears new berries, they get noticed only if they have a use; because they aid survival, the ancestor notes them and returns when needed, or picks them and stores them for later. The **berry bush** is the perceptual side of conceptual survival: the identity you are building becomes a filter that makes relevant information visible and memorable, as the identity you had made other information visible before.

#### The key reversal: threatened by failure instead of by success

The 2026 video states the same idea in its most dramatic form (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). "This is the mind-blowing part if you get it. What if you felt threatened when you stopped pursuing a meaningful goal? So much so that achieving the meaningful goal didn't require much discipline, because it's just a part of who you are. It's what your mind wants to survive." The aim is to reprogram the mind so that the survival mechanism works for the goal: "You can change from a person who is threatened by success to a person who is threatened by failure." He calls this "**weaponize your survival mechanism**." And then a qualification: you will still suffer until you transcend the game.

The principle he derives is stated as an aphorism (same source): "The core difference between winners and losers is that **losers are afraid of success, while winners are afraid of mediocrity**." The explanation is the theory of identity threat. The person who does not identify as fit feels threatened when committing to health; the fit person feels threatened by the opposite. The words "winners" and "losers" are provocative, but the definition he gives is purely structural: the difference is not talent or character but which outcome the identity experiences as a threat.

Two examples make the mechanism concrete (same source). The first is the unfit person. You may not consciously identify as unfit, "but you identify with the preferences, habits, and abilities, or the **properties of the self**" that an unfit person would have. When you try to become healthy, your mind sends warning signals: "What happens to going out and partying with my friends? What happens to the late night snacks while watching Netflix? What happens to relaxing on the couch instead of going on a walk?" "You're telling me I can't add an entire stick of butter?" Koe then asks a counterfactual question: if you had never had butter in your food, or did not know it existed, would you resist? Would bland food be good to you because that is all you know? When the opportunity comes to go out or add butter, can you break the ingrained pattern? The counterfactual shows that the resistance is not to the healthy behavior itself but to the loss of a property of the current self.

The second example reverses the direction (same source). "The fit person would be threatened by the opposite. This is the key to changing your life in general", and it baffles the unfit person. If a fit person travels and the only place to eat is McDonald's, they "almost spontaneously combust", thinking they will lose their gains: "I've worked so hard for this. This is who I am ... If I touch this one single seed oil, then I die." Why? Because they adopted the anti-seed-oil meme from Twitter or Instagram as part of their identity, joined a tribe, and eating one would mean being "cast out from the tribe." The mind, wired like the ancestors', cannot distinguish then from now and feels that it will die, so it "fights tooth and nail" to keep them fit. The same applies to money: someone whose business is part of their identity "would feel like a failure if their revenue started to go down", so they would do anything (study, learn, talk to people, find the bottleneck, implement new things, fire people, compete, copy competitors' features) and could not sleep until it was resolved. Koe admits these are hyperbolic, extreme cases, but says that even subtle versions are enough to influence behavior "to the point of staying the same." The McDonald's example is gently ironic (the fit person's panic is irrational), and that is part of the point: the mechanism is the same whether it serves you or not. Weaponizing it does not make it rational; it makes it useful.

The 2026 video summarizes the method in three moves and immediately limits it (same source). "That's kind of how you can **weaponize your survival**: you start to identify as the person you want to become. You put yourself in that environment. You reprogram your mind so that naturally, almost automatically, you start just doing the actions that are going to make you that person." But "you're still playing the game. You're still going to be stuck in this kind of cycle of suffering." This qualification is a real development in Koe's position. In 2023 the positive identity was a way out of unnecessary suffering; in 2026, even a well-chosen identity keeps you inside the survival game, which, as Chapter 3 noted, "cannot be won", only transcended and included. Weaponizing survival is presented as the best way to play the game, not as the end of it. The way beyond it belongs to Part XIV.

#### Containers, votes and evidence: identity lists

Two ideas come from a 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, a guest whose ideas Koe adopts and comments on. They should be attributed to Bush, with Koe's responses noted.

The first is a metaphor (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). Among about ten takeaways from his first year after leaving BlackRock, which he reflected on during a week off the grid in Sedona without a phone, Bush lists: "**the liquid takes the shape of its container**." When you craft a container, for example "I'm someone who works four hours a week" (sic in the transcript; the context is four hours a day), you find the actions that vote for that type of person. Bush ties it to James Clear ("every action you take is a vote for that type of person") and to Alex Hormozi ("figure out who you're trying to be and then gather a ton of evidence for that person"; "overwhelm yourself with evidence"). He also warns himself: he must be careful with labels, because he will execute them "for a long period of time without looking up ... for better or worse." Done correctly, it is a superpower; but "obsessive personalities ... applied to the wrong thing, not good." The warning anticipates Koe's later caution against dogmatic identification.

The second is a method Bush calls **identity lists** (same source). The problem it solves: aspirational identity change ("I want to be someone who works four hours a day ... I need to be focused, organized") is hard because "it's not very easy to see tangible progress towards that." The steps:

1. Whenever he wants to start a behavior or change, he makes a list titled with the identity and pins it as a note on his phone.
2. He defines what that identity entails.
3. Every action he takes toward it, he writes in the note (for example, "I plan my day the day before").
4. Over time this becomes "a big list of evidence" supporting the identity.

The mechanism, in Bush's words, is that it "hacked the dopamine": he takes the action partly because he knows he gets to write it down. It is "a new source of gamification" that reinforces the belief, and "that flywheel starts to spin more and more." Koe summarizes it in his own terms: make a goal conscious, change your behavior to actualize it, and write down each action to reinforce it; Bush: "you start to do it so you can write it down ... and it reinforces it." The cadence Bush describes is about one item per week on each of six or seven identities, which over a year gives about fifty pieces of evidence per identity; after six months he had about a hundred items in total. The benefit is that the list "becomes a source of truth" for the question "am I really making progress on this?", which otherwise stays vague. He had started the practice that year (2023).

Identity lists turn the abstract mechanism of conceptual survival into a daily ledger. Each entry is a small reinforcement of the chosen identity, and the accumulation answers the question that the vicious cycle of motivation never answers: is the change real?

#### Not "fake it till you make it"

An obvious objection to identifying with who you want to become is that it sounds like pretending. Koe addresses this in 2022 (**Source:** `How To Create A Better Life For Yourself.md`, 2022-06-12). The first instruction for working with the future self is to think about it "from an **immaterial perspective**, not a material perspective": "we're imagining things here; this isn't physical, this isn't matter." The common saying "act in alignment with who you want to become" is often mistaken for "fake it till I make it", faking results until you have them. That is not what is meant. The correct reading uses an analogy: just as you can reminisce about a past negative experience (a breakup) and bring its emotions into the present, you can do the same with the unknown aspects of life if you visualize them clearly, and bring in the experience that comes with them. The distinction is between pretending to have results (material) and adopting, in imagination, the perspective and emotional state of the future self (immaterial), so that it shapes present decisions.

#### The alter ego

The most developed tool in this section is the **alter ego**, presented in a 2025 video. The term is not Koe's, and he says so (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). He credits the performance concept of the alter ego to Todd Herman, author of *The Alter Ego Effect*, and quotes two ideas from him: the alter ego "is a bridge between who you are now and who you are meant to be", and it allows you to step outside yourself to see the bigger picture. Koe's adaptation adds three things of his own: the alter ego as a "sub identity", "like a warrior going into battle"; an explanation of why it works, based on his model of attention; and, later in the video, the idea that it is "a vessel for identity change" rather than a mask. He also connects it to stages of ego development.

**Complementary context:** Todd Herman is a performance coach whose book *The Alter Ego Effect* (2019) describes how athletes and performers adopt deliberately constructed personas to access qualities they struggle to express in their everyday identity.

The video opens with examples (same source). Martin Luther King Jr. would wear non-prescription glasses to become a distinguished intellectual for his talks. Kobe Bryant adopted the "Black Mamba" during a difficult period of his life, reflecting the mindset he wanted on court ("fast, smooth and terrifying"); Koe calls it Kobe's "secret weapon." Eddie Hall, a strongman, said in a clip that he could not lift his competition weights in normal training, but when he visualized in detail his family being crushed by the weights or by a car, he tapped into superhuman strength. Strongman competitors, Koe says, are "adopting the qualities of the identity that would be in a terrifying situation." The lesson: bringing qualities from a different situation or identity into the current one lets you thrive. (The examples are likely drawn from Herman's book, though Koe does not say so.)

Koe's explanation of why the alter ego works is his own (same source). There are many psychological reasons, he says, but his main one is attentional. When you adopt an alter ego, your mind focuses on how you should act; your finite mental energy (about 50 bits of conscious information per second, against about 11 million for the unconscious, numbers discussed in his book *The Art of Focus*) is consumed by the shift in attention, so you lack the bandwidth for self-doubt and insecurity. If you give those 50 bits to self-doubt, "it becomes your world: **you become what you focus on**." It takes effort to expand into a new mind. So you need three things: a mental frame that focuses attention away from self-doubt and fear (the vision); a new identity to expand into with repetition and learning (the alter ego); and a plan, so that attention narrows further and you live in flow. The explanation is a plausible hypothesis built on his attention model; it is not presented with evidence beyond the examples.

The construction of an alter ego follows three steps (same source).

1. *Choose two or three **aspirational archetypes**:* people who achieved what you want. Building a startup, Koe draws qualities from Steve Jobs, or from John Hu and Vitalii, founders of Stan, "a $30 million company built relatively quickly" (he has a podcast with them and they have helped him; "cool to have a mentor"). For meaning or spirituality, he suggests Jesus Christ or Gautama Buddha. The internet lets you follow these people and absorb their advice.
2. *Study them and write down the qualities to adopt:* Steve Jobs as articulate, creative and high-agency; the Buddha as mindful, calm and sharp. "These are the qualities you're going to jump into when it's time to act."
3. *Give the alter ego a descriptive name* to make it more real: a person, an animal, anything descriptive. Koe says he thought of himself as Goku going Super Saiyan when trying to hit a gym record.

In the same video he makes a point about the most developed possible model (same source). "What would Jesus do?" can be the singular source of a good life. Koe says he is not a Christian but "gets the point": Jesus is symbolic of someone who achieved a high stage of consciousness, which some circles call "**Christ Consciousness**." He speculates that people back then did not have the words "consciousness" or "awareness"; otherwise the differences between religions would be few. He refers to his earlier video on becoming more intelligent, which covered stages of ego development (you go up and down, he says, but there is a baseline). The instruction: create an alter ego "more conscious and developed than you", so that you adopt their perspective in what used to be difficult situations. "Your alter ego is who you become to achieve your vision and execute your plan." The speculation about religions is his interpretation and is offered as such.

The decisive conceptual move comes at the end (same source). Creating an alter ego is not a silly practice; you have probably done it unconsciously, and making it conscious and tapping into it at will is powerful. "It's not a mask that you put on that just disappears once you take it off; it's a **vessel for identity change**." You consciously expand into a new level of mind; the more time you spend there, the more it becomes your natural state, since you view life from that lens; you will shift in and out of it. This is what turns the alter ego from a performance trick into a tool of this chapter: the temporary identity is a scaffold for a permanent one.

A later passage raises a question the corpus does not settle. In a 2025 video on developmental levels, Koe describes "false transformation" as putting on "the mask" of a higher level without solving the problems of the current one, which prolongs development. That looks like the opposite of the alter ego, which is explicitly "not a mask." Koe never contrasts the two. A criterion that fits both passages is the difference between adopting qualities in order to act and solve problems (the alter ego as vessel) and adopting the appearance of a higher stage in order to avoid problems or gain status (the false transformation as mask). That criterion is an inference, not a statement of the author's.

Koe's own symbol for the alter ego has a history that changed between tellings. In early 2024 he explained his new buzz cut (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18): "Focused work is only one part of changing your life." After "losing a few hairs and my already high genetic hairline receding a bit further, I had an existential crisis, even though I probably didn't actually lose anything." He worried "for weeks on end because I was overly identified with my glorious hair", researched the buzz cut hoping to look like an actor whose name the transcript renders as "Joe Delaney", and "decided to cut off the piece of myself that lived on my head to see if my worries would hold their weight, and I just wanted to know what I look like." In this telling, the haircut is an experiment against over-identification: a part of the self is removed to test whether the fear attached to it was real.

In 2025 the same act is told differently (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). At first, he says, he buzzed his head out of fear of going bald, to get used to the look he thought he was doomed to have ("rip the metaphorical Band-Aid off"); he also thinks too many men depend on their hair for self-confidence. It turned out he was not going bald: his hairline "looks like the top of an iPhone", as Ian, an engineer at his company Cortex, illustrated. After doing it a few times, shaving became "symbolic for shifting into an alter ego", the sub-identity that helps him achieve goals faster with pure focus, "like a warrior going into battle." At that moment, with Cortex picking up and new features coming, he had to work more, and shaving signaled "it's time to **lock in**." It is also a way of making himself so vulnerable that he has no option but to be okay with it.

The two tellings differ in diagnosis (an "existential crisis" over hair he "probably didn't actually lose" in 2024; a fear of balding that turned out to be unfounded in 2025) and in meaning (a test against over-identification versus a ritual of entering a chosen identity). There is no contradiction about the act itself. The shift illustrates the theme of this section: the same gesture that once served to detach from an unwanted identification later serves to attach to a chosen one.

#### The highest version of yourself

The alter ego borrows qualities from others. A related set of tools uses a figure built from your own vision: the **highest version of yourself**, also called the ideal self or the future self.

The simplest instruction is from 2023 (**Source:** `Distraction Is The Greatest Trap Of The 21st Century (How To Avoid).md`, 2023-01-22). "The highest version of every single one of you ... is open-minded." Try to expand your mind to embody that identity this week: first think about what you want in the future and who you have to become to get there; then start viewing situations from that perspective. The claim that the highest self is open-minded is significant: it means that identifying with it should loosen, not tighten, the defensive filter described in Chapter 3.

The daily version is from 2024 (**Source:** `The 7 Best Internet Business Models (From Zero Experience To Advanced).md`, 2024-01-21). After a cold open ("you weren't born to work 40 hours a week doing something you hate, numb your mind with endless scrolling and wake up one day wondering where the time went"), Koe gives the instruction: "every day zoom out and think: what would the highest version of myself do? Do that", and ignore anyone who tries to convince you something else is more important.

The decision-making version appears twice with nearly the same words, in 2023 and 2024 (**Source:** `Society Is A Pyramid Scheme (Take Back Control Of Your Life).md`, 2023-01-15; `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). It has two steps. First, "bring your purpose to the top of your mind; this will help you filter signal (importance) from noise (distraction)." "If it isn't conducive to achieving your purpose then it isn't important, unless you've been convinced that it is by someone else." Second, gain "**multi-perspectival understanding**." One method is to adopt the perspective of the highest version of yourself: what do they look like, what do they want to do with their life? You need a vision of your future, and you must "**transfer your consciousness** into that person and see what decision they would make." His example: "If I'm a bodybuilder I can pretty much understand why a bodybuilder would decide to eat chicken and rice aside from going out", because they have a meaningful goal. Then ask how you can view the situation in a way that is conducive to action: "perceive a situation in a way that allows you to act in alignment with your purpose." "Transferring consciousness" is a phrase Koe uses in several contexts (empathy, writing for a reader); here it means occupying, in imagination, the perspective of the future self.

A 2024 heuristic adds a time frame and a limit (**Source:** `My Honest Advice For Those Who Feel Lost & Confused.md`, 2024-05-30). "When you face a difficult situation or you have to make a decision, view the situation from the perspective of your ideal self." It is not "I know exactly who I want to become, so I'm going to think through their lens"; it is more "I need to zoom out", deciding how far you zoom into the future: "10 years from now, 20 years from now, what is the decision that you wish you would have made, and how did that lead to you becoming the person you want to be?" His example: you are starting a business and friends invite you out, and you know it will affect "the lever moving task" you must do (a term for the priority task that moves a project forward; see Chapter 12). "Do I actually want to go out with friends or is that just a distraction?" "This is how you filter what I should do from what I shouldn't do", and consistently making the should-do decisions makes it a habit, "and success compounds." The limit: "this also requires you to actually think for yourself. Is going out with your friends a good thing to do? Have you stayed in so many times that you can afford a night out?" The ideal self is a lens for judgment, not a substitute for it.

A 2025 method turns the lens into a habit with four paired questions (**Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`, 2025-07-27). Make it a habit to adopt the perspective of the highest version of yourself and consult them before deciding, until it is "more than second nature", automatic whenever an important decision arises. The questions: Who do I not want to become? Who do I want to become? What life do I not want to live? What life do I want to live? Let decisions be shaped by the answers. You do not need to be perfect: "most people never make a conscious decision in their life", and simply making them puts your life ahead of theirs. The paired structure (not want / want) is the anti-vision and vision structure of Chapter 7 compressed into a decision rule.

An earlier exercise gives anchor questions for growing into the perspective of the ideal self consciously (**Source:** `Most People Are NPCs (Don't Waste Your Life On Autopilot).md`, 2023-05-08). What does my ideal day look like? What do I want to do as my life's work? How do I want to impact people's lives? What do I want to look and feel like? What values and habits will I have to adopt to bring those to life? Then "practice putting on your new perspective as you go about your days." The questions work as anchors: fixed points that define the perspective so it can be "put on."

Finally, the conversation with Dickie Bush contains Koe's explanation of what the future self does to perception (**Source:** `Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md`, 2023-03-16). In every newsletter with step-by-step advice, he says, his first step is always to set a goal or frame a project. The principle: "you can start to perceive your life from your future self, which is a goal in itself." If your ideal self is fit and you are tempted by a buffet, without that lens you might make the bad decision and not even register it as a bad decision or a mistake ("and you're supposed to make mistakes"). The same holds for learning: "I'm building this thing and now I'll read a book on it" is how you register what helps the project survive. Scrolling Twitter "is no longer mindless consumption simply because I have a goal": you notice even tips outside the topic (a mindset tip while pursuing fitness) that apply to the goal, and you try them. "If you don't have the goal to view that from, that's just another tweet that's going to scroll on by ... you pay attention to life in a completely different way when you are working towards things." This is the berry bush again, in daily life: the future self, being a goal, is a filter.

#### Perspective and perception: zooming out and zooming in

Koe organizes these tools with a distinction between perspective and perception, which he gives in two forms in 2024.

The first is from a day-in-the-life video (**Source:** `A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md`, 2024-04-02). "There's perspective and there's perception." **Perspective**: "you zoom out and you think about the future that you don't want and the future that you do want and where your current actions are leading you." His gym example: where do you not want to end up? "Fat, ugly, feel like [crap]." Where do you want to end up? "Jacked, rich, tan, happy" (you can get more specific). **Perception**: "bring clarity to your day-to-day actions so it becomes easier to make better decisions." "You're of course still going to make mistakes and fall off track, but it's good to think of it on an averaging basis: over the average of a year, are you at least 51% making good decisions? If so, you're on track." In perception you hold your perspective "in the top of your head" and break it down "into what you need to learn and what you need to practice." "The way you filter out what's useful and what's not useful for your life: that's perception." You determine whether something leads to your ideal future, your perspective. The 51 percent rule is a heuristic of tolerance: change is measured on average, not by perfection.

The second form is a method for better decisions (**Source:** `Disappear And Come Back Unrecognizable (12 Rules To Change Your Life).md`, 2024-04-14). Two things: who you want to become ("perspective and zooming out into the future") and the choices that will take you there ("perception and zooming in"). "The good life is created by constant reminder of your vision and programming the identity that would actualize it through aligned action." Daily, zoom out and remind yourself of what you do not want; "you don't need to focus on what you want, because that will make itself apparent through your choices." Hold that frame at the top of your mind and do not let distractions penetrate it. When a choice comes up, zoom out and align: "will this benefit the future I am trying to create?" Then be decisive. "Allow failure into your life so you can correct your behavior the next time around." The emphasis on reminding yourself daily of what you do *not* want, rather than what you want, is distinctive: the anti-vision is the more reliable daily anchor, and the positive picture is allowed to emerge from choices.

The relation between perspective and the frame is explained with a camera metaphor from 2022 (**Source:** `How I Turned My Life Into A Video Game.md`, 2022-12-11). "Your perspective, or your worldview, is the frame in which you view reality; it's like a camera": the field of view, even with a blurred background, constrains what registers in the frame. Molding your perspective lets you perceive "what used to be a huge problem as a minor road bump", because if you are dead set on your hierarchy of goals and your attention is narrowed on it, it is hard for a distraction to register in the frame. (Chapter 3 discussed how Koe's camera metaphors for perspective and perception evolved; this 2022 version, which he calls the "frame of the game", treats perspective as the frame itself.)

A 2024 argument shows what this means for identity (**Source:** `Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md`, 2024-02-18). "Perspective is everything. It allows you to identify problems and opportunities that, when solved, result in achieving the goal of that perspective. It was nearly an automatic decision to be where I am today, because anything that threatened the idea of me working four hours a day was seen as a problem." The contrast: "Most people ... expect to work 8 hours a day, so anything outside of that is perceived as a problem: oh, I have to work 30 minutes extra today, that sucks, I'm anxious, I'm bored ... I'm overwhelmed. For me that happens at 4 hours." This is the most concrete demonstration in the corpus of the 2023 claim that identifying with the four-hour workday makes it happen. The chosen identity sets the threshold at which something registers as a problem, exactly as standards did in section 3.6, and from there the decisions become "nearly automatic."

The same 2024 video gives an image for holding both perspective and perception at once (same source). "What I mean by **singular focus** is it's like a funnel, it's like a cone, where your mind is open to your vision for your ideal future and it is focused on the present steps that you are taking to get there." Along the way, "the clarity that you have to create and maintain to prevent your mind from declining into chaos are the goals, the skills, the education, the awareness that you gain along that path." "You can't expect to know everything along that path; that's why it's open to the future: having faith and trust in your ability and taking action on a daily basis to get there." The transcript describes the image verbally, and its exact geometry (which end is wide) is not fully specified; the sense is clear: wide at the vision, narrow at today's action.

#### The perspective ladder

The broadest frame for these tools comes from Koe's book *The Art of Focus*, which he describes in several videos between 2023 and 2025.

In 2023 he presents three perspectives from the book (**Source:** `You Won’t Be The Same Person In 6 Months (Master Anything, Fast).md`, 2023-12-17). The goal is to learn to adopt "the perspective of the universe": zoom out and see situations for what they are, rather than getting caught in "petty technical details" that make your emotional waves "go crazy like a storm." He compares it to "what would Jesus do", which "has a lot of wisdom", but says it is more like "what would the universe do": how would it perceive the situation? "To bridge the gap between you and the universe is the perspective of your ideal self." The book's symbol represents the three: you, the ideal self, the universe. (The transcript describes the symbol without showing it.) In practice, adopting the perspective of your ideal self and its goals lets you perceive from a better lens and make better choices, instead of narrowing on the here-and-now and choosing comfort time after time.

Two months later he gives the practice a name and specifies the symbol (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11). To zoom out, "move up the **perspective ladder**", adopting a higher, more inclusive perspective to see situations for what they are and learn from them.

| Rung | What it involves |
|---|---|
| 1. Current perspective | Take inventory of your current situation; understand it; ask "why are you thinking about this?" |
| 2. Perspective of your ideal self | What would the highest version of yourself do? Koe zooms out to his vision (work four hours a day, a good body, thriving relationships), and only from there can he align learning, education, execution and building with that future now. If those are not in focus because you only adopt your current perspective, how will you grow? |
| 3. Perspective of the universe | "Beyond all limits": "most of our worries are irrational and meaningless from the highest perspective" (the transcript says "rational", most likely a slip for "irrational"). You see things for what they are and act in a way "truly authentic to you." |

These three, he says, are the meaning of his book's symbol: three concentric circles, "kind of a bullseye, meaning Focus, but it's how do you focus? You zoom out and you zoom in." In 2025 he describes the symbol once more (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02): a line on the outside, one in the middle, a dot inside, representing "your perspective, the perspective of your ideal self and the perspective of the universe", and the ability to move in and out of them in order to act better and navigate life and its problems. None of the transcripts specifies which ring corresponds to which perspective, so the mapping cannot be reconstructed with certainty. One could guess that the dot is the current perspective and the outer ring the universe, as the order of zooming out suggests, but the source does not say so.

In the 2024 video the ladder is preceded by a sentence that summarizes the whole practice (**Source:** `The Fastest Way To Acquire High-Value Skills (And Finally Change Your Life).md`, 2024-02-11): "The solution to most of your problems: zoom out, adopt the perspective of your ideal self, break out of the narrow focus that traps you in a chaotic loop of negative thoughts, understand where you are, choose where you want to be, and bridge the gap with education and execution." The sentence links this section back to the start of the chapter. You are not where you want to be because you are not the person who would be there (4.1); the ideal self is that person, used as a lens; and the gap between the two is crossed by education and execution, which is to say, by immersion and building (4.4, 4.6).

The relation among the tools of this section can now be stated. The future self (or ideal or highest self) is a goal used as a perspective. The alter ego is a sub-identity built from borrowed qualities and entered at will, a vessel through which the future self is practiced until it becomes the natural state. Identity lists provide evidence that the change is real. Weaponizing survival is what all of them do at the level of mechanism: they move the threshold of threat, so that the mind defends the new identity instead of the old one. And the perspective ladder places them inside a larger movement of zooming out, from the current self to the ideal self to the universe, which is also, in Koe's framework, the direction of raising the level of mind (4.7).

### 4.6 Reinvention Protocols and Failures That Stack

Koe has offered several step-by-step sequences for recreating yourself, each from a different period. They are best read together, because the differences between them show how his thinking developed: from a linear "origin story" in 2022 to overlapping patterns that reinforce each other in 2026. The second half of this section takes up a related idea that explains why reinvention does not mean losing what came before: starting over is not starting from zero, because the skills and lessons of each attempt stack. Koe's own business failures are his main evidence.

#### 2022: the origin story

The earliest sequence is a summary at the end of a 2022 video (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25). Realizing what you do not want, creating a strategy, launching yourself in the opposite direction, and prioritizing long-term success and mastery: this, Koe says, is "the **origin story**" you will hear in every successful person's story. With the new year approaching, he tells the viewer to "flip the switch inside of you" and build a foundation that will carry them into the life they want. The four elements correspond to four parts of this book: the anti-vision (Chapter 7), the plan (Chapter 8), the leap into the unknown (4.3) and mastery (Chapter 15). "Every origin story" is a generalization from the stories he knows, not a survey; its value is as a template.

#### 2023: four steps to recreate yourself

In 2023, in the video on self-architecture, Koe gives a four-step process (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19).

1. *Pursue a goal that is challenging enough.* Start small and superficial (health, finances, mindset) and put one foot into the unknown. The advice to begin with superficial goals anticipates Chapter 9, which explains why surface-level goals are the right starting point.
2. *Acquire the skill necessary to achieve the goal.* This means self-education: immersing the mind in information that programs a new identity (the method of section 4.4).
3. *Hunt and stack whys, or reasons, to cultivate a personal philosophy.* The reasons accumulate around the action until it becomes self-motivating (section 4.4).
4. *Turn your valuable pursuits into contributions.* In context, this ties personal development to the creator economy: what you learn in pursuing your goals becomes content, products or services for others (Parts VIII–XI).

The sequence moves from self to others: a goal, the skill it requires, the reasons that sustain it, and finally the transformation of the pursuit into value for others. That last step is distinctive of Koe's framework, in which personal reinvention and the one-person business are two sides of the same process.

#### 2025: Vision, Clarity, Identity, then war mode

The 2025 video on the alter ego organizes change in three steps followed by an execution phase (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). Step one is **Vision**, which he describes as an "attention anchor." Step two is **Clarity**: a plan. Step three is **Identity**: the alter ego, the answer to "who must you become?" (section 4.5). "We have Vision, Clarity, Identity: that's how you change your life." Then comes the execution phase, which he calls **war mode**:

1. Commit with a physical, irreversible act.
2. Learn by embracing chaos: 30–60 minutes of learning and 30–60 minutes of building.
3. Build mind, body and business.
4. Expose yourself to massive experience for a period of two to three months, then recover and repeat.

The structure places identity after vision and plan, not before. This is consistent with the attentional explanation of the alter ego: the vision gives the frame that pulls attention away from self-doubt, the plan narrows attention further, and the identity is the vessel through which the plan is executed. War mode is the intense register of section 4.3: a deliberate, time-limited period of maximal exposure, explicitly followed by recovery. Elsewhere in the corpus, war mode is the complementary opposite of "monk mode" (Chapter 13).

#### 2026: four overlapping patterns

The 2026 reinvention video replaces steps with patterns (**Source:** `How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md`, 2026-05-02). Koe begins with a warning that earlier versions lacked: "This won't happen overnight. You're going to go through withdrawals. You will have relapses." Then he presents four things he has found "the most potent for recreating yourself." He is careful about their status: the list is not exhaustive and may not help you specifically, "but these are what helped me." And they are not sequential: "all of these four things overlap and synergize with each other. So, one leads to another and they stack."

| # | Pattern | Where this chapter treats it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A reason with extremely high gravitational pull; when it fades, find another | Section 4.2 |
| 2 | Become brutally aware of who you don't want to become | Section 4.2; Chapter 7 |
| 3 | Change your environment faster than the identity can recalibrate | Section 4.4 |
| 4 | Increase the gap between impulse and response | Summarized below; Chapter 11 (section 11.5) |

The fourth pattern works at a different scale from the other three. A reason and a new environment are prepared outside the moment of temptation; they create better conditions, Koe says, "but they don't help you in the 3 seconds when your hand is reaching for your phone", and it is not only the phone but everything. For those seconds his answer is paradoxical: "The way that you detach from old patterns is to do nothing. You increase the gap because doing nothing is the only way that you can interrupt the survival mechanism in real time." The task is to "starve the old self that is trying to survive of the normal feedback it receives": to notice, through the day, whether you are repeating yesterday on autopilot, or whether you are hardening a position by arguing in your head with an opinion you just read. "When you sit with this uncomfortable feeling without making a decision, you train your nervous system to tolerate the gap between impulse and action. From that neutral state, it's much easier to make a conscious choice in the right direction" (same source). The pattern is the moment-to-moment version of the mechanism this chapter describes in section 4.4: the old identity dies because it is starved of the information that reinforces it, and here the starving happens one impulse at a time. Section 11.5 treats the practice in full, as part of interrupting autopilot.

#### How the protocols evolved

| Year | Protocol | Structure | What it emphasizes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022-07 | Two routes: rock bottom or stacking small wins (4.2) | Alternative paths | Gradual change as more sustainable |
| 2022-12 | Origin story | Linear, four elements | Anti-vision → strategy → leap → mastery |
| 2023-05 | Four steps to recreate yourself | Linear, four steps | Goal, skill, reasons, contribution |
| 2023-12 / 2024-05 | Launch into a new environment (4.4) | Single leap | Starving the old identity of information |
| 2025-03 | Vision, Clarity, Identity + war mode | Three steps + execution phase | Attention, plan, alter ego; intensity for 2–3 months |
| 2025-10 | Dissonance, uncertainty, discovery; the evolution loop (4.3) | Phases and cycle | What change feels like; repetition at each level |
| 2026-05 | Four overlapping patterns | Mutually reinforcing | High-gravity reason, environment, the gap between impulse and response; relapses expected |

The evolution moves from linear steps to overlapping patterns that reinforce each other, and the environment and the high-gravity reason gain weight. The reason Koe gives in 2026 is pragmatic ("these are what helped me"), with an explicit acknowledgment that the list is not complete. The versions are compatible: each emphasizes a different aspect of the same process. In 2026 he adds, as noted in 4.5, that even a successful reinvention by weaponizing survival leaves you inside "the cycle of suffering."

#### Starting over is not starting over

A reinvention implies abandoning something: a goal, a career, a project, an identity. Does that mean the time spent on it was wasted? Koe and one of his guests answer no, in several ways.

The clearest formulation comes from a conversation with Devon Eriksen, a guest, and should be attributed to him (**Source:** `The Most Important Skill To Learn In The Next 10 Years With Devon Eriksen.md`, 2024-12-08). Koe asks how one finds fulfillment if what one loves (for example, being an artist) may not be relevant, or may not let you survive, in the future. Devon's "base level answer" is a question: "whoever said you had to do one thing with your whole life?" He has done many different things, each very fulfilling at the time. "Our wants change and that's okay." We leave behind goals that were achieved or abandoned "because they weren't quite right for us, but we take with us all the learning and all the learning how to learn." So "we become deeper, richer, more developed characters as we go through this process." Even if you learn to draw and then AI draws better, you still learned how to learn to draw, how to pursue an ambition, and "a lot about envisioning a scene and creating it that you can now do with AI tools." The idea fits Koe's complexity of self (4.1): each abandoned pursuit adds to the character.

Koe's own version dates from 2022 and is stated as a lesson from his failed businesses (**Source:** `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md`, 2022-06-18). "Don't be afraid to start over." Starting over on the unconventional path is not really starting over: you consistently pick up valuable information. When the dots connect, "it creates this way of doing things that is very unique to you that other people can't replicate", and "that is what you're looking for." The last clause links reinvention to differentiation, a theme of Chapters 19 and 20: the path of repeated restarts produces a combination nobody else has.

The most theoretical version is from 2026 (**Source:** `If you have multiple interests, do not waste the next 2-3 years.md`, 2026-01-20). "Every interest you've ever pursued leaves behind a **residue**. Every interest increases the number of connections that can be made. Every interest expands and increases the complexity of how you model and interpret reality. And the more complex your model of reality, the more problems you can solve, opportunities you can see, and value you can create. Specialism completely halts this process, and your shiny object syndrome has been trying to tell you this whole time." From birth, billions of bits of information processed in your unique location, situation and culture have cultivated "a way of seeing things that others cannot access. AI cannot think this way until you tell AI to think that way." The concept of residue gives a mechanism for the previous two formulations: what remains of an abandoned pursuit is not only skill but a more complex model of reality.

The same 2022 video that said "don't be afraid to start over" also says "try everything" (**Source:** `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md`, 2022-06-18). People warn against shiny object syndrome, but Koe says he has never seen it be the problem. You must try everything to see what sticks. You cannot find what you are interested in until you try something, discover you are not interested, and follow it down another path that was revealed because you tried (his example is photography revealing an interest in editing). "You have to try what you are aware of now in order to expand your awareness into these other areas that you had no idea existed before." In the same video he also says you must focus on something long enough to judge whether you are interested, which qualifies "try everything": trying is not sampling for a day.

A 2025 step gives the method a name (**Source:** `How To Become So Self-Disciplined It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-08-24). **Self-experimentation**: "Problems that aren't solved through experimentation aren't solved for good. Rather than being assigned another goal, you try everything until you find the one thing that you can't pull yourself away from. Then you repeat that process until it becomes who you are." The final clause closes the loop with the chapter's thesis. Experimentation finds the goal; repetition turns it into identity. The phrase "rather than being assigned another goal" connects back to Chapter 1: experimentation is the alternative to receiving goals from outside.

#### Failures that stack: Koe's own case

Koe tells the story of his failed businesses many times, and the telling is not always the same. The core is stable: before his current business, he failed at about seven business models, and the skills from those failures became the basis of his success.

A general argument comes first (**Source:** `Turn Your Knowledge Into A Business (How To Productize Your Mind).md`, 2023-11-26). People who try and fail at many businesses (dropshipping, Facebook ads, a social media marketing agency) eventually do very well "because they stack so much lessons and skills from their failures"; the next endeavor "just makes sense because they have all of the pieces." He adds a warning: if you have never learned any modern skills, it will take a while, and you will get overwhelmed and anxious "at the thought of a future you don't have the skill to create clarity with." Stacking failures is a long route, and its early stages are uncomfortable.

The personal versions vary in their emphasis.

In 2023 he names the skills as his **point of leverage** (**Source:** `Value Creation The Single Skill That Built My $1M Solo Business.md`, 2023-04-09). The skills learned through failed business models (Photoshop and others) "created who I am today": he uses Photoshop for his own thumbnails, Instagram designs and website ("I create everything") because he learned those skills trying to build businesses, until he "stumbled upon being a creator" and started "from that point of leverage."

In 2024 he describes them as an **irreplaceable set of skills** (**Source:** `The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md`, 2024-05-19). Being a generalist came naturally to him. He failed at drop shipping, e-commerce, SEO, digital art and more, but in pursuit of business goals he stacked an irreplaceable set of skills. "One or two skills didn't bring me much in terms of results, but five, six, seven skills allowed me to spot and act on profitable opportunities", because he had more tools for more problems. Each failure left a specific skill: drop shipping taught him branding (to stand out from bigger brands with low shipping times); e-commerce taught him advertising and product design (to be more attention-grabbing); digital art taught him graphic design and the nuances of building an audience on social media. "All of those failures are the reason that I can do what I do today."

In 2023 he described the same period as "a brutal path of skill acquisition and business failures" (**Source:** `How To Reprogram Your Mind & Build Your Intelligence.md`, 2023-09-24). Deciding to earn a living with his mind led him to learn graphic design and web design, try drop shipping with e-commerce, take on any opportunity, learn web development and get a web development job. Even though these failed, he learned and stacked the skills. For a brief time he felt they had no applicability and would not earn him income, because if he had to get a job, "my degree goes out the window most of the time, all the skills that I learned go out of the window most of the time." What they led to was "the ability to solve creative problems." Today he earns a living with the skills he learned and his interest in writing, and sustains a high quality of life "by pulling two levers: the inputs and outputs of my mind." This telling includes the doubt: the period when the stack looked useless.

In 2026 he uses the story to explain why growing on social media was easy for him (**Source:** `How To Grow An Audience If You Have 0 Followers (It's Only 2 Habits).md`, 2026-05-14). He opens with "growing on social media is easy", admitting that it sounds arrogant and is a good hook but saying he means it: he "never really had a problem growing on social media on any platform." When outlining the video he asked himself why, and the answer was that he had failed "at like seven other different business models before." "I wasn't starting from scratch. I had a bunch of high-value skills under my belt, even though those prior businesses, thanks to shiny object syndrome, failed." His list in this telling: digital art with Photoshop; photography with in-person photo sessions; multiple agencies (Facebook ads, SEO, web design); drop shipping twice; an e-commerce brand ("pretty much drop shipping"); and a few week-long attempts he does not even count. The pattern was always the same: start something, build the website, get excited about forming the LLC, "then the dopamine would fade and then I'd quit." Later in the same video he qualifies the opening: if growing were as easy as posting, it would work for everyone; there is a very high skill cap. It is easy *for him* because of the accumulated skill.

In 2024 he asks viewers not to use the label (**Source:** `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control, Here's The Solution To Feeling Lost.md`, 2024-03-10). "Avoid labeling this as shiny object syndrome; this is potentially the best thing you could do." He had "like a 2 to 3 [year] **experimentation phase**, failing at seven different business models, but that was the only thing that allowed me to gain clarity on what I'm doing now and actually pursue it full force." The condition is important: "you're also not just trying things and then leaving them; you're trying, you're learning, you're getting skills, you're actually doing things, you're buying courses." Experimentation counts only if something is learned and done.

In 2023 he goes further and credits the syndrome itself (**Source:** `How To Escape Wage Slavery (Entrepreneurship Is Modern Survival).md`, 2023-08-01). "I attribute my unique success to shiny object syndrome. I failed at seven different businesses and learned even more skills along the way just because they sounded interesting to me, and what most don't see is that I wouldn't be here without those failures. I had to become a **modern renaissance man** in order to make this business model work. The skills I learned with each failure compounded into better opportunities: my tweets wouldn't be the same without copywriting, my web pages wouldn't be the same without web design, my profile picture and branding wouldn't be the same without Photoshop art; the list goes on."

The same 2023 video makes the strongest practical claim of the series, under the heading of "the balance of skill and opportunity" (same source). "I'm a slave to many things in my life, but I would like to believe that I've escaped wage slavery, because if my business got wiped from the face of the earth I could still create a product or service that someone else needs, call, email, advertise and spread internet content to drive traffic to that offer, make 5 to 10K a month and improve that with the data I've gathered." Why can he? "Over the past 10 years I've learned graphic design, web development, digital art, Photoshop, Illustrator, copywriting, psychology, marketing, sales, video editing, audience building, advertising, product design, course creation, idea generation, lifestyle design, bodybuilding, nutrition, social media and more." "With zero dollars to my name I could create a product or service and sell it to someone else that I know needs it." The figure of $5–10K a month is his own estimate of what he could rebuild, not a demonstrated result; the argument is that the stack, not the business, is the real asset.

Two passages describe the inner experience behind the stack. The first, from 2023, describes a decade of obsessions (**Source:** `The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack (Learn In This Order).md`, 2023-01-08). Ever since he can remember, Koe has been obsessed with learning: even when gaming three to six hours a day, he carved out time to learn something new, which "took away from the quality of my schoolwork or job", though he "always knew that things would work out." At first he thought this thirst for learning was "the dreaded shiny object syndrome" that all successful people warn against, but "if my bursts of obsession were true shiny object syndrome I don't think I would be where I am now." Over ten years he obsessed over fitness, nutrition, SEO, web design, web development, graphic design, digital art, social media, content writing, marketing and sales, copywriting and funnels, neuroscience, philosophy, spirituality, self-development, "and the list goes on." Each obsession lasted about one to three months. He did not laser in on a single thing, which he does not think is really possible: if he is obsessed with spirituality and a nutrition podcast he is curious about appears in his notifications, he will listen; "I'm not just gonna ignore my curiosity because I'm so dedicated to something else." Later in the same video he distinguishes good from bad shiny object syndrome (the bad kind takes you off the path of mastery; learning something complementary while you build does not).

The second, from 2026, describes the same pattern as **obsessive cycles** (**Source:** `If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md`, 2026-06-13). "I'm a very obsessive person, because when I find something that I am interested in, truly interested in, nothing else matters. My focus becomes seamless. I could work 24 hours a day if I didn't value sleep." He has gone through these cycles many times: fitness, then spirituality, digital art, photography, web development, psychology and certain branches of philosophy, and "various high-value skills that I was told to learn, like marketing, copywriting, sales", the things required to start a business. Many people share this quality, he says, but few around them display it.

A last passage places the beginning of the stack in college (**Source:** `I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md`, 2025-07-27). As a "dumb college kid" who partied, Koe was still always working on something that "would somehow buy my freedom", without knowing what: opportunities to start a business, getting healthy, learning to master his mind. He was always into self-improvement ("not what self-improvement has become"), because even young he knew he did not want to end up overweight, obese, low-energy or financially unstable. Even while partying, he felt the desire to set aside "just an hour a day" to research, try and build things. Most failed, but "when you do something in reality, that's when you learn"; even if a business does not take off, you stack skills that lead to something working when the right pieces come together. The hour a day is the small-scale version of "do what you want to do in your future now" (4.1).

#### The shifting meaning of "shiny object syndrome"

The label "shiny object syndrome" (the habit of jumping from one interest or project to the next) is used by Koe in incompatible ways, and the reader should see the range.

| Date | Use of the label | Source file |
|---|---|---|
| 2022-06 | "People warn against it, but I've never seen it be the case"; try everything | `7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses…` |
| 2023-01 | Feared at first; then distinguished into good and bad kinds; "bursts of obsession" | `The $1 Million Dollar Skill Stack…` |
| 2023-08 | "I attribute my unique success to shiny object syndrome" | `How To Escape Wage Slavery…` |
| 2024-03 | "Avoid labeling this as shiny object syndrome"; experimentation phase | `If Your Life Is Spiraling Out Of Control…` |
| 2024 (Chapters 14 and 19) | The seven failures as a trap (being "the architect of the business" without customers); shiny object syndrome as a sign that business "hasn't clicked" | — |
| 2026-01 | "Your shiny object syndrome has been trying to tell you this whole time" that specialism halts development | `If you have multiple interests…` |
| 2026-05 | The businesses failed "thanks to shiny object syndrome", though they left skills | `How To Grow An Audience…` |
| 2026-08 | They were not businesses at all but "hobbies": he learned the skill, started the LLC, built the website, but "when it came to actually getting a customer… I was nowhere to be found" | `The Art of Strategic Thinking…` |

The label works, at different times, as a defense of curiosity, as a negative diagnosis, and as a social weapon that he rejects (in 2025 he criticizes parents, teachers and friends who use it to discredit people with multiple interests). The last use adds one more rereading of the seven failures. In August 2026 Koe says he "got stuck in tutorial hell": he took all the courses and tried seven business models (Facebook ads, SEO, drop shipping, web design, digital art and a few more), but he would not call them businesses. Each step "felt good", the skill, the LLC, the website, the copy, yet he never got anyone to pay him. The missing piece, he says, was not one more tactic but "the ability to think strategically", and he admits he still collects tactics "quite often right now" (**Source:** `The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md`, 2026-08-19). Section 14.3 returns to this passage. Here the failures are explained neither by curiosity nor by its abuse, but by the absence of a customer and of a strategy. He does not explain the shifts. The positions can be reconciled with a distinction the corpus supports: the same seven ventures *failed as businesses* (in part because he jumped before acquiring customers) and *succeeded as skill acquisition*. Jumping between niches in search of the most profitable one is the bad kind; exploring interests while learning and building is the good kind.

The details of the story also vary. The number seven is stable across tellings, but the list and the duration are not: a 2022 telling includes a fitness YouTube channel, photography and art, a Facebook ads agency, drop shipping, blue-light glasses, wallets and content and SEO agencies, over four to five years; in 2024 the experimentation phase lasts two to three years; other tellings add a print shop, a drop-servicing agency and an animated-video agency; the 2026 list is the one quoted above. These are inconsistencies of detail in a story told many times from memory, not changes of position. What matters for this chapter is constant: none of the failures was a reset, because each added a layer to the stack.

The lesson for the theory of identity change is that reinvention is cumulative. The old identity dies (4.1), but its skills, lessons and "residue" pass into the new one. That is why Koe can say both that you must let your old self die off and that starting over is not starting over: what dies is the identification; what survives is the capacity.

### 4.7 The Level of Mind

Several sections of this chapter have pointed toward the same concept: the level of mind dictates which values are available (4.1); you must return "from a higher level of mind" to solve a problem (4.2); pushing outward while chiseling inward raises the level of mind (4.3); the alter ego is a conscious expansion into a new level of mind (4.5). This section defines the term, shows how Koe arrived at it, explains how the level of mind is raised, and reconstructs the four-step process he gave for raising it, including his central concept of the perception threshold. It closes the chapter because it generalizes everything before it: identity change, seen from far enough away, is movement between levels of mind.

#### Definition: the height and openness of your perspective

The most frequently repeated definition comes from 2023 (**Source:** `Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md`, 2023-04-20; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). "Your **level of mind** dictates your quality of life. Your level of mind is pretty much your perspective: how high and open of a perspective you have from which you interpret the problems in your life." With a low level of mind, focused on a problem "within that level of mind, like paying the bills", you are "trapped in this mental thought loop" and "don't learn the skills and gain the experience necessary to increase above that, to view the problem from a lens that does not bother you." The key claim follows: "**your problems don't go away, you just get better at perceiving them.**"

The claim is easy to misread as resignation, and it is the opposite. It does not say that problems are unsolvable but that the same objective situation (bills to pay) is experienced differently depending on the height from which it is seen. At a low level, the problem fills the frame and produces a loop of anxious thought; at a higher level, it is one problem among others, visible in context and solvable. The **mental thought loop** is the signature of being trapped at the level where the problem was created.

A slightly earlier passage gives the most careful definition, and also shows where the term came from (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05). It begins with a quote Koe gives without attribution: "No problem can be solved from the same level of consciousness that created it." Problems, he says, are inherent, "baked into the system", and they are the spark that creates the gap between expectation and reality, especially if you lack the skill to match the challenge. Only by closing that gap and seeing problems for what they are can you solve them better, and that requires zooming out to a higher perspective, which is to say, increasing your level of consciousness. And here he announces the reformulation: he will call it "level of mind." "Here we will define mind as how you interact with reality." The perspective or level of mind "houses" all your beliefs, ideas, concepts, skills, experience and potentialities for the future (what you think is possible). The broader the perspective, the higher the level of mind and the better the lens for problems, and the harder it becomes to latch onto any specific expectation, concept or ideology, "because you've experimented without identification and broken out of the bubble multiple times."

Two features of this definition matter for the chapter. First, "mind" is given a specific, functional meaning: not the brain or the stream of thought, but *how you interact with reality*. Koe is using a common word in a sense of his own. Second, the level of mind contains the same elements that Chapter 3 said compose identity (beliefs, ideas, skills, experience) plus the horizon of what seems possible. The level of mind is therefore not separate from identity; it is identity seen from the standpoint of its range. And the last clause describes what a high level of mind does to conceptual survival: it makes identification harder, because you have "experimented without identification" and broken out of the bubble several times. A higher level of mind is a less defended identity.

The terminology changes over the corpus.

| Period | Term | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 2023-03 | "Level of consciousness" → "level of mind" | Reformulated explicitly in the passage above |
| 2023–2024 | "Level of mind" | The standard term in the videos this chapter draws on |
| 2023-11 | "Levels of consciousness, or levels of mind" | Used as equivalents when showing a "consciousness scale" |
| 2024-11 onward | "Level of development", "level of psychological development" | Adopts the vocabulary of developmental psychology (Part XIV) |

Koe does not explain the changes. They are consistent with his gradual adoption of the vocabulary of ego development and stages; the terms designate the same thing.

#### Unlike a car, the mind must be understood to be driven

Why study the mind at all? Koe answers with a metaphor (**Source:** `Your 20s Are Meant To Build (How To Not Waste Them).md`, 2023-04-20). "You don't need to understand every single part of a nice car in order to have a nice ride, but with the mind it doesn't work like that. You have to understand your mind in order to use it and in order to drive it, and that alone will dictate the quality of your human experience in and of itself." The contrast justifies a program of study (same source): "Schedule time to study philosophy, study spirituality, study metaphysics, study psychology, study epistemology, study stages of ego development, study Spiral Dynamics, study the mind. This is a lifelong pursuit of mastery. You will become powerful."

In 2023 the stage models appear only as items on this list, without authors and without explanation of how he uses them. That changes later: in 2024 he presents Susanne Cook-Greuter's nine stages of ego development in detail; in 2025 he condenses them into four macro-stages and then builds his own model, Human 3.0; and in late 2025 he names Maslow, Cook-Greuter, and Beck and Cowan (Spiral Dynamics) as "orienting generalizations." That development, and the question of whether higher stages are "better" or merely more inclusive, belongs to Chapter 38.

**Complementary context:** Spiral Dynamics, developed by Don Beck and Christopher Cowan from the work of Clare Graves, describes a sequence of value systems ("memes", coded by colors) through which individuals and cultures develop. Stages of ego development refer mainly to the work of Jane Loevinger and Susanne Cook-Greuter, which describes how the way a person makes sense of self and world becomes more complex over a lifetime.

#### Your problems reveal your level

How is the level of mind raised? The 2023 video gives the principle (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05). With every problem you solve, you perceive life through a more effortless or enjoyable lens, because you understand that problems can be solved and that there is a way. And "the degree of problems that you are facing is a prime indicator of what level of mind you have" and what phase of life you are in. He gives a sequence of surface-level problems: fixing your health, fixing your bank account, then relationships (after those two), then emotional intelligence and happiness. Once you solve them ("**master your survival**"), you reach a level at which you can be more creative and navigate life "with artfulness and grace."

He extends the principle to the collective (same source). Life, and the universe throughout evolution, is about solving problems; the stages of evolution are the collective mind evolving by solving problems. Once survival is mastered, you can join collective minds and communities, help others, and combine minds toward a shared goal to solve bigger world problems, which raises the **collective level of mind**. Each problem solved forces you to close the gap between expectation and reality and presents a lesson; "this is how you gain experience." The cosmological claim is Koe's interpretive frame, of a piece with the acorn and oak of section 4.1. The practical claim is that problems are the rungs of the ladder: you climb by solving the problems of your current level, and the kind of problem you have tells you where you are.

This links directly to the 2023 provocation quoted in section 4.1: "You don't care about global problems because you haven't solved the personal problems that restrict your mind from seeing them as important." The sequence of problems (health, money, relationships, emotional intelligence, then collective problems) is the reason. A mind still occupied with survival-level problems cannot register larger ones as important.

A 2023 concept adds that the level of mind determines values automatically (**Source:** `People Dumber Than You Are Making Millions.md`, 2023-11-12). Koe shows on screen "a picture of the consciousness scale, or the levels of consciousness, or levels of mind" (the image is not recoverable from the transcript, so it cannot be identified). "Your level of mind determines your values." "You didn't choose to sit around and think about money, social media and skill acquisition; you make an automatic decision based on the structure of your psyche." "For many, they were assigned ideology, beliefs, systems and business models that sounded good, and began defending their beliefs as the best and only." "When you zoom out, push into the unknown and make progress by solving problems that increase your level of mind, you can see money for what it truly is: a piece of paper that can benefit yourself and others massively." The example of money is chosen deliberately for the video's audience; the general point is that values are not freely chosen at a given level. They are given by the structure of the psyche, which can be changed only by raising the level.

#### A season of internal struggle

Raising the level of mind is not free. A 2023 concept describes what it gives and what it costs (**Source:** `Most People Won’t Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md`, 2023-05-19). As you increase your level of mind, "you can view the game from the top down and see the structure of the game itself." In business, when starting out you have no clue what you are doing until you solve problems and have "all of the pieces that form the structure of business itself"; then you can navigate within it. Give yourself one to two years to accumulate enough business experience to run it well and make enough money, and then it becomes "automatic and seamless." And the cost: "**a season of internal struggle is the requirement to reach your next level of mind**." When you acquire the necessary skill or knowledge, you reach the next level of mind, the one that solves the problem you faced. This is the same video that stated the paradox of personal development (4.3): pushing into the world requires digging into yourself, and that "doesn't come in an instant."

A 2023 argument gives the full chain from consciousness to identity (**Source:** `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md`, 2023-09-05). Starting from the line "no problem can be solved from the same consciousness that created it" (which he attributes to Einstein in that passage): to raise your consciousness you must raise your level of mind; to raise your level of mind you must open your perspective; to open your perspective you must identify problems, take on the challenge, acquire the skill and receive the knowledge necessary to transcend your previous identity. "**With each level of mind your identity must change**." "The knowledge, skill, belief and experience you order in your mind as self determines the opportunities available to you." At level one you can access opportunities slightly above your level; the opportunities you can register exist on a spectrum; as you level up, you keep access to everything beneath you and to those slightly above your skill. "It is a massive disservice to your life to distract yourself and never see what you are capable of."

This chain is the hinge of the whole chapter. It says explicitly what the earlier sections implied: identity change and raising the level of mind are the same process described at two scales. Each identity flip (4.3) is a step up; each step up requires that the identity change. The idea that higher levels keep access to the lower ones ("everything beneath you") is a simple version of the formula Koe takes from Ken Wilber, "transcend and include" (introduced in Chapter 3, section 3.4, and developed in Chapter 38).

#### The Einstein quote and its attribution

The quotation that anchors the concept appears throughout the corpus, and the way Koe attributes it changes over time.

| Date | Attribution | Use | Source file |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023-03 | None ("No problem can be solved from the same level of consciousness that created it") | Introduces the reformulation as "level of mind" | `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult…` |
| 2023-09 | Used in the chain above, attributed to Einstein | Raising consciousness requires raising the level of mind | `Life Is A Video Game (You Can't Escape The Matrix).md` |
| 2023-10 | Einstein, without reservation ("you cannot solve a problem from the same mind that created it") | Increasing mental capacity comes first | `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md` |
| 2024-11 | "Einstein supposedly said" | Goals evolve with your level of development | `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md` |
| 2025-03 | "Supposedly said" | Most problems are a lack of perspective | `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md` |

**Complementary context:** There is no reliable source showing that Einstein said or wrote this sentence in any of its popular forms. It resembles, loosely, a 1946 statement in which he wrote that "a new type of thinking is essential if mankind is to survive." Koe's own shift to "supposedly" is consistent with that uncertainty.

The three later uses each draw a different consequence.

In 2023 the consequence is about order (**Source:** `If Your Life Sucks, Here's How To Reprogram Your Mind.md`, 2023-10-22). "You cannot solve a problem from the same mind that created it", so increasing mental capacity comes first, and it comes from pushing into the unknown, cultivating new skills, understanding new knowledge, and leaving "the known, the comfortable routine and cycle that you have dug yourself into." This is the same video that said your life is the result of thousands of tiny choices (4.2): first raise the capacity, then the problem looks different.

In 2024 the consequence is about goals (**Source:** `How To Become More Intelligent Than 99% Of People.md`, 2024-11-03). Introducing the stages of development, Koe adds a layer to his theory of goals: "you have the goals of your level of psychological development; those are what are important to you." Goals evolve: once achieved, or while being pursued, a goal "may evolve in perspective." By increasing your level of development over the years "you slowly refine what your version of success is and are more able to achieve it, because you have the skill and knowledge and development to match." "When you have a higher goal you can think more intelligently toward that goal, but you can't expand to a higher level of mind in an instant ... it takes an entire lifetime to reach the highest stages." This is an important corrective to the speed of the titles discussed at the start of the chapter: individual identity flips can be fast; the ascent through levels takes a lifetime.

In 2025 the consequence is about the nature of problems (**Source:** `How To Change Your Life So Fast It Feels Illegal.md`, 2025-03-02). We need to tap into a higher level of mind to overcome the struggles of pursuing goals, "because most of your problems aren't problems; they're a lack of perspective, and thus awareness and consciousness." This is the same claim as "your problems don't go away, you just get better at perceiving them", stated more sharply: many problems are artifacts of a low perspective.

#### The process for raising the level of mind

The 2023 video on mental mastery gives a four-step process for raising the level of mind, gaining clarity and overcoming overwhelm (**Source:** `Life Is Supposed To Be Difficult (The Way To Mental Mastery).md`, 2023-03-05). It is the most complete practical procedure in this chapter, and each step has its own reasoning.

*Step 1: pursue a challenging goal.* The goal must be challenging, but not impossible for your current state of mind. If the goal is too high or too far, create more steps, or lower the goal and perceive the accompanying problem from a better lens. You need a goal you can act on every day, and it must not be one assigned to you (like a job): it must be your own goal, one that solves your life's problems, with at least a 30- to 60-minute time block every day. At the beginner phase, goals will probably revolve around health, wealth and relationships: start in the gym or improve your finances. He recommends tackling relationships afterward, so that you approach them "from a better light." And: "**peace of mind is a byproduct of pursuing these goals, not necessarily achieving them**." The calibration of difficulty ("challenging, not impossible") anticipates the challenge–skill balance of flow (Chapter 10). The order (health and money before relationships) repeats the sequence of problems given above.

The same video makes the calibration vivid with a metaphor (same source): there must be balance or progression. Expecting to climb Mount Everest when you would run out of breath in the first mile is just another expectation; "you can't climb Mount Everest if you can't even walk 10K steps every single day." Progression, not leaps.

*Step 2: stay conscious of present-moment difficulty.* While pursuing the goal, ask what judgments or connotations you attach to words like struggle, difficulty, discomfort and hard. Do they lead to compulsive thoughts about the future, to immediate expectations, or to memories of past mistakes that cause fear to the point of inaction, or to a mixture of these, "leaving you in a **Vortex of anxiety**"? These projections are not reality. On a treadmill, when it gets hard, stay focused on the actual sensory perceptions rather than on expectations of how it should be; or become conscious of the expectations themselves ("this should be easy", "I should be doing something better", "I could be walking right now"). Those are not the reality, and "the reality of these steps is what actualizes your goals." This step separates **present-moment difficulty**, which is real and bearable, from the projected difficulty that the mind adds to it.

*Step 3: cross the perception threshold.* Koe calls this "the third and most powerful step." The **perception threshold** is "a time when difficult becomes enjoyable after you push through the discomfort." He showed it as a graph on screen, which the transcript describes. At the start there is not much *perceived* difficulty (he emphasizes "perceived", not actual). As you continue, perceived difficulty rises, as the mind narrows in on little things in your head. Once you accept reality for what it is in that moment, perceived difficulty ("the thing in your mind that is going to make you stop") decreases, and you find more enjoyment in the actual difficulty. His cases: writer's block; thirty minutes into a 5K; the last rep of a 365-pound squat; hesitating to post your first piece of content; forty-five minutes into meditation with your mind begging to check notifications. In each, thoughts and emotions flood in, and "the only way out is through." The runner's high, or hitting your stride, is the perception threshold. It may take five minutes, a day, a year or a lifetime, depending on the problem. And a formula: "**Chaos is a prerequisite to Clarity, and Clarity is the most potent fuel in the world**."

The graph can be reconstructed in words, with the caveat that the exact shape shown on screen is not recoverable:

| Phase | Perceived difficulty | Actual difficulty | Experience |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | Low | Present | Little resistance |
| Narrowing | Rising, as the mind fixes on "little things in your head" | Roughly constant | Urge to stop; thoughts and emotions flood in |
| Acceptance of reality | Falls ("the thing in your mind that is going to make you stop" decreases) | Unchanged | Enjoyment of the actual difficulty |
| Beyond the threshold | Low | Same or higher | Flow, "hitting your stride" |

*Step 4: reach a new level of mind.* Crossing the threshold forces you to zoom out beyond "your little **web of beliefs** and thoughts" and adopt a higher perspective, from which you see the problem or difficulty for what it is. The excuses are reframed. Not having ideas for writing becomes "a silly excuse." Not performing the last rep, the one that yields the most gains, becomes "I'm being a little [wimp], push through." Not posting your first piece of content becomes "if I don't do this it becomes habit and I'll be stuck in my current situation for life." "Deep satisfaction is the result from this process."

The four steps compress the whole chapter into a single cycle. A self-generated goal at the edge of your ability (the anchor thrown into the unknown, 4.1). Present-moment awareness that separates real difficulty from the projections of the defended identity (the survival response of 4.3). A threshold crossed by acceptance rather than by force (the pain that signals change, 4.2). And a new level from which the old excuses are visible as excuses (the new identity, 4.5). They also match the personal evolution loop of 4.3: desire, step into the unknown, chaos, acquired capacity, expanded identity.

#### The perception threshold in daily work

Koe applies the perception threshold to focused work in a 2023 video (**Source:** `Change Your Life In 6 Months (My Deep Work Routine).md`, 2023-03-26; repeated in `Get Ahead Of 99% Of People With Deep Work & Monk Mode.md`, 2024-03-21). Referring back to the video above, he describes "the perception threshold, where it's very difficult at the beginning but slowly becomes more pleasurable with time, where it's going to be difficult and your mind is going to perceive it as more difficult than it's going to be, and that perceived difficulty is going to increase ... until you hit that breakthrough." After the breakthrough "you're able to systemize things, and then it just flows a lot smoother, and then you can fit so much more into that hour." The practical consequence: "Your first hour of focused work isn't going to be that focused. You have to stick it out and refine your system with time." In this version the threshold has a second meaning: not only the moment within a session when difficulty becomes enjoyable, but the period (days or weeks) after which a new practice can be systematized. It is the same pattern as the routine guideline of section 4.1: "for the first month ... you're supposed to feel overwhelmed ... slowly then all at once everything flows."

A 2022 concept describes what happens after many thresholds have been crossed (**Source:** `How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of People (In 6-12 Months).md`, 2022-12-25). **Mastery over misery**: mastery comes in stages. You practice each stage over and over, "like how you were learning to walk, until it becomes effortless or unconscious", which opens up more mental energy to move on to the next thing. His examples: sushi chefs take years to perfect the preparation of rice; a tennis player first learns how to hold the racket (in a very specific way), then how to angle it, then how to hit the ball. This is what takes you ahead of people trying to get a ten-year result in two weeks, "that's just not going to happen." The concept connects the level of mind to skill: each stage of a skill, once made unconscious, frees attention for the next stage, just as each level of mind, once stabilized, frees the mind to perceive the problems of the next.

Koe later qualifies this emphasis on repetition. In 2023 he says that persistence and iteration matter more than consistency and repetition, and in 2024 that consistency maintains progress but does not create it. The qualification belongs to Chapter 15, which treats the mechanics of skill; it does not contradict mastery over misery so much as add that repetition must be accompanied by iteration to keep moving up.

With the level of mind, the model of identity change is complete. Identity is a web of goals that defends itself (Chapter 3). It changes when a trigger opens a crack (4.2), through phases of dissonance, uncertainty and discovery, best handled by precise taps and a progressive entry into the unknown (4.3), by immersion in the information and environment of the future self (4.4), and by turning the survival instinct toward the chosen identity (4.5), in protocols whose failures stack rather than reset (4.6). Each successful change raises the level of mind, from which the old problems are seen differently and new ones become visible (4.7). What the model has not yet explained is the mechanics of the mind that does all this: why it falls into disorder when left alone, why attention is the scarce resource on which every step depends, and why a goal changes what is perceived. That is the subject of Part III.

### Exercises

1. **Diagnose an abandoned change.** Choose one change you attempted and abandoned within weeks (a habit, a project, a resolution). Using the distinction between first-order and second-order change, describe what you changed at the level of behavior and what remained unchanged at the level of identity. Then identify which of the four reasons Koe gives for his own habits sticking (painful problem awareness, evidence for a desirable future self, changed environments, time blocked "for life") was missing. What would you need to do differently, and at which level?

2. **Map your trigger.** Koe's account of what triggers change moves from rock bottom (2022), to a psychological rock bottom you can provoke (2025), to a pre-existing "crack" that is hard to fake (2026). Recall the most significant change in your life so far. Which of these accounts describes it best? Was there a moment when you were "right before rock bottom: not painful enough to change, just comfortable enough to stay the same"? Based on your own case, evaluate Koe's 2026 claim that the visualization exercise fails because "the self doing the exercise is the self being evaluated." Is there a way to design the exercise so that the current identity cannot soften the blow?

3. **Choose the piece of the city.** Using the city metaphor (section 4.3), list five "buildings" of your current identity, from peripheral (a habit, a preference) to central (a core belief, a relationship, a profession). For a change you want to make, decide whether it requires pulling out a small piece or the heart. Then decide which register, gradual or extreme, you would use, and defend the choice against the strongest argument for the other register. Use the reconciliation proposed in the chapter (gradual for central pieces and fragile situations, extreme for the environment and brief periods of intensity) and say whether you find it convincing.

4. **Design an immersion.** Describe the environment of the person you want to become in a specific domain: the people they follow, the books they read, where they spend time, what is on their phone, what they wear, what they eat. Then design a "flip the switch" day following Koe's instructions (different wake-up time, different plan, different food, different people, different content). Predict which changes will trigger the strongest "threat response" and explain why, using the idea that "your old self is partly stored in your surroundings."

5. **Build and test an alter ego.** Follow Koe's three steps: choose two or three aspirational archetypes, write down the specific qualities you want from each, and give the alter ego a name. Use it in one recurring difficult situation for two weeks, and keep an identity list (Dickie Bush's method) with every action taken from that identity. At the end, assess: did the alter ego work as a "vessel for identity change" or as a "mask"? What criterion would you use to tell the difference, given that Koe never contrasts the alter ego with the "false transformation" he describes elsewhere?

6. **Weaponized survival and its limits.** Koe claims that the difference between "winners" and "losers" is which outcome the identity experiences as a threat. Identify one domain where you are "afraid of success" and one where you are "afraid of mediocrity." Then examine his own 2026 qualification: even a weaponized survival mechanism leaves you "still playing the game … in this cycle of suffering." Construct an example in which a successfully weaponized identity (the fit person at McDonald's, the founder whose revenue falls) becomes a source of suffering or bad decisions. What would it mean, practically, to hold such an identity "firmly but loosely"?

7. **Locate your level of mind through your problems.** List the problems that occupy most of your attention right now. Using Koe's sequence (health, money, relationships, emotional intelligence, then collective problems), what do they suggest about your current level of mind? Choose one problem that produces a "mental thought loop" and describe how it might look from the perspective of your ideal self and from "the perspective of the universe." Then question the framework: is the order of the sequence universal, or does it reflect particular values? Can a person be at a "high level" in one domain and a "low level" in another?

8. **Cross a perception threshold deliberately.** Pick an activity in which you usually quit when it gets hard (a run, a writing session, a meditation, a difficult conversation). Before your next attempt, write down the expectations and projections you usually attach to the difficulty (Koe's step 2). During the attempt, note when perceived difficulty peaks and whether it falls after you accept the reality of the moment. Afterward, compare your experience with Koe's description of the threshold. Where did it match, and where did it not? Under what conditions would "the only way out is through" be bad advice?
