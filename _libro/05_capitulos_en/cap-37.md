## Chapter 37 — The Author's Trajectory and the Step from One Person to a Team

The previous parts of this book presented a system: how a mind is programmed, how identity changes, how attention and goals organize experience, how to learn, write, build an audience, run a one-person business, create value and think about money, agency and AI. This chapter does two things with that system. First, it follows Dan Koe's own trajectory as a single integrated case: the long series of failed ventures, the moment the pieces "clicked," the income figures he gives as credentials, the books, and what he believes sets his work apart. Second, it follows him past the edge of the model he became known for. After years as "the one-person business guy," he co-founded a software company (first Cortex, also spelled Kortex, later rebuilt and renamed Eden), and in two long conversations he examined, with his own co-founders and with the founders of the creator platform Stan, what changes when a business stops being one person and becomes a team.

The chapter therefore works both as a case and as a limit. The first two sections show how the principles of the earlier chapters (build to learn, you are the niche, writing as the base skill, the one-person business as a stage of the roadmap, leverage over hours) appear in a real life, with all the noise, contradictions and revisions that a real life contains. The last four sections show where those principles stop transferring one to one: speed, product-market fit, team structure, hiring, and alignment are problems a solo creator never has to solve.

Three cautions govern how the material is presented.

First, the biographical material comes from dozens of videos recorded between 2021 and 2026, and the details shift from one telling to another: the list of the "seven" failed businesses, the size of the loan from his father, his salary at the agency job, his income figures, the number of hours he writes. The chapter reports the versions side by side and does not choose a winner silently.

Second, from Section 37.3 onward a large part of the material does not come from Koe himself. It comes from guests: John Hugh and Vitali, co-founders of Stan, interviewed in "How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup" (January 2025), and Matt and Ari, Koe's co-founders at Kortex and Eden, in "Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake" (November 2025). Koe asks the questions, adds examples and draws lessons, but the frameworks about team design, hiring and alignment are mostly theirs. The text marks who says what.

Third, much of what Koe says about his software is promotional. Where a passage is a product pitch, the chapter says so and extracts the method or the lesson rather than the claim.

### 37.1 From Seven Failed Businesses to a Body of Work

Koe tells the story of his early failures in many videos, and he tells it for a reason. It is not offered as nostalgia. It is offered as evidence for claims made elsewhere in the system: that you cannot know what interests you without trying it (Chapter 14, build to learn), that you are your own best market research (Chapter 19, you are the niche), that skills learned in "failed" projects stack into something none of them could produce alone (Chapter 15 and Chapter 20), and that progress is not linear but arrives as "nothing happens, then everything happens." Reading the catalogue carefully, venture by venture, shows how each of those principles was extracted from a particular experience.

#### Before the businesses: the channel, the handle and the drive

Koe says he "never thought" he would have a personal brand. Around 2016, as a college freshman, he and a friend on the same dormitory floor started a YouTube channel. It is, he notes, the same channel he uses today, with all the old videos deleted. They made calisthenics videos, eating challenges (including a 10,000-calorie challenge) and talking-head videos, and "it didn't get anywhere. We didn't know what we were doing." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md.

The same period gave him the name under which he is known. He always had, in his words, "this desire to do something creative," and he experimented with photography, digital art and photo editing (he points out that those edits are still visible at the bottom of his Instagram). In summers he worked as a lifeguard, and his co-workers suggested shortening his name; "the Dan Koe" "just stuck." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md.

The driving force behind everything that followed was negative rather than positive. He did not know what he wanted to be, but "I did know that I didn't want to be like most people," and above all he did not want to get a job after college. **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md. This is worth noticing because it matches the structure the book described in Part IV: the anti-vision (what you refuse to become) often becomes clear long before the vision does, and it is enough to generate years of motion.

He also explains how he got into business in the first place, and the explanation is not a story of ambition. When he started he was "just curious" and did not know where to start, what to learn or what business to build. He took a few courses that made no sense to him: a digital marketing course taught Mailchimp, and he remembers thinking "What do I do with this? Why am I learning Mailchimp? What do you mean emails? What do you mean lead magnet?" What finally taught him was immersion. Everything he read, every video he watched, every person he followed and all his conversations revolved around business. He picked up tips from those sources, "but more importantly, in this environment, if I wasn't building a business, I didn't fit into the environment. And so, that felt wrong. And so, inadvertently, I started a business so I could fit in." **Source:** How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md (2026).

The anecdote is an application of the theory of identity and environment developed in Chapters 3, 4 and 11: an identity is maintained by the information it consumes and the people it imitates, and when the environment changes, behavior follows in order to restore a sense of belonging. Koe presents it as an unintended mechanism, not as a strategy he chose. Its lesson for the reader is that the environment can carry you into action before you have a clear reason to act.

#### The catalogue of failures

The most detailed version of the catalogue is the 2022 video "7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures)." Later videos add, remove or rename items. The number "seven" stays stable; the composition does not. The following reconstruction follows the 2022 sequence and then adds what later versions contribute.

**Failed business 1: a fitness YouTube channel.** His time before business had been dedicated to the gym, so his first attempt was a fitness channel made with a friend: 10,000-calorie challenge videos, general lifting videos, vlogs and food challenges. After a few months they quit, because doing it together took too much time. He saw some growth and began to understand social media, for example the tactic of commenting on other accounts to "leverage their audience." Afterward he dropped business for a while, studied, partied and got "the college experience." **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

**Failed business 2: a second channel filmed on a phone.** With renewed drive he spent a summer making videos at home with his phone: acne videos and talking-head videos on self-improvement and spirituality, "kind of like Elliott Hulse," filmed in his garage while "trying to be like a tough alpha male" giving harsh-truth advice. He finds this funny now and wishes he still had the videos. His comment on this stage is the thread that runs through the whole catalogue: "I've been trying and failing at this for a long time and that's a common theme here." **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

**Complementary context:** Elliott Hulse is a strength coach and YouTuber who was popular in the early 2010s for direct, motivational talking-head videos mixing fitness, masculinity and spirituality. Koe lists him among the fitness creators of that era whose style he imitated.

**Failed business 3: photography and digital art.** In his sophomore year he bought a camera for YouTube, stopped making the videos and picked up photography (street, landscape and portrait) from YouTube tutorials. He did not care much for photography itself. What he cared about was the editing, which sent him "down a Photoshop rabbit hole." He would spend six to eight hours in his room, in a flow state with music, creating surreal composites from his own photos and stock images. He stopped because he did not know how to monetize it. The lesson he draws is the central claim of build to learn: "how in the world could I know that I was very passionate and interested in editing as opposed to say photography without actually diving into photography first?" Certain things have to be tried before you can find what you are truly interested in. He adds a retrospective observation: with the marketing, sales and course-building knowledge he has now, he could build a course teaching composites, and "is that not the dream": doing what you love and teaching it to people who are interested in it. It is, he says, a matter of supply and demand, of interest. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

The burnout version of the same episode appears in 2025. He gained some followers with digital art but burned out trying to post one edit a day: he "couldn't handle taking Adderall every morning and busting out 6 hours staring at Photoshop." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md. The two tellings complement each other. The 2022 version emphasizes the discovery (editing, not photography) and the missing skill (monetization). The 2025 version emphasizes the unsustainable method (a daily output quota maintained with a stimulant).

**Failed business 4 (in the 2022 sequence): a Facebook ads agency.** The 2022 video and later summaries mention a Facebook ads agency among the failed ventures; the material gathered for this chapter does not include a separate account of it.

**Failed business 5: a dropshipping rave clothing store.** He was into EDM and festivals and knew that audience "like the back of my hand": what people wore and how events were promoted. The business lesson is the one later formalized in Chapter 19: to market accurately to an audience, immerse yourself in it. If you do not go to raves and want to sell to ravers, he has a "leg up," because "I am the market research at that point." He took a dropshipping course (branding, store setup, shipping from China) and used what he knew about Facebook ads. One product was a diamond-studded flashy bra. He sold nothing. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

The coexistence of a correct insight and a total commercial failure is instructive. Knowing the market did not compensate for the absence of other skills (product quality, shipping, promotion, copy). "I am the market research" is a necessary condition in Koe's system, not a sufficient one.

**Failed business 6: blue light glasses for developers.** By then he was immersed in another market: he was studying programming and knew that developers are passionate and stare at screens all day, so a brand of blue light glasses made for them made sense. He called his father to ask for a few thousand dollars, and to his surprise his father said yes. It was the first time he had made an argument and an idea convincing enough for someone to invest in it, and because his father is not wealthy, "it meant the world." He ordered product, set up the store and the ads, and studied influencer marketing (his first ad featured his hedgehog, Momo, who has since died). He "blew" the $3,000: shipping times were long, packaging was a problem, and he was promoting without much money. It did not work, and it produced another low point ("why can I not make this work?"). He had to call his father and tell him he had wasted his money. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

In a 2024 retelling he adds what went wrong strategically. He was following the common advice to "pick a niche, get very specific about your customer avatar," and targeted developers at a time when blue light glasses were being marketed to everyone. "I wanted to market to developers and I thought I could pull that off but I couldn't just because I was unskilled." **Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md. The diagnosis is not that niching is wrong, but that the advice assumed a level of marketing skill he did not yet have.

In a 2023 talk he tells the emotional side of the same episode more starkly. He failed at seven businesses (freelancing, dropshipping, agency work, e-commerce brands, "all of that") with minimal results, went through "mental turmoil," and ended up crying on the phone to his father, asking him not to ruin his life, because he had borrowed and lost "however many thousand dollars" to order product for an e-commerce store. **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md.

**The agency job and failed business 7: the wallet brand.** His father suggested he use his web development skills for a better-paying job. He got a job at a web design agency, for what he describes in 2022 as about $60,000 to $70,000 a year, "a lot for a college student," and he dropped out of college to spend his free time building businesses. He started a second e-commerce brand, minimalist leather wallets. He ordered them, got professional photos, "did everything right," but "when it came time to promote I just... I just didn't do it." Out of fear "or something," he gave up. In the same passage he mentions failing at a content marketing agency and an SEO agency. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

The wallet brand is the only failure in the catalogue that did not fail in the market. It failed before the market, at the moment of exposure. It belongs with the material on fear of judgment in earlier chapters: the work that could have produced feedback was finished, and the one action that would have produced it was the one he avoided.

**What later versions add.** In a 2024 video he describes his first real job as a web designer at a marketing agency, building plug-and-play e-commerce sites for furniture and appliance retailers. Before that he had worked part-time at a print shop while living with seven other men in a six-room fraternity house converted into seven rooms, each paying $250 to $400 as "broke college kids." To the list he adds freelance web development, freelance SEO, a "drop servicing" business called a "2-hour agency" (pick a skill, create an offer, attract clients and outsource all the work to Fiverr; it failed "for obvious reasons," and it is the origin of the name of his later product "2 Hour Writer"), and an animated explainer video agency. "All failed." **Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md.

In 2025 he gives a compressed financial version. He built three e-commerce stores (dropshipping rave clothes, minimalist wallets, blue light glasses), took Facebook ads courses, borrowed $2,000 from his father and accumulated a total of $8,000 of debt as a college junior, after a series of short-lived ventures (the three stores, an SEO agency, a content marketing agency, web design, Facebook ads). To keep rent at $200 to $300 a month he lived with seven roommates while working part-time: "a lot of stress." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md.

The courses deserve a separate mention because Koe uses them to make a point about responsibility. He bought courses on freelancing, Facebook ads, e-commerce brand building, copywriting, sales and client acquisition, plus "at least 15 web development courses that are irrelevant but noteworthy." He did not see much success with the business models those courses taught, "and that's on me not them, that's my execution problem and lack of... the distinction between knowledge and understanding." **Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md. The distinction between knowledge and understanding, developed in Chapter 14, is here applied to himself: he had the information but not the embodied skill that only comes from building.

The variations between tellings are worth stating plainly, because a reader comparing videos will notice them.

#### How the failures became a foundation

Koe reads the catalogue in three ways, and each reading connects it to a principle developed elsewhere in the book.

The first reading is **awareness through failure**. After four or five years of trying seven different business models, "everything clicked." The principle he derives is that "you gain awareness from your failures": by diving in, you find things that would have led to success earlier but that you did not know existed. **Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md (2022). Failure here is not a moral test. It is an information-gathering process, which is why the system insists on building as the method of learning.

The second reading is **the failures as a skill stack**. In 2025 he says he was in "beginner hell for almost 5 years," not the three to six months most creators go through, jumping between dropshipping, three e-commerce stores, digital art and photography, a Facebook ads agency and an SEO content agency, until he had to take a web design job "because the loans were piling up." At that job he procrastinated on his assigned work in order to do freelance web design. When he later started writing on social media to land clients, he did not experience beginner hell again, "because I knew how to avoid it": a select few skills learned across all those failures led to his own click. **Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md. The term "beginner hell," which the book uses elsewhere for the phase of publishing into the void without growth, is applied here to his own five years. The point is that the skills were not wasted when the ventures failed. They were stored, and they combined later.

The third reading is **the click as architecture**. In 2023 he describes the moment the skills combined. After the turmoil and the call to his father, it hit him all at once: "this skill, this skill, this skill, these all piece together, I can see the structure now." He could see the image the puzzle formed and knew how to navigate "this new realm." He calls it an "avalanche called insight" that "crashed into my psyche," and summarizes the pattern with a phrase he uses often: "as I like to say, nothing happens then everything happens." He adds a crucial qualification: the click is not a single event. It solves only the current level and sets a new "level of mind." As you keep pursuing external goals, it happens again and again: "a click click click click click." **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md.

"Nothing happens then everything happens" is one of Koe's coined phrases. In this passage it means that after a long period without visible results, previously separate pieces suddenly fit together. In other videos he renames it "slowly then all at once" and, later, compares it to a star that collapses and then explodes; the idea of invisible accumulation followed by a sudden jump stays the same. The "avalanche called insight" is his name for the moment in which he sees the structure his skills form. Both connect the biography to the model of levels of mind in Part II and Part XIV: each solved level of problems produces a new identity that can see problems it could not see before.

There is a fourth thread that runs through the catalogue without being stated as a lesson: **the market was always himself**. The rave store sold to the scene he lived in; the glasses sold to the developers he studied with; the composite course he imagines in retrospect would sell to people like the person he was. When the click came, the business he built sold to people like him the skills he had learned. This is the biographical root of "you are the niche."

#### Self-funded from near zero

Koe insists that he started without capital. He was raised in a middle-class family and never had money except what his father lent him, which he lost in a business that did not succeed anyway. Everything since has been self-funded or done with close to zero dollars: earning a little at a part-time job and then a full-time job, and investing in inexpensive technology and software (design software, email marketing software). He had to create his own substantial source of income before he could invest in larger projects such as his software startup or the publication of a book. **Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md (2024).

The claim matters to the argument of the system. If the one-person business required capital, the critique of the default path in Part I would offer an exit only to people who already had resources. Koe's insistence on his own lack of capital is meant to show that the exit is available to people in the position he was in, though the chapter should also note what his story does include: a father able and willing to lend a few thousand dollars, a scholarship-supported college education (mentioned in other videos), and a technical skill (web development) that produced a salary when the ventures failed. These are modest advantages, not wealth, but they are part of the case.

#### The numbers and how to read them

Koe says repeatedly that he dislikes talking about numbers and does it "for credibility." The figures are scattered across videos and years. Read together, they tell a story of growth, but they also show the problems of using a moving target as evidence.

**Credentials by type of offer.** In 2022 and again in 2024 he says he has been a freelancer, a coach and a consultant, and has done "every one-person business offer under the sun," selling more than $100,000 of almost everything he went into: freelancing, coaching, consulting, digital products and cohorts. He gives two reasons for speaking about the one-person business: direct experience, and knowing the steps well enough to see that people "overcomplicate it way too much." In the 2024 version he adds two things. First, self-reliance is "possibly the greatest value in my life." Second, when people "screamed" at him to go short-form (Reels) or to jump to the next business model, he already had something working and went in the opposite direction, starting long-form newsletters: "when people told me to go short form I started writing long-form newsletters." "Success is counterintuitive," he says, and he counts counterintuitiveness and skepticism among his values. **Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md (2022); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024).

"Success is counterintuitive" is another of his phrases. In the 2024 guide it has two senses: the principle that forcing himself to work less and build solutions brought him the most growth, and, in this passage, the practice of moving against what everyone recommends once you have evidence that your own approach works. It is not a license for contrarianism without evidence; in the anecdote he already "had something good going."

**Writing as the base of the income.** In August 2022 he said that he averaged $40,000 to $60,000 a month from his writing, in about two hours of writing a day. He works longer than that, because he builds side projects, but if he stripped away everything except Twitter, his newsletter and his Twitter threads he would keep 80 to 90% of his income, since he does not really promote anywhere else and his YouTube and Instagram are based on what he has already written. In the 2024 compilation where the same figure reappears, he adds that he is "not a writer" and has no English degree, and that you do not need one: the world wants good, authentic, impactful writers. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md (2022); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024).

Whether Koe calls himself a writer is itself an unresolved point in the corpus. He says "I'm not a writer" in this passage, describes himself elsewhere as "a writer and brand adviser" (2025, 2026), and says as late as July 2026 "I still don't call myself a writer." The contradiction may depend on whether he is referring to his professional identity or to the stereotype of the academically trained writer; he does not explain it. **Source:** evolution entry EV-299.

**Monthly and annual figures.** In November 2022, recounting what people say to him when they do not understand what he does, he quotes: "oh so you're a YouTuber but you make a hundred thousand dollars a month." **Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md. In April and May 2023 he said that his "little one-person business" made $800,000 in 2022, had made $1.5 million so far in 2023, and was projected to reach about $2.5 million (he called it a projection and promised to be transparent about the actual figure). All of it came from selling information products and education, because "some of the most valuable things there are is what's in your head and how you operate within the world." From that revenue he was writing a book and building software, projects with meaning for him, and from a place of less stress about money he could "strategize and visualize the impact I want to leave on the world": "it's creating for fun, work has turned into play." **Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md. In a video opened with the statistic that "the average writer makes sixty nine thousand five hundred and ten dollars a year," he said he made $800,000 in 2022 "writing for two hours a day and just a few days a week," then clarified that he works more than that, but writing is "the base of my business, everything I do revolves around writing, and I do that for around two hours every single day." **Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md (2023). The same passage contains both "a few days a week" and "every single day," a small internal inconsistency.

In August 2023 he said he had become "a liquid millionaire at 26," until he invested 50% of it into a new business, and that he had generated $2.5 to 3.5 million in total as a one-person business, which he was "now evolving past." He shared this in response to an Instagram post claiming that every millionaire under 30 got there through inheritance, a high income or a large income event such as crypto; the person sharing it, around 35, had been budgeting for ten years and was halfway to a million. Koe agrees that generating wealth is slow, but "not that slow." He shares his figures for perspective, remembering that he himself was inspired when he heard of someone doing what he did reaching a first $100,000 month. Nobody could have taught him to do this, he says, and the lesson is that such advice makes you believe there is no new path to forge and no education or skill that lets you surpass average, self-imposed limits; but technological change makes this a "beautiful time," and "you may have been right 10 years ago but now there are many case studies arguing against that." **Source:** Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md.

In January 2024 he said he had sustained $100,000 to $250,000 a month, fluctuating with product launches and content performance up to $500,000 to $750,000 a month, and he specified that this is revenue, not profit. He produces it by writing tweets, newsletters and threads, repurposed into Instagram, YouTube and LinkedIn. "Writing is the force multiplier," he says, the thing that makes him pay twenty times his previous web design salary in taxes every year ("a good problem to have"). It is not all or nothing: if he stripped everything except X and the newsletter, a large part would remain. And money does not jump from zero to a million: you go from 0 to $10,000, to $20,000, to more, and you can make life-changing money in one to three years, "however long it takes for things to click" and to internalize "the skill of making money, which houses many other skills." **Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md.

Two coined expressions appear in that passage. "Force multiplier" is used by Koe here for writing as the skill that multiplies every other part of the business; in another 2024 video he uses the same phrase, taken from Sam Altman, for focus as what multiplies work. "The skill of making money" is his name for a meta-skill that contains many others (writing, marketing, selling, building a product) and takes one to three years to internalize.

In April 2024 he said: "Two years ago I have a video where I made $800,000 writing 2 hours every day; last year I was doing the same exact thing but I made 4.1 million." The transcript says "last year or two years ago," so the timeline is slightly ambiguous. **Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md.

In February 2025 he gave the only complete series, by year of building products: about $10,000 the first year, about $100,000 the second, then $150,000, then $800,000, then about $4 million, and "last year" about $2 million "because revenue split between myself and Cortex." He frames it as anything but "get rich quick mumbo jumbo": it is the result of learning product marketing and promotion, skills any business owner has to learn. **Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md.

**The arithmetic.** In mid-2024 he explained how he earns a few million a year working two to four hours a day. Over five years he grew to millions of followers, which means traffic. People love to buy things, and some love to increase their value and learn skills; they are like him: "I am the niche, you are the niche." He sells to people like himself by teaching skills he learned, under his own brand. When he sells an ebook, a course or a template, he is usually selling something he already bought from someone else, but created his way. Millions of followers, times a product of $150 or more, times the fraction who buy, equals millions. His rough figures: 10 to 20 million impressions a month across platforms, converting a fraction to a $150 product or his $28 book. That, he says, is what happens when an entrepreneur controls customer acquisition, the product and the skills in between (marketing, sales, operations). He closes with a question for the viewer: how long will it take you to learn social media well enough to generate impressions, send them to a landing page, and offer a product with a buy button? **Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md.

The 2025 series is the only complete one. **Source:** evolution entry EV-287.

The hours behind the figures also move. He mentions 30 minutes of writing each morning, 90-minute blocks, two hours a day, two to three hours, the first two to three hours of the day and, later, "two hours of writing in the morning, that's my entire routine" or about 60 minutes a day. The principle (morning writing as the base of the business) stays the same, and he explains part of the variation by growing efficiency: he needed more time at first because he lacked skill. **Source:** evolution entry EV-160. The hours he works in total also change with the stage of the business: during years of maintenance most of his days were under four hours, while building Cortex he worked "much more" and says he likes sixteen-hour days, though not for his whole life. **Source:** evolution entry EV-286. This will matter in Section 37.2.

What the figures are offered to prove is narrower than the figures themselves. They are not proof that anyone will earn the same amounts. They are offered as evidence for three specific claims: that a business built on writing and information products can reach seven figures without a team; that growth is gradual (0 to $10,000 to $20,000) rather than a single jump; and that the mechanism is understandable (traffic times product times conversion). The reader should take them as a case, not as a forecast.

#### The books: The Art of Focus and after

The book was Koe's first very long-term project, and its history shows his working method at a larger scale than a newsletter.

In a conversation with Dickie Bush in March 2023, Koe described the "intense season" that produced the draft. It began in Texas. He was still dabbling in fitness content but no longer enjoying it, and he realized that to produce the value needed to make an impact in fitness he would have to make it his whole brand; meanwhile he had "this unique frame of the one-person business that I can take a long way." That was when his program Digital Economics was born, and 2 Hour Writer was broken off from it. That clarity carried over to the book. He reread the first draft he had written two years earlier and reacted with embarrassment ("this is dog... I can't believe I thought this was good writing two years ago"), re-outlined it, filled it in again, and in about 30 days wrote a new first draft of 230 pages, which was then with an editor. He clarifies that the "30 days" was a culmination: about six months writing a very first draft, another six months re-outlining and picking up ideas, a process of about two years. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

The same conversation shows him describing his working life in terms of seasons, a concept developed in Chapter 39. At that moment the book was "on the come down" and the software "on the come up": developers were building it while he pieced together marketing, outreach, content and positioning. He was in a "middle phase," sustaining projects and knowing that an intense season (the software launch or the book promotion) was coming: "I don't know what it is that's going to cause that click." **Source:** same. The word "click" connects this passage to the avalanche of insight described above: the intense phases of his life start when pieces fit, and he does not try to predict exactly when that will happen.

He wanted the launch to be special. The first 1,000 copies would have custom covers with silver foil; a purple-foil copy for Joey, who had been helping him; engraved copies for Dickie, Justin and others: "little minor touches." He was grateful to be at a point where "I can treat this one as a passion project... I want to have fun with this without expectation." He did not know the launch date: one person had done eight revisions, his own first revision took about a week per chapter or two, he hoped for the third or fourth quarter of 2023, and "I'm learning on the go... I don't even know what the publishing process looks like yet." **Source:** same. "Passion project" is an ordinary phrase, but in this passage it has a precise sense: a project done for its own sake, financed by a business that no longer depends on it, and released without commercial expectations. That freedom is a consequence of the income described above.

The book's calendar moved. In March 2023 he hoped for the third or fourth quarter; in June 2023 he said it was finished and would go on pre-sale soon; in August 2023 he planned to relaunch his planner together with the book at the end of September. **Source:** evolution entry EV-297.

In February 2024 he described what the book is and is not. Discussing the estimate that humans can consciously process about 50 bits of information per second, "approximately 125 billion bits in their lifetime" (a figure he presents as approximate), he mentions that he treats it in The Art of Focus, which he calls "a philosophy for working less, earning more and enjoying life. Focus is only one part of it, and focus is so much more than just deep work. There's one chapter on deep work in the book; the rest is the holistic philosophy for living a better life, doing what you want, creating your own career, not being assigned one... and how to navigate that mentally and build that mental strength and emotional resilience." **Source:** Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md.

**Complementary context:** the figure of about 50 bits per second for conscious processing is commonly associated with Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's discussion of the limits of attention in Flow, where he draws on earlier information-theory estimates of how many bits per second consciousness can handle. The published estimates vary by source and are rough orders of magnitude (Csikszentmihalyi's own figures are somewhat higher than 50), so the number is best read as an illustration of a hard limit on attention rather than as a measurement. Koe himself presents his figure as approximate.

In January 2024 he recorded an "official book summary" in order to give the correct context before others summarized the book. He defines The Art of Focus as "a practical philosophy to find meaning, reinvent yourself and create your ideal future," notes that much is left out of the summary, and lists the structure covered: an introduction, then chapters titled The Principles, The Three Pillars, The Universe, The Self, The Game, The Lens and The Formula, with further chapters later in the summary. **Source:** The Art Of Focus – Official Book Summary By The Author Dan Koe.md.

#### What sets his work apart

Koe offers three explanations of why his work stands out, and each comes from a different angle.

The first is **re-meaning an existing term**. "I didn't come up with the concept of one person business, but I took it and I gave it an entirely new meaning." Before, the phrase simply meant operating a business as one person with internet technology. He gave it the sense the book developed in Part X: productize yourself, self-monetization, turning yourself into the brand, truly doing what you love for a living, backed by systems that get the result. He also names processes that appear elsewhere in his videos, such as "intelligent imitation," "tactical stress" and "the unknown" (the last is not defined in this passage; Chapters 1, 10, 14 and 39 develop what he means by the others and by exploring the unknown). The source of this naming ability, he says, is the application of his interests (spirituality, philosophy, neuroscience) to his expertise (online business, marketing, branding). With enough exposure across those domains, "because I'm not a specialist," he notices common patterns and pieces together long-form content that is difficult to replicate. **Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024). This is a self-description of the generalist synthesizer of Chapter 20: the differentiation comes from the intersection, not from depth in a single field.

The second is **depth and meaning as the reason people follow him**. From reading DMs and emails he narrowed down why people watch his content. He talks about business, lifestyle design, productivity and writing, but tries to make it "a bit deeper, less superficial." "I want my work, and therefore your work, to have meaning," and he wants people to feel a sense of control over their lives. He teaches what he has achieved and what he wants to achieve, and people resonate with those specific goals. **Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md (2024). This is the audience-side version of the claim that your goals are your niche: people follow a person heading somewhere they also want to go.

The third is **a goal that changed its nature over time**. Koe says he always had the goal of being a "deep, free and original thinker," but at first it was not a conscious goal. "As the self-actualization process goes," it began as an unconscious and shallow goal driven by the need for acceptance: he wanted to sound smart, intellectual and deep, and to be accepted by others "in a non-mediocre way," to be different. He did not really care about being a deep thinker. "With time and after burning through that Karma I realized that becoming a deep thinker is more than that, it is the key to creative success," or to success in any endeavor. If you want original success, or to open a new path for your own life and for others to follow, you have to prioritize deep, free and original thinking. **Source:** The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md (2022).

The arc is a small illustration of a pattern the book describes in Part II and Part XIV: a goal can start as an ego-driven, unconscious strategy for belonging and become, after it has been lived through, a conscious value. "Burning through that Karma" is his metaphor for that process; the source does not define the word "Karma" further, and it is best read as "living through the consequences of the shallow motivation until it is exhausted," not as a doctrinal claim. The episode parallels the "started a business so I could fit in" anecdote above. In both cases a motive that was about acceptance became, through practice, something he endorses for its own sake.

#### Guest stories as counterpoints

Three guest stories in the corpus are useful precisely because they are not Koe's. They show the same principles operating in different trajectories, and they introduce the people whose frameworks occupy the second half of this chapter.

**Justin Welsh: from a scaled company to one person.** Introducing Welsh in 2021, Koe describes him as someone who "over the last decade has helped build two $50 million annual recurring revenue companies, teams of 150 plus people, and has raised over 300 million in venture capital. That was until he burned out." Then "he saw the opportunity in the attention economy" and started a one-person business. At the time of recording, his income streams were advising early-stage SaaS companies in healthcare technology, a private community for online creators, two digital products for creators, and a one-on-one coaching business. Welsh adds that he spent five years as an early employee at a unicorn SaaS company (Zocdoc) and then became chief revenue officer at PatientPop, after about ten years in high-growth SaaS. **Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md.

**Complementary context:** "annual recurring revenue" (ARR) is the yearly value of a company's subscription revenue, the standard size measure for software-as-a-service (SaaS) businesses. A "unicorn" is a private startup valued at over a billion dollars.

Welsh's path is the inverse of Koe's. Koe went from one person to a team; Welsh went from large teams to one person. Together they show that the one-person business is not a stage everyone passes through in one direction. It is a structure chosen for what it gives (autonomy, profit margin, freedom from managing people) and left for what it cannot give (scale, challenge, a product that requires many hands).

**John Hugh: authenticity before polish.** John Hugh, co-founder of Stan, made his first $10,000 of passive income from a file he did not change. He posted a video that went viral unintentionally: he green-screened his old résumé from a junior internship (he had to recover his Dropbox password to find it) and said, "these are literally the bullets I did." The very first version of Stan was built for his own account. He uploaded that same file without changing anything ("the 2016 .docx or whatever it is") and sold it for $10. "That's how I made my first $10,000 of passive income." Why it worked: "it was authentic to what my audience wanted, which was help on their resumes, and they wanted a template." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md. The case is a compressed version of the minimum viable product argument of Chapter 29: the product was not designed; it was found in what the audience already asked for.

**Koe's onboarding to Stan: what a one-person operation looks like from outside.** The founders of Stan also describe working with Koe. Vitali, who says he spends "70% of my time thinking about hiring," calls onboarding Koe his best experience with a creator of that caliber, in part because Koe's content operation is just Koe. With creators of that size there is usually bureaucracy ("talk to my assistant... review... hand off... take a month"). With Koe they connected on a Thursday or Friday, shared ideas through Loom videos, and on Monday Vitali logged in to find "three products, $10,000, like everything is firing." When Koe wanted Loom videos embedded in his course, John relayed the request, and Vitali called an engineer: "I need this in 2 hours." It shipped the same day. John's comment is the point of the story: this "speaks towards the agility of an organization we would like to keep as we continue to scale," now at forty-some people and heading toward a hundred-some; "it's a choice to operate that way." **Source:** same.

The anecdote is the hinge between the two halves of this chapter. From the outside, the one-person business looks like speed: one decision-maker, no handoffs, a weekend from idea to revenue. The founders of Stan admire that speed and say that preserving it while growing is a deliberate choice, not something that happens on its own. The rest of the chapter is, in large part, about why that is so hard.
