## Chapter 37 — The Author's Trajectory and the Step from One Person to a Team

The previous parts of this book presented a system: how a mind is programmed, how identity changes, how attention and goals organize experience, how to learn, write, build an audience, run a one-person business, create value and think about money, agency and AI. This chapter does two things with that system. First, it follows Dan Koe's own trajectory as a single integrated case: the long series of failed ventures, the moment the pieces "clicked," the income figures he gives as credentials, the books, and what he believes sets his work apart. Second, it follows him past the edge of the model he became known for. After years as "the one-person business guy," he co-founded a software company (first Cortex, also spelled Kortex, later rebuilt and renamed Eden), and in two long conversations he examined, with his own co-founders and with the founders of the creator platform Stan, what changes when a business stops being one person and becomes a team.

The chapter therefore works both as a case and as a limit. The first two sections show how the principles of the earlier chapters (build to learn, you are the niche, writing as the base skill, the one-person business as a stage of the roadmap, leverage over hours) appear in a real life, with all the noise, contradictions and revisions that a real life contains. The last four sections show where those principles stop transferring one to one: speed, product-market fit, team structure, hiring, and alignment are problems a solo creator never has to solve.

Three cautions govern how the material is presented.

First, the biographical material comes from dozens of videos recorded between 2021 and 2026, and the details shift from one telling to another: the list of the "seven" failed businesses, the size of the loan from his father, his salary at the agency job, his income figures, the number of hours he writes. The chapter reports the versions side by side and does not choose a winner silently.

Second, from Section 37.3 onward a large part of the material does not come from Koe himself. It comes from guests: John Hugh and Vitali, co-founders of Stan, interviewed in "How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup" (January 2025), and Matt and Ari, Koe's co-founders at Kortex and Eden, in "Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake" (November 2025). Koe asks the questions, adds examples and draws lessons, but the frameworks about team design, hiring and alignment are mostly theirs. The text marks who says what.

Third, much of what Koe says about his software is promotional. Where a passage is a product pitch, the chapter says so and extracts the method or the lesson rather than the claim.

### 37.1 From Seven Failed Businesses to a Body of Work

Koe tells the story of his early failures in many videos, and he tells it for a reason. It is not offered as nostalgia. It is offered as evidence for claims made elsewhere in the system: that you cannot know what interests you without trying it (Chapter 14, build to learn), that you are your own best market research (Chapter 19, you are the niche), that skills learned in "failed" projects stack into something none of them could produce alone (Chapter 15 and Chapter 20), and that progress is not linear but arrives as "nothing happens, then everything happens." Reading the catalogue carefully, venture by venture, shows how each of those principles was extracted from a particular experience.

#### Before the businesses: the channel, the handle and the drive

Koe says he "never thought" he would have a personal brand. Around 2016, as a college freshman, he and a friend on the same dormitory floor started a YouTube channel. It is, he notes, the same channel he uses today, with all the old videos deleted. They made calisthenics videos, eating challenges (including a 10,000-calorie challenge) and talking-head videos, and "it didn't get anywhere. We didn't know what we were doing." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md.

The same period gave him the name under which he is known. He always had, in his words, "this desire to do something creative," and he experimented with photography, digital art and photo editing (he points out that those edits are still visible at the bottom of his Instagram). In summers he worked as a lifeguard, and his co-workers suggested shortening his name; "the Dan Koe" "just stuck." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md.

The driving force behind everything that followed was negative rather than positive. He did not know what he wanted to be, but "I did know that I didn't want to be like most people," and above all he did not want to get a job after college. **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md. This is worth noticing because it matches the structure the book described in Part IV: the anti-vision (what you refuse to become) often becomes clear long before the vision does, and it is enough to generate years of motion.

He also explains how he got into business in the first place, and the explanation is not a story of ambition. When he started he was "just curious" and did not know where to start, what to learn or what business to build. He took a few courses that made no sense to him: a digital marketing course taught Mailchimp, and he remembers thinking "What do I do with this? Why am I learning Mailchimp? What do you mean emails? What do you mean lead magnet?" What finally taught him was immersion. Everything he read, every video he watched, every person he followed and all his conversations revolved around business. He picked up tips from those sources, "but more importantly, in this environment, if I wasn't building a business, I didn't fit into the environment. And so, that felt wrong. And so, inadvertently, I started a business so I could fit in." **Source:** How To Completely Reinvent Yourself In 6-12 Months.md (2026).

The anecdote is an application of the theory of identity and environment developed in Chapters 3, 4 and 11: an identity is maintained by the information it consumes and the people it imitates, and when the environment changes, behavior follows in order to restore a sense of belonging. Koe presents it as an unintended mechanism, not as a strategy he chose. Its lesson for the reader is that the environment can carry you into action before you have a clear reason to act.

#### The catalogue of failures

The most detailed version of the catalogue is the 2022 video "7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures)." Later videos add, remove or rename items. The number "seven" stays stable; the composition does not. The following reconstruction follows the 2022 sequence and then adds what later versions contribute.

**Failed business 1: a fitness YouTube channel.** His time before business had been dedicated to the gym, so his first attempt was a fitness channel made with a friend: 10,000-calorie challenge videos, general lifting videos, vlogs and food challenges. After a few months they quit, because doing it together took too much time. He saw some growth and began to understand social media, for example the tactic of commenting on other accounts to "leverage their audience." Afterward he dropped business for a while, studied, partied and got "the college experience." **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

**Failed business 2: a second channel filmed on a phone.** With renewed drive he spent a summer making videos at home with his phone: acne videos and talking-head videos on self-improvement and spirituality, "kind of like Elliott Hulse," filmed in his garage while "trying to be like a tough alpha male" giving harsh-truth advice. He finds this funny now and wishes he still had the videos. His comment on this stage is the thread that runs through the whole catalogue: "I've been trying and failing at this for a long time and that's a common theme here." **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

**Complementary context:** Elliott Hulse is a strength coach and YouTuber who was popular in the early 2010s for direct, motivational talking-head videos mixing fitness, masculinity and spirituality. Koe lists him among the fitness creators of that era whose style he imitated.

**Failed business 3: photography and digital art.** In his sophomore year he bought a camera for YouTube, stopped making the videos and picked up photography (street, landscape and portrait) from YouTube tutorials. He did not care much for photography itself. What he cared about was the editing, which sent him "down a Photoshop rabbit hole." He would spend six to eight hours in his room, in a flow state with music, creating surreal composites from his own photos and stock images. He stopped because he did not know how to monetize it. The lesson he draws is the central claim of build to learn: "how in the world could I know that I was very passionate and interested in editing as opposed to say photography without actually diving into photography first?" Certain things have to be tried before you can find what you are truly interested in. He adds a retrospective observation: with the marketing, sales and course-building knowledge he has now, he could build a course teaching composites, and "is that not the dream": doing what you love and teaching it to people who are interested in it. It is, he says, a matter of supply and demand, of interest. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

The burnout version of the same episode appears in 2025. He gained some followers with digital art but burned out trying to post one edit a day: he "couldn't handle taking Adderall every morning and busting out 6 hours staring at Photoshop." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md. The two tellings complement each other. The 2022 version emphasizes the discovery (editing, not photography) and the missing skill (monetization). The 2025 version emphasizes the unsustainable method (a daily output quota maintained with a stimulant).

**Failed business 4 (in the 2022 sequence): a Facebook ads agency.** The 2022 video and later summaries list a Facebook ads agency among the failed ventures, but the corpus preserves no separate account of what happened in it, beyond the fact that the Facebook ads knowledge he gained resurfaces in the stores that followed.

**Failed business 5: a dropshipping rave clothing store.** He was into EDM and festivals and knew that audience "like the back of my hand": what people wore and how events were promoted. The business lesson is the one later formalized in Chapter 19: to market accurately to an audience, immerse yourself in it. If you do not go to raves and want to sell to ravers, he has a "leg up," because "I am the market research at that point." He took a dropshipping course (branding, store setup, shipping from China) and used what he knew about Facebook ads. One product was a diamond-studded flashy bra. He sold nothing. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

The coexistence of a correct insight and a total commercial failure is instructive. Knowing the market did not compensate for the absence of other skills (product quality, shipping, promotion, copy). "I am the market research" is a necessary condition in Koe's system, not a sufficient one.

**Failed business 6: blue light glasses for developers.** By then he was immersed in another market: he was studying programming and knew that developers are passionate and stare at screens all day, so a brand of blue light glasses made for them made sense. He called his father to ask for a few thousand dollars, and to his surprise his father said yes. It was the first time he had made an argument and an idea convincing enough for someone to invest in it, and because his father is not wealthy, "it meant the world." He ordered product, set up the store and the ads, and studied influencer marketing (his first ad featured his hedgehog, Momo, who has since died). He "blew" the $3,000: shipping times were long, packaging was a problem, and he was promoting without much money. It did not work, and it produced another low point ("why can I not make this work?"). He had to call his father and tell him he had wasted his money. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

In a 2024 retelling he adds what went wrong strategically. He was following the common advice to "pick a niche, get very specific about your customer avatar," and targeted developers at a time when blue light glasses were being marketed to everyone. "I wanted to market to developers and I thought I could pull that off but I couldn't just because I was unskilled." **Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md. The diagnosis is not that niching is wrong, but that the advice assumed a level of marketing skill he did not yet have.

In a 2023 talk he tells the emotional side of the same episode more starkly. He failed at seven businesses (freelancing, dropshipping, agency work, e-commerce brands, "all of that") with minimal results, went through "mental turmoil," and ended up crying on the phone to his father, asking him not to ruin his life, because he had borrowed and lost "however many thousand dollars" to order product for an e-commerce store. **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md.

**The agency job and failed business 7: the wallet brand.** His father suggested he use his web development skills for a better-paying job. He got a job at a web design agency, for what he describes in 2022 as about $60,000 to $70,000 a year, "a lot for a college student," and he dropped out of college to spend his free time building businesses. He started a second e-commerce brand, minimalist leather wallets. He ordered them, got professional photos, "did everything right," but "when it came time to promote I just... I just didn't do it." Out of fear "or something," he gave up. In the same passage he mentions failing at a content marketing agency and an SEO agency. **Source:** 7 Lessons From 7 Failed Online Businesses (And Making 7 Figures).md.

The wallet brand is the only failure in the catalogue that did not fail in the market. It failed before the market, at the moment of exposure. It belongs with the material on fear of judgment in earlier chapters: the work that could have produced feedback was finished, and the one action that would have produced it was the one he avoided.

**What later versions add.** In a 2024 video he describes his first real job as a web designer at a marketing agency, building plug-and-play e-commerce sites for furniture and appliance retailers. Before that he had worked part-time at a print shop while living with seven other men in a six-room fraternity house converted into seven rooms, each paying $250 to $400 as "broke college kids." To the list he adds freelance web development, freelance SEO, a "drop servicing" business called a "2-hour agency" (pick a skill, create an offer, attract clients and outsource all the work to Fiverr; it failed "for obvious reasons," and it is the origin of the name of his later product "2 Hour Writer"), and an animated explainer video agency. "All failed." **Source:** How Smart Creators Will Grow An Audience From Zero In 2025.md.

In 2025 he gives a compressed financial version. He built three e-commerce stores (dropshipping rave clothes, minimalist wallets, blue light glasses), took Facebook ads courses, borrowed $2,000 from his father and accumulated a total of $8,000 of debt as a college junior, after a series of short-lived ventures (the three stores, an SEO agency, a content marketing agency, web design, Facebook ads). To keep rent at $200 to $300 a month he lived with seven roommates while working part-time: "a lot of stress." **Source:** How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md.

The courses deserve a separate mention because Koe uses them to make a point about responsibility. He bought courses on freelancing, Facebook ads, e-commerce brand building, copywriting, sales and client acquisition, plus "at least 15 web development courses that are irrelevant but noteworthy." He did not see much success with the business models those courses taught, "and that's on me not them, that's my execution problem and lack of... the distinction between knowledge and understanding." **Source:** Niche Down Is Terrible Advice (For Smart People).md. The distinction between knowledge and understanding, developed in Chapter 14, is here applied to himself: he had the information but not the embodied skill that only comes from building.

The variations between tellings are worth stating plainly, because a reader comparing videos will notice them.

#### How the failures became a foundation

Koe reads the catalogue in three ways, and each reading connects it to a principle developed elsewhere in the book.

The first reading is **awareness through failure**. After four or five years of trying seven different business models, "everything clicked." The principle he derives is that "you gain awareness from your failures": by diving in, you find things that would have led to success earlier but that you did not know existed. **Source:** If You Want To Secure Your Future… Follow This New Career Path.md (2022). Failure here is not a moral test. It is an information-gathering process, which is why the system insists on building as the method of learning.

The second reading is **the failures as a skill stack**. In 2025 he says he was in "beginner hell for almost 5 years," not the three to six months most creators go through, jumping between dropshipping, three e-commerce stores, digital art and photography, a Facebook ads agency and an SEO content agency, until he had to take a web design job "because the loans were piling up." At that job he procrastinated on his assigned work in order to do freelance web design. When he later started writing on social media to land clients, he did not experience beginner hell again, "because I knew how to avoid it": a select few skills learned across all those failures led to his own click. **Source:** Don't Quit - How To Get Ahead Of 99% Of Personal Brands.md. The term "beginner hell," which the book uses elsewhere for the phase of publishing into the void without growth, is applied here to his own five years. The point is that the skills were not wasted when the ventures failed. They were stored, and they combined later.

The third reading is **the click as architecture**. In 2023 he describes the moment the skills combined. After the turmoil and the call to his father, it hit him all at once: "this skill, this skill, this skill, these all piece together, I can see the structure now." He could see the image the puzzle formed and knew how to navigate "this new realm." He calls it an "avalanche called insight" that "crashed into my psyche," and summarizes the pattern with a phrase he uses often: "as I like to say, nothing happens then everything happens." He adds a crucial qualification: the click is not a single event. It solves only the current level and sets a new "level of mind." As you keep pursuing external goals, it happens again and again: "a click click click click click." **Source:** Most People Won't Change (How To Recreate Yourself).md.

"Nothing happens then everything happens" is one of Koe's coined phrases. In this passage it means that after a long period without visible results, previously separate pieces suddenly fit together. In other videos he renames it "slowly then all at once" and, later, compares it to a star that collapses and then explodes; the idea of invisible accumulation followed by a sudden jump stays the same. The "avalanche called insight" is his name for the moment in which he sees the structure his skills form. Both connect the biography to the model of levels of mind in Part II and Part XIV: each solved level of problems produces a new identity that can see problems it could not see before.

There is a fourth thread that runs through the catalogue without being stated as a lesson: **the market was always himself**. The rave store sold to the scene he lived in; the glasses sold to the developers he studied with; the composite course he imagines in retrospect would sell to people like the person he was. When the click came, the business he built sold to people like him the skills he had learned. This is the biographical root of "you are the niche."

#### Self-funded from near zero

Koe insists that he started without capital. He was raised in a middle-class family and never had money except what his father lent him, which he lost in a business that did not succeed anyway. Everything since has been self-funded or done with close to zero dollars: earning a little at a part-time job and then a full-time job, and investing in inexpensive technology and software (design software, email marketing software). He had to create his own substantial source of income before he could invest in larger projects such as his software startup or the publication of a book. **Source:** The Age Of The Generalist (How To Earn A Living Doing What You Enjoy).md (2024).

The claim matters to the argument of the system. If the one-person business required capital, the critique of the default path in Part I would offer an exit only to people who already had resources. Koe's insistence on his own lack of capital is meant to show that the exit is available to people in the position he was in, though the chapter should also note what his story does include: a father able and willing to lend a few thousand dollars, a scholarship-supported college education (mentioned in other videos), and a technical skill (web development) that produced a salary when the ventures failed. These are modest advantages, not wealth, but they are part of the case.

#### The numbers and how to read them

Koe says repeatedly that he dislikes talking about numbers and does it "for credibility." The figures are scattered across videos and years. Read together, they tell a story of growth, but they also show the problems of using a moving target as evidence.

**Credentials by type of offer.** In 2022 and again in 2024 he says he has been a freelancer, a coach and a consultant, and has done "every one-person business offer under the sun," selling more than $100,000 of almost everything he went into: freelancing, coaching, consulting, digital products and cohorts. He gives two reasons for speaking about the one-person business: direct experience, and knowing the steps well enough to see that people "overcomplicate it way too much." In the 2024 version he adds two things. First, self-reliance is "possibly the greatest value in my life." Second, when people "screamed" at him to go short-form (Reels) or to jump to the next business model, he already had something working and went in the opposite direction, starting long-form newsletters: "when people told me to go short form I started writing long-form newsletters." "Success is counterintuitive," he says, and he counts counterintuitiveness and skepticism among his values. **Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md (2022); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024).

"Success is counterintuitive" is another of his phrases. In the 2024 guide it has two senses: the principle that forcing himself to work less and build solutions brought him the most growth, and, in this passage, the practice of moving against what everyone recommends once you have evidence that your own approach works. It is not a license for contrarianism without evidence; in the anecdote he already "had something good going."

**Writing as the base of the income.** In August 2022 he said that he averaged $40,000 to $60,000 a month from his writing, in about two hours of writing a day. He works longer than that, because he builds side projects, but if he stripped away everything except Twitter, his newsletter and his Twitter threads he would keep 80 to 90% of his income, since he does not really promote anywhere else and his YouTube and Instagram are based on what he has already written. In the 2024 compilation where the same figure reappears, he adds that he is "not a writer" and has no English degree, and that you do not need one: the world wants good, authentic, impactful writers. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Thrive In The Next 10 Years.md (2022); The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024).

Whether Koe calls himself a writer is itself an unresolved point in the corpus. He says "I'm not a writer" in this passage, describes himself elsewhere as "a writer and brand adviser" (2025, 2026), and says as late as July 2026 "I still don't call myself a writer." The contradiction may depend on whether he is referring to his professional identity or to the stereotype of the academically trained writer; he does not explain it. **Source:** How To Make Money As A Creative On The Internet.md (2022-06-25); How To Build A Better Personal Brand Than 99% Of People.md (2025-06-08); If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md (2026-06-13); The Writing System That Saved My Brain (Learn Faster & Think Clearly).md (2026-07-27).

**Monthly and annual figures.** In November 2022, recounting what people say to him when they do not understand what he does, he quotes: "oh so you're a YouTuber but you make a hundred thousand dollars a month." **Source:** 11 Lessons From Growing A 7-Figure One Person Business.md. In April and May 2023 he said that his "little one-person business" made $800,000 in 2022, had made $1.5 million so far in 2023, and was projected to reach about $2.5 million (he called it a projection and promised to be transparent about the actual figure). All of it came from selling information products and education, because "some of the most valuable things there are is what's in your head and how you operate within the world." From that revenue he was writing a book and building software, projects with meaning for him, and from a place of less stress about money he could "strategize and visualize the impact I want to leave on the world": "it's creating for fun, work has turned into play." **Source:** Solve Your Own Problems And Sell The Solution (Your Niche Is You).md. In a video opened with the statistic that "the average writer makes sixty nine thousand five hundred and ten dollars a year," he said he made $800,000 in 2022 "writing for two hours a day and just a few days a week," then clarified that he works more than that, but writing is "the base of my business, everything I do revolves around writing, and I do that for around two hours every single day." **Source:** I Made $800,000 In One Year Writing 2 Hours A Day.md (2023). The same passage contains both "a few days a week" and "every single day," a small internal inconsistency.

In August 2023 he said he had become "a liquid millionaire at 26," until he invested 50% of it into a new business, and that he had generated $2.5 to 3.5 million in total as a one-person business, which he was "now evolving past." He shared this in response to an Instagram post claiming that every millionaire under 30 got there through inheritance, a high income or a large income event such as crypto; the person sharing it, around 35, had been budgeting for ten years and was halfway to a million. Koe agrees that generating wealth is slow, but "not that slow." He shares his figures for perspective, remembering that he himself was inspired when he heard of someone doing what he did reaching a first $100,000 month. Nobody could have taught him to do this, he says, and the lesson is that such advice makes you believe there is no new path to forge and no education or skill that lets you surpass average, self-imposed limits; but technological change makes this a "beautiful time," and "you may have been right 10 years ago but now there are many case studies arguing against that." **Source:** Asking For Advice Is Keeping You Unsuccessful (Here's Why).md.

In January 2024 he said he had sustained $100,000 to $250,000 a month, fluctuating with product launches and content performance up to $500,000 to $750,000 a month, and he specified that this is revenue, not profit. He produces it by writing tweets, newsletters and threads, repurposed into Instagram, YouTube and LinkedIn. "Writing is the force multiplier," he says, the thing that makes him pay twenty times his previous web design salary in taxes every year ("a good problem to have"). It is not all or nothing: if he stripped everything except X and the newsletter, a large part would remain. And money does not jump from zero to a million: you go from 0 to $10,000, to $20,000, to more, and you can make life-changing money in one to three years, "however long it takes for things to click" and to internalize "the skill of making money, which houses many other skills." **Source:** This High-Income Skill Will Make You Irreplaceable (Full Guide).md.

Two coined expressions appear in that passage. "Force multiplier" is used by Koe here for writing as the skill that multiplies every other part of the business; in another 2024 video he uses the same phrase, taken from Sam Altman, for focus as what multiplies work. "The skill of making money" is his name for a meta-skill that contains many others (writing, marketing, selling, building a product) and takes one to three years to internalize.

In April 2024 he said: "Two years ago I have a video where I made $800,000 writing 2 hours every day; last year I was doing the same exact thing but I made 4.1 million." The transcript says "last year or two years ago," so the timeline is slightly ambiguous. **Source:** A Day In The Life Of Dan Koe (My Routine As A Writer & Founder).md.

In February 2025 he gave the only complete series, by year of building products: about $10,000 the first year, about $100,000 the second, then $150,000, then $800,000, then about $4 million, and "last year" about $2 million "because revenue split between myself and Cortex." He frames it as anything but "get rich quick mumbo jumbo": it is the result of learning product marketing and promotion, skills any business owner has to learn. **Source:** A Full Guide To Making Your First Profitable Product (Beginners, Take Notes).md.

**The arithmetic.** In mid-2024 he explained how he earns a few million a year working two to four hours a day. Over five years he grew to millions of followers, which means traffic. People love to buy things, and some love to increase their value and learn skills; they are like him: "I am the niche, you are the niche." He sells to people like himself by teaching skills he learned, under his own brand. When he sells an ebook, a course or a template, he is usually selling something he already bought from someone else, but created his way. Millions of followers, times a product of $150 or more, times the fraction who buy, equals millions. His rough figures: 10 to 20 million impressions a month across platforms, converting a fraction to a $150 product or his $28 book. That, he says, is what happens when an entrepreneur controls customer acquisition, the product and the skills in between (marketing, sales, operations). He closes with a question for the viewer: how long will it take you to learn social media well enough to generate impressions, send them to a landing page, and offer a product with a buy button? **Source:** Change Your Life In 365 Hours (The New Rich Focus On These Tasks Daily).md.

What the figures are offered to prove is narrower than the figures themselves. They are not proof that anyone will earn the same amounts. They are offered as evidence for three specific claims: that a business built on writing and information products can reach seven figures without a team; that growth is gradual (0 to $10,000 to $20,000) rather than a single jump; and that the mechanism is understandable (traffic times product times conversion). The reader should take them as a case, not as a forecast.

#### The books: The Art of Focus and after

The book was Koe's first very long-term project, and its history shows his working method at a larger scale than a newsletter.

In a conversation with Dickie Bush in March 2023, Koe described the "intense season" that produced the draft. It began in Texas. He was still dabbling in fitness content but no longer enjoying it, and he realized that to produce the value needed to make an impact in fitness he would have to make it his whole brand; meanwhile he had "this unique frame of the one-person business that I can take a long way." That was when his program Digital Economics was born, and 2 Hour Writer was broken off from it. That clarity carried over to the book. He reread the first draft he had written two years earlier and reacted with embarrassment ("this is dog... I can't believe I thought this was good writing two years ago"), re-outlined it, filled it in again, and in about 30 days wrote a new first draft of 230 pages, which was then with an editor. He clarifies that the "30 days" was a culmination: about six months writing a very first draft, another six months re-outlining and picking up ideas, a process of about two years. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

The same conversation shows him describing his working life in terms of seasons, a concept developed in Chapter 39. At that moment the book was "on the come down" and the software "on the come up": developers were building it while he pieced together marketing, outreach, content and positioning. He was in a "middle phase," sustaining projects and knowing that an intense season (the software launch or the book promotion) was coming: "I don't know what it is that's going to cause that click." **Source:** same. The word "click" connects this passage to the avalanche of insight described above: the intense phases of his life start when pieces fit, and he does not try to predict exactly when that will happen.

He wanted the launch to be special. The first 1,000 copies would have custom covers with silver foil; a purple-foil copy for Joey, who had been helping him; engraved copies for Dickie, Justin and others: "little minor touches." He was grateful to be at a point where "I can treat this one as a passion project... I want to have fun with this without expectation." He did not know the launch date: one person had done eight revisions, his own first revision took about a week per chapter or two, he hoped for the third or fourth quarter of 2023, and "I'm learning on the go... I don't even know what the publishing process looks like yet." **Source:** same. "Passion project" is an ordinary phrase, but in this passage it has a precise sense: a project done for its own sake, financed by a business that no longer depends on it, and released without commercial expectations. That freedom is a consequence of the income described above.

The book's calendar moved. In March 2023 he hoped for the third or fourth quarter; in June 2023 he said it was finished and would go on pre-sale soon; in August 2023 he planned to relaunch his planner together with the book at the end of September. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03); If You’re Lost - Here’s How You Find Clarity For The Future.md (2023-06); I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md (2023-08).

In February 2024 he described what the book is and is not. Discussing the estimate that humans can consciously process about 50 bits of information per second, "approximately 125 billion bits in their lifetime" (a figure he presents as approximate), he mentions that he treats it in The Art of Focus, which he calls "a philosophy for working less, earning more and enjoying life. Focus is only one part of it, and focus is so much more than just deep work. There's one chapter on deep work in the book; the rest is the holistic philosophy for living a better life, doing what you want, creating your own career, not being assigned one... and how to navigate that mentally and build that mental strength and emotional resilience." **Source:** Disappear For 2-4 Hours A Day (The Millionaire Productivity Routine).md.

**Complementary context:** the figure of about 50 bits per second for conscious processing is commonly associated with Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's discussion of the limits of attention in Flow, where he draws on earlier information-theory estimates of how many bits per second consciousness can handle. The published estimates vary by source and are rough orders of magnitude (Csikszentmihalyi's own figures are somewhat higher than 50), so the number is best read as an illustration of a hard limit on attention rather than as a measurement. Koe himself presents his figure as approximate.

#### What sets his work apart

Koe offers three explanations of why his work stands out, and each comes from a different angle.

The first is **re-meaning an existing term**. "I didn't come up with the concept of one person business, but I took it and I gave it an entirely new meaning." Before, the phrase simply meant operating a business as one person with internet technology. He gave it the sense the book developed in Part X: productize yourself, self-monetization, turning yourself into the brand, truly doing what you love for a living, backed by systems that get the result. He also names processes that appear elsewhere in his videos, such as "intelligent imitation," "tactical stress" and "the unknown" (the last is not defined in this passage; Chapters 1, 10, 14 and 39 develop what he means by the others and by exploring the unknown). The source of this naming ability, he says, is the application of his interests (spirituality, philosophy, neuroscience) to his expertise (online business, marketing, branding). With enough exposure across those domains, "because I'm not a specialist," he notices common patterns and pieces together long-form content that is difficult to replicate. **Source:** The One-Person Business Model (How To Productize Yourself Full Guide).md (2024). This is a self-description of the generalist synthesizer of Chapter 20: the differentiation comes from the intersection, not from depth in a single field.

The second is **depth and meaning as the reason people follow him**. From reading DMs and emails he narrowed down why people watch his content. He talks about business, lifestyle design, productivity and writing, but tries to make it "a bit deeper, less superficial." "I want my work, and therefore your work, to have meaning," and he wants people to feel a sense of control over their lives. He teaches what he has achieved and what he wants to achieve, and people resonate with those specific goals. **Source:** 4 Frameworks To Write Follow-Worthy Content (+ Kortex Template).md (2024). This is the audience-side version of the claim that your goals are your niche: people follow a person heading somewhere they also want to go.

The third is **a goal that changed its nature over time**. Koe says he always had the goal of being a "deep, free and original thinker," but at first it was not a conscious goal. "As the self-actualization process goes," it began as an unconscious and shallow goal driven by the need for acceptance: he wanted to sound smart, intellectual and deep, and to be accepted by others "in a non-mediocre way," to be different. He did not really care about being a deep thinker. "With time and after burning through that Karma I realized that becoming a deep thinker is more than that, it is the key to creative success," or to success in any endeavor. If you want original success, or to open a new path for your own life and for others to follow, you have to prioritize deep, free and original thinking. **Source:** The Matrix Is Real (How To Break Free With Your Mind).md (2022).

The arc is a small illustration of a pattern the book describes in Part II and Part XIV: a goal can start as an ego-driven, unconscious strategy for belonging and become, after it has been lived through, a conscious value. "Burning through that Karma" is his metaphor for that process; the source does not define the word "Karma" further, and it is best read as "living through the consequences of the shallow motivation until it is exhausted," not as a doctrinal claim. The episode parallels the "started a business so I could fit in" anecdote above. In both cases a motive that was about acceptance became, through practice, something he endorses for its own sake.

#### Guest stories as counterpoints

Three guest stories in the corpus are useful precisely because they are not Koe's. They show the same principles operating in different trajectories, and they introduce the people whose frameworks occupy the second half of this chapter.

**Justin Welsh: from a scaled company to one person.** Introducing Welsh in 2021, Koe describes him as someone who "over the last decade has helped build two $50 million annual recurring revenue companies, teams of 150 plus people, and has raised over 300 million in venture capital. That was until he burned out." Then "he saw the opportunity in the attention economy" and started a one-person business. At the time of recording, his income streams were advising early-stage SaaS companies in healthcare technology, a private community for online creators, two digital products for creators, and a one-on-one coaching business. Welsh adds that he spent five years as an early employee at a unicorn SaaS company (Zocdoc) and then became chief revenue officer at PatientPop, after about ten years in high-growth SaaS. **Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md.

**Complementary context:** "annual recurring revenue" (ARR) is the yearly value of a company's subscription revenue, the standard size measure for software-as-a-service (SaaS) businesses. A "unicorn" is a private startup valued at over a billion dollars.

Welsh's path is the inverse of Koe's. Koe went from one person to a team; Welsh went from large teams to one person. Together they show that the one-person business is not a stage everyone passes through in one direction. It is a structure chosen for what it gives (autonomy, profit margin, freedom from managing people) and left for what it cannot give (scale, challenge, a product that requires many hands).

**John Hugh: authenticity before polish.** John Hugh, co-founder of Stan, made his first $10,000 of passive income from a file he did not change. He posted a video that went viral unintentionally: he green-screened his old résumé from a junior internship (he had to recover his Dropbox password to find it) and said, "these are literally the bullets I did." The very first version of Stan was built for his own account. He uploaded that same file without changing anything ("the 2016 .docx or whatever it is") and sold it for $10. "That's how I made my first $10,000 of passive income." Why it worked: "it was authentic to what my audience wanted, which was help on their resumes, and they wanted a template." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md. The case is a compressed version of the minimum viable product argument of Chapter 29: the product was not designed; it was found in what the audience already asked for.

**Koe's onboarding to Stan: what a one-person operation looks like from outside.** The founders of Stan also describe working with Koe. Vitali, who says he spends "70% of my time thinking about hiring," calls onboarding Koe his best experience with a creator of that caliber, in part because Koe's content operation is just Koe. With creators of that size there is usually bureaucracy ("talk to my assistant... review... hand off... take a month"). With Koe they connected on a Thursday or Friday, shared ideas through Loom videos, and on Monday Vitali logged in to find "three products, $10,000, like everything is firing." When Koe wanted Loom videos embedded in his course, John relayed the request, and Vitali called an engineer: "I need this in 2 hours." It shipped the same day. John's comment is the point of the story: this "speaks towards the agility of an organization we would like to keep as we continue to scale," now at forty-some people and heading toward a hundred-some; "it's a choice to operate that way." **Source:** same.

The anecdote is the hinge between the two halves of this chapter. From the outside, the one-person business looks like speed: one decision-maker, no handoffs, a weekend from idea to revenue. The founders of Stan admire that speed and say that preserving it while growing is a deliberate choice, not something that happens on its own. The rest of the chapter is, in large part, about why that is so hard.

### 37.2 Beyond the One-Person Business

Throughout Part X the one-person business appeared as the recommended starting point: low cost, high margin, built on writing and information products, run in a few hours a day. This section shows that, in Koe's own framework, it was always a stage rather than a ceiling, and then follows him as he left it. The reasons he gives for leaving are as instructive as the reasons he gave for starting, and his first lessons as a founder already show which principles survive the transition and which do not.

#### A stage, not a ceiling

In a 2023 roadmap for one-person businesses, Koe lists a fifth and final step: **branch into new opportunity**. As you create and sell products, build your audience and community and distribute your philosophy in a creator economy that, in his view, "cannot be saturated," you will eventually have enough resources to pursue bigger goals and "desaturate the market," while still being on social media, leaving your legacy and checking in. He cites Hamza (a self-improvement creator) as someone who had recently done this, even if he may be starting YouTube-related projects and selling something related, which Koe calls "a good pivot." The horizon he recommends is long: he thinks in terms of ten to twenty years. **Source:** The Future Of One-Person Businesses (Take Advantage Now).md.

"Branch into new opportunity" and "desaturate the market" are his names for this step. The second phrase is slightly paradoxical, since he also says the creator economy cannot be saturated. The source does not reconcile the two, but the most likely sense is that, once you have resources, you can take on problems in markets that are not crowded with other creators selling information products, using the audience as a distribution advantage.

In 2025 he gives the same idea a structure, which he calls **levels of uncertainty**. In business, uncertainty depends on the level. Level one is a local business, agency work, freelancing or information products: uncertain, but simple, with "a cap of about 1 to 5 million a year" before you have to raise the stakes by hiring a team or expanding into software or physical products. He makes a parallel scale for investing: level one is a certain retirement account (a 401k), level two the stock market (somewhat predictable, probably higher returns), level three businesses, crypto or other uncertain assets (the highest potential returns, but requiring that you navigate and understand the risk). The rule that follows is to hedge against risk, especially when you are starting to change your life: "you punch just above your weight," moving from level one to two to three rather than accepting a challenge you cannot handle. If you take on a level-three challenge anyway, hedge it by changing your environment: move into a cheap apartment and "live on beans and rice for a year." And he adds the warning that connects this framework to the critique of the default path in Chapter 2: "The more responsibilities that you adopt before you've had a chance to take risks in your youth, the more difficult it is to justify changing your life. That's the horrible trap of the default path." **Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md.

| Level | Business | Investing | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Local business, agency, freelancing, information products (cap of about $1–5M a year) | A 401k-type retirement account | Uncertain but simple |
| 2 | (Implied: the step up, hiring a team or adding software or physical products) | The stock market | Somewhat predictable, more return |
| 3 | Software, physical products, larger businesses | Businesses, crypto, other uncertain assets | Highest returns, requires understanding risk |

The source names levels one and three explicitly for business and all three for investing; the business entry for level two in the table is an inference from his statement that level-one businesses reach a cap "before you must raise stakes by hiring a team or expanding to software or physical products."

The framework explains Koe's own path in his own terms. His information-product business was a level-one business that reached the upper part of its cap; Cortex and Eden were the step up. It also carries a tension with something he says elsewhere. In another 2025 video he recommends moving into an apartment you cannot quite afford as a deliberate "glitch," a form of tactical stress that forces you to rise to the new cost. Here he recommends the opposite, a cheap apartment, as a hedge. The two pieces of advice can be reconciled by context (tactical stress for someone confident in their skill and needing pressure; hedging for someone taking a level-three risk), but Koe does not reconcile them explicitly. **Source:** 12 Rules To Change Your Life In 12 Months.md (2025-09-14); A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md (2025-10-19).

**Complementary context:** a 401(k) is the main employer-sponsored retirement savings plan in the United States, usually invested in diversified funds; Koe uses it as the example of the most certain, lowest-return option.

A different way of going beyond the one-person business came from Justin Welsh, as early as 2021. Asked whether business is moving toward one-person operations, Welsh says "I don't know that I see things moving more towards that." He compares what one-person businesses might do to DAOs, while admitting "I'm not a web3, NFT, crypto guy." He has seen people on Twitter build "product studios," "studios that create product nonstop, and they rely on people with heavy distribution to go out and deploy those products." His idea is the mirror image: "I want to create a distribution studio." Creators with large followings would bundle together and say to a company: "you want to take a SaaS platform to market?... an info product... a community? Collectively, we do 500 million in impressions every year. Like what's that worth to you? What are you willing to give away for that?" If one-person businesses "pair together to really improve their power, then they can start to own... a small percentage of a lot of businesses." **Source:** Justin Welsh Shows You How To Start & Grow A One-Person Business.md.

The distribution studio is Welsh's concept, not Koe's, and it does not reappear in Koe's later videos. It is included here because it shows an alternative to the route Koe took. Koe used his distribution to finance and launch a company he would operate himself. Welsh imagined distribution as an asset to be exchanged for ownership in companies that others operate, which keeps each creator a one-person business while giving them exposure to larger outcomes.

#### Building beyond the digital

The second way past the information-product model is to make physical things. In 2022 Koe insists that products need not be digital or courses. He talks about self-development and focus a lot, drinks coffee, and wants to create a coffee brand with L-theanine. If he talks about it more and shows himself using it, people will buy it, because it is good and useful in their lives: "if I like coffee and I use it to focus then I'm going to create an l-theanine coffee brand and sell it to my audience while talking about productivity and focus. It is that simple, given time and not quitting because you didn't see results after two weeks." **Source:** If You Are High Value, Start A One-Person Business.md.

**Complementary context:** L-theanine is an amino acid found in tea that is often combined with caffeine in supplements marketed for calm focus. Koe's example depends on the product fitting what he already talks about, not on any specific claim about its effects.

The example is an application of the logic of Chapter 19: if your interests are your niche, the products you sell can follow your interests across categories, as long as the audience sees you use them. The qualification at the end ("given time and not quitting... after two weeks") is the same warning about patience that runs through his advice on content.

In the 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush, the idea grows into a physical place. Bush says: "the whole point of building any kind of business online is to eventually have some kind of in-person manifestation of that community, and make that a byproduct of the type of person you attract." Both had arrived at the idea independently, probably because of fitness YouTubers who opened gyms. Financially they could start already, but Bush says he is not ready in terms of skill: he wants to be at the stage of "managing more than doing" on the business side. He calls the concept **the shipyard**: a gym, a co-working café, a large reception area that could be cleared to hold about fifty people for a presentation, a healthy steak-bowl-style restaurant, office space, perhaps a mini hotel as the last level, and then expansion or larger versions. His model is Christian Guzman, his favorite fitness YouTuber, who started a home gym and wants to expand. His rationale comes from Ship 30 for 30, his writing program: people who write every day start improving every other area of their life (they take fitness seriously, they see business potential), so it makes sense to create a place where that kind of person can gather. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

Koe's version, which he calls a **mastery facility**, starts from the problems of where he lived at the time: no good gym, no good coffee shop, no place to eat "the bro stuff" (steak, white rice, seasonings) at an everyday price. He imagines an espresso bar, co-working space, and a build-your-own restaurant, "Chipotle but with good stuff, no seed oils." "The Mastery facility is a physical synthesis." **Source:** same. The shipyard is Bush's idea and name; "mastery facility" and "physical synthesis" are Koe's reformulation. "Synthesis" here has the sense it has in his writing on generalists (Chapter 20): combining separate interests (training, nutrition, focused work, community) into a single coherent thing.

The same conversation contains a near miss that reveals one of Koe's decision rules. He was "very close to just dropping like 150 to 200k on like gym equipment and finding a place." Then he reflected: "this sounds fun, but I'm not... bought into it 100%... I feel like I'm forcing my hand as to what's next." So he sat it out, and the software he later built "fits the puzzle piece to perfection." **Source:** same. The heuristic implied, which he names "forcing my hand," is: do not force the next chapter of your life; wait until the piece that fits appears. The puzzle metaphor is the same one he used for the click that ended his years of failure. In both cases the right move is recognized by fit, not chosen by willpower.

The fitness thread has a later development. In 2023 he had stopped making fitness content because he would have had to make it his entire brand. In October 2025 he launched Superhuman 90, a training, nutrition and detox product, after finding a way to integrate it without the "beast mode" aesthetic; he says the obsession with health was always there, but he did not know how to fit it into a productivity and business brand. **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md (2023-03); A Full Guide To Reinvent Your Entire Life (In 6-12 Months).md (2025-10). The mastery facility has not, in the corpus, been built.

#### Why he built Cortex

Koe's account of why he left the one-person model combines a constraint, a financial strategy, a temperament and a philosophy of growth.

**The constraint.** In January 2025 he admitted: "hiring was the scariest thing for me." The "main reason" he was "the one person business guy for a decent amount of time on YouTube" was that "I just didn't know how the [expletive] to hire." Fiverr and Upwork would probably not get him anywhere for big tasks. "That was my constraint, and I think it helped me a lot, because I put it off for the longest amount of time." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md. The confession reframes the earlier biography. The model he taught was, in part, the shape of a limitation he had not yet overcome, and he regards the limitation as productive: by not hiring he was forced to develop systems and leverage that did not depend on other people.

**The financial path.** He stayed a one-person business until he reached $50,000 a month, then hired an editor who also handles support, so that "technically I'm still a two-person business under my personal brand." Cortex, which he was then starting, was fueled by much of the money he had made, so "we don't have to take on VC or investors or any kind of external funding to build it," and they were free to build it how they wanted. **Source:** Build A One-Person Business As A Normal Person (From $0 To $10K).md (2024). In another 2024 video he insists that he did not "just start building Cortex." They took no investment because he first built an audience and a cash flow business, "and you can honestly be happy with that." He knows people who are leaving big businesses to start one-person businesses in which "they're making less revenue, they're making a lot more profit." In a one-person business you control how much you work: write content for thirty minutes to an hour a day to build the audience, run a newsletter, build a product once. You could reduce your work to one or two hours of writing in the morning, but many people do not find meaning in that repetition, and that is why he built Cortex: more challenge, more improvement, more evolution. He still recommends the one-person business as the starting point for most people without money, after which the audience helps the startup succeed. **Source:** Minimalist Productivity Work Less, Do More.md.

"Cash flow business" is a common term, used here in a specific sense: a profitable one-person business whose surplus finances a riskier venture without outside investors. His own resource ladder, as he described it in 2026, makes the sequence explicit: "The way that I went about that is social media. I knew that it was free to access. I didn't need any money to start the business. I didn't need any money to create an info product. I didn't need any money to write a book, and then that allowed me to acquire the resource of money so that I could invest into a bigger business that I wanted to build, which is Eden, the software business. And eventually, soon, Saunder, the nootropic business" (the name of the last company is given as transcribed). **Source:** The Art of Strategic Thinking (How to Become Wildly Successful at Anything You Do).md.

| Rung | What it required | What it produced |
|---|---|---|
| Social media | No money; time and skill | Attention, audience |
| Information product and book | No money | Revenue; proof that the audience trusts him |
| Money | (The output of the previous rungs) | The ability to fund larger projects without investors |
| Eden (software) | Capital and a team | A company beyond the one-person model |
| A nootropics business | Capital, a team, physical production | (Planned at the time of the video) |

The ladder is the levels-of-uncertainty framework seen from the inside: each rung is paid for by the one below it, so risk is taken only with resources already earned. It also explains why he recommends the same sequence to others.

**The temperament.** In May 2025 he said he could have stayed one person indefinitely, with written content on two or three platforms such as LinkedIn and Twitter, a newsletter and a product, working no more than two hours a day, perhaps four sometimes. "But that's not how I'm wired." He wants "the cycles and intensity to continue": "I like the 16-hour workdays, I don't like doing them for the entirety of my life." That is why he started Cortex and keeps building new products; it "comes naturally." **Source:** Harsh Truth You Don't Need To Grind 247 To Be Successful.md. The model of seasons and phases developed in Chapter 39 explains this: periods of intensity alternate with periods of maintenance, and a person who has reached stability in one area may need a new source of intensity elsewhere.

**The philosophy.** In February 2025 he described the alternative he rejected. He could have pulled back and "chilled," "but I'm young and I want to do more, and I like how personal development and business development are intertwined, and how business can be a vessel for personal growth for me." So he started Cortex (described at that time as a writing, note-taking and idea-capture desktop application, with a mobile app coming) and asked himself: "what's holding me back from building a team, why am I so focused on this one person business thing?" Building the company, he began "realizing my own faults": he needed to improve himself, provide for his team, and make sure the product sustained the team and benefited its users. **Source:** How To Thrive With Multiple Interests.md. "Vessel for personal growth" is his phrase for the business as a vehicle through which the person develops, an extension of the idea (Chapter 28) that a business can be a life's work rather than a means to an end. The startup exposed faults that the one-person business had never put under pressure.

**The challenge, and losing money as tuition.** The earliest version of this reasoning comes from the 2023 conversation with Dickie Bush. He is building software, though he "definitely couldn't do it by myself." "The challenge is what makes it fun": they have proven they can make anything work, so "it's not fear anymore, it's just like challenge." Labeled "the one-person business guy," he knows the software challenges his own beliefs: he will have to hire and manage a team, and he does not know whether he will like it. "I won't know if I like it or not if I don't do it." He will put money in, and if one day it does not belong in his life or causes too much suffering, he will get rid of it and lose a lot, "but that's... what you pay to learn." Life gets better, he says, because "you're actually building and allowing problems to come into your life so that you know what you don't want." **Source:** Dan Koe & Dickie Bush On One-Person Businesses, Creative Workflows, and Lifestyle Design.md.

This is the learning theory of Chapter 14 applied to a career decision: you cannot know whether you like managing people without managing people, and the cost of finding out is tuition. It is consistent with the catalogue of failures in Section 37.1, where each failure revealed an interest or a missing skill.

**The change of position.** Koe's attitude to the one-person model did not move in a straight line. In March 2023 he acknowledged that the software challenged his label. In June 2023, in "The One Person Business Model 2.0," he said the opposite: he had pushed too far into a business model he did not like, which showed him what he did not want and therefore what he did want. He was "actively trying to get back to being one person with a VA" (a small team is good), because above all he wanted to write, take on creative challenges through his writing, work on his health and maintain his routine, knowing that peeling back the business would reduce his income. **Source:** The One Person Business Model 2.0 (Turn Yourself Into A Business).md. From 2024 onward he was building a team and a startup, and by July 2025 he called himself "CEO of a small scrappy startup." **Source:** I'm 28. Here's How To Get Ahead Of Most 20 Year Olds.md (2025-07-27), and the 2024–2025 videos cited in this section.

The two positions are not simply contradictory. The June 2023 passage does not say which business model he disliked, and he may have been referring to a specific offer rather than to having a team at all. And his later position preserves the earlier one as a recommendation for others: the one-person business remains the starting point, and the startup is a later stage financed by it. What changed is his own placement on that path. He moved from defending the solo model as his identity to questioning his attachment to it, and he gives his reasons: he wanted cycles, intensity and challenge; he regarded the business as a vessel for growth; and his inability to hire, which had been a productive constraint, was itself something to grow past.

#### The bet on distribution

Koe went into software with a theory of why he would succeed where most software founders fail. In August 2023 he explained that he had three to four million followers built over four to five years, that he had sold products and services that changed the lives of the people he would now sell to, and that they understood his value and the power of his systems. Turning those systems into software would make their lives easier, and "the marketing strategy is already tested, validated, laid out." He had a course that cost $0 to create and made millions, and he was turning that course's system into software (which cost more money, but he now had it). "I already know the software is going to sell because it's sold before." Most developers, by contrast, lack an audience that trusts them, promotions that catch their target market's attention, a specific burning problem, a plan to raise problem awareness over time, the ability to create their own customers through content, and the "firepower" to write good content and ads and turn viewers into customers. **Source:** I Had To Learn These High-Income Skills (If I Wanted To Make Money).md.

A month later he described the strategy more concretely. The usual route is to build the product, test it and market it to a specific audience; "this works," but he wanted to "skip the sink or swim phase," aware that churn, developer costs and ingraining software into people's daily lives would be hard. The plan was to take the company to $5–10 million in its first year ("a lofty goal"): build the company's social media "like we would a personal brand, with an avatar as the face of the brand"; use that audience plus "a plethora of creator audiences" to fuel a cohort-based course on the subject of the software; let that education product, operating at 95% margins, drive revenue in year one, so that it would not matter whether the software itself was profitable; and then "play long term and slowly inject it into the creator economy as the one true software." **Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md (2023). In December 2023 he announced Cortex University, an expensive cohort-based program to help people build careers as "synthesizers," in which students would get "version zero" of the Cortex software before the public. **Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md.

In September 2024 he was confident in a different way. Cortex, he said, "isn't anything revolutionary or new; it's just a bit better" than products that people had grown tired of (he mentions Notion and Obsidian). Even if they did not reach Notion's level, which he was "fairly confident" they could in five to ten years, they had a fraction of the team size and costs, took no VC money, and could pivot and move fast; "even if we fail catastrophically, I think the failure landing point for something like Cortex would be at least eight figures annual." **Source:** The Fastest Way To Build A One-Person Business (Beginner Guide).md.

These passages should be read alongside what happened next, which Sections 37.3 and 37.4 reconstruct. In November 2025 Koe and his co-founders called Kortex "the $3 million mistake": the product was not compelling, Koe had spent too long justifying it, and they were "forced" to stop and rebuild it as Eden. The theory in the 2023 passages was that distribution and an education product could carry a software company through its weakest phase. The 2025 diagnosis was that the central problem had been the product and its fit with the market, which distribution cannot solve. The corpus does not say whether the cohort-course strategy was carried out as planned, nor does it reconcile the "eight-figure failure floor" with the later verdict. **Source:** Micro Education Businesses Are The Future & How To Start With Zero Dollars.md (2023-09-17); Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md (2025-11-24). The contrast is the clearest example in this chapter of a principle from the one-person business (an audience and a validated system sell anything built on them) that did not transfer one to one to a different kind of business.

#### From Cortex to Eden

The product itself changed name and definition several times. Cortex was first presented in 2023 as "second brain software," with a version zero for students of Cortex University. **Source:** The Future Of Work Is Play (How To Create A Digital Career).md. In 2024 it was "a second brain" for writers and creators, a bit better than Notion or Obsidian. In 2025 the company spelled it Kortex. After the rebuild described in Section 37.4, it became Eden.

In January 2026 Eden, still behind a waitlist, was described as an **AI canvas and drive**: you add any files, paste links (for example a YouTube video, which Eden downloads and transcribes) so that they can be searched and referenced with AI, drag them onto a canvas, connect them to AI chats, and work "in this unique and different way." The next waitlist round was planned for late January, followed by preparation for a public launch. **Source:** How I'd build a one-person business (if I started over in 2026).md.

In the same video Koe describes how he used AI to build Eden's help center, and the process is worth reconstructing because it is one of the few detailed accounts of his own operational work as a founder. The previous month he had AI write about thirty articles in two hours. The steps were these:

1. In Claude, he asked the model to research how large companies such as Notion and Miro structure their help centers, and, given information about his company and its features, to propose a list of every article Eden would need.
2. The model produced the list and offered to write the articles. He said yes, "but ask clarifying questions along the way so that they're accurate. Do not make assumptions."
3. He spent an hour or two answering its questions, and all the articles came out.
4. For each new feature, the process repeats: he describes the feature and asks for clarifying questions; the model pulls from the existing articles and proposes an outline; he approves it; the model fills it in; he reads the result for accuracy.

His reasoning for each choice is explicit. Writing thirty decent articles by hand would be long and mind-numbing, whereas answering an AI's questions "is kind of like a game." The instruction to ask questions rather than assume is what makes the output accurate. And to critics who say AI makes mistakes: "Just read through and correct the mistakes. And that still saves you a ton of time." The help center then serves as a knowledge base, so that support agents, human or future AI, can query it instead of reading all thirty articles. The company uses Intercom, whose AI agent Fin answers redundant questions from the knowledge base before a human agent is needed, in cases where no human in the loop is required. **Source:** same.

This is the method of Chapter 36 (the human as director of AI, not as a typist) applied to a founder's task. It also sits in interesting tension with a view the Stan founders express in Section 37.5, where Vitali dismisses AI support as solving for customers who ask questions that "customers that matter to you" do not ask. Koe's use is narrower than what Vitali criticizes: Fin handles redundant questions before a human, it does not replace the human.

By June 2026 the product had been redefined again. Koe says he built Eden "because it makes it nearly impossible to not generate ideas worth writing about, and then it guides you through actually writing them. It's your AI content strategist" (what they are building it to be). It researches what is popular in your niche or interest, understands who you are, your voice and your existing content, and synthesizes the two into daily ideas "that you can take, make your own, and run with." It is also an "outlier tool," replacing the tools that content strategists for large creators use; an "identities" feature extracts your voice through a guided conversation. They were working with the content teams of large creators, and one user reported that two posts based on Eden-sourced ideas, written "in their own voice" and posted the same day, got three million views each. **Source:** If You Have Multiple Interests, Start A One-Person Business.md. A few weeks later he added that Eden now had scheduling for all major platforms, completing the content workflow: research outliers on any platform, reverse engineer them and create from them; ask the AI chat to search for the top carousels, thumbnails or posts on any topic or creator; use it from Claude, with what he describes as the unique ability to connect Claude to any creator, topic or post. The company had pivoted "from Cortex to Eden to this Eden"; perception was now trending up after much work, and a mobile app had arrived "after three freaking years." **Source:** How To Become So Valuable AI Can Never Replace You.md (2026).

These passages are promotional, and the user result is anecdotal. The method underneath them, however, is the one the book developed in Chapters 22 to 24: research what already performs, then reframe it through your own perspective and voice. "Outlier" in Koe's sense is content that performs far above an account's normal level, used as evidence of what an audience wants.

In August 2026 he gave the shortest summary of the whole arc. He had started with Roam Research, which changed how he wrote newsletters because his notes became "preparative research." A few years ago he built Cortex, which was not meant as a second brain but became one because he tried to put his writing process into software. "Cortex inevitably failed... it didn't really fail, but it very painfully evolved into the version of Eden it is today," which is why he had not been posting much. **Source:** How To Learn Anything 10x Faster Than Anyone.md. The phrase "it didn't really fail, but it very painfully evolved" is his final framing, and it is consistent with the "series of necessary mistakes" framing of Section 37.4.

#### The first lesson: solo principles do not transfer one to one

The most general lesson Koe drew from the startup concerns his own principles. It emerged from a specific episode, told in "Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake."

Matt, Koe's product co-founder, took the blame for setting aggressive deadlines (Koe was "all for it"). His reasoning was this. Perhaps there was not enough pressure on the team. Structuring the company like a big tech firm had created big-tech mindsets: "okay, it's a 9 to 5." But at big tech companies "product market fit is guaranteed," whereas a startup's number one mission early on is to find product-market fit, to "make something people want," and they were not spending their time on that. So: "Let's set these goals. Let's make them really aggressive and see what happens," because pushing a system to "the speed limit" reveals what breaks. The result: "we saw that the whole car fell apart." "What's the worst thing imaginable for a note-taking app? You write stuff and it disappears." Notes were disappearing, largely because of the team's own sync engine, which they had been confident would not fail: "the cardinal mistake." Attention split again, and they dug deeper into "this tech hole," further solidifying an identity they had not intended. **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md.

The deadline was an experiment, and it worked as one: it revealed the weakest part of the system. What it revealed is the subject of Section 37.4 (the decision to build infrastructure in-house). What matters here is the lesson Koe took from it about himself. He thought the skills from his content creator journey, his freelancing and his products "would transfer over one-to-one to a startup." Instead he had to "refine them to the point of revealing what the principle actually was." His example is exactly the deadline: "set a deadline, pressure that kicks people into action." Applied to himself, it works, because he knows what he is trying to do and has all the variables accounted for. Applied to a team, "you don't know how it'll work," and when it fails you have to ask whether "this framework of how I work [is] wrong as a whole," and "what's the actual deeper underlying truth there." His summary: "the second that you think you know what you're doing, you're kind of [screwed]." **Source:** same.

"Deeper underlying truth" is an ordinary phrase used here in a precise way: the principle behind a personal practice, stripped of the conditions that made it work for one person. Deadlines work for Koe because he holds all the context, controls all the variables and chooses the pressure himself. A team member holds part of the context, controls few variables and receives the pressure from outside. The same practice is a different intervention. The underlying truth might be something like "commitment to a near, concrete target concentrates effort when the person committing can see the whole path," and that version predicts both the solo success and the team failure.

This is a refinement of a position Koe held strongly. His teaching on tactical stress and "bursts of intensity" presents self-imposed pressure as an engine of action. The Kortex experience does not make him abandon deadlines; it makes him abandon their one-to-one transfer to other people. **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md (2025-11-24).

A practical heuristic came out of the same experience. Asked when Eden's mobile app would arrive, Koe said "Mobile will come out when it comes out" rather than give a date: "something that we've learned is if you have a deadline or if you have a public date you want to give out, push it back by like two weeks. Just give yourself some breathing room." Early-access users were asked for feedback, knowing the product was not at its full potential but "much further along the path than Cortex ever was." **Source:** same. "Breathing room" names the buffer of about two weeks between the internal target and the public promise. It reconciles the two halves of the lesson: keep the pressure of the internal deadline, but do not let the public commitment depend on everything going right.

The episode states, in miniature, the thesis of the rest of this chapter. The one-person business trains a set of skills (decisiveness, self-imposed pressure, holding all the context, iterating without coordination) that are real advantages for one person. A team needs most of the same outcomes, but reaching them requires different mechanisms: structures that preserve speed, tests of product-market fit, hiring, and deliberate alignment. The next four sections take those mechanisms one by one.

### 37.3 What a Startup Needs

This section gathers what the two conversations say a startup needs and a one-person business does not: speed preserved by structure, product-market fit, a customer-first filter on what to build, and a clear sense of who the founders are. Most of it comes from the founders of Stan (John Hugh and Vitali) and from Koe's co-founder Matt, with Koe asking, adding and drawing lessons. The section ends with Koe's own diagnosis of why Kortex was not a compelling product, which is the negative image of everything before it.

**Complementary context:** "product-market fit" (PMF) is a standard term in startup culture for the state in which a product satisfies a real market demand strongly enough that customers pull it from the company rather than the company pushing it on them. "Make something people want," which Matt quotes as the definition of a startup's first mission, is the motto associated with Paul Graham and the startup accelerator Y Combinator.

#### Speed: the one advantage a startup has

Matt states the principle plainly: "As a startup the number one advantage that you have is that you're small, you can move fast." Why will the big players not simply catch up? "It's speed." Kortex structured its team and its technology "in a way that was antithetical to moving fast," so "we threw away the one and only advantage that we really had as a startup." The symptom was visible to users: bugs got fixed, but promised updates arrived a month late, and people emailed to ask where they were. **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md.

The way they threw speed away is the first mistake in the conversation's list. Ian, an early team member, built the first features of the editor and kept consulting Ari, who had built a text editor for his own first startup, until he suggested that Ari simply join. The team then grew by giving each new person a domain: "the very first mistake we made was how we structured our team." They would bring someone on and make them, for example, front-end lead: "compartmentalized based on tech stack." Koe admits his part: coming from a one-person business background, "I don't know how to freaking manage a team... as long as I saw some form of progress, I'm like, ah yeah, we're actually doing something." A year or two in, they realized it was "so slow": "you're structuring your team the way big tech companies structure their teams for the wrong reasons." The correct approach, in Matt's account, is that everyone is laser-focused on product and product-market fit, and "everybody should be full stack," working on features rather than on parts of a feature that serve another person. **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** a "tech stack" is the set of technologies a software product is built with, usually divided into layers such as the front end (what the user sees in the browser or app), the back end (servers, databases, business logic) and infrastructure. A "full stack" engineer works across all of these layers; a team "compartmentalized by tech stack" assigns each layer to a different owner.

Matt also explains why compartmentalization kills iteration, and he explains it "to the non-tech person," that is, to Koe. A small team that does not yet know what it is doing has to iterate: change things, test them, "put something out there, see how people used it, see how we used it," and then decide "this isn't really going to work, let's rip it out." With compartmentalized ownership, the person who built a part resists removing it, because ripping it out "throws everything else off and introduces all these other bugs that is like cross domain." Instead of speeding up toward "something that's good," the team slows down. The theme of the whole conversation, he notes, is that "you can't improve without mistakes." **Source:** same.

The mechanism is worth stating in the vocabulary of earlier chapters. In a one-person business, iteration is cheap because the person who decides to remove something is the same person who built it, and there are no other owners whose work depends on it. A compartmentalized team turns every experiment into a negotiation between owners, and each owner's identity is now partly invested in what they built. The same law of conceptual survival that Chapter 3 described for individuals (an identity defends the structures it has built) appears here at the level of a team: a component becomes something someone defends.

Matt draws the positive conclusion from the same mistake. The scope of what they are now building with Eden "is like 10x bigger": from a note-taking app to building the equivalents of Google Drive, Miro, Poppy, Frame.io and the "sketchy" downloaders people use for Instagram reels and YouTube videos. "We're able to do that because of all the mistakes that we made": now every person is laser-focused on a project and is full stack, not responsible for a compartmentalized part, and the technology is structured to let them build and move quickly. "Yes, Cortex was a mistake, but it was a necessary learning process." **Source:** same. The claim of a tenfold scope is Matt's assessment of their ambition, not a measured result.

**Complementary context:** Google Drive is a file storage service; Miro is a collaborative online whiteboard; Frame.io is a platform for reviewing video with collaborators; Poppy appears to be an AI-assisted visual workspace for creators. Matt names them to show that Eden combines functions usually spread across several products.

Koe's own view of his team changed on this point. In July 2024 he praised Cortex's "high agency" team. He did not see them as employees ("one, because they aren't, they're contractors") but as "entrepreneurs collaborating with entrepreneurs, or one-person businesses collaborating with one-person businesses," who share the product's vision, solve problems when they see them, and bring the solution back. His example was Ian, who spotted a problem, worked on it in the background, and "came to us with a complete app redesign": "Yes, it set us back a bit, but the app is in so much better a spot now." **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md. In November 2025, the same Ian appears at the start of the story of the compartmentalized structure, and Koe admits he did not know how to manage a team. The two accounts are not strictly contradictory: individual agency and team structure are different dimensions, and a team of agentic contractors can still be organized in a way that slows everyone down. But the emphasis moved from celebrating the autonomy of one-person businesses inside a company to diagnosing the structure as the cause of slowness. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md (2024-07-13); How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28); Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md (2025-11-24).

#### Product-market fit, measured by the customer's outcome

The Stan founders give the most developed account of product-market fit in the corpus, and they define it in a way that departs from the usual metrics.

Asked by Koe for three levers behind Stan's jumps in growth, Vitali begins after product-market fit and states its foundation first. Stan has PMF "not because of how many customers we have, but because of how much money those customers are making": the evidence is a body of end-to-end success stories, from "I didn't create content" to "I made a million dollars." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

On that foundation he names two levers. The first is service: "Stan is not a software company, we are a people company, and we use software to deliver on the promise," the promise being that "if you work with us you'll make the maximum amount of money possible." The second is speed: Stan "moves much faster than most companies" in "time from the idea to value." He does not appear to name a third lever explicitly before John takes over. **Source:** same.

"People company" is Vitali's term for a company whose product is an outcome delivered through software, not the software itself. "Time from the idea to value" will reappear in Section 37.5 as the main measure of an exceptional employee. Here it is a property of the whole organization.

John adds a description of how product-market fit is reached, and his description removes the drama usually attached to it. Noting first what he calls the "yin-yang model" of their partnership ("same core values but different perspectives"), he says that PMF "very rarely comes as one big 'aha'"; "if you're lucky you're deeply intuitive." It "comes from a lot of painful iteration cycles... super unglamorous": you ship a product, talk to the people who really like it and ask them why, ask the people who only kind of liked it what would make them really like it, "and you just keep improving." For Stan, "it wasn't like a step function thing": they always had some version of it, and it compounded until "it's just fundamentally the best thing out there now," with many ways still left to improve. **Source:** same.

The two accounts fit together. Vitali says how to know you have product-market fit (your customers' outcomes), and John says how you get there (iteration driven by asking enthusiasts why and lukewarm users what would change their minds). Both are close to principles Koe teaches for the one-person business, in particular the idea in Chapter 31 that value is measured by the result in the customer's life, and the build-to-learn loop of Chapter 14. What is different in a startup is the cost of each iteration, which is why the structural point about speed comes first.

#### Customer first, and the core job to be done

Stan had about ten engineers when Koe asked how a company that small decides what to build. Vitali answered: "you start with the customer first." The alternative he warns against has a name in his vocabulary, **"features, features, features"**: building one function after another out of enthusiasm for the technology rather than to solve a problem a customer actually has. The mistake many companies make, especially those led by technical people, is to "get excited about technology, not realizing that technology is just the means to the end... anything worth existing exists to serve humans." (The transcript says some companies are "overly passionate about the customer," which in context appears to be a slip for "about technology"; the passage is ambiguous.) Stan's number one cultural value is "Creator first," placed first deliberately, because everything the company does comes from time spent with customers and from the team being creators themselves. They listen a lot, but "we don't do most of the time everything that's being asked." They try to understand the pain point and the problem, then apply their own judgment and understanding of the world and translate it into software. "We are not here building features, we are here solving problems," and that principle eliminates work: they go farther "by not doing things and by doing them well." The sentence is the direct answer to "features, features, features": a feature is justified only by the problem it solves. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

The concrete case is landing pages. For years customers have asked Stan for more flexible landing pages. Stan's pages are "extremely simple... literally rich text editor"; you cannot even reorder sections. Vitali gives two reasons. First, "we optimized everything out, we just know that those pages convert the best." Second, they tried to build flexible pages twice and watched people "spend so much time trying to perfect their landing page and move things around, they don't finish it... death by complexity." "We can build the world's best landing page editor, except the world doesn't need another one"; they focus on what the customer is actually trying to do, which is to make as much money as possible. **Source:** same.

"Death by complexity" is Vitali's name for the outcome of giving users more flexibility than they can use: they spend their effort perfecting the tool instead of finishing the work. The two terms describe the same error from two sides: "features, features, features" is what the company does when it follows technology instead of the customer, and "death by complexity" is what that does to the customer. The phenomenon is the customer-side version of something Koe describes for creators, "procrastination disguised as productivity" (endless tinkering with tools and systems instead of doing the work), and it is one reason the book has treated simplicity as a design principle throughout Part X.

John names the principle behind the refusal: "the core job to be done," which he calls the core product principle Vitali talks about often. It answers the question of how to choose among the infinite features you could ship. People ask for custom landing pages, and John himself would like them, but Stan's core job to be done is the guarantee that "there is no platform out there where you will make more money," so the company "optimizes the heck out of your business for you." The principle also brings clarity about what software is: "we get so obsessed with the technology, but the software is actually friction, the software is a medium through which to achieve the core job to be done." His thought experiment: "if we completely wiped out the software and just had an AI agent who did everything for you and just made you money, you would probably sign up for that over having to set up the software yourself." Imagining that agent is another way to prioritize the roadmap: features that bring the product closer to it are worth building; features that add configuration are not. Koe says he will take the idea back to Kortex as "our core job to be done." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** "jobs to be done" is a framework associated with Clayton Christensen and his collaborators, which holds that customers "hire" a product to make progress in a particular circumstance, and that products should be designed around that progress rather than around customer demographics or requested features. John and Vitali use the term in this sense without attributing it.

"Software is friction" is John's phrase, and it reverses the usual self-image of a software company. Every screen, setting and step stands between the customer and what they want. The best software, by that standard, is the one that requires the least of itself to deliver the outcome. The thought experiment also shows an implicit position about AI that Section 37.5 will complicate: John is willing to imagine the product as an agent that replaces the software entirely.

#### Know who you are

The second lever John names "is going to sound as silly as truly understanding who you are," starting with "buzzwords like values." Entrepreneurship "really forces you to look at yourself in the mirror; you get punched so hard, but if you persist for long enough you learn a lot about what your values [are] and what value you can give in this world," that is, "you actually start to learn what your differentiation is": what you care about, who your kind of people are, how you can make a living. Through hiring mistakes and through working with great people, they learned "these are our people." Stan's values, as he states them: "extremely high integrity, unquestioned integrity, good kind person, also exceptional." That clarity serves hiring and "brand differentiation," knowing why people come to you. "That takes years... of pattern matching, years of just showing up every day and doing boring stuff." After three or four years they realized that competitors would not come close, "because they don't understand content and brand and will never care about our customer as much as we do." He calls entrepreneurship "a persistence game and an observation game." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

The parallel with Koe's own story in Section 37.1 is close. Koe's differentiation emerged from years of failures that revealed what interested him and what skills he had; John's emerged from years of building and hiring that revealed what his company valued. In both cases differentiation is discovered by doing, not decided in advance.

Vitali adds two related realizations. The first is **founder-market fit**: Stan's brand is "for creators by creators," beginning with John being a creator himself; many people on the team create content, and they hire creators. "Most companies out there just don't have any business being... in the business." Founder-market fit "is incredibly important," because when you build for yourself you "just feel what's the right thing to do," instead of needing a "three month research to find out what customers are objectively trying to tell you." The second is **consistency in the one channel that works**. They have been "very consistent and very selective" about the one thing that worked for distribution, partnering with creators: "the only thing that we've done for literally four years, never spend a dollar on marketing," not on Twitter ads or Facebook ads. When growth specialists propose other channels, the answer is "yeah, but we are not, because we know what we are doing and we are good at it." The principle: "finding that one thing that works for you and sticking with it and actually giving it time to succeed," a consistency and belief "founded in data and insights." The main channels they mention for creators are Instagram, TikTok, YouTube and, increasingly, LinkedIn and newsletters. **Source:** same.

Founder-market fit is the startup version of "you are the niche." Koe's one-person business sold to people like him; Stan's founders built a product for creators because they were creators. Both rely on the founder's own experience as a substitute for, or a complement to, external research.

#### Two tensions: persist or quit, and building for yourself

Placed next to the Kortex story, the Stan principles create two tensions that the corpus does not fully resolve.

The first concerns **persistence**. Stan's founders describe entrepreneurship as a persistence game, product-market fit as long iteration, and success as giving a channel time to work. In the Kortex conversation, Ari, praised as the team's most skilled engineer, says that what really makes a difference, "especially in startups, is just persistence," more than raw technical skill; his own first startup attempt began with no web or front-end knowledge and the choice to build a text editor, "an insane first project to take on." He also says that people often tell you "this is too hard, you shouldn't do it," and "until you actually try, giving up at that point is meaningless"; "even Eden wouldn't exist if we kept to that advice." Investors reacted to the ambition of building something "better than Google Drive" with "how are you going to do that? It's too hard," and months later he thought "we actually have a better product." **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md. Yet in the same conversation Koe says of Kortex: "I spent too much time justifying that. That was my mistake... we should have called it quits earlier," and Matt says they "should have scrapped things way, way earlier." **Source:** same.

The tension is not chronological; the two positions coexist. The material suggests a way to hold both, though no single speaker states it in full. Vitali conditions persistence on a belief "founded in data and insights." What Stan persisted in was a mission and a channel already validated by results. What Koe regrets persisting in was a specific implementation (a note-taking app) that he defended against what users were telling him, because of "a different image in his head." Persist in the mission and in what the evidence supports; do not persist in an implementation the evidence contradicts. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28); Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md (2025-11-24). This is consistent with the shift in Koe's own teaching from consistency to "persistence and iteration" noted in Section 37.1: persistence is the commitment to the goal, iteration is the willingness to change the means.

The second tension concerns **building for yourself**. Vitali says that founder-market fit lets you "feel" the right thing without months of research, and elsewhere in the same conversation he recommends using your own product, while also requiring an opinion about what is good, a great deal of time with customers, and a measure based on customer outcomes. Koe used Kortex for all his writing and "couldn't see myself living without it," yet, as the next subsection shows, the product did not convince the market, and Matt's friends said it did not look like something they would use. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28); Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md (2025-11-24). Building for yourself is a compass, not a guarantee. Stan combined it with intensive contact with customers and with outcome metrics; Kortex, by Koe's own account, relied on his attachment to it.

#### Not a compelling product

Koe's diagnosis of Kortex is the most self-critical passage in the corpus, and it applies, in reverse, each principle of this section.

The product was missing basic surfaces. It needed mobile, desktop and a light mode: "that's how slow we were going... it was difficult to get light mode out of all things." Mobile is "huge" for a note-taking app. Kortex had 80,000 users, but the team was siloed. About 80% of people discover the brand on their phone (through YouTube, Koe's tweets or Instagram), go to the Kortex website, sign up, log into the web version and think "this is crap": "sure, we have this many users, but how many did we lose along the way?" **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md. The 80% figure is given as an estimate in conversation.

The business model did not fit the product. Kortex started free and later added a paid tier that "didn't even make sense, because we were a note-taking app only." Revenue only came with the AI feature, whose unique selling point was access to all the major models (in their words, quote-unquote replacing ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini) while referencing your notes. That was a selling point, but "nothing that you don't get in, like, say, Notion, or even in Claude built in now." **Source:** same.

And the founder could not see it. Koe says: "it's good. It works. I do all my writing in it... I couldn't see myself living without it." But it did not feel like a startup: "it feels like we're just rebuilding Obsidian with an AI chat latched on. And I didn't want to face that for the longest time." People commented exactly that, and he would say no, because he had a different image in his head. "I spent too much time justifying that. That was my mistake... we should have called it quits earlier." They were stuck on maintenance and bug fixes, "marching in place." **Source:** same.

"Marching in place" is his phrase for a team that is busy without moving: effort spent on keeping an existing product alive rather than on finding the product that should exist. It describes, at the scale of a team, the difference between activity and progress: the hours are spent, but the distance to the goal does not shrink.

The messaging made things worse. The branding needed to be brighter, something they could imagine "getting into millions of people's hands," and "Kortex" did not sound like a household name ("what do you take notes in? Cortex"). Anecdotally, when Matt showed the app to friends, especially female friends, the reaction was "this doesn't look like something I would use." Koe adds that if they had shipped mobile and called it "Cortex Notes," it would have been honestly what it was, a note-taking app with AI tacked on. Instead they were "in this state of dissonance": "we're not a note-taking app, but our product reflects that," while the messaging described a "really cool system... you store all your knowledge." "We overcomplicated it to make it more novel than it was, because we knew we needed to build something more novel, and we were marketing the product as more novel when it wasn't. So that was like a point of confusion." **Source:** same.

Koe uses "dissonance" in other videos for the first phase of deep personal change, when you feel lost before a new identity forms. Here the word has a different sense: the gap between what a product says it is and what it is. The two uses share the image of an identity that does not match reality, but they should not be confused.

| Principle (from the Stan founders and Matt) | What Kortex did instead, by Koe's and Matt's account |
|---|---|
| Speed is the one advantage | Team compartmentalized by tech stack; light mode hard to ship; updates a month late |
| Product-market fit measured by customer outcomes, reached by iteration | A note app with an AI chat; paid tier that did not make sense; revenue only from a feature others already offered |
| Customer first, software is friction | Users lost between mobile discovery and a poor web experience |
| Know who you are, and say it | Messaging claimed more novelty than the product had; "dissonance" |
| Persist in what the evidence supports | Persisted in an implementation that comments and friends were questioning |

The table is this book's synthesis of the two conversations, not a list either set of founders presented. It shows why Koe called the experience a series of necessary mistakes rather than bad luck: each mistake is the absence of a principle he would later hear stated by people who had followed it.

### 37.4 The Kortex Case

Section 37.3 used Kortex as a negative example of what a startup needs. This section tells the case as a story: how the team formed, the technical decision that sank thousands of hours, the secret rebuild that proved it, the decision to start over as a new company, and the vision and rollout of Eden. Almost all of it comes from one conversation, "Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake" (November 2025), between Koe and his co-founders Matt (product) and Ari (the "deeply technical" co-founder, the CTO). Where a passage reflects Matt's or Ari's view rather than Koe's, the text says so.

The title deserves a word first. Koe says it is "very attention grabbing and polarizing," and he frames it with his general philosophy: "mistakes, you have to make them in order to actually improve." Matt calls the story "a series of necessary mistakes for us to get where we need to go": the idea of building Kortex, a second brain app, was not bad, but as first-time founders they made many mistakes. Koe had talked about Kortex nonstop for months, even years, and then stopped; they decided to pivot and build a new app, Eden, rolled out to Kortex users on the Monday before Black Friday, with a public early-access window over that weekend (web only, treated as a beta). Where they stopped with Kortex "wasn't our vision for it... we were forced to." **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md. The transcript gives no breakdown of the three million dollars in the title.

#### The founding story

Matt and Ari were students at the University of Toronto, in computer engineering and computer science, who wanted a non-conventional path. Matt, disillusioned, went part-time early, took odd jobs and hopped through several startups (making content, demo videos, front-end work). Realizing that he did not want to do front-end work for the rest of his life, he wrote a plan in an Apple Note two days before meeting Koe: start with ghostwriting, find clients online, systematize the work into software, "and eventually I'll probably partner with Dan Koe." On a call for a ghostwriting cohort run by Dakota Robertson, Koe mentioned in passing that he was building software. Matt unmuted "in front of everybody... like 50 strangers," asked what software, and "shamelessly" pitched his past work. Koe, who had heard many such pitches ("a lot of it doesn't bear any fruit"), gave him his Telegram handle to move the conversation offline. Matt then flooded him with messages, and Koe was at first not interested. **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md.

At that point Koe and his partner Joey had already started building. It was "mine and Joey's first dive into software," and they had outsourced the development and believed they were "doing everything right." Koe sent Matt what they had. Matt's reply, as Koe summarizes it: "you need to burn this all down. It sucks." Koe: "Oh, okay. Now I'm listening." That weekend they fired the developer and brought Matt on; Koe incorporated the company, the name Kortex came about, and Koe put in the first seed funding. The previous developer "had spent like 5 months building basically a CRUD app, which Ari can pull off in like two hours" ("and now... in like 30 seconds"). Koe says the conversation shows "what iteration actually is, and it's a lot." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** a "CRUD app" is a basic application that only lets users create, read, update and delete records, the most elementary pattern in software development. The comparison of five months with two hours is a way of saying the outsourced work had produced almost nothing.

Ari's background adds a contrast that Koe himself points out. Ari's parents conditioned him to believe that "anything's possible": he would always have a home with them, "so don't worry about being safe. Try experimenting. Try something crazy." Koe's reaction: "total opposite what my parents told me." Ari's father told him that even if you sell a toothpick, if you can sell it to enough people you will make a lot of money; "start small... you'll make it work somehow." An incubator Ari approached wanted business proposals and revenue projections before any product existed; he disagreed ("maybe it works for different kinds of startups") and built a note-taking prototype instead. The incubator told him he was "too young" and should come back when he was about to graduate, as a fourth-year student. It "feels really bad," he says; he thought there should be no age requirement. So he combined his second and third years, taking eight credits in a single year, to become a fourth-year student, a few weeks before meeting Koe's team, while also doing a year of software engineering co-op at a company called Flip. **Source:** same.

The founding story illustrates several principles from earlier chapters in concrete form. Matt's Apple Note is a written vision with a specific next step, the kind of self-generated goal Chapter 8 describes, and his unmuting in front of fifty strangers is the agency of Chapter 35 (acting without permission, accepting the risk of embarrassment). Ari's upbringing is the opposite of the conditioning described in Chapter 1: a family environment that made experimentation feel safe. Koe's remark that his own parents told him the "total opposite" connects the story to his account of the default path in Chapter 2. And the outsourced developer's five months are a case of the knowledge-versus-understanding problem: Koe and Joey did not yet know enough about software to recognize that the work was poor.

#### The "own the IP" trap

The deepest technical mistake, according to Ari and Matt, was building infrastructure themselves. When Ari joined part-time, a couple of months after the company was set up, he found a stack designed the way a big technology company would design it: their own hosted open-source authentication system, their own EC2 instances, "guys running around with Terraform scripts." "A lot of things that we decided to build ourselves, that was the fundamental problem." A mid-sized company has five or six people working full-time on that kind of infrastructure. Kortex was five or six people doing that and the product. And once users exist, "you can't back out." **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md.

**Complementary context:** EC2 is Amazon Web Services' service for renting virtual servers; Terraform is a tool for defining and managing such infrastructure in code. Both are standard in large engineering organizations. Using them directly, rather than relying on higher-level services that handle servers, scaling and authentication for you, gives more control at the cost of much more maintenance work.

The example they give for non-technical listeners is authentication: logging in, signing up, "sign up with Google." It "should be really, really simple, unless you try to build it yourself." First-time founders, they say, believe that owning all the intellectual property and not relying on third-party vendors is valuable. But scaling prematurely means "sinking literally thousands of dev hours" into those things instead of into the product. "Entire companies are built solving authentication": by building your own, you partly become an authentication company, split away from what you are actually trying to build. "What kind of company are we? We say we're a second brain... does our time reflect that? No." Other examples were their own sync engine (the component whose failure made notes disappear, as described in Section 37.2) and requests for an offline mode ("Obsidian has offline"; Notion, they note, took about four years to ship offline). **Source:** same.

The question "does our time reflect that?" is the most transferable part of the passage. It is the company-level version of a test Koe applies to individuals throughout the book: your goals are revealed by where your attention actually goes, not by what you say they are. A company that says it is a second brain but spends its hours on authentication is, in practice, partly an authentication company. The diagnosis also connects to the speed argument of Section 37.3: every hour sunk into infrastructure is an hour not spent iterating on the product, and the commitment to owned infrastructure made later changes more expensive.

The aggressive deadlines described in Section 37.2 fit into this story as its stress test. When Matt pushed the team to the "speed limit," the part that broke was the sync engine they had built themselves. The experiment did not cause the problem; it revealed the cost of a decision made months earlier.

#### The secret rebuild in Japan

Frustrated by the lack of progress on the product, Matt and Ari took a trip to Japan. Before leaving, they had discussed with the team the idea of setting everything aside and rebuilding "in a drastically different way that is much leaner, much quicker to iterate on," not entrenched in the technologies they had built themselves. The team said rebuilding was probably not a good idea. To the rest of the team, the trip looked like a vacation. The two of them decided, "in secret from the rest of the team," to try anyway: "we haven't tried. Why don't we just try?" **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md.

Their hypothesis was specific. By choosing technologies they were familiar with, and by "outsourcing... and abstracting away a lot of the problems to third party providers," they could rebuild all of Kortex, and more, in much less time. "Literally three weeks into being in Japan, that hypothesis turned out to be true." **Source:** same.

The lesson they draw is uncomfortable: "we should have scrapped things way, way earlier, because if we can do it in three weeks... what's stopping someone else from doing it in a month?" If the whole product could be rebuilt that quickly, it had no defensible advantage. They also describe the emotional side of being stuck. Every time someone shared a great new app in the team's group chat, it gave them "a short hit of inspiration," but they could not compete: they were "stuck in the mud," six months away from shipping anything comparable. **Source:** same.

The rebuild is a test in the sense Koe uses throughout the book: a hypothesis stated in advance, tried with a small commitment, and judged by its result rather than by opinion. Its secrecy is notable. The team's judgment, given in good faith, was that rebuilding was a bad idea; the two co-founders treated that judgment as an untested opinion and tested it. The book does not have enough material to say how the rest of the team experienced this, and Koe does not comment on the ethics of it; he presents it as evidence that action beats debate.

#### Clean slate: from replacement to a new company

Originally, the rebuild was only meant to replace Kortex. But many technology companies use what Koe calls "blurple branding" (blue and purple, glowing gradients, a dark theme), and Kortex had leaned dark because Koe's own brand is black and white and they would be launching to his audience. Looking honestly at the result, they concluded: "Kortex doesn't look that good. People like it because it resonates with them and they're more of like that dark techy vibe, but we're not reaching the audience for the vision that we were going to achieve." So they decided on a rebrand as a whole and an entirely different technology stack: "this is us starting a new company, clean slate." The constraint was loyalty to existing users: Kortex users had trusted them, and they had to find a way to make it up to them and deliver what those users had been waiting for. **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md.

"Blurple branding" and "clean slate" are Koe's terms here. The first names a visual convention that signals "tech product" and therefore speaks mostly to people who already like tech products. The second names the decision to treat the rebuild as a new company rather than a new version, which freed them from the identity of the old product.

The repositioning began with a question about time: "think of the company 10 years from now: who do you want to serve? Who's attracted to it?" Kortex had been for creators, and that was still true, but which creators? "People who still deeply care about their craft." Then a second question: "what's the opposite of AI slop? And it's craft, or like artisan work." So Eden is for artisans, or for people who want to become artisans: "in the future, when AI slop becomes so noisy, the artisans are going to be the ones that stand out." They describe it as "taking a bet on the future of the artisan." The result was "a really cohesive brand vision": every detail has to focus on craft and artisan-level work, because those users "have a very high expectation of quality and polish." **Source:** same. The source attributes the passage to the conversation between Koe and Matt; the framing in terms of AI slop matches Koe's other videos, where "slop" is the generic output of AI used without taste (Chapter 36).

The method is transferable beyond software. Define the audience by asking whom you want to serve in ten years, not whom you serve now; define the position by asking what the opposite of the dominant failure in your market is; then let that position set the standard for every detail. It is the brand-level version of the anti-vision and vision exercise of Part IV: start from what you refuse (slop), derive what you stand for (craft), and use it as a filter.

The name came from a pattern they recognize in their own creative process. They brainstormed names related to craftsmanship (Da Vinci, already taken by the video editor DaVinci Resolve), Greek names, Renaissance names, artistic names. Then someone said "Eden." The pattern: "There's a cycle in the ideas that we have when we're trying to refine a feature or... the name... we run through so many ideas to the point of nearly giving up and just like forgetting about it, and then we throw one out and it's like, oh okay, yeah, that one's good." Eden fit because it is "the garden of knowledge... the place where knowledge began for humanity," and they want the app to be "your home as a creative." **Source:** same.

The naming cycle is a small instance of the incubation pattern Koe describes in his writing on creativity (Chapter 18): exhausting the obvious options, nearly letting go, and then recognizing the right idea when it appears. It is also another occurrence of "nothing happens, then everything happens," at the scale of a single decision.

The logo has its own small story. They had a logo they loved, four petals, fitting the name and good as an app icon, and they showed it at the end of one of Matt and Ari's first YouTube videos. A commenter asked: "isn't this the same logo as Flora?", another canvas- and node-based app. It was exactly the same. They changed it ("if anyone from Flora is watching this, we changed it"), and the new one "has grown on me a lot." The old style, Koe says, was "duplicated across so many startups"; now they have "something extremely unique." (He credits the videographer, Jason, in the same passage.) **Source:** same. The episode is minor, but it shows the public iteration Koe practices: something was released, the audience found a flaw, and the flaw was fixed in the open.

#### Eden's vision and rollout

Matt's original vision, carried over into Eden, is "a really seamless way to capture anything at a moment's notice and then recall exactly what you want later": a great capture system plus "a really, really good search system." Eden's search goes beyond text to "specific visual frames in any footage you've ever captured." In a prototype Ari built with anime episodes, searching "pink sweater" surfaced every frame containing a pink sweater, at the exact timestamp: "YouTube doesn't even do that. Google search doesn't do that." They discovered afterward that some tools exist, at "$600 a month," priced "terabyte per terabyte," and that each company solves "one fragmented part of the entire creative workflow." Capture works through a single keyboard shortcut while watching a YouTube video, or by pasting a link. **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md.

Koe prefers a different framing: "a drive for anything... a drive for all media that's transcribed." Any YouTube video you paste is downloaded (with no need for a "sketchy app"), transcribed, readable, and searchable by frames and by transcript; the same applies to three-hour podcasts, Instagram reels (shared to Eden from a phone and searchable even by style, such as "cinematic moody"), tweets and Substack articles. Koe is "big on the canvas feature"; Matt is big on search. **Source:** same. The difference in emphasis between the co-founders is worth noticing: Matt's vision is retrieval (find exactly what you saved), Koe's is synthesis (place sources on a canvas and connect them to AI chats), which matches the role of note-taking in his writing process as "preparative research." By January 2026, as Section 37.2 showed, Koe's framing ("AI canvas and drive") had become the public description, and by June 2026 the product had moved further toward content strategy.

The rollout plan applied what they had learned about demand. Eden would go first to Kortex users, "from highest payment tier, just because there's less people," then down to the free tier, from the Monday before Black Friday to the Thursday; public early access would be open only from Friday to Monday; the full public launch, with desktop and mobile, was planned for 2026. In between, they might let Eden users invite others with a limited number of links ("like three to five"), as "a fun way to see how quickly Eden grows and how much people actually want to use it." **Source:** same.

The staged rollout is a method with three purposes, and each answers a lesson of the Kortex years. Starting with the highest tier keeps the first group small, which protects the team's ability to fix problems quickly (speed). Short public windows limit the exposure of an unfinished product (the lesson of the 80,000 users lost to a poor first experience). And limited invitations turn word of mouth into a measurement: how fast the invitations are used is a signal of how much people want the product (an approach to product-market fit that measures demand rather than assuming it).

#### Reading the case as a whole

The Kortex case can be read as a sequence in which each stage corresponds to one of the principles of Section 37.3 and to one of Koe's general themes.

| Stage | What happened | Principle it illustrates | Koe's broader theme |

| Outsourced start | Five months of development produced a basic CRUD app | Knowledge versus understanding; you cannot judge work you do not understand | Build to learn (Chapter 14) |
| Team formation | Hired by domain: "front-end lead"; compartmentalized | Speed is the startup's only advantage; everyone full stack | Structure shapes behavior |
| Infrastructure | Own authentication, servers, sync engine | Software is friction; "does our time reflect" what we say we are | Attention reveals the real goal |

| Messaging | Claimed more novelty than the product had | Know who you are and say it honestly | Dissonance between identity and reality |
| Rebuild | Three weeks in Japan, in secret | Test hypotheses instead of debating them | Action produces information |
| Rebrand | Clean slate; artisans as the opposite of AI slop | Position for whom you want to serve in ten years | Vision as a filter (Part IV) |
| Rollout | Highest tier first; short windows; invitation links | Measure demand; protect speed | Validation before scale |

Koe's own last word on the experience, in the same conversation, concerns leadership rather than product. He asks how to apply his worldview to a team, not to impose it, but to understand the team well enough to guide it: "I can lead myself just fine... sure, I'm an idiot sometimes." It frustrates him. He feels he needs to guide people, "but it's also a value of mine to let people do their own thing," so where is the boundary? He wants everyone to feel they partly own the company, with "the ownership mindset to the point where they can make decisions, solve problems on their own" ("whether they want it or not is another problem"). He calls it "a very interesting and rewarding challenge": "new sets of problems... better sets of problems." **Source:** Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md. The phrase "better sets of problems" is his general theory of progress (Chapter 8 and Chapter 39: solving one level of problems earns you a higher level of problems) applied to the transition this chapter describes. The remaining two sections take up exactly the problems he names: how to design a team, and how to keep it pointed in one direction.

### 37.5 Designing the Team

Koe admitted that he stayed a one-person business partly because he did not know how to hire. The conversation with the founders of Stan is, in large part, his attempt to learn. This section reconstructs what John Hugh and Vitali told him about how large a team should be, where to add people and where not to, when to hire, what to hire for, what makes an exceptional person, and how to find such people. The frameworks are theirs. Koe's contributions are his questions, a citation (Valve's handbook) and one qualification about who can apply this advice.

#### A billion-dollar company with ten engineers

Stan's stated goal, as Koe raises it, is a billion-dollar company with ten engineers. Vitali explains both numbers. The billion "as a number is frankly trivial," "a currency describing the degree of achievement." He and John are "investing the best years of our life" to create as much value as possible, and a billion "sounded like a good number to start with." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

The ten engineers come from what he calls a "hot take": "tech companies are extremely overstaffed." His argument has three steps. First, Twitter cut about 90% of its headcount a couple of years earlier, stayed afloat and began innovating more rapidly, which he calls "a very strong first principle signal of what it actually takes to build software." Second, at the most foundational level, "you just need one person to build software," because engineering today is straightforward. Third, for reliability and redundancy (having systems in place if something happens to someone), "10 feels like a good number." Stan's track record supports the claim, in his view: in three years they built one of the most feature-rich all-in-one creator stores with eight engineers (and two open positions). Being intentional about size means "not being distracted by solving arbitrary problems, such as let's hire a big team and spend time managing them," along with the human resources situations that come with a large team. **Source:** same.

"First principle signal" and "arbitrary problems" are Vitali's terms. The first is evidence that bypasses convention and shows what something actually requires; he reads Twitter's layoffs this way. The second is the class of problems a company creates for itself by growing (managing, coordinating, resolving conflicts) that have nothing to do with the customer. His reading of Twitter is an interpretation, not an established fact: whether the company innovated "more rapidly" after the cuts is a contested judgment, and he offers it as a signal, not as proof.

**Complementary context:** Elon Musk acquired Twitter (later renamed X) in late 2022 and cut most of its staff, roughly 80% according to figures Koe cites in another video. Vitali's "about 90%" is his own round figure.

From this Vitali derives a general principle: "people have this very strong misconception that more people is the better; more people is not the better. Growth is not proportional to headcount, like by a long shot; in my opinion [it is] reversely proportional after a certain threshold." **Source:** same. The illustration is the story already told in Section 37.1: when Koe wanted Loom embeds in his course, a single engineer shipped them in two hours. A large organization would have needed a ticket, a prioritization meeting and a sprint.

The principle is the organizational counterpart of what Koe teaches individuals about leverage in Chapter 34: output depends on leverage, not on hours, and adding hours (or people) beyond a certain point reduces output because coordination consumes what the additional hours add.

#### Organization design by bottlenecks

The ten-engineer goal seems to clash with something else the founders say in the same conversation: Stan has about forty employees and expects to reach a hundred. John resolves the apparent contradiction with a mental model he describes as "a factory line, or the concept of bottlenecks." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

The model starts from the leverage of code. "A single person can write code that scales to the entirety of humanity," and with AI agents, he suggests, one-person billion-dollar businesses may even occur. So engineering can be "hyper-disciplined" and small. "The rest of the organization ends up being the things that are harder to scale, the things that are more human, closer to the human, require more nuance, harder therefore to systematize or programmatize." That is where people are needed. **Source:** same.

He describes Stan's roughly forty full-time employees in those terms:

| Area | Approximate size | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Product | About a dozen | Engineers, plus product and design (wireframes, store themes) |
| Support and service | About a third | Answering creators, solving problems, helping with monetization |
| Growth | About a third | Half an in-house content team (creators on staff making content for Stan), half partnerships with creators aligned on success and values |

**Source:** same. Growth from forty to a hundred, he says, will come in operational areas not yet automated. The figures for support vary within the conversation ("at least a third," then "more than half," then "15 or so" full-time), so the proportions should be read as approximate.

John adds a remark about talent in high-leverage roles: a "10x engineer" is really "a thousand X better than the average employee," because of the leverage of code, and the same can be said of creators and creative talent. Vitali reframes the whole model in one sentence: "what scales, and definitely is technology... what doesn't scale at all actually is human connection." Serving 60,000 people, then 600,000, then six million, is where humans come in. **Source:** same.

Support is the clearest application. John describes Stan's support promise: "you can email them a question anytime, they'll get back to you in less than an hour, and it's a real human being," not an automatic ticket. "That's just like a hill we've chosen to die on." They could be more profitable with "a shitty clunky AI agent or a call center," and they choose not to be. Vitali calls it "a big differentiation for Stan": few organizations can confidently promise a reply within an hour "with a solution, like a personalized Loom literally solving your problem." The structure behind it is about ten internal support people ("the brain of the support operation") plus "a few dozen contractors" in different time zones. And support goes beyond technical triage: "hey, you need help monetizing, we'll help you come up with a strategy." **Source:** same.

Vitali is openly skeptical of AI in this area. He disagrees with "uninformed opinions" that "AI is going to solve a ton of problems," points to questionable results of AI in content, and says that companies trying to solve support with AI are "solving for a customer that asks like a stupid thing that you can look up on Google; customers that matter to you don't ask those kinds of questions." Stan will add people in areas of human connection (content, marketing, support), while technology stays small and grows "significantly at a slower pace." **Source:** same.

Two tensions are visible here, and the founders themselves resolve them only partly.

The first is between a small team and a growing headcount. Vitali says growth is inversely proportional to headcount past a threshold and that he is not eager to grow; John says Stan is forty people and heading to a hundred. The bottleneck model reconciles them: the principle of minimal headcount applies to technology, where leverage is near-infinite, and growth in headcount applies to human connection, where there is no leverage. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28).

The second is about AI. John imagines one-person billion-dollar businesses built with AI agents and uses an agent that "just makes you money" as a thought experiment for the product (Section 37.3). Vitali distrusts AI for support and rejects an AI agent even at a higher profit. These are not opposed in time; they apply to different domains. AI amplifies the leverage of code and product; human connection remains the differentiator that does not scale. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28). Koe's own practice, seen in Section 37.2, sits between them: he used AI to build Eden's help center and lets Intercom's Fin answer redundant questions, with humans available behind it. He does not comment on Vitali's position.

The bottleneck model is a useful lens for any business, including a one-person one. A solo creator already applies it implicitly: writing is systematized and repurposed across platforms (the high-leverage, "code-like" part), while the personal element (replying, coaching, the voice itself) stays human. The model says that when you grow, you should add people where the human element is the product and keep the leveraged part small.

#### When to hire

Koe's question about hiring was personal: he had avoided it for years. John's answer begins with a feeling. "You will literally feel the pain of it, you will feel so overwhelmed." You feel it at different stages as the organization grows. The first few times, you persist through it, "but at some point something will feel like it's breaking, and that's where it's really important to ask really intentional questions about hiring." And there is no shortcut to learning it: "you have to earn your stripes on hiring, you have to make a ton of mistakes." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

Vitali adds a stricter version: "you need to get dragged into it." If you hire "because I feel like I need help, you will spend more time educating... than you actually need," so "you hold back as long as you need to." **Source:** same. ("Earn your stripes" and "get dragged into it" are the founders' phrases.)

Placed next to Koe's confession, this advice reads almost as a vindication. He put off hiring "for the longest amount of time," and he thinks the constraint helped him; Vitali says you should hold back until you are dragged. Koe's first hire, an editor who also handles support, came at $50,000 a month (Section 37.2). The difference is in the reason. Koe held back because hiring frightened him; Vitali holds back because premature hiring costs more in training than it saves. The behavior is the same, but only one of the two reasons will tell you when to stop holding back.

#### What to hire for

Once you hire, John says, you must be "hyper-intentional" about "what are the jobs to be done by these new hires." Your job as a creator or founder "is to maximize the value you give to your business, AKA operate within your zones of genius." His method has three steps:

1. Write down the top two things you are fantastic at ("I say two, not even three") and obsess over spending almost all your time on those.
2. Write out "the 17,000 things" you dislike doing.
3. Those are what you hire for.

For a solo creator, he adds, one of the first key hires is "some sort of executive assistant," for captions, email, booking and negotiating, "the crud of running the back end of a business." His own examples of things outside his zone of genius are making graphics in Canva, responding to certain emails and scheduling calls. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

"Zones of genius" is John's term for the few activities in which a person creates disproportionate value. The method is a delegation filter that works by subtraction: rather than asking what help you need, you list what only you should do and hand off everything else. The insistence on two, not three, is the same logic of focus Koe applies to priorities in Chapter 12: the third item is usually where dilution begins.

**Complementary context:** the expression "zone of genius" was popularized by the psychologist Gay Hendricks in The Big Leap (2009). John uses it without attribution, in his own sense.

How to define the role is the next question. John recommends what he calls a visual exercise: "close your eyes and be like, if I hired someone, what would success look like," and describe it "to a joyful degree." Then write those descriptions out as job characteristics. His example is an assistant who replies to emails immediately "with the exact copy and tone" you want. The exercise counters the usual first hire, grabbing "this random friend, please come help me." After the description come two filters. The first: "will I get along with this person?" Do we share the same core values, and do I respect them on those values? "You don't want to work with someone you dislike." The second: "are they functionally the best person possible that I can hire, that will actually do these job requirements to the degree that I specifically need?" The combination minimizes the chance of failure, since a hire can exceed expectations or simply not work out. **Source:** same.

The visual exercise transfers the logic of the vision exercise in Part IV from a life to a role. A vague desire ("I need help") produces a vague hire; a vivid, specific picture of success produces criteria that can be checked. The two filters separate the cultural question (values) from the functional one (ability), so that neither compensates for a failure in the other.

#### The 10x person

Vitali defines a "10x person" across industries by three traits. First, "a 10x person has range": they are not "single-threaded people who can do just one part of the job," which limits what they can contribute. Second, "when I am in the room with someone, at the very least they need to be an expert that I'm going to learn from"; there is no one at Stan they spend personal time with from whom they do not learn. Third, and most important, "time to value: how long does it take you to get from an idea to the thing that is actually live." "10x is that in minutes, 10x is that in hours, 10x is that by the end of the day," where others need weeks or months. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

He gives examples of range. Design is "one of the easiest industries to get into" (take a Udemy course, sign up for Figma and call yourself a designer), so "most people in the world are probably like half X designers or just like 3x designers": when you discuss a design problem, they do not notice what is obvious to a trained eye. A designer must do both UI and UX ("one doesn't exist without another"), and a 10x designer is also exceptional at copy. Otherwise an engineer ends up coordinating between a designer and a copywriter, the copy turns out too long, it goes back, and the work stalls: "if you're in charge, please be in charge of the whole thing." In engineering, range means that front end, back end, infrastructure, cloud and security all sit within one person's skill set, because "this ability allows you to get things done quickly." Stan hires only full-stack engineers. **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** UI (user interface) design concerns how a product looks and how its controls are laid out; UX (user experience) design concerns how a person moves through the product and whether it serves their goal. Figma is the dominant tool for both.

Koe connects the definition to a source he knows. He cites the employee handbook of the game company Valve: "they only hire T-shaped people," which he explains as people who are "specialized in one thing but they're also generalists; they have the umbrella of the surrounding skills that makes them exceptional." Vitali says he had not thought of it that way but agrees: "you have to have a significant vertical part of the T to be an expert in your area of the craft, but you also need to have an extremely wide horizontal part of the T to be able to be competent and get other things done," to complete things "entirely, in a way, by yourself." **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** Valve's employee handbook, published online in 2012, describes the company's flat structure and its preference for "T-shaped" people, who combine broad general skills with deep expertise in one area.

The 10x person, as Vitali defines it, is close to the figures this book has met before. The T shape is the "specialized generalist" of Chapter 20. Range is the reason Matt wanted everyone "full stack" at Eden (Section 37.3). And time to value is, for one person, what speed is for a startup: the interval between an idea and its reality. Most of all, the 10x person resembles the one-person business owner, who by necessity carries a whole process from idea to delivery. That is perhaps why the Stan founders found working with Koe so fast. A one-person business forces range; a team has to select for it.

#### Finding 10x people

John approaches recruiting "from a first principles basis," and he turns it into a marketing problem. The question is how to attract 10x people and convince them to join. Put yourself in the shoes of "my ideal customer persona, in this case, which is the 10x person." What do they want? To work with the very best people; to work on something meaningful and fulfilling; and to make a lot of money, with financial upside. **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

Stan reverse-engineered those three desires. For money: top-of-market salary and equity ("if you can find anyone who has a higher salary, let us know, we'll comp you better"), on the reasoning that hiring good, kind, hard-working, talented people "will pay for itself." For meaning: a mission-first culture. For ownership: "be your own entrepreneur, create your own legacy." Then "it's once again a marketing problem," solved either by outbound (cold email, one-on-one outreach) or by marketing (for example, talking about the company in content). "You're productizing a company, not on the product level but on the company level." Koe summarizes it as "thinking of Stan as the offer for these people." John describes his own role in those terms: "my job now is to go find awesome people... even better than us." **Source:** same.

"Productizing a company" is John's term, and it is a deliberate echo of "productize yourself" (Chapter 33). In Koe's model you turn your knowledge into an offer for customers; here the founders turn the company into an offer for candidates. The same tools apply: an avatar (the 10x person), their desires, an offer built backward from those desires, and distribution.

Vitali explains where such people are found. He and John met through a shared customer who introduced them (Vitali was building another software product; John was looking for a technical co-founder); they had known each other for a month before starting Stan ("we basically got married"). The foundational idea is that "10x people are extremely hard to come across": "people who are actually 10x don't even have time to apply; opportunities come to them; you need to go after them." "The most certain way... is through introductions and referrals," because "every single person knows at least one 10x player." It takes discipline and consistency: ping your network ("I'm looking for this; do you know someone you think highly of?"). The result he cites: in the fourth quarter, Stan's support team hired fifteen people, thirteen of them through referrals, with the highest retention and success rates. The tactic, stated by one of the co-founders (most likely John; the attribution in the transcript is ambiguous): "ask the friend you're most impressed by: who are all the smartest people you know that you're impressed by," talk to them, most will not fit, "but then you ask them the same question, and you just compound that flywheel." **Source:** same.

The referral flywheel is a network search with a built-in quality filter. Each person is asked for the people who impress them, so each step moves toward the higher end of the distribution, and each conversation produces more leads than it consumes. It compounds in the same way an audience does.

Koe adds the qualification that keeps the advice honest. "It probably becomes a lot easier when the offer of Stan [to] join is right; if you're trying to do that when you don't have something to give, or something that's better, or something worth their time, then [it] may not turn out how you like." "That's the initial hump of entrepreneurship: you're just fighting uphill," and as you add value to your value proposition, "it gets a little easier to roll the ball." **Source:** same.

The qualification ties the hiring advice back to the beginning of this chapter. A company like Stan, with tens of millions in revenue, can offer top-of-market pay, a proven mission and the chance to work with people already known to be excellent. A new company cannot. For a founder at the start, the 10x person is, like the first customer, someone you have to earn by building value first. The order Koe recommends (one-person business, audience, cash flow, then a team) is in part a way of arriving at the hiring problem with something to offer.

### 37.6 Scaling Without Losing Alignment

The last problem the Stan founders describe is the one a one-person business cannot have at all: keeping many minds pointed at the same target. This section reconstructs their account of what "scale" physically means, why the founder's own strengths become a ceiling, what breaks first, and the practices they use to hold a growing organization together. The frameworks belong to John Hugh and Vitali; the section closes by drawing together what the chapter has shown about which of Koe's principles transfer to a team and which do not.

#### What scale physically means

John finds "scale" "such an abstract term... so unhelpful," and he tries to make it "physical and visceral." Alone, things are easy: "you don't have to catch up anyone on your context," and many skills you take for granted live inside you without needing to be explained. In Stan's early days he filmed content, called and messaged customers, onboarded them, and knew what product to build: "like 25 different jobs." Now, with 60,000 people to serve, you have to hire, align values and synchronize definitions of success ("my definition of success could be very different from Vitali's"). **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

His analogy is travel: "you know when you go on solo travel it's so chill, and then like at four or five people... it just becomes chaos; it's like, why is it so hard to get [to] an 11:00 lunch?" An organization follows the same path: John in his dorm room; then a co-founder, with "a ton of mind sync" but daily catch-ups; then more and more people. **Source:** same.

"Context" and "mind sync" are the key terms. Context is everything a person knows about why things are the way they are; mind sync is the degree to which two people share it. A solo founder has perfect mind sync with himself, so every decision is made with full context and no communication cost. Every additional person is a mind that has to be synchronized, and the synchronization is never complete.

John adds a structural claim: "the complexity of the network grows exponentially as you add more nodes on the network." At about ten people, "it's still pretty chill": everyone is on the same page, they can talk weekly, everyone can give input. "As you get to 50 people, say 50 is the number where it breaks," and Stan was getting close to that number. **Source:** same.

**Complementary context:** a common way to formalize this intuition counts the possible one-to-one communication lines in a group of n people, n(n−1)/2: 45 lines for 10 people, 1,225 for 50. Strictly speaking that growth is quadratic rather than exponential, but the point John makes, that coordination costs grow much faster than headcount, is the same one made by Fred Brooks's well-known observation that adding people to a late software project makes it later. The figure of 50 as a breaking point is John's estimate from experience, not a measured threshold.

#### The curse of the individual contributor

John's most personal contribution is an account of how his own strength became Stan's limit. "The curse of an individual contributor is maybe they don't end up being a good manager and a leader," just as "your best salesperson won't always be your best VP of sales." He was wired as a "single player," and he took Stan "from zero to close to 30 million ARR just off of my insights alone," with a scrappy team but mostly himself "making every single decision and giving every direction." It worked at the time. But in recent months "things really started to break down": "we got to success and then we just hit this flatline, because we had hit the limits of the structure of the organization." The structure was John at the top and people doing what he said, and "John can only make so many decisions in a day." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

The fix was a restructuring: from "a single node... a creator-based business... personality-driven business, to a real organization of incredible people who are owning their own nodes." The founders' role becomes making sure "we're all rowing to the literal exact same point," with each node held by someone "better than us," who builds their own team, "10x-ing... the capacity of the organization." He calls it "a painful period of evolution" and a large personal lesson: "this is on me for limiting the business longer." **Source:** same.

He describes the same shift as the third lever of Stan's growth: learning "how to build an organization rather than doing a thing." It means going from "a creator-based business where a single person... John" does something, to "how cool would it be if we could 10x the number of creators who are even more successful than me." The organization becomes "this abstract entity, this engine that goes about replicating and finding more people that are way better than me at 100x the volume, and training them... and letting them just go do their own thing," which "from a volume perspective is just going to outweigh literally anything I could do myself." **Source:** same.

"Single player," "single node," "personality-driven business" and "owning their own nodes" are John's terms. Together they describe the transition this whole chapter has been approaching. A one-person business is, by definition, a single node, and Koe's system is built to make that node as effective as possible: leverage, focus, writing as the base skill, decisions made with full context. John's account says that the qualities that make a single node effective (deciding everything, holding all the context, being the best performer) become the bottleneck as soon as the organization's goals exceed one person's daily capacity for decisions. The founder's task changes from doing the thing to building the engine that does it.

The parallel with Koe's own experience is close, and Koe seems to recognize it, since he asks these questions as a founder learning to lead. He told his co-founders that he can "lead myself just fine" but did not know where to draw the line between guiding people and letting them do their own thing, and that he wanted everyone to have an "ownership mindset" (Section 37.4). John's "owning their own nodes" is a structural answer to the same question: ownership is not a mindset you ask people to adopt, but an area of responsibility you give them, held by someone better than you at it.

#### What breaks first: alignment

Vitali names what breaks first: "the thing that breaks is alignment." Because network complexity grows so fast, it becomes harder to keep everyone on the same page. When Stan was about thirty people, someone asked team members "what are we doing, and why are we doing it," and "you would not believe how many different versions of reality we've heard." Vitali had spent a great deal of time in leadership meetings on the what, the how and the why, yet an engineer building a feature explained his work, in effect, as "Vitali told me we need to get it done." "It's embarrassing how little alignment there was." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md.

The lesson is to "step up our communication game" and be intentional about how information flows "from the top nodes down the tree": the all-hands meetings, what managers talk about, the business review meetings. He adds that his lack of eagerness to grow headcount comes from realizing that more people means putting more time into "the systems of operating this machine that has actually nothing to do with your business," rather than into the customer and the product: "a whole new world" for a first-time founder. **Source:** same.

"Alignment," as John defines it, is "everyone thinking about the same thing and knowing exactly where we're all going." "Versions of reality" is Vitali's phrase for what happens without it: each person builds their own interpretation of the company's purpose from fragments, and the interpretations diverge. The engineer who knew only that "Vitali told me" is the organizational equivalent of a person pursuing an assigned goal without knowing its reason, the condition Chapter 1 described as the default state of a programmed mind. The work gets done, but it cannot adapt, because the person doing it does not know what it is for.

John's metaphor shows why small divergences matter: "if everyone's one degree off, like you wanted to go to Boston but you ended up in New York, whatever that travel is across the Atlantic... it magnifies [with] scale and complexity." **Source:** same. The image is of a long voyage in which a tiny error of heading grows into a large error of arrival. The larger the organization and the longer its horizon, the more a one-degree misalignment costs.

#### Practices that hold a team together

The founders describe four practices, each aimed at a different part of the alignment problem.

**Repetition.** Vitali's "most impactful" tip: "if you said something less than 10 times, you haven't said it enough." They begin every large company meeting with "this is what we are doing this year," reminding everyone where "north" is, and all conversations should relate back to it. "Nothing is too much: broken record, just repeat, repeat, repeat." **Source:** How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md. The principle runs against a founder's instinct. A founder who has said something once feels it has been communicated, because in his own mind it is obvious. Repetition is how the founder's context, which other people do not share, becomes their context.

**Ritual.** John describes how a gimmick became a ritual. Stan holds a weekly All Hands meeting at which leaders talk about what matters most. One day, after reviewing the year's goals, he added a "Pop Quiz" slide: "what's our year-end goal?", count to three. Everyone got it wrong. He forgot to remove the slide the next week, and it "became a thing" people look forward to: "Pop Quiz, what's our goal for 2025, 3, 2, 1," and everyone says together, "acquire and serve 200,000 entrepreneurs." It "seems so silly, because you would never need to tell yourself that," but with fifty people on different pages "you've got to find these specific ways to maximize shared time together." **Source:** same. The line "you would never need to tell yourself that" is the exact point of difference between a single player and a team. One person never has to remind himself of his goal in a shared ritual. A group does.

**Meta time.** Since their early days, the co-founders have held a Sunday meeting (3 p.m. in Toronto, lunchtime in Los Angeles): "two hours, unstructured, just in the presence of each other, catching up." It used to be daily; now it is weekly. Vitali explains why it exists: their worlds are connected but also disconnected; they observe each other's decisions without context and often would not agree with them; things are complex enough that a decision is not necessarily wrong, but it requires understanding. "What you don't talk about, people will assume, and those assumptions oftentimes would be incorrect." John describes what happens in it: sometimes there is an agenda, sometimes they are "arguing to all hell," sometimes they just catch up. "It's important to have meta time on top of doing time," because from Monday to Friday they are "drinking from the fire hose," with no time to think at a higher level. **Source:** same.

"Meta time" and "doing time" are the founders' terms. Doing time is execution; meta time is time spent thinking about the execution and synchronizing context. The concept has a clear analogue in Koe's teaching for individuals: the weekly review and the separation between working in a system and working on it. The difference is that for a team, meta time must be shared, because the context that needs synchronizing is distributed across several minds.

**Shared presence.** John's "hot take": "if you're going to build an organization of success, I think you need to be in person for a lot of it," because "shared time together, that ambient time," matters, together with the leader's intentionality so that everyone knows where they are going (through live calls or other content). Vitali agrees and "would live and die on the hill": remote work has advantages and can be productive, and they spend a lot on offices (their first was a WeWork). The reason is that you are creating "the systems and the environment where good ideas can thrive." "Building a company is not about efficiently moving in a particular direction at the fastest way possible; the hardest thing about building a company is actually figuring out what is the right thing to do," and those ideas come from "random catch-ups," "water cooler conversations" and eating lunch together. In Toronto and Los Angeles, about twenty people move tables together to eat, and those conversations spark experiments and clarity. If you have to schedule a Zoom call to talk about something, it "would never happen"; Slack helps, but nothing comes close to being "shoulder-to-shoulder with the people that you think extremely highly of." Koe adds that his own team in Toronto, Matt and others, works around a kitchen counter, and four of them are roommates. **Source:** same.

"Ambient time" and "shoulder-to-shoulder" are their terms for unplanned shared presence. The argument rests on a distinction worth keeping: efficiency in a known direction can be achieved remotely, but discovering the right direction depends on chance encounters between ideas, and chance encounters need shared space. It is the team-level version of the role Koe gives to unstructured time in creativity (Chapter 18): insight comes from collisions you cannot schedule.

The in-person argument marks a change in Koe's own story. In 2024 he presented Cortex's team of autonomous contractors as "one-person businesses collaborating with one-person businesses" and celebrated their independence (Section 37.3). In 2025 he agreed with the Stan founders' case for in-person work, described his Toronto team around a kitchen counter, and later diagnosed the team's structure as a cause of its slowness. He does not give his own reason for the shift; the reasons in the corpus are the Stan founders' (good ideas come from ambient time) and the experience of the Japan rebuild, in which two co-founders working side by side moved much faster than the distributed team. **Source:** Learn This Skill If You Want To Be Relevant In 10 Years.md (2024-07-13); How To Grow An Audience With Zero Followers & Build A Profitable Startup.md (2025-01-28); Kortex The $3 Million Dollar Mistake.md (2025-11-24).

#### What transfers from one person to a team, and what does not

The chapter has followed one person's system into a context it was not designed for. It is now possible to say with some precision which parts survived the trip and which had to be rebuilt. The table below is this book's synthesis of the material in Sections 37.2 to 37.6; neither Koe nor his guests present it in this form.

| In the one-person business | What happened in the team | What the principle became |
|---|---|---|
| Self-imposed deadlines create pressure and action | Aggressive deadlines broke the product (Kortex) | Pressure works when the person under it holds the context and controls the variables; give the team context first, and add "breathing room" to public dates |
| The founder holds all the context | Context had to be synchronized; "versions of reality" multiplied | Alignment must be built deliberately: repetition, rituals, meta time, shared presence |
| One person decides everything, fast | The founder's decisions became the ceiling ("John can only make so many decisions in a day") | Build an organization of people who own their own nodes |
| Iteration is cheap: build, test, rip out | Compartmentalized owners resisted removing their parts | Structure for iteration: everyone full stack, each person owning a whole feature |
| "I am the niche": build what you would use | Koe used Kortex daily, and the market did not want it | Building for yourself is a compass; it must be checked against customer outcomes |
| An audience and a validated system sell what you build | Distribution did not fix a product without product-market fit | Distribution amplifies a product; it does not substitute for one |
| Range: one person carries the whole process | The startup had to select people with range | Hire T-shaped, full-stack people measured by time to value |
| Leverage over hours | More people did not mean more output | Growth is not proportional to headcount; staff up only where the work is human |
| Persistence and iteration | Koe persisted too long in one implementation | Persist in the mission and in what evidence supports; iterate on the means |

Two observations follow from the table.

First, almost none of Koe's principles turned out to be false. What turned out to be false was the assumption, which he states explicitly, that they "would transfer over one-to-one." Each principle worked for a single person because certain conditions were automatically true for a single person: full context, full control, perfect mind sync, no other owners, a single identity to defend. A team removes those conditions, and the principle has to be restated in a form that does not depend on them. This is what Koe meant by refining a practice "to the point of revealing what the principle actually was."

Second, several of the remedies the team needed are things the one-person business trained without naming them. The 10x person (range, expertise, time to value) is close to a description of a capable solo operator. Founder-market fit is "you are the niche." The core job to be done is the value equation of Chapter 31 seen from the product side. Repetition of the goal is what an individual does privately with a written vision. The difference is that in a team these have to become structures (hiring criteria, product principles, meetings) rather than habits of one mind.

This is also why Koe keeps recommending the same order to others. The one-person business is where the principles are learned under the simplest conditions, and where the money and audience that make a riskier venture possible are earned without outside capital. The team is a later stage, chosen, in his case, for its challenge and for what it reveals about the person who builds it. In his words, it brings "new sets of problems... better sets of problems."

### Exercises

1. **Reading your own catalogue.** List the projects, jobs or ventures you have started and abandoned, as Koe does with his "seven" failed businesses. For each one, write what it revealed (an interest, a missing skill, a market you knew from the inside) and which skills it left you. Then try to describe, as concretely as Koe does with "this skill, this skill, this skill, these all piece together," what structure those skills could form together. Where does your list resist being read this way, and what would that resistance tell you?

2. **Evaluating evidence.** Koe offers his income figures "for credibility." Using the figures and their variations reported in Section 37.1, write a short assessment: what do these numbers legitimately support (for example, that a writing-based business can reach seven figures without a team), what do they not support, and what additional information (costs, profit, time period, audience size before monetization) would you need before using them to make a decision about your own career?

3. **Persist or quit.** The Stan founders describe entrepreneurship as "a persistence game"; Koe says of Kortex, "we should have called it quits earlier." Choose a current project of yours. Separate the mission (what you are trying to achieve) from the implementation (the specific form you are pursuing it in). For each, list the evidence for and against continuing. Then state a criterion, decided in advance, that would tell you to abandon the implementation while keeping the mission.

4. **Finding the deeper underlying truth.** Koe discovered that a personal practice (self-imposed deadlines) failed when applied to a team, and concluded that he had to refine it "to the point of revealing what the principle actually was." Pick one practice that works well for you alone (a routine, a planning method, a way of making decisions). Identify the conditions that make it work for you, and rewrite it as a principle that would still hold if you had to apply it to a group of five people who do not share your context.

5. **Designing a first hire.** Apply John Hugh's method to your own work, real or imagined. Write your two zones of genius (two, not three) and as many of "the 17,000 things" you dislike doing as you can. Then do the visual exercise: describe, "to a joyful degree," what success would look like with a person who took those things off your hands, and turn the description into job characteristics. Finally, state when, by Vitali's standard of being "dragged into it," you would actually be justified in hiring, and how you would know.

6. **Diagnosing alignment.** Think of a group you belong to (a team, a family business, a club, a project with friends). Without consulting anyone, write in one sentence what the group is trying to achieve this year and why. Then, if you can, ask three other members the same question and compare the "versions of reality." What would a "broken record" practice or a "Pop Quiz" look like in that group, and what would the group need to share that it currently assumes?

7. **Questioning the model.** The chapter presents the one-person business as a stage and the team as a later step, while Justin Welsh moved in the opposite direction, from large teams to a one-person business, and imagined a "distribution studio" as a third option. Construct the strongest argument you can for staying a one-person business permanently, using Koe's own principles. Then identify the specific conditions (temperament, kind of product, ambition, life stage) under which that argument would stop applying.
